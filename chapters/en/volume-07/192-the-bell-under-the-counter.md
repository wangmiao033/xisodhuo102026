# Chapter 192: The Bell Under the Counter

Zhou didn't grab the jacket.

He threaded two empty coat hangers through the basket's handles and lifted its base clear of the floor. The blue sleeve stiffened inside the holes. The knocking against the pressing-room door stopped. Holding both hangers, Zhou didn't dare change his grip. Sweat soon appeared at his temples.

“Where?”

Lin Mo looked at the counter. Beyond a ledger, cup, and half-empty packet of biscuits, a clear patch lay under the end of the track. Xu Qin moved the cup left-handed. Xiao Lu carried the biscuits and ledger to the low cabinet without opening the book. Zhou slowly lowered the basket onto the counter.

The sleeve relaxed as plastic met wood. Its cuff remained through one hole, creased against the edge. Sun Cheng's breathing filled the shop. That was enough, he said. Stop lifting.

Lin Mo watched Xu Qin's wrist. No thread disappeared, but the buried section went no deeper. He asked her to sit and describe what she'd felt.

“Numb. Same as before.”

“When it went into the basket?”

“No worse. I heard him hit the door. That frightened me.”

More useful than calling it safe. Lin Mo explained the sequence to Qiu: the sleeve pulled toward the inner room while low down; raising it to counter height stopped the pulling. Nobody's skin had touched the jacket. Qiu crouched beside the basket without tugging its trapped cuff.

“Keep it like this. Can the person inside speak?”

“Yes.” Sun Cheng sounded hoarse.

“Did you break the skin on your head?”

“I can't feel blood. My throat's bad.”

Qiu asked him not to keep straining to clear it. Just describe the discomfort. Listening, Lin Mo realized he'd been holding his own breath. Releasing it left his shoulder aching. Zhou noticed and asked whether he wanted to go outside.

Instead he asked to move back half a wheel's width, leaving room on his left. Xiao Lu offered water without pressing the cup to his mouth. He took it. Cool now, but still water he could swallow.

“Did you know the basket would work?” she asked quietly.

“No. I wanted to stop someone catching it.”

Her fingers tightened around the cup, then released. She heard him admit uncertainty without using it to insist on returning to their room. They hadn't reached agreement. They could at least leave the disagreement where it was.

The mains switch was on the street-side wall. Qiu had already had Xu Qin disconnect the appliances, leaving one lighting circuit. He explained that everything would now be switched off and called for portable lights. Once beams covered the counter and doorway, she pulled the switch with her left hand.

The ceiling lights died. The dryer didn't move. The blue jacket lay quietly in the basket.

Several breaths later, metal clicked under the counter. Xu Qin stood. Qiu asked her to wait and shone his light underneath. An old electric bell was fixed below the wood, its cut cable coiled nearby. A separate cotton thread attached to the clapper passed through a hole toward the back of the counter.

“It wasn't powered anyway,” Xu Qin said. “It used to connect to the machine. Broke two years ago.”

At Lin Mo's request, Zhou slid blank paper under the bell with a hanger. A moving clapper might mark it without anyone putting a face inside the cabinet. They waited. Nothing rang. The thread hung loose as a scrap left by a tailor.

Could it be cut? Xu Qin immediately shook her head at Qiu's question. Lin Mo said they didn't know whether it was connected to the threads in her wrist. Cutting those had already constricted the man inside. Another strand wasn't an invitation to repeat it.

Someone outside asked what was happening and was moved farther away. The roller shutter was fully raised, the street visible, yet Lin Mo felt the air thinning. He recognized his own tension rather than announce the shop was consuming oxygen.

Xu Qin produced an old white sheet, washed but torn at the edges, normally used under bulky items on the counter. It could line the shallow basket. Lin Mo looked at the trapped cuff and asked Zhou not to pull it free. Until they understood where the jacket came from and where it belonged, they couldn't tell whether the cloth was caught or holding someone.

Qiu approached the inner door. More people had come, he told Sun Cheng through the existing gap, but nobody would repeat the forced entry. Sun Cheng asked after his wife. She said she was sitting down, leaving her wrist unmentioned. Lin Mo didn't announce it across the room for her. He asked her to rest her forearm on a folded towel instead of holding it suspended.

The gap was less than a palm wide. Light revealed a cloth shoe pointing outward. Qiu asked Sun Cheng to move his foot only if it didn't hurt more. The toe turned left, revealing a table leg behind it. Turning right, it stopped. His right shoulder was being pulled backward, Sun Cheng said. He didn't dare continue.

A pale patch beneath the sole proved to be old tile when illuminated. Not every white thing was paper. Lin Mo asked whether the door pressed against him. No, space remained behind it. His right arm felt enclosed in a sleeve too tight to wear. Moving forward tightened the collar.

“What are you wearing?”

“Gray undershirt. Shorts.”

“Not the jacket?”

“No. I touched it, and then I was in here.”

Xu Qin bit her lip. Tears fell onto her apron. She'd bought breakfast and called him out to eat. He'd said to wait. After their argument over the jacket, she'd thought he was sulking. Only when she saw that his shoe couldn't advance a single step did she understand he wasn't brushing her off.

Lin Mo thought of the chopsticks on his sheet. Refusing help might mean wanting another try, or being unable to explain the fear. He asked Qiu to keep the light beside the shoe instead of on Sun Cheng's face and let him recover his breath.

Xiao Lu soaked up the floor water with thick layers of tissue. Her knees weren't ready for prolonged crouching. She nudged the paper into place with her shoe, then collected it with a long-handled dustpan. Afterward she sat by the low cabinet without waiting for an instruction. Her injured palm lay upward; the other hand held water.

They turned to the disused dryer. Beyond the work-area barrier, its round door stood open on old wooden hangers. No footprints or hole behind it. A flat metal box rested on top, dusty, Sun Cheng's name written at the edge. Old receipts, Xu Qin said. Box and track had come with the shop. Nobody had sorted them.

“Could the jacket be the previous owner's?” Zhou asked.

She didn't know. The owner had moved; the number had changed. Last night someone answering the old number had denied knowing them. No convenient call would bring an explanation.

A narrow opening in the machine's side had once held a receiving basket. Flaking paint and dust concealed printed instructions. Xiao Lu wiped away the loose dust with a dry tissue. Lin Mo could read the last words: ORIGINAL INTAKE.

Qiu shifted his beam. The complete instruction emerged.

RETURN REJECTED ITEMS WITH THE REJECTION COPY TO THE ORIGINAL INTAKE.

An arrow pointed toward the narrow opening. Xu Qin explained that the old equipment had returned sorted small items along the track. After the drum failed, they'd used only the overhead section for hanging clothes, leaving the lower intake alone.

“What color is the rejection copy?”

“White. Bottom sheet. Yellow and pink above it. All the old slips were like that.”

She fetched the old blank book hanging beside the counter. Their new receipts were printed by computer; a few brittle pages remained here. Intake on top, handover in the middle, rejection beneath. The final sheet was completed as appropriate. An empty space didn't mean anyone had rejected anything.

Lin Mo didn't ask her to tear out a fresh one. Blank paper couldn't make what had happened unhappen.

Xiao Lu placed the book near, not touching, the jacket. The yellow collar slip had matching boxes, held by a red clip. A pink copy lay below, but no white one. The narrow corner Lin Mo had seen belonged to the pink sheet.

47.

Beneath the number was Sun Cheng's crooked signature beside his handwritten COLLECTED. Lin Mo couldn't recognize his normal handwriting. Xu Qin could, and Sun Cheng confirmed it from behind the door. He'd wanted to leave his name so the unclaimed jacket wouldn't get mixed into customers' things. Only after writing did he feel someone inside the collar smiling against him.

He'd meant to take medicine to his mother last night. A customer's suit was two days late, Xu Qin wanted the counter cleared, and they'd argued. He'd gathered the unregistered old things. This jacket was in no bag and still felt damp. He assumed someone from an earlier batch had left it.

“I wrote collected to cancel the slip. My name, my account. She shouldn't have to pay for it later.”

Xu Qin looked down. After a while, she said a neighbor had delivered the medicine. His acknowledgment held an ordinary man's relief. He was more than a shadow waiting for them to solve it.

“Who was it?” she asked.

“No face. I just felt someone waiting for me to take it.”

He stopped. Lin Mo didn't force one frightened glimpse into a complete person. What they had was a jacket with a torn left cuff, two collar sheets, a missing copy, and a man who'd signed the wrong place.

Xu Qin suddenly searched the ledger on the low cabinet, then the wastebasket. Zhou stopped her long enough to ask what she wanted.

“White paper! There was some last night. Cheng said he didn't need it, tore it off, left it on the pressing table. I thought it was rubbish. I swept it farther inside.”

“Did you see its number?”

“No.”

Sun Cheng's shadow had withdrawn slightly from the door. He couldn't see the floor, he said. Midway through the sentence something seemed to jerk him, clipping his voice short.

The thread beneath the counter tightened again.

The jacket's right sleeve slid over the basket's rim. Its collar clip clicked. At the same moment, the black thread lifted Xu Qin's wrist, catching her apron edge. She hadn't reached to take the garment, but the pull drew her toward the counter.

Lin Mo told Zhou to steady her stool and help her sit. The jacket's shoulders rose as if a chest were filling them. The basket remained firmly on the counter. Raising it once hadn't solved everything.

At the first bell stroke, the right cuff passed the counter's outer edge.

Lin Mo asked Qiu to hold a hanger crosswise beneath it, not pulling or hooking, simply preventing the cloth from dropping farther. The cuff landed on the bar. A second stroke sounded, its tail half as long. Sun Cheng swore, then said his shoulder felt a little looser.

“It wants to come outside,” Zhou said.

“Outside where?”

Nobody waited in the collection space. But Xu Qin looked at the window, lips shaking.

An empty blue collar appeared in its reflection at the height of Lin Mo's head. Zhou and the wheelchair reflected clearly. Across Lin Mo's shoulders lay a jacket he wasn't wearing.

He slowly moved his left hand clear of the sleeve.

The reflection didn't follow.
