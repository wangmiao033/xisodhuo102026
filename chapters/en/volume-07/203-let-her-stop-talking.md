# Chapter 203: Let Her Stop Talking

Tang Jing's mouth had stopped moving, but people were still talking to her.

First came a medic, asking her not to rush an answer, just to raise her chin. Then a man approached from the stairs, asking what had happened and whether Luo Huaisheng could confirm an equipment fault. The questions collided. Tang Jing moved her lips and managed only a dry cough.

Lin Mo rested his left hand on the armrest.

“Let the medic see her first.”

The man wore a staff badge. Sweat shone on his forehead. He said he wasn't taking a statement; he needed something to tell the families downstairs. The afternoon activity had to be canceled too. They couldn't say nothing.

“Say they're out and being examined.”

“But why they came out—”

The medic asked him to move aside. Tang Jing pressed trembling fingers below her throat. Lin Mo didn't add a louder voice to the argument.

Xiao Lu freed a bag strap from beside his footrest. Asked whether she wanted to go outside first, she said they'd go together once his leg was settled.

His knee hadn't learned that the screen had gone dark. Heat pressed through his trousers, and his braced wrist lay awkwardly against a fold of cloth. When Zhou offered to help, Lin Mo pointed with his left hand. Only that fold needed moving.

Han Wei came out without the microphone. He asked the staff not to move the machinery. Someone demanded why powerless equipment needed guarding.

“She was still speaking after the power went off,” Han said. “Moving it might expose somebody else. Leave it here first.”

His hand remained curled as though holding a cable. He opened it, knuckles pale, and sat against the wall. He didn't turn their attention into an announcement that he understood everything.

Luo Huaisheng was helped into a chair. He asked for his coat.

“The blue one. My keys are in the pocket.”

Told they'd fetch it later, he asked again. His arm could bend now, but he still hesitated to extend it. Zhou consulted the person guarding the doorway rather than going back himself. A responder brought the identified coat out whole. Only when Luo felt his keys did his shoulders lower.

“Wasn't I supposed to be talking about myself?”

He looked at Han Wei.

“Last week I talked about a bridge. Across the river. They demolished it later. That wasn't what she was reading.”

Han said the displayed passage differed from the recording. He took out his phone, paused, then put it away without playing a comparison for everyone.

“You don't have to tell us about the bridge now,” Lin Mo said.

Luo stared. Xiao Lu stepped closer and repeated the words slowly, letting him answer for himself. He asked whether he had to return another day.

“Get examined today. This place is closed for now,” a responder told him.

That didn't settle every possible next visit. Luo gripped his coat and stopped asking. Lin Mo kept the unheard bridge story separate from the diary in his bag.

A corner of the copied notes showed. Zhou held it down against a draft. The staff member noticed and asked whether it was a record retrieved from the machine. Could he photograph it for the incident report?

Lin Mo checked which page he meant.

“That's personal writing. I can describe the operations separately.”

The phone stayed at the man's chest. Lin Mo heard how softly he'd said personal, almost apologizing for making a fuss. People were injured; everybody was busy. Who was he to delay them over a page bearing his name?

Xiao Lu folded the bag's lip across the exposed lines and asked what the report actually needed. The man lowered his phone. Why they'd consulted the text, how they'd accessed it, and what happened afterward.

“I can give you that,” Lin Mo said. “Not the family history.”

Han Wei raised his head. Retrieval and playback were different operations. Then he stopped to ask whether he'd understood the narrow display correctly; he hadn't seen all its lettering from where he'd stood.

“Leave what you didn't see blank.”

They moved beside a window, clear of the transfer route. Outside, half a tree's leaves were gray with dust, the rest pale in sunlight. Nobody behind it was waiting to hand Lin Mo a complete past.

The person recording the account balanced paper on a clipboard. Lin Mo described the movements before and after Tang Jing's repetition stopped, the sounds from her and from the equipment, the old man's arm lowering under his own control. He kept the order slow enough for the writer to follow. A sound coming from a particular place was something he'd heard; what the machine intended was another matter. The narrow display had shown certain words, and they had acted on what they could inspect, not on an assurance that every similar device would behave the same way. When the writer used a broader phrase, Lin Mo brought him back to the people they'd just watched leave their positions.

His index came from an earlier text-storage attempt. He didn't know every entrance it might reach.

When the pen accelerated, he paused.

“Don't write that we found a way to restore memory.”

The writer crossed out half a line.

“Then what?”

“We retrieved words. I understand them. I don't remember the events.”

Suddenly he wanted to open the bag. His past lay under a layer of cloth. In quiet, perhaps he'd notice a familiar turn of handwriting, a pressure in a stroke, some place he'd once stood.

He left it closed. His sister had been mentioned in those pages, but she was standing here now, tired and waiting for a way home. He could not turn her into the answer to every gap between two lines. Even if she remembered an event, she might not want to spend this corridor explaining it, with other people's phones and questions so close. He looked at the clipboard again and waited for the next practical question.

Tang Jing passed, holding tissues. She seemed about to thank him, but the medic reminded her to rest her throat. She pressed her lips together, embarrassed. Lin Mo nodded without waiting for a complete sentence.

A few steps later she turned back. He assumed she still wanted to thank him until she pointed at her throat, then the medic's water. Asked whether she wanted a drink, she shook her head and coughed.

Han borrowed a pen from the staff and let her write on the paper she already held.

Even swallowing saliva hurts.

Lin Mo could see the cramped words. They wouldn't heal her throat or stop every copy elsewhere. They were simply what she needed to say now, and it wasn't gratitude.

The medic acknowledged it and told her not to force another swallow while he looked again. She put down the pen, tucked her badge inside her coat and went with him. Lin Mo returned to the clipboard to correct two remaining details.

During the second readback, Han asked the writer to stop.

“There's something I hadn't explained. Earlier, while she was on the pedal, I thought changing the rhythm might help. I asked her to count with me.”

Tang Jing was no longer at the doorway.

“Put that in too. We didn't know what to do from the start.”

How many numbers? Two, Han thought. He couldn't tell whether the third had left his mouth. His face reddened, as if this uncertainty devalued everything he'd said.

Lin Mo didn't supply a number.

“Did she seem worse?”

“She coughed harder. That's why I stopped.”

“Then include what you saw.”

Han wiped his forehead without explaining again that he'd meant to help. Lin Mo watched the new line appear. He too had almost wanted the retrieved diary to prove he'd always known when to stop. The broken umbrella on his unread pages wouldn't cooperate.

A vehicle door shut outside. Luo hadn't been forgotten. His coat lay on his knees; through the glass, he gave a small wave without another arm leading his own. Han returned the borrowed pen to the staff member. Lin Mo listened to the entire sequence and corrected one direction by pointing at the real doorway.

“Home, or sit somewhere nearby first?” Zhou asked when they finished.

“Home. Slowly.”

Xiao Lu tested the bag's weight before lifting it. Lin Mo noticed its old bottom seam and told her not to strain herself.

“Only as far as the car,” she said. “I'm not promising for the next few hours.”

In the courtyard someone called Han Wei over to ask about moving the afternoon's supplies. Voices bounced off the walls. Lin Mo barely listened until he noticed Xiao Lu had stopped by the exit.

Several displaced chairs blocked their route, leaving only a narrow gap.

“Wait,” she told Zhou.

He had seen it too. He asked the movers to shift one chair instead of squeezing past her. Apologizing, a man pulled it; one leg caught another, and the seats scraped together with a piercing noise.

Lin Mo's shoulder clenched. He didn't say he was fine. He asked them to stop until the dizziness eased, then indicated a route that didn't require shifting the whole row. One chair at a time, they made space. Getting people out alive hadn't made anybody obliged to move faster now.

Xiao Lu checked the level ground outside and returned beside him.

“Need a rest too?” he asked.

“In the car. Don't choose for me again.”

He nodded. Zhou wheeled him through the cleared gap.

Beside the car, Lin Mo found his water still in Zhou's hand. He asked for a drink, then for the bottle to be placed within reach on his left. The water was warmer than when they'd left. His throat eased; a sentence he'd failed to explain by the clipboard remained difficult.

He asked Zhou to adjust the seat before they attempted the transfer. His left foot wasn't secure on the first attempt, so they stopped and repositioned it. He didn't use the injured right leg to make up the difference.

Xiao Lu waited with the bag at her feet, examining the back of her hand as though counting dust already gone.

“You didn't explain the umbrella for me,” he said quietly.

She looked up. “Did you want me to?”

“No. I noticed, that's all.”

She lifted the bag without smiling for that discovery. The doorway shadow crossed half her face. Her head hurt too, she said. When they got back, she didn't want to listen to him read immediately.

“Then I won't read to you yet.”

Lin Mo settled into the seat and waited until the wrapped notebook was somewhere he could see. No light appeared on the cloth, no line ordered him to open it now. He rested his injured hand on its support, heard Xiao Lu shut the door, and asked the driver to lower the window just a little.
