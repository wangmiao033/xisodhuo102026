# Chapter 204: She Didn't Take the Umbrella

The old notebook wasn't the first thing placed on the table when they reached the rental.

Xiao Lu set down their unfinished water and went to draw the curtain. Outside, someone dragged a drying rack across concrete. She waited until the scraping ended, then left a hand's width uncovered. She was going to lie down. Don't call her for food.

“I'll ask later,” Lin Mo said.

“Later.”

Zhou positioned the wheelchair beside the bed. They adjusted things in stages, without assuming yesterday's success excused today's pauses. Lin Mo stayed in the chair, his knee supported, the ache in his shoulder spreading toward his neck. Only after the cushion lay flat did he ask for the notebook.

“Which one?”

He could only say the old one. He knew what its different papers were called, but their combined weight wasn't familiar. Zhou opened the bag and let him point, removing the cloth-wrapped volume without scattering its contents.

“Want me to stay?”

“Don't you have things to do?”

“I'm asking what you want.”

Lin Mo nearly said no. Needing someone beside him just to read his past seemed too much. Then he tried to bring the book closer and his shoulder stopped him.

“Help me put it where I can reach. Then go.”

Zhou found clean, stiff cardboard to support it and checked the water was reachable too. His phone would be on. Once Lin Mo had tested the cup, he left.

In the quiet, Lin Mo freed a loose thread from a tiny split in the paper before turning a page. No pulling, no cutting. The mattress rustled beyond the curtain. Xiao Lu had finally lain down.

The old photograph slipped forward. The woman's face wasn't as different from his reflection as he'd hoped. His sister's hand remained blurred by movement. He put it back without checking the word sister on the reverse again. Knowing what a paper said and being able to bear it today were different things.

The original diary was messier than he'd expected. Its first line sloped downward; a puncture admitted a speck of shadow. Another hand wrote more tightly below, squeezing words against the edge. Fang Ji had helped transcribe it. The clearest handwriting not being his didn't make the whole thing a stranger's story.

He compared the notes brought home today with the introduction. Those few lines agreed. The events beyond hadn't been retrieved at the site. He set the notes aside and read the original paper.

I took her umbrella before she'd agreed to lend it, then made her put it away when I came back.

That was the line he'd happened upon earlier.

He understood umbrellas, understood asking before borrowing. But the person who carried one under his arm, slowing near acquaintances to look as though he had serious business—that particular, embarrassing person remained inaccessible.

The page offered him little dignity. His sister refused to take it; he felt challenged and called her petty. When the cracked handle still worked, he used that to prove she'd overreacted. The date wasn't certain. Whether anyone else stood at the doorway was left unresolved. His own habit of putting the crack after her refusal was not.

His palm grew damp against the edge.

He thought of wanting to explain himself when the staff member asked to photograph his private words, afraid of seeming difficult. The situations differed. He hadn't surrendered his family history for appearances. Still, he didn't need anyone to explain the heat of she made me look foolish.

Understanding that wasn't remembering.

The drying rack scraped again. He jerked his head up, pulling his knee painfully. It was today's metal frame, not an umbrella striking a wall. He waited before reading further, without adding the startle to the past.

Later, the page said, he'd asked why she no longer used an umbrella. Hard to fold, she'd answered. Whether it was the same one remained uncertain. No replacement was purchased in the account. No forgiveness concluded it.

Lin Mo suddenly wanted to buy her an umbrella.

Not for rain. An empty space beside the table seemed made for something he could accomplish immediately: choose a color, pay, let her accept it. This unfinished text could move forward, instead of leaving him without memory beside a crack she might still remember.

He reached for his phone. The search box was empty when Xiao Lu spoke behind the curtain.

“Can you reach the cup?”

“Yes.”

“I heard you moving things.”

He studied the phone, then said he was reading the umbrella passage.

The curtain didn't move.

“Do you need to ask me now?”

“I was thinking of buying you a new one.”

A pause. The mattress shifted.

“Don't order it yet.”

“I haven't.”

“I don't want to choose a color right now.”

That unsettled him more than a refusal to forgive. He'd already delivered a generous-looking choice to her exhaustion, without asking what she needed. He set the phone facedown. He didn't ask what had happened to the old umbrella.

“Rest. I'll read by myself.”

“You don't have to give me an answer the moment you've finished that either.”

He agreed, waited for the quiet, and turned another page.

An edge caught against a folded sheet in a different hand. Old Chen's private fold remained closed. Failing to remember the moment he'd been told not to read it didn't cancel the request. Lin Mo's fingers struggled to separate the papers. Rather than wake Xiao Lu, he held the diary with the original cloth and stayed on this side.

This side was difficult enough.

Trying to bring the cardboard closer, he struck the cup. Water trembled over the rim. His right hand moved instinctively to catch it; pain stabbed through the wrist and stopped him.

The old page stayed dry. A wet ring spread across the table. Hurry could damage more than the meaning of a sentence.

The tissues were out of reach. He didn't trap the book on his knees and maneuver the wheelchair toward them. He used a dry corner of cloth to protect the paper, waited for his shoulder to ease, and messaged Zhou to ask how far away he was.

At the corner, Zhou replied. Should he return?

Lin Mo didn't want to recall him over tissues, but the question had already stopped him. He explained the spill, the dry paper, the lack of urgency. When Zhou came back, could he move the tissues closer?

Zhou agreed and asked about food. Lin Mo said they'd order their own, rather than adding that task to his.

As the wet ring shrank, Lin Mo eased the board back with his palm. He didn't know how his former self would have handled such an awkward little failure. Today he hadn't made his injured wrist prove his independence. Beyond the curtain, Xiao Lu turned without being summoned.

He checked the copied introduction line by line. Much more remained unread. They hadn't continued retrieval at the site; that didn't mean the words were missing from the stored copy. Today's notes stopped where their inspection stopped.

On a new margin he wrote unread. His uneven character needed a correction. For a moment, the old puncture made his hand feel familiar: urgent to leave a sentence, finding paper softer than expected.

The feeling stayed at his fingertips. No former room appeared, no childhood sister spoke. He loosened his grip on the pen. The ache belonged to the writing he'd just done. He could feel where the barrel pressed, could see the little correction on the new sheet. None of that showed who had been beside him years earlier. He wanted the small familiarity to lead somewhere, but wanting a path didn't put one beneath it.

Later Xiao Lu drew the curtain aside to get water. Her hair stuck up on one side. She noticed his phone without asking whether he'd placed an order.

“Did you sleep?”

“No. Closing my eyes helped.”

He almost shut the book, then asked whether she minded it open. She glanced at it and sat farther from the pages. As long as it wasn't blocking the cup.

“It says I took it without your agreement,” he said. “I don't remember, but I've read it. You don't have to tell it again to make me believe you.”

She looked into her water.

“You don't have to believe every detail.”

He raised his eyes.

“Don't agree with everything to be kind to me, then remember something different one day and say I made you believe it.”

Her unhurried words dismantled his prepared apology. He'd wanted complete admission to guarantee a correct position, as if standing against his former self necessarily put him beside her.

“I'll keep the sources separate. This is what I left then, not something I can experience now. But wanting to buy a new umbrella today—that was mine.”

“Then don't buy one today.”

“All right.”

She pointed at the first line.

“Was this added afterward?”

From the marks, it seemed so, he said. He didn't claim to remember making them.

“Did you hear this part?”

“Some of it. Not every page. I didn't agree to check every event for you.”

“I know.”

She watched to see whether those words could stop there. He let them, without asking about rain, people at the door or the handle's color.

Outside, the drying rack scraped farther away. Xiao Lu wanted something warm, not too salty. He showed her the nearby delivery choices and described only how his own stomach felt after the noodles, rather than selecting their whole meal.

She chose congee and steamed egg.

“Scallions?” she asked.

“Not today,” he said.

“Separate, then.”

After ordering, she went to wash her face. He finally freed the caught fold and returned it to its place. He wrapped the notebook without pinching a corner. Unread remained on the new notes; he didn't replace it with accepted. A chapter of someone else's account might be closed with an opinion. These pages would still be here after dinner, bearing their old gaps, while the person beside him needed food and a chance not to explain herself.

Zhou returned and moved the tissues without demanding how such a simple thing had produced a spill. Seeing the book safely wrapped, he replaced the damp cardboard with dry support. Lin Mo thanked him specifically for coming back to help with that, rather than hiding it inside a general thanks for everything.

While they waited for food, he rested. The dark phone faintly reflected his face. He turned it aside and watched the strip of light beside the curtain instead.

Xiao Lu emerged with water on her forehead and asked who would answer the door. Zhou volunteered and told her to wring her towel out first. She twisted it twice, then complained it was too thick. Tomorrow she wanted a thinner one.

Lin Mo listened without opening a shopping page for her.

The breeze entered. The paper stayed still.
