# Chapter 207: Don't Lift the Heel

That night Lin Mo reached the passage about the shoe.

No teammate's name appeared. After training, before roll call, someone's fingers had trembled as he bent. Seeing loose laces, Lin Mo had lifted the tongue of the shoe to help him finish faster. The man pulled away. His sock was stuck to broken skin; pulling upward would tear it loose.

Lin Mo's fingertips rested against his trouser seam.

He knew the movement. He knew how to support a heel without pulling or making someone twist to escape pain. But the shoe's color, the wear at its opening, the position of whoever called roll—none of that appeared to him.

A little rain began outside.

Xiao Lu washed the oranges without interrupting his reading to ask whether he remembered. She set one on a plate and put the rest aside. Watching moisture shine on the peel, he asked whether Fang Ji could hold a pen now.

“I don't know. Why?”

“He wrote this with his left hand. I just thought of it.”

She could ask whether he wanted a call, but not call him straightaway at this hour. Better to message. She passed along his contact details without composing anything. Lin Mo typed slowly with his left hand. Are you all right felt too easy to answer. He added a question about his hand and whether he could talk tomorrow.

The screen didn't change immediately.

Lin Mo set it down without checking every few seconds. Xiao Lu peeled her orange, leaving strands of white on the plate. One segment made her squint. She pushed the other orange away: that one could wait until tomorrow.

“May I try a piece?”

She passed one over. He bit into it and felt the back of his tongue tighten. Seeing his expression, she shook with laughter. Only after swallowing did he agree it was sour. Her choosing it didn't require him to pretend otherwise.

“I didn't ask for praise.”

He laughed too, then eased off when his old cheek injury pulled.

The diary lay beyond the plate: a named present beside a past whose participant remained unnamed. Neither vanished because the fruit was sour. He moved the plate away from the paper and continued.

Later, his former self had held the heel at the angle the teammate requested. Someone hurried them from a distance. He wanted to hurry too, but kept his hand steady. A note said the name couldn't be established. It supplied no convenient substitute.

Another line said he'd asked teammates where they hurt, then often forgotten to ask at home. He paused over home. The following account of moving his sister's clothes couldn't make the shoe into a tidy lesson he'd permanently learned.

Xiao Lu cleared the peel and asked whether he needed his foot repositioned. His trouser leg had settled close against the ankle. Saying he could do it, he reached and stopped at a pull through his shoulder.

She waited instead of taking over.

“Tell me how to help.”

He asked her to move the cushion first, not lift his leg. His trouser hem loosened. The shoe's edge pressed against the sock; he couldn't see whether skin beneath was rubbed red. Lack of pain wasn't permission to pull.

“Loosen the opening first.”

She crouched, touching the back of the shoe lightly. He almost followed a familiar sequence and told her to lift. The words stopped. This was his foot, not the one on the old page. Reading a correct action didn't replace current sensation.

“Wait. The angle's wrong.”

She stopped.

He tried relaxing his foot, but tension still reached his knee. Finally he decided not to remove the shoe yet. Xiao Lu settled the opening again and asked whether to call Zhou. He wanted a moment to recover first. They didn't have to finish at this instant.

She sat down, stretching tingling legs after crouching.

“Were you about to follow the book?”

“Yes.”

“The book doesn't know today's foot.”

She amused herself with that. Looking at the untidy page, he laughed too. He'd found his former self too impatient to ask. One reading later, he could still mistake knowing a sequence for knowing where someone hurt.

Rain thickened. Somebody collected a bucket outside. Xiao Lu went to close the window, leaving a narrow gap. Watching her, Lin Mo suddenly felt her shoulders were lower than he remembered.

The feeling had no source.

He knew he'd been taller before his body changed. She was bending, lit from behind. Today's distance might be making a picture feel old. He didn't call out that he remembered. On new paper he wrote that their relative heights had briefly felt familiar, cause unknown.

His heart still beat fast afterward.

Uncertainty couldn't always be flattened into a few words. For an instant he'd almost reached something. Admitting it might be today's shadow felt like closing the door himself. He nearly added certainly, a small place he could stand.

Xiao Lu returned and asked whether he felt ill.

“Something seemed familiar. I don't know if I imagined it.”

She sat without demanding a description.

“I'm afraid if I make it sound smaller, it'll disappear.”

“You don't have to make a feeling bigger for it to have happened.”

She sounded tired. He didn't tell the height impression again and ask her to make it certain. His phone lit up with Fang Ji's answer: the hand still hurt. Tomorrow afternoon was fine. Not early.

Lin Mo thanked him without sending a string of diary photographs.

“Disappointed?” Xiao Lu asked. “You reached the person who wrote it, but you still have to wait.”

“Yes.”

She nodded, not requiring him to replace that with it's all right.

He slept unevenly that night. Once he woke almost hearing a shoe scrape and somebody hurrying them. Reaching for the notebook, he touched bedding and remembered Zhou had helped him transfer before sleep. His hand wasn't beside the reading chair's armrest now.

The sound faded quickly.

Rather than wake Xiao Lu, he looked toward the window. A car passed through rainwater, tires making a similar dragging noise. He couldn't separate dream from what he'd actually heard. Trying harder made the shoe seem clearer. The sock felt certainly gray.

In the morning he nearly mentioned that color as fact.

Xiao Lu brought water and asked why he'd stopped mid-sentence. He requested the old page. There was no color in it. His back chilled. A movement read the night before had grown, within one night, a detail that felt witnessed.

“I dreamed something. I daren't count it as a memory.”

“What frightens you about that?”

“That every passage I read might grow things I add myself. Then I'll think I've recovered them.”

She didn't promise to distinguish them for him. Would he record the dream separately? He wrote shoe noise, hurrying, gray sock, then noted he'd read the passage the previous night and the original gave no color.

He lingered over gray. The most visual detail was hard to surrender. He wouldn't let it slip unnoticed onto the old page.

When he finished, Xiao Lu asked whether he still wanted the call. Yes: to ask about what had actually been written, not whether Fang Ji could certify a dream. She nodded and went to hang her towel where air could reach it.

Lin Mo drank without forcing the dreamed shoe into sharper focus. He could wait until afternoon and ask someone who'd held the pen. That man might not remember either, but at least he wasn't agreeing from inside Lin Mo's sleep.

When Fang Ji answered that afternoon, water ran in the background. He asked Lin Mo to wait, knocked something over and swore quietly. Lin Mo stayed silent until he returned.

“Just a cup. Don't sit there imagining I've fallen. Need help reading?”

“I wondered whether you kept anything I hadn't finished saying.”

“There were things you said you didn't remember. I didn't invent the rest.”

Fang Ji said some crowded writing wasn't mysterious, just awkward left-handed work. Read him the specific line. They needn't begin with why he'd transcribed the diary.

Lin Mo read the shoe passage.

At the uncertain name, Fang Ji said no name was missing. He'd asked at the time; Lin Mo had refused to put an uncertain name into the account. Fang Ji also remembered telling him not to make it sound as though military service had taught him how to be a person. That was dialogue he recalled now, not a line in the diary. Don't quietly turn this phone call into an original record.

Lin Mo separated it in his new notes without altering the old page.

“Why did you help me write?”

A pause.

“You asked whether I could. I could do a little.”

“That's all?”

“What answer do you want?”

Heat rose behind Lin Mo's ears. He wanted to hear that Fang Ji had seen somebody worth saving, or stories worth preserving. But the man had been hurting. He'd agreed to a specific task; he owed no grander reason.

“None. I asked too much.”

“You can ask.” Fang Ji sighed. “I don't have the energy to give every little thing meaning. How far have you read?”

“The second account. The shoe. Not the third carefully yet.”

“Take your time. Don't schedule a performance for everyone again.”

Somebody called him. Fang Ji answered, then said he had to go. Lin Mo asked whether he could send a small excerpt if particular writing remained unclear. Yes, not the whole book.

Before they ended, he asked if the hand was any better than when they'd returned. Fang Ji could hold light things; bottle caps were difficult. Being able to hold a pen didn't mean he could write for someone all afternoon.

“I only wanted to know about your hand.”

After a pause, Fang Ji said he knew. A little easier than yesterday.

Lin Mo didn't pursue it. That specific improvement could please him without guaranteeing tomorrow or requiring the pain to be minimized. After the call, only traffic on wet roads remained.

He didn't tell Xiao Lu that Fang Ji really cared. Help and fatigue had both been there. He needn't preserve one by throwing away the other.

Xiao Lu was repairing the bag's old bottom seam. She'd emptied it onto a clean chair, leaving the notebook separately on the table.

“Only the bag,” she said when he looked. “Not the papers.”

“Enough light?”

“Not on this side.”

He could reach the little bedside lamp's switch, but couldn't lift its base to her. Zhou returned in time to move it where she indicated. Lin Mo suggested closer. She looked up and said it was enough.

He stopped.

The third old account lay before him: clothes he'd moved without asking, his sister eventually requesting only the lamp. He didn't ask Zhou to move today's lamp again and make this scene resemble the old one.

Xiao Lu threaded her needle and began. The light fell on today's bag, proving nothing about the former garment's color. Lin Mo turned to that page and read its first sentence while she stitched the part she wanted to finish.
