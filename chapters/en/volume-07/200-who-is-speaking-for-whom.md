# Chapter 200: Who Is Speaking for Whom?

Old film posters covered the community room's windows.

Sun had bleached one corner almost white, leaving a mountain and half a smiling face. Seeing firefighters at the entrance, Lin Mo asked Zhou to stop short of the temporary barrier.

It was the eighth day since their return. That morning they'd heard the account and agreed to help observe, not take over rescue. Actual transport expenses would be recorded; any other payment remained a separate discussion, not a price suddenly presented to someone trapped. Xiao Lu had chosen to come after seeing photographs of the entrance. Now she stood behind and to one side of the wheelchair, hands off its handles.

“I can watch from outside,” she'd said before leaving.

He remembered agreeing. He didn't send her ahead to test the doorway.

He had asked to bring the old book. Xiao Lu thought the three copied technical pages might help them recognize unfamiliar writing. She hadn't promised an instruction manual for every remnant. He hadn't asked her to read his past aloud before they left. Zhou had brought the cloth bundle, its private inner fold still closed.

The bag hung clear of the ground behind the wheelchair. Nobody here had been invited to search it. His medical file was at the rental; he'd brought only the arrangements necessary for the outing. He didn't want to sift through someone's private past to find a paper concerning his leg.

The person overseeing the scene was called Liang. After looking at the wheelchair and supports, he moved a chair away from the side entrance, leaving room to retreat. Nobody crowded the main door. Medical staff waited inside. Ordinary power had been off since yesterday.

“She isn't refusing to come out,” Liang said. “She wants to. Moving her legs makes the words speed up. We tried helping her and stopped.”

Lin Mo asked Zhou to correct the account they'd been given. The person inside shouldn't have to answer for a secondhand accusation of stubbornness.

Tang Jing sat near the screen, a woman in her thirties with a gray jacket beside her. Water marked her collar. Her lips moved; he could hear only when the noise outside subsided.

“I'm here. I'm willing to continue.”

A few breaths, and again.

She tried to swallow between repetitions. The medical workers used those gaps to attend to her, not to obtain a longer recording at the expense of her throat. Luo Huaisheng rested against another chair. His right hand rose repeatedly toward his chest, as though someone kept asking him to vote.

Seeing the newcomers, he gripped the seat with his left hand.

“Don't ask me to agree again.”

“I haven't asked you anything,” Lin Mo said.

The old man stared. His right hand rose once more. He tried pressing it down with his left; a medic warned him not to twist and hurt himself.

A bespectacled man beside them introduced himself as Han Wei, responsible for filming and editing the original recordings. He laid an unplugged power cord where everyone could see it. The project was an oral history of the old neighborhood. Tang Jing helped with subtitles.

“Mr. Luo couldn't hear the recording clearly. There was a section he thought was wrong. He wanted to record it again. She offered to read it for him first.”

“The original, or edited words?”

Han Wei tightened his grip on his notes.

“Edited. Not maliciously. Removing pauses, joining incomplete sentences.”

“I said I didn't want to talk about that part,” Luo Huaisheng interrupted.

Tang Jing closed her eyes. Her mouth didn't stop.

Han Wei lowered his head.

The screen should have been white. A faint gray frame remained on it, holding Luo Huaisheng's motionless face. Beneath that face, subtitles appeared in time with Tang Jing's voice. A fine line returned to the beginning whenever she reached continue.

Lin Mo didn't ask them to reconnect the power or play the original again. He asked what anyone had pressed since the trouble began.

“Pause. Unplugged it. Removed the microphone. All tried.”

Han Wei pointed to the ordinary microphone on the floor, its cable disconnected. Tang Jing didn't need it to speak. Luo Huaisheng's arm hadn't stopped either.

The mouth on the screen was still. The moving mouth belonged to Tang Jing. The subtitles joined them into one person's statement.

“Who said those words were Mr. Luo's?” Lin Mo asked.

For a moment Han Wei looked angry, as if criticism of his editing were an intolerable distraction. Then he fetched the paper record of the interview and showed Liang, keeping it away from Tang Jing's face.

Visit arrangements. Questions. No declaration about being willing to continue.

“That sentence appeared during the test screening,” he said. “It asked for confirmation underneath. I thought she could read it and he could nod.”

“I didn't nod,” Luo Huaisheng said coldly. His right hand rose.

“I know. After that—”

Tang Jing's voice broke. A medic bent toward her, asking whether she could catch her breath. She nodded, tears reaching the corners of her mouth, with no space to say anything else.

Lin Mo asked Han Wei to step back so she could see the door instead of a crowd waiting for answers.

Her gaze followed him, then stopped at a flask near the doorway. Asked whether she wanted water, she briefly closed her eyes. The medical staff worked within the intervals in which she could swallow. Nobody poured a cup down her throat to suppress the words.

Han Wei said the flask was hers; it had been there all night. A rabbit sticker peeled at its edge, one ear crooked. Tang Jing came into focus as more than a problem waiting to be solved. She brought a cup to work. She didn't always remove a peeling sticker immediately.

“Does her family know she's here?” Lin Mo asked.

Liang said they'd been contacted. Not allowing everyone into the room didn't mean nobody had told them. Lin Mo didn't propose bringing relatives in to call her name as another instrument to try.

Luo Huaisheng became anxious at the mention of family. His son was out of town; he shouldn't be made to rush for a train. A medic explained that making contact meant keeping people informed, not demanding they arrive within minutes.

The old man's breathing eased. His hand continued rising.

His left thumb rubbed the chair edge. That small movement was his, unlike the right arm repeatedly announcing assent. Anyone filming only the raised hand could assemble a convincing picture of cooperation.

“Mr. Luo, we heard you say you don't want to agree again.”

He stared at Lin Mo. His thumb stopped. Nobody demanded another movement to prove he'd understood.

“There's more writing underneath,” Xiao Lu said.

She hadn't gone inside. From her angle, a half-lowered blackout curtain no longer obscured the bottom of the gray frame. Liang had a flashlight redirected. Its ordinary beam couldn't erase the gray light, but revealed the screen rolled around an old bar at its lower edge.

They often rolled it up to clear the temporary stage steps. Some writing now faced the back.

“Can we unroll it?” Han Wei asked. “It's manual. No motor.”

“Tell them what we're moving first,” Lin Mo said.

Liang positioned people at both ends. They touched the ordinary supports, not the screen fabric. One old nut was loose. One person steadied that joint while another removed an empty bottle that could roll beneath someone's foot.

Lin Mo waited for a real working space to be cleared. Visible writing wasn't a reason to hurry a heavy bar over people's bodies.

Tang Jing watched urgently. She tried lifting a hand to point, but the next subtitle returned and her hand fell to her lap.

Luo Huaisheng reported no greater tightness. Liang had both ends loosened a little together. The lower screen descended, its bar supported instead of dropped onto the front chairs.

A narrow strip appeared below the frame.

[Reading on another's behalf in progress.]

Beneath it were small directional marks. White projection light obscured the left; the right said continue. Lin Mo looked from that white patch to Tang Jing's feet.

A controller with two pedals lay on the floor. Her right foot rested on the right pedal. A board for holding papers sat on her knees. Its ordinary cable ran to disconnected equipment, but the same pale gray light outlined the pedals.

“What did those do before?”

“Turned the subtitles,” Han Wei said. “She could use her foot while her hands handled the script.”

The medical staff hadn't yanked her foot away. Each attempt to lift it accelerated her voice and raised Luo Huaisheng's arm higher. They had noticed. Every attempt made caring for the two of them harder.

Lin Mo glanced at his footrest. He nearly made a familiar mistake: seeing a foot that couldn't move and telling someone simply to move it.

He asked Tang Jing whether she could answer with her eyes.

She looked at him.

“Do you want to stop?”

She closed her eyes immediately, then opened them. He asked whether she wanted that movement to mean yes. She repeated it. He asked only that, not for agreement to a list of unexplained actions.

“Correction,” Han Wei said suddenly, pointing.

A white box floated beside the old face. Its letters were much larger than the narrow strip's.

[Please correct on the subject's behalf.]

Han Wei leaned forward, recognizing something like an ordinary editing command. Lin Mo stopped him. He didn't press it himself.

“Still asking someone to stand in.”

“We can't leave her talking forever.”

“Then find how she can stop. Not another person to speak for her.”

His voice came out too hard. His shoulder tensed. He leaned back and slowed down. Han Wei was helping and had made mistakes. Humiliating him wouldn't uncover the left pedal.

From the doorway, Xiao Lu used a borrowed light to show the controller without touching Tang Jing's foot. A fallen soft pad covered the left pedal, one corner caught beneath a chair leg. When Tang Jing moved her knees, the board and her body tilted together. Her right foot pressed deeper on continue.

Lin Mo told Liang that different movements might be combining whenever she tried to lift her foot. They needed to consider the board and chair separately, rather than attempting to carry the whole person out at once.

Liang asked Tang Jing before removing the board. She closed her eyes. The medics lifted it gently from both sides and put it on a distant table. Nobody took it onto their own lap to occupy her place.

When the subtitles returned, she was still speaking, but her weight didn't drive her foot lower.

Luo Huaisheng's hand stopped at chest height, below his chin.

“Only no higher,” Lin Mo said, catching himself before he called it relief.

His knee began to ache. Zhou asked whether to retreat outside. Liang was still dealing with the pad. Lin Mo asked for half a step back. Maintaining one viewing angle wasn't worth pinning his body there.

The footrests stayed clear of the barrier. Xiao Lu withdrew with him and said she didn't want to stand directly in front of the image.

“Stay here.”

Han Wei straightened his scattered papers and offered to read the subtitles aloud. Lin Mo and Liang refused almost together. Flushing, he folded the papers. He didn't fill his panic with more reading.

Half of the word end finally showed on the strip's left side. Beside it ran smaller gray lettering, like a maintenance note emerging from an old crack. It didn't belong to the oral-history title design.

Lin Mo asked for the light a little lower.

Those letters didn't return to the start with Tang Jing's voice. His fingers rested on the armrest. The habit of distinguishing these gaps remained. He couldn't remember who had once written in a place like this.

“Don't open the bag yet,” he told Zhou. “Let me see what it says.”
