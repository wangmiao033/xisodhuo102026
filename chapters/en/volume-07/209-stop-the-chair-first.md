# Chapter 209: Stop the Chair First

Xiao Lu didn't come in the vehicle that morning.

At the rental door she handed Zhou the medical papers and told Lin Mo his water was in the outer pocket. A few steps down the other street, she turned to ask about dinner. He didn't know yet. She laughed, waved and went on without waiting for a plan.

Watching her coat disappear, he felt he'd forgotten something important. Zhou was confirming where they could stop outside the hospital. Nobody suggested calling her back. Lin Mo left his hand beside the water and sent no message after her.

On the entrance ramp, the wheelchair pulled right. Zhou stopped. Lin Mo checked his footrest, assuming he'd displaced his leg. His knee remained supported; nothing caught his trousers.

“Something in the wheel,” Zhou said. “Wait. Let me get us onto level ground.”

They backed to a clear space. Zhou applied the brakes and asked the entrance staff for small pliers. Shoes passed through Lin Mo's view. Somebody squeezed along his right side; a bag struck the footrest. He raised his left hand defensively and felt the pull in his shoulder.

The person was already gone. Lin Mo almost drew his injured leg inward to accommodate someone who hadn't stopped. He left it supported instead. The impulse to make himself smaller lingered after the stranger had passed. His knee wouldn't become less injured because he felt embarrassed by the room it occupied. He looked down until he could distinguish that embarrassment from an actual need to change its position. A staff member moved an empty chair to make more room.

Zhou returned with long tweezers and tissues. A thin piece of plastic packaging had wound around the caster axle. After removing it, he asked before pushing half a step. The chair still veered.

He didn't pretend to understand the whole mechanism. An attendant checked it, found the swivel stiff, and offered a loan chair while they examined the old one.

Changing chairs took longer than expected. The new footrest couldn't simply match the old position. Lin Mo nearly said close enough, then saw that his leg wasn't fully supported. They adjusted it and transferred him in stages. His military background wasn't made an excuse for asking him to stand briefly.

Once seated, his shirt trapped behind his waist and pulled tight across his chest.

“Wait. My shirt.”

Someone was asking about the loan paperwork. He said it louder. Zhou stopped; the attendant helped ease the trapped edge before returning to the form.

Sweat stood at Lin Mo's temple. Looking down at his collar, he suddenly felt an entirely different tug: cloth against a flatter chest, his own fingers opening the neck, water dripping from his chin inside.

There was no mirror, no other person. Yet his right hand almost rose to pull today's collar.

The support stopped it.

His heart quickened. Today's cloth had really been trapped; he could indicate where. The other sensation had no location. It felt like a body he'd once used from within, rather than a page telling him he'd been taller.

“Still tight?” Zhou asked.

“No. Let me sit a moment.”

Zhou parked by the wall and offered a tissue. Wiping sweat, Lin Mo remembered the sock that had become gray in his dream without any evidence. A sensation against skin could feel truer than a picture. That didn't establish its source.

He didn't immediately write. Their number was approaching. At registration he confirmed his name. The next field still matched the altered real-world records. The clerk glanced at him and asked naturally whether she was the patient herself.

Zhou didn't speak over him. Lin Mo said yes and gave the details required. The contact number remained correct. The sheet came back unchanged by the bodily impression he'd just experienced.

He wanted to add something beside it, tell this stranger he wasn't what she saw. But the queue moved, the clerk had asked about a number, and he wasn't prepared to surrender his whole past to this counter. Putting away the paper didn't remove the bitterness.

The doctor examined his knee and asked about further falls or twists. No fall, Lin Mo said, but he'd almost pushed through his left shoulder yesterday, and today's transfer had briefly pulled painfully. He didn't omit those awkward incidents because they seemed too small to report.

The examination didn't release him to stand. The existing weight-bearing restrictions continued; he couldn't remove the wrist support merely to make writing easier. Zhou clarified the follow-up instructions and positioning for transfers instead of leaving them to guess later.

Asked about sleep, Lin Mo mentioned dreams and sudden, brief sensations of the past. He couldn't tell whether memory was returning or whether reading had made new images. The doctor didn't diagnose an extraordinary account from a few sentences, asking instead about continuing discomfort and disrupted rest, and advising him to describe the episodes during further care.

No immediate verdict disappointed him, then relieved him. He feared not receiving an answer. He also feared receiving one so easily that every subsequent tremor would have to fit it.

They needed to collect another examination document across the corridor. A delivery trolley blocked the short route. Its handler was searching for a dropped slip and suggested squeezing past.

Lin Mo's first thought was that he could draw his feet in. Zhou checked the chair's width instead. The trolley shifted, struck the wall and stopped again.

For two minutes Lin Mo felt watched. The others behind him were waiting too. A woman carrying a food flask went ahead and returned with news of another, longer route. Zhou checked its turning and whether there were steps before taking it. Someone saying they could get through wasn't the same as having seen the way.

Sunlight filled that corridor. Lin Mo had resented the distance; halfway along he saw packaging staples on the far side of the trolley, close to where his footrest would have passed. One useful detour didn't prove every longer route correct. It did make him reconsider the few steps he'd been so eager to save.

At the collection desk, a nurse noticed damp on the edge of the papers: condensation from the water container. The writing remained clear. Zhou moved the water into another compartment. Lin Mo tried spreading out every sheet, but the nurse indicated a dry patch and asked for only the one needed, away from the draft.

By the time the folder closed, his left hand ached strangely. He asked Zhou to carry it as far as the vehicle. Zhou didn't mock his earlier intention to manage the documents himself. Lin Mo watched the folder lie flat inside the small bag, paper corners protected, before lowering his hand.

They stopped beside an empty bench. The loan chair's straighter back tired his shoulders. Once supported, he asked whether he'd said anything odd after the transfer.

“You asked to sit a moment.”

“Nothing else?”

“Nothing I heard.”

Zhou checked the time and put his phone away. His afternoon obligation hadn't vanished, but there was still time to finish their agreed morning.

“I felt as though I'd used a different body. Flatter chest. Wet collar. I don't know where.”

Zhou didn't supply a barracks or washroom. Was he uncomfortable now?

“A little. Not because that body was bad. It felt mine, and I couldn't say why.”

He realized he didn't have to condemn today's body to give the former one a place. His left hand remained by his present knee. The wrist pain marked today's limits. Holding both experiences was harder, and more honest, than he'd expected.

On his phone he noted the time, the transfer and the wet-collar sensation. No shoe, teammate or reflected face. At the end he typed that he wanted to remember more, then gave the wish a separate line so it wouldn't blend into the apparent facts.

A man helping his companion into a coat held out a bag, already beginning to release it, and asked Lin Mo to take it.

“No,” Lin Mo said, raising his left palm. The man caught it before it landed across his legs. Surprise became an apology; he put the bag on the bench. There was no argument, no demand to know why the person sitting still couldn't do this one small thing. Lin Mo had prepared an explanation before there was anyone left to explain to. He let it go unsaid, although the answer had cost him more breath than he'd wanted.

Lin Mo's heart kept racing. Refusing was possible. Having the bag withdrawn without being made to exhibit his injuries was harder to get used to. The man resumed untangling the sleeve, saying slow down while being the more impatient of the pair. Eventually both men laughed.

They returned for the original wheelchair. The attendant had cleared material around the swivel and checked it, then asked Zhou to test it on the open floor. It no longer dragged right. If it stiffened again, they were to have it examined rather than force it; the old chair wasn't guaranteed forever.

Before the return transfer, Lin Mo mentioned his shirt. Zhou asked about the footrest first. More room on the right, Lin Mo said. They didn't have to repeat every humiliation of the earlier attempt to do this one better.

While they waited outside, Xiao Lu sent a photograph of a bagged flatbread. Did he want that kind? No string of anxious questions about the examination accompanied it.

One piece, he replied, then volunteered the results: still no weight-bearing, material cleared from the caster, waiting for transport.

She acknowledged it and said she might eat hers first. She wouldn't keep his hot.

Don't wait, he answered, smiling.

When the vehicle arrived, Zhou inspected the stopping place and asked the driver to move forward so the ramp would rest on level ground. Yesterday Lin Mo had wanted to eliminate waiting. Now it was part of a stretch of road somebody had actually made usable for him. He paid the extra agreed cost, without allowing familiarity to obscure it.

Back at the rental, before Zhou left for his afternoon business, they placed water, phone and necessary items within reach. Lin Mo didn't wait for Xiao Lu to return before asking about transferring to bed. This transfer still required assistance. He stayed resting in the wheelchair until someone was actually there to help.

A hawker called outside in a coarse voice. The wet-collar impression didn't return. Lin Mo didn't tug repeatedly at his clothes to summon it. He set the medical papers apart from the old diary.

Xiao Lu opened the door holding half her bread. He looked at that first.

“Dry?”

“All right.” She bit into it. “I haven't bitten yours.”

He accepted his portion with his left hand and ate what was in front of him.
