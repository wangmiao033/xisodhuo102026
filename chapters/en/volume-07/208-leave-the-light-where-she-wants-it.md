# Chapter 208: Leave the Light Where She Wants It

The needle brought a tuft of old thread out through the bottom of the bag. Xiao Lu picked it away and dropped it into a little dish beside her foot.

The lamp stood where she'd asked for it. Lin Mo kept watching its round base as though recognizing it might reveal the furniture of their former home. This was the rental's lamp. A chip in its plastic rim had nothing to do with his diary.

He looked down and read the third account.

Home from the army: straighten his shoes, check the window catch, fold the garment his sister had left across a chair. Plan tomorrow's departure for her. Draw the shorter route. Hear her say she didn't want her day arranged like that. Only afterward notice the thread protruding from a pocket. The garment had been waiting to be mended.

“What were you sewing then?”

Her needle stopped.

The question was already out. The page admitted uncertainty about the color, yet he'd asked the person sewing in front of him to fill its gaps.

“Read your copy first,” she said. “I've got a hole here too.”

She was studying another worn patch underneath the bag. Pulled slightly, it whitened. She hadn't stopped solely to answer him; she was deciding where the next stitch could go.

“Does it need a patch?”

“I haven't got suitable cloth.”

Zhou, gathering things near the door, offered a clean scrap. Too thick, she decided; folded inside, it would make a hard edge. A thinner wrapping cloth from another bag worked better. She measured it without cutting immediately.

Lin Mo wanted to advise her to stitch farther out. His fingers even rose. He couldn't reach her work, and his knowledge of sewing wasn't better than hers. He lowered his hand and continued. His left hand could reach his own pages; the bag was beyond it. The distance made an instruction terribly easy. He wouldn't have to struggle with the cloth or discover that his suggested stitch was wrong. She would. The thought kept him from offering the advice a second time, dressed up as a question.

His former self had returned the garment to the chair, arranging the sleeve as it had been. When his sister came home, she said nothing. He waited for her to notice his repair, then finally asked whether she needed help.

She wanted the lamp moved.

He'd wanted to take over the needle. He hadn't. Heat crept into Lin Mo's face at finding even that small restraint recorded, as though an unspoken remark could count as hard work.

“Was I very annoying?”

“Now or then?”

He hesitated.

“That's your third question now. I want to finish this.”

“All right.”

“You're allowed to speak. Tell me if the thread falls.”

He laughed, then actually saw its end brush her trousers. He pointed. She retrieved it without waiting for a larger apology.

Zhou went to buy food. In the quiet, Lin Mo picked up fresh paper, intending to add an explanation of today. He'd written so many uncertainties lately. Each seemed capable of sealing an uncomfortable feeling away.

This time he didn't write.

Xiao Lu measured her patch twice. The needle caught the light. For a moment another hand passed through his mind, smaller, pressing cloth flat. A shallow mark lay beside its thumb: perhaps pressure, perhaps shadow.

No face came with it.

His chest contracted. The image disappeared. Today's Xiao Lu was still biting her thread to pull it straight. He pressed his left hand against his thigh.

“What is it?”

He feared that speaking would make her ask whether he remembered, and then he wouldn't be able to take it back. She merely parked her needle in the cloth and waited.

“I thought I saw something. A hand.”

“Dizzy?”

“Not quite. Maybe I've watched you sew too long.”

Should she switch off the lamp? No, he said. She needed it; he could look elsewhere. She resumed instead of holding a pose to preserve his experience. That helped.

Outside, a man repairing a bicycle scraped his hand against the chain, swore and searched for something to wipe it with. That hand was visible. The other one had no established place or time.

When his heartbeat slowed, Lin Mo wrote that he'd briefly pictured a hand while reading the garment passage and watching Xiao Lu sew. No face, no confirmed date. He didn't turn its mark into a scar or invent an injury.

She turned her work over, frowned and unpicked several stitches.

“Wasted effort?” he asked.

“Too far in.”

“I thought it was finished.”

“Looks finished outside. Won't open properly inside.”

She showed him the fold she'd narrowed. He thought of the person who'd arranged the sleeve again. From a doorway everything might have looked restored; the interruption to his sister's work would remain. Xiao Lu threaded her needle without waiting for his interpretation. Showing today's bag didn't oblige her to spread out yesterday's life.

The bicycle bell rang three times. His shoulder tightened; his knee followed. Before he'd decided what to do, his body had tried to move somewhere it couldn't. He settled against his support and checked his leg was still resting properly.

“Close the window more?” she asked.

“No. It's not too loud. I got ahead of myself.”

She moved the thread dish out of the wheels' possible path. Watching that practical little clearance, he could breathe again.

The diary ended without forgiveness or an embrace. They'd eaten afterward, spoken about other things. Even its writer hadn't remembered everything. The missing pieces hadn't all been caused by the clearing. There had been gaps while he could still remember.

“I've finished it,” he said.

“All three?”

“Yes. I'm not asking you to tell them again.”

She dropped the last cut thread into the dish.

“I remember asking you to move a lamp once. I can't tell whether it's that time. There may have been several.”

He scarcely dared lift his head.

“Once you moved it and stood in front of it. I had to ask you to get out of the way.”

He laughed. She did too, then warned him not to ask the shade's color. She truly didn't know. He was tempted to ask whether the light had been warm or white, whether the chair had stood by a window. One answer might have made that brief hand easier to place. But she'd given him a small recollection of her own, not an invitation to empty it of every usable detail. He let the missing shade remain missing.

“Did I move?”

“Yes. How else could I see?”

The impatient little answer tightened his throat. He knew she was his sister. Now he could almost imagine someone standing in the light, satisfied with his assistance and oblivious to his own shadow. He didn't call the imagining a recovered memory.

“Am I blocking you now?”

“No. You couldn't from there.”

The wheelchair was well away from the lamp. His right leg still required support. His laugh faded into an ache that wasn't entirely unhappiness. He needn't drag today's body into the light to establish that a taller man had existed.

Before Zhou returned, a message arrived about tomorrow's follow-up. Would they use the same vehicle? A longer wait would cost extra.

The amount wasn't impossible, but Lin Mo suddenly hated the thought of another person spending half a day outside a hospital for him. He typed no need to wait, intending to say they'd manage the journey home.

Xiao Lu asked if the vehicle was unavailable. He showed her the message. Her first question was whether he could wheel directly out of the follow-up department, not whether he resented the money.

He remembered the ramp, the crowd beyond it—and that he hadn't maneuvered through either himself. Without Zhou, she would push the chair, carry the papers, visit the desk and arrange transport. The waiting hadn't vanished. He'd hidden it inside her hands.

“I don't want him stuck with me.”

“Ask what he has to do. Don't start by saying we can manage.”

He deleted the words and asked how long Zhou was available. Then he set the phone down. Xiao Lu loosened a crooked stitch; giving useful advice hadn't made her permanently responsible for his planning.

Zhou replied that morning was possible, but he had something to do after lunch. If the hospital ran late, another arrangement would be needed.

That specific limit steadied Lin Mo. They could ask the hospital about the likely sequence and arrange transport around the actual finish, not promise Xiao Lu could transfer him into a back seat alone. Extra waiting would be an explicit request, not a favor discovered after somebody was already trapped into doing it.

“I want to buy some things of my own in the morning,” Xiao Lu said. “If I'm finished early, I'll come over.”

Did she want photographs of his medical papers? Not for now. She didn't need his examination forms along on her shopping trip. He laughed again and added no instructions to the phone.

Zhou knocked twice before bringing lunch in. They needed the table clear. Xiao Lu counted her needles, then couldn't find the little scissors. Everyone looked at the cloth around the book.

“Don't move the papers yet,” Lin Mo said. “You put them beside your knee.”

They had slipped onto the stool beside her leg. She closed the blades and put them in a separate pouch, then showed them the repaired bag. It remained old at the edges. An empty food container inside didn't pull the new seam apart when she lifted it.

“Not the book yet. Let's see whether it loosens by tomorrow.”

Zhou found another dry bag. The notebook lay flat in its cloth, separate from the meal. He didn't discard the apparently useless old papers. Lin Mo checked where it now stood so he wouldn't reach wrongly in the night. Today's note stayed outside the old pages.

At lunch Xiao Lu found the vegetables salty and drank more water. Zhou asked about tomorrow. Only the planned follow-up, Lin Mo said, not an added visit to the sealed activity room.

He was disappointed by his own answer. The retrieval entrance outside had seemed a more promising route to the past than this ordinary lamp. Yet the food was warm and his sister was choosing a piece she wanted. He exchanged his awkward chopsticks for a spoon without asking anyone to select his meal.

Afterward she washed up. Zhou adjusted the support until the tightness around Lin Mo's knee eased. Lin Mo admitted he'd nearly pushed through his left shoulder earlier. They rearranged the cushion; not falling hadn't proved the setup was right.

The lamp remained beside the repaired bag. When Xiao Lu returned, she asked whether he still needed it.

He shook his head. She switched it off, darkening that patch of the room. He didn't reach to turn it on again.
