# Chapter 205: She Ate Her Own Lunch First

Yu Mei chose a small restaurant by the hospital's rear gate, not a ward.

Xiao Lu passed on the message. They'd exchanged contact details before separating after discharge. The previous evening she'd asked whether Yu Mei wanted to meet, without sending photographs of the notebook. The reply came late: after her dressing change the following morning, briefly. She had other things to do afterward.

The first thing Lin Mo saw was a half-eaten bowl of rice.

Yu Mei sat against the wall, clothes uneven over her shoulder. She held a spoon on her less restricted side. Hearing the wheels, she looked up with her mouth still full and raised the spoon: wait a moment.

Zhou moved a chair to make room. Xiao Lu sat opposite and offered to bring the water closer. Yu Mei pointed out that she could reach it where it was.

“Have you eaten?”

“Not yet.”

“Order, then. I'm not waiting for you.”

She took another mouthful.

Lin Mo had rehearsed saying he'd found the diary. Each version had sounded as if he'd recovered something more. With her food still hot, he could only inspect the menu and choose something he wouldn't need both hands to tear apart.

Xiao Lu asked whether the dressing change hurt. Yes: one corner had stuck. The nurse had told Yu Mei to slow down, and she'd been the impatient one. She glanced at Lin Mo.

“Don't look like that. I'm not changing yours too.”

He touched his forehead, uncertain what his expression had been. Xiao Lu laughed. Yu Mei joined her until her shoulder pulled tight. She put down the spoon and waited.

The table fell quiet.

Lin Mo knew how other people's pain had changed their movements these past days. He couldn't remember how many times he'd watched her stop like this before. Her name was on the papers; she'd been there on their return. Facts arranged themselves. Familiarity did not. Knowing that she'd accompanied him along a difficult route could not supply the experience of looking toward her and already knowing how she would answer. He was grateful, and awkward with that gratitude, and aware that neither feeling obliged her to become more familiar for his sake.

When her breathing steadied, Yu Mei asked what he'd come to tell her.

He described the screening room, keeping to what she needed. At the similar retrieval entrance, her spoon paused. Which paper had supplied the index? He indicated the bag without spreading its contents beside lunch.

“The copy in the old book. We retrieved part of it. I compared it with the paper yesterday.”

“Did the words match?”

“The parts we read did.”

She nodded and kept eating.

He waited for more. Instead she scraped the remaining vegetables onto her rice and recommended the soup to Xiao Lu. Pale didn't mean undercooked. Xiao Lu accepted half a bowl and blew on it before drinking.

Lin Mo realized he'd wanted Yu Mei to say this meant he'd soon be better. She had heard him and cared. She hadn't turned lunch into a celebration.

The server nearly placed a hot plate beside his injured wrist. Yu Mei pointed with her spoon to the other side. After a few careful mouthfuls, Lin Mo brought out the notebook, clear of grease and water. Xiao Lu moved the soup farther away.

Yu Mei didn't touch Old Chen's private fold. At the cramped last line of the clean copy, she identified Fang Ji's hand. His right palm had been injured; he'd used his left, pausing often, finally complaining that Lin Mo talked too much.

“Really complained?”

“I didn't come to be polite for him.”

Lin Mo almost smiled. This weighed more than a name on paper: a tired man whose hand hurt, who could grow impatient, rather than someone eternally recording a noble sacrifice. The irritation didn't make the surviving words less valuable. He could begin to imagine how slowly they'd filled the page.

“Do you remember saving it?”

“My part. How much detail do you want?”

He'd nearly answered all of it. Her shoulder stopped him. He asked what they'd stored first, and whether the diary came afterward.

“A harmless test sentence. There's dust by the wall. We backed out and retrieved it. The diary came later.”

The index in the corner looked tiny. Somebody had once stood by a gray door, hurting, and checked each mark. He didn't know which side the wall had been on. No sudden picture supplied it.

“You asked whether it stored only words,” she said. “That's what the explanation said. It didn't put people inside. You asked who else could read them too.”

“Someone with the index might.”

“Yes. So don't decide today's retrieval means only you can retrieve it.”

She reached for the restaurant bill. He started to help, but her fingers already held it. She checked the price and reminded the owner of an omitted portion of rice. After paying her share, she put away her phone. Lin Mo paid for himself and Xiao Lu, without trying to purchase every former kindness at the same table.

Yu Mei's spoon slipped against her bowl. His hand rose; she noticed, changed her grip and didn't pass it to him.

“You still watch how everybody holds things.”

“Did I used to?”

“Sometimes. I'm talking about just now.”

He lowered his hand. He'd been fitting every remark around his former self. Even a present complaint became a possible recovered piece.

She shifted her bowl to give her elbow space, then said he'd sometimes refused to be touched during the clearing.

He knew the events from what he'd been told. Yet being reminded he'd feared her approach felt like an insult to the person opposite him.

“Did I say something cruel?”

“You told me not to touch you yet.”

“What happened?”

“I stopped. Later, you agreed to talk it through before we continued.”

She didn't make stopping sound like an act of great generosity. As he prepared an apology, she asked whether he now thought he shouldn't have refused.

“I don't know. You helped so much.”

“That doesn't mean I get to touch you whenever I like.”

She finished her last mouthful and wiped her lips without waiting for a suitably grateful answer. Lin Mo let the heat of indebtedness ebb. Her visit didn't require him to apologize again for every frightened moment.

Xiao Lu steadied her soup. Nobody added that everyone had meant well. He picked up his spoon and stopped watching Yu Mei's hand.

Seeing the notebook wrapped, she asked how reading felt.

He thought for a while.

“That man was good at making excuses.”

“The umbrella?”

“And me now. Yesterday I wanted to buy her a new one, as though that would finish the story.”

Yu Mei looked at Xiao Lu rather than answering for her. Xiao Lu said she hadn't wanted to pick a color. That seemed sufficient. Yu Mei didn't ask whether she resented her brother.

“He did more than make excuses,” she told Lin Mo. “But I didn't know him his whole life. Don't ask me for a biography.”

His brief laugh tugged his old cheek injury. She didn't exploit it to tell him a story about his fearlessness. Pulling her sleeve over the edge of her dressing, she said she needed easier clothes. The old neckline was difficult.

She named a little shop across the street, unsure what it stocked.

“Can we come?” Xiao Lu asked.

“Buying something?”

“A thinner towel.”

Lin Mo had assumed she was merely accompanying him again. The towel recalled last night's complaint. Her day contained more than his past.

The restaurant entrance was being mopped. Zhou asked for a dry route; the bucket moved, a wider strip was wiped. Yu Mei stood resting against the wall without grabbing the wheelchair.

Someone behind them complained about their pace. Lin Mo nearly asked Zhou to hurry, then saw a shallow hole ahead of the caster. He let the other person go around while they realigned. A trouser leg brushed past. His injured knee stayed supported; he didn't move it to make the apology larger.

At the crossing, Yu Mei asked Xiao Lu how she slept.

“Sometimes all right.”

“Then sometimes. Don't report that every night is fixed.”

Watching the red light, Xiao Lu said she woke listening for somebody calling. Yu Mei didn't prescribe a solution. Door gaps still made her expect something to be fed underneath; she'd kicked her own shoe the previous night.

Lin Mo heard them without converting the exchange into questions for later. They were discussing themselves, not conducting an exercise in his recovery.

The light changed. Zhou wheeled him across first. Nobody used a confidence to demand the conversation continue.

The shop threshold was low, but the interior narrow. Lin Mo stayed in the shade outside with Zhou, able to see the front racks through the open door. Xiao Lu chose towels; Yu Mei asked for front buttons. Offered a pullover, she tried raising her arm, winced and handed it back.

“That won't work.”

Nobody called her difficult. The owner found another pile and said front-fastening garments cost more. Yu Mei asked how much and felt the cloth. Injury hadn't made prices disappear. Nor did she offer her history in exchange for charity. Only when the amount suited her did she agree.

At first Lin Mo found the waiting long. Then he welcomed it. They could argue about fabric and a few coins without every sentence demonstrating something they'd learned in danger.

Xiao Lu emerged holding two thin towels.

“Which?”

“For you?”

“Yes.”

“Choose the one you want.”

She stared at him. “I'm asking which looks better, not asking you to run my life.”

Zhou suppressed a laugh. Lin Mo smiled and indicated the paler one. He liked that color, though dirt might show more. She considered both, bought the other, and looked perfectly satisfied with it over her arm.

Yu Mei didn't change into her new shirt there. She picked up the bag and said she was going the other way; no escort needed.

Lin Mo thanked her for coming.

“I needed lunch too. Next time, tell me how long you want to ask questions.”

“All right.”

After a few steps she turned.

“I didn't say don't ask.”

He nodded without requesting reassurance again. He watched her avoid a raised paving stone until the corner blocked his view, then asked Zhou to turn them homeward.

They carried an unfinished old notebook and a newly bought towel. Xiao Lu offered the fabric for him to feel. His left hand touched its soft edge.

“Will washing make it thinner?”

“We'll know after washing it.”

He returned it without pretending he'd always known what she preferred. Zhou steered around a parked car. Xiao Lu folded the towel into its bag and walked beside them, still considering whether to wash it separately.
