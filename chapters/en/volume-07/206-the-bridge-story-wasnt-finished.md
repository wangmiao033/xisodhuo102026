# Chapter 206: The Bridge Story Wasn't Finished

Han Wei called while Lin Mo was trying to pick up a piece of steamed egg.

His left hand tilted. The egg fell back, splashing his shirt. He set down the chopsticks and wiped it clean before asking Zhou for the phone. Han wanted to add something to Tang Jing's account. Lin Mo immediately thought of returning to the screening room; his back tightened.

Hearing the pause, Han hurried to say that wasn't necessary.

“It's still sealed. I wanted to ask about the original interview.”

“Which interview?”

“Mr. Luo's bridge story. The original file is on my own equipment, saved before this happened. Not what came from the screen. He doesn't want everyone to think those other sentences were all he ever said.”

Lin Mo remembered the old man settling only after finding his keys. He knew nothing about restoring audio, he said. Han wasn't asking for that. Luo wanted somebody to listen. Tang Jing couldn't talk much, and whenever Han produced equipment, Luo waved it away. He didn't want the original deleted either.

“Does he know you mean me?”

“Yes. He said the man in the wheelchair. I haven't promised you'll come.”

Lin Mo set the phone on the table, loud enough for himself. Xiao Lu asked what was happening. After his brief explanation, she glanced at the unfinished food.

“Eat first. Decide afterward.”

The following afternoon they met Luo Huaisheng beside a small hospital flowerbed.

He wore the blue coat with one corner of its collar tucked inward. Han introduced the middle-aged woman beside him as his daughter, Luo Min. She offered her stool. Lin Mo thanked her; room for the wheelchair was enough. Zhou parked in shade, clear of the path.

“Now whenever I start talking, she tells me to rest,” Luo complained.

His daughter offered water instead of arguing. He pushed it back. He'd just drunk some. He hadn't even begun and somebody was already telling him to drink.

Lin Mo didn't explain that she meant well.

“What would you like to tell us?”

“The bridge. Not those things at the end.”

Han put his bag on the ground without opening it. No equipment today. Luo could speak as far as he wanted. After a moment, his daughter removed her hand from the cup.

The bridge had stood near where Luo lived as a young man. Its wooden boards were narrow, green and slippery after rain. Once, carrying baskets across, he'd seen a child approach from the other side. He'd called for the child to stay still, panicked himself, stepped on a board's edge and let a basket slide off.

He stopped, frowning.

“Not both. If both went over, how could I have got anything back?”

Luo Min remembered hearing about one basket. Asked when, she couldn't say. He shook his head and rested his hands on his knees to think. Lin Mo didn't choose a number for him.

Wind sent litter toward a caster. Zhou blocked it with his shoe. Watching, Luo suddenly said he'd done something like that: held the remaining basket with his foot, then found he dared move even less.

“What happened next?” Xiao Lu asked.

“The child came around and steadied the carrying pole.”

Luo Min nearly spoke. Her father noticed and told her to say it. In the version she'd heard, he'd led the child across first and the basket fell afterward.

Silence settled beside the flowerbed.

Han removed his hand from his bag. Lin Mo could feel everyone seeking a version that let them continue, preserving the old man's dignity without making his daughter a faultfinder.

“I might have made it sound better before,” Luo said at last.

His daughter looked down without smiling.

“Or I might have it wrong today. Don't decide for me right now.”

“I haven't.”

“That look on your face.”

“I was thinking of how you told it when I was little. I don't remember every part either.”

Lin Mo thought of the diary line that had first made his sister's refusal the problem. That didn't give him special knowledge of this family. He couldn't turn them into a lesson for everyone with missing memories.

“Does it need a certain ending today?” he asked.

“Can't keep it if it isn't certain?”

“I didn't say that.”

Han said the original interview could remain unchanged, without being edited into a definitive version or released. If Luo wanted, today's telling could be a separate addition. Or they could wait without recording it. He wouldn't use the old file to silence the man, or replace its words with today's.

Luo considered him.

“Don't write it down yet. When you write, I want to make it come out neatly.”

Han nodded and drew his bag closer to his feet.

Luo Min produced a printed photograph and asked whether it showed the bridge. Only a corner of railing was visible beside several people. She folded their faces under and offered him the wood.

He shook his head, then asked to see it closer. Perhaps it had been repaired later. The bridge he meant hadn't had that crosspiece.

His daughter didn't snatch the photograph away as a failed answer. There were albums at home; they could look another day, when he had energy.

“Not a crowd testing me.”

“Just us.”

“And don't ask who's that in every picture.”

Hurt, she said she wanted names while somebody still knew them. He looked at her, then said she could ask, but when he didn't know, don't offer ten people to choose from.

She nodded slowly and folded the picture away.

Lin Mo didn't ask whether this exchange belonged in the oral history too. The man feared examination, his daughter feared running out of time, and Han feared producing his equipment. Knowing a few sentences didn't let Lin Mo arrange them into a story with one wholly correct person.

A small insect landed on Luo's sleeve. He swatted and missed. His daughter gently shooed it away with the photograph's edge. They watched it fly, having accomplished something together that tested nobody's memory. Xiao Lu suppressed a smile. Asked why, she said the insect was busier than all of them.

The old man's earlier refusal to be written down still surprised Lin Mo. He'd marked unread on his diary and almost decided records could now help everyone. Luo wanted to stop halfway across a bridge, letting the baskets sway without a pen hurrying him ashore.

“Is the bridge still there?” Xiao Lu asked.

“Demolished. They built a concrete one.”

“Have you crossed the new one?”

“Much wider. First time I still moved over. Somebody told me to stop hugging the edge.”

He laughed. The laugh didn't drag the screening room's sentences behind it. His hand didn't follow anybody else's. Lin Mo saw it and felt his chest loosen a little. He couldn't declare all danger gone from one easy laugh, but he could be glad it happened.

Luo Min opened the cup and put it within her father's reach without urging him. After describing that unnecessary step aside on the new bridge, he drank of his own accord.

Han checked a message from Tang Jing before asking whether Luo wanted to hear it. She wondered whether there'd been a stall selling flatbread near the bank. She remembered a line in the original recording and worried she'd only retained its ending.

Luo brightened. Yes. The bread was hard enough to defeat his teeth; he didn't know why they'd sold it for so long.

“You bought it every time,” his daughter said, finally laughing.

“Cheap.”

Han asked permission to send Tang that remark. Luo agreed, then added that she shouldn't reply by voice if her throat hurt. Type instead. Han sent the message without recording them.

Sun warmed a patch of Lin Mo's trouser leg. Zhou turned the chair, then stopped at his wince. The footrest had touched the raised edge of the tree bed. A small retreat freed it.

Luo started to offer help. His daughter checked him. Annoyed, he said his hand wasn't useless. She let go: it wasn't that he couldn't help, but there was no room for everyone to stand.

He looked, saw the narrow gap and sat back.

Lin Mo didn't deliver a speech about asking first. He pointed out the footrest and suggested approaching from the other side. There was plenty of level pavement beyond this particular patch of shade.

Once they'd moved, Xiao Lu sat on the low border and wiped her hands with her new towel. Washed and half-dried, it folded more softly than yesterday. He recognized the darker color she'd chosen. His preference for the other one didn't oblige her to like it better.

“Brother and sister?” Luo Min asked.

“Yes,” Xiao Lu answered, without looking to him.

It was as ordinary as saying the towel had been washed. He knew the fact and wanted to hear her say it. The shared years didn't fill themselves into that single word.

Luo asked whether Lin Mo had found all the old writing he'd been searching for at the screening room.

His hand stopped. He didn't know how much Luo had noticed or been told. Words encountered in a room with other people didn't stay entirely inside his bag.

“I haven't read it all.”

“Why not keep looking?”

“I need to read what I already have carefully first.”

“Oh.”

Luo Min touched her father's sleeve and reminded him it was personal. He nodded without arguing and looked toward the flowers. Lin Mo let the warmth of sudden attention subside without offering another piece of history to improve the mood.

Preparing to leave, Han confirmed the original interview would be kept and not played again for now. Could he contact Luo if they wanted to add to it later?

“Yes. But don't start every conversation asking whether to record. We can talk while walking after lunch too.”

A gust lifted Luo Min's bag and exposed examination papers. She pressed them down. Her father held the other side without insisting on doing the whole task. They went slowly along the wall, still discussing the new bridge in lower voices. Xiao Lu didn't follow to hear the ending.

Once they were farther away, Lin Mo asked Han whether the screen had shown his writing again.

“The people guarding it haven't let us back in. I don't know.”

Disappointment came with relief. Lin Mo had almost found another reason to send everyone back for words he already possessed on paper.

“Ask the people responsible if anything changes. Don't open it yourself to check for me.”

Han agreed and left. Zhou asked whether Lin Mo wanted to finish reading at home. Seeing Xiao Lu watching a fruit stall, he suggested first getting something she wanted, without staying out too long.

She selected oranges, rejecting one soft underneath. He watched her pay and return with the bag. A faint peel scent escaped. It recovered no past, but he could remember which orange she'd chosen today.

On the way home Han sent a final message: Tang Jing had replied with a smiling face, no audio. Lin Mo showed Xiao Lu. She acknowledged it without asking whether it proved complete recovery. The oranges knocked softly together. He asked Zhou to slow at the next corner; the wheels passed without striking the crack.
