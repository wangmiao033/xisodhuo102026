# Chapter 191: The Clothes Nobody Brought

On the sixth morning, Lin Mo dropped his chopsticks while putting a piece of fried egg into his rice porridge.

Two fingers of his left hand refused to close properly. The chopsticks struck the bowl and landed on the sheet. Xiao Lu reached for them as he did. Pain caught in his shoulder, stopping them both. She went to replace them. He waited, watching the oil spread across his porridge.

He remembered doing this yesterday. Yesterday she'd laughed and blamed the slippery hospital chopsticks. Today she said nothing.

Six days hadn't returned his past. He remembered who had come to ask the doctor's questions a second time. Two pieces of the fruit Old Zhou had brought remained; he knew where. The man who had visited twice was Fang Ji, and he'd changed the dressing on his palm. Why they had entered that gray place together was missing. When people spoke of before, Lin Mo could only wait for the sentence to end.

Xiao Lu returned and turned the spoon handle toward his left hand.

“Use this for now.”

He almost said he still knew how to eat. Then he saw the washed chopsticks in her hand. She hadn't taken his bowl. She pulled up a chair and ate her own steamed bun.

After another examination, they were allowed to rest at a short-term rental near the hospital. No weight on his right leg; the wrist support stayed on. His left shoulder wasn't to take pushing or lifting loads. The doctor arranged follow-up and separately emphasized worsening headache, vomiting, or changes in consciousness. Zhou wrote down the time without promising they would encounter no more trouble.

Xiao Lu folded the bill, miscalculated something, and tried again on Lin Mo's phone, recharged at the hospital. From the angle of her bowed head, Lin Mo could see how little room the figures left. Asking how she'd earned money before would send her back through a place where he was absent.

Instead he asked whether they had enough for today.

“For today.”

That afternoon, they'd barely wheeled his original chair, checked by the transport crew, into the ground-floor room when Zhou's phone rang. He stayed nearby. Listening, he glanced at Lin Mo, then told the caller this wasn't a good time. Stay out, leave the street door open, wait outside.

A brief ring came down the line, like a bicycle bell reduced to a thin piece of metal.

Zhou ended the call. It was the laundry he used. The owner had said her husband was ill with a lung problem. Now she admitted he wasn't in hospital at all. He was in their shop and couldn't get out.

“I've just left hospital,” Lin Mo said.

“That's why I said it wasn't a good time.”

Sitting on the bed, Xiao Lu removed her scraped palm from the sheet. Had the woman called for help? Police and firefighters had come, Zhou said. At first nobody could hear a voice inside. During forced entry, someone had begun choking; the wider the opening, the deeper the mark around his neck. They'd stopped, cleared the frontage, and left people there. Now she wanted someone who'd heard of such things.

Lin Mo couldn't separate fear from lies at this distance. He asked Zhou to call back on speaker and asked only what she'd personally seen.

The woman cried, then abruptly quieted. Her husband, Sun Cheng, was forty-six. Last night he'd reached for a blue work jacket on the overhead rail. The jacket’s right sleeve had been drawn in, and he'd backed into the pressing room with it. The inner door wasn't shut, but he couldn't cross it. Once she'd pulled him, and he'd coughed cotton through his collar.

“Who brought the jacket?”

“I don't know. There's no entry.”

“Can the people outside see him?”

“Now they can. Not before. He was right there! They asked me to move, and the gap was this wide.”

Paper rustled. Perhaps she'd made a gesture the phone couldn't show. He asked her to leave the inner door alone. She didn't have to demonstrate.

She said she was outside. Then the bell rang again.

This time he heard two strokes: one short, one with a long tail. Her breathing moved rapidly away. Zhou called “Xu Qin” twice. Something knocked against something else. A man coughed. Each attempt to inhale sounded as if cloth were being pulled back down his throat.

“What did you do?” Lin Mo demanded.

“Caught the clothes. When it rings, I have to. Otherwise he—”

Zhou was already reaching for his coat. Lin Mo stopped him. Don't go behind the counter alone. He needed to see the objects and their positions; a telephone wasn't enough to tell an uninformed person where to put his hands.

“You're going?” Xiao Lu asked.

“To look from outside. Then decide.”

She picked up his light coat but didn't put it on him. Would she end up alone at another doorway?

He couldn't produce a forgotten past to prove otherwise. Today he couldn't stand or catch her; he could do less than she could. If the situation required leaving the chair for a narrow doorway, they'd stop and find another approach. Only then did she lay the coat over his legs.

The laundry was less than two hundred meters away, with a shallow cement ramp for carts. Zhou pushed. They passed a noodle shop, steam clouding its window while an employee wiped away a handprint. Lin Mo suddenly wanted hot noodles. It wasn't a recovered favorite. He told Xiao Lu, and she said they'd see whether it was still open coming back.

A uniformed man stood beside unused entry tools. Zhou explained. The man, Qiu, didn't treat them as experts arriving to take over. Could they offer specific observations? Water was on the floor; nobody was to touch the appliances. Lin Mo said he didn't know the solution, but could look at how the garment and man were connected. Qiu kept his colleague at the entrance and asked Xu Qin's consent. She nodded, gripping her apron.

A broken patch interrupted the ramp. Zhou approached from the side to avoid jolting the small wheels. Lin Mo tightened his abdomen as his knee shifted, then had to release the effort. Even this short journey hurt. A new danger couldn't displace the old injuries.

“Where will you sit?” Xiao Lu asked.

“Somewhere I can get back outside.”

She moved a folding chair out of the entrance without venturing deeper. Qiu identified an unplugged extension lead on the other side. It wasn't a barrier. She avoided stepping on it anyway.

Xu Qin looked younger than her voice. Her hair was tightly pinned and her apron stained with water. Several loops of black thread encircled her right wrist. Near the base of her palm, a short section entered the flesh. She hid it when Lin Mo looked.

“I didn't put a needle through it.”

Zhou sat her on a plastic stool and poured drinking water from the shop's supply. Taking it left-handed, she spilled half over her knees. Xiao Lu asked where to find tissues. Xu Qin pointed to a low cabinet beside the street window.

A counter divided collection space from the work area. Two washers stood left, hanging rails right. At the back, frosted glass filled the upper half of the pressing-room door. An old overhead track ran from beside it to the counter. A hook at the end held a faded blue jacket. Yellow paper was clipped at its collar, too far away to read.

No broadcast. No face growing from a wall. Lin Mo could still smell roasted sweet potatoes as an electric scooter passed outside. But the jacket's chest slowly swelled without a breeze, as though someone had drawn breath inside it.

“Sun Cheng?” Xu Qin called.

A dark shape appeared behind the frosted glass. Zhou braked the chair inside the street entrance, two steps from the counter. Xiao Lu stood ahead and to Lin Mo's left, clear of the hanging rail.

“Don't give it to them,” the man said.

Xu Qin raised a hand and lowered it.

“Sometimes he says that. When it gets bad, he tells me to catch it. I don't know which to listen to.”

Asked to describe the sequence, she said the jacket had been found in a disused dryer, unplugged long ago and used to store old hangers. Annoyed by the unclaimed garment, Sun Cheng had brought it to the counter. There was a signature space for the person collecting it. He'd signed to see who was playing a trick. The bell rang. He backed into the pressing room and never crossed out again.

She'd tried throwing the jacket away. It returned from the rubbish bag to the hook. Only once; she hadn't tried carrying it into the street. In the morning, catching it when the bell rang let her husband breathe—and put a loop of thread around her wrist. She hadn't dared stay away long since.

As she wiped her face, the embedded thread pulled. She winced without crying out. Could she move her fingers? Half a fist; her thumb wouldn't meet her little finger. Xiao Lu placed the water by her left hand without trying to cut the stitches.

Qiu said they'd tried cutting an outer strand. Closing the scissors had produced a fresh mark on the man's neck. They'd stopped. The scissors lay nearby, a single fiber on their tip.

Lin Mo asked whether Xu Qin wanted him to keep looking. Yes, but not to experiment on her husband.

“I won't ask him to do something dangerous to test it. I can't promise one look will tell me everything either.”

She uncovered her wrist and rested it on her apron.

“Where's the paper he signed?”

“Still at the collar.”

“Don't take it. Let me look first.”

Zhou turned the chair diagonally toward the counter. Without approaching the hook, Lin Mo could see a narrow second corner under the yellow sheet, like an incompletely torn duplicate. There was no sewn label inside the collar. An ordinary red plastic clip held the yellow paper at the collar. The left cuff was torn; the right was whole.

His fingers suddenly wanted to curl inward, as though they already knew what to avoid when catching a hanging sleeve. No remembered scene accompanied the impulse. He returned his hand to his lap rather than call it an answer.

The clapper moved.

A thin thread beneath the counter tightened first. Then came the sound. Xu Qin sprang up; Zhou stepped forward. As the second stroke trailed away, something scraped in the pressing room. The shadow abruptly shrank.

The jacket traveled along the track toward the outer counter. Its sleeve dropped straight in front of Xiao Lu.

“Don't catch it!” Xu Qin shouted.

She didn't reach. Lin Mo told Zhou to slide the empty plastic basket beside the counter underneath. It knocked on the floor and received the falling garment. The jacket went still. So did the bell.

Xu Qin's face had barely eased when a rapid tapping began against the basket's base.

The blue sleeve was threading itself through the holes. It didn't reach for the nearest person. It stretched toward the pressing room behind the counter and dragged the basket half an inch, leaving a white scrape on the floor.

Sun Cheng's head struck the door behind the glass.

“Lift it,” he said. “Don't let it touch the floor.”
