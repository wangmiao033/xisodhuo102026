# Chapter 163: The Crack in the Umbrella Handle

His sister had once refused to carry an umbrella for him.

Lin Mo stopped there. He wanted to add that it had been raining heavily, but the first thing he remembered was a dry shoe insert. Had the rain started on the way home? Had there been rain when he left? His pen pierced the paper. Fang Ji steadied the notebook without touching his injured forefinger.

“What comes next?”

“Leave it for now.”

Fang Ji reached for the pen, assuming his hand had failed. Lin Mo explained that he could not invent rain simply because it suited an umbrella.

“What else do you carry umbrellas for?” He Cen asked, eyes half open.

“Looking important.”

The handle had been black, with a crack near its end. There had been no crack when he took it. He had been taller than his sister, fond of walking with the umbrella tucked beneath his arm. Passing people he knew, he would slow down a little. He wanted it to look chosen, rather than whatever he could find at home.

He had announced an important errand. When his sister asked what errand, he told her children didn't need to know.

She had been young enough to open and close a new umbrella just to admire the way its fabric stretched. Old enough, too, to recognize importance as a word that prevented questions.

She had not agreed to lend it.

He said he would bring it straight back, and took it anyway. He could no longer remember whom he met, or whether he accomplished anything. Returning was clearer. He pushed the umbrella toward her and told her to put it behind the door.

She wouldn't take it.

“Yours. You put it away.”

“It's your umbrella.”

“You forgot that when you took it.”

He had immediately felt humiliated in front of other people. Were there actually other people by the door? He couldn't remember. The heat of being contradicted still clung to his ears. He called her selfish. Was her own brother not allowed to borrow an umbrella?

Yu Mei loosened her sleeve knot.

“That's what you're going to write?”

“There's more.”

He put it behind the door. The handle struck the wall. He hadn't thrown it; that was what he had told himself. His hand had opened a fraction too early. He hadn't checked the movement. The sound of plastic hitting plaster had been sharp.

His sister crouched, felt the white split with a fingertip, and carried the umbrella away. She hadn't cried. That made it easier to believe he hadn't done much harm.

It still opened the next day. He had used that as evidence she had made too much fuss. Later, he always remembered her refusal first, as though the crack had formed by itself afterward.

Lin Mo looked at his first sentence.

“That isn't enough.”

Fang Ji positioned the notebook beneath his left hand. Underneath he wrote: I took her umbrella without permission, then expected her to put it away. The characters wandered toward the edge. He stopped and asked Fang Ji to copy them clearly.

Giving up the pen felt uncomfortably easy. Speaking badly of his younger self might become another way to earn approval.

Yu Mei was attending to her shoulder. She offered none. He Cen pushed up his glasses.

“So rain wasn't the problem.”

“No.”

“Forget the rain, then.”

Fang Ji did not supply a replacement umbrella for the ending. Lin Mo had considered one: something new, an apology, preferably a sister who had already forgotten. He remembered asking once why she no longer used an old umbrella. She had said it was awkward to fold. Whether it was the same one, he couldn't be sure.

“I don't remember what happened afterward.”

Fang Ji left a space.

The seatkeeper adjusted his injured leg against the wall without approaching the paper.

“Would someone believe it later?” He clarified that he meant the writer who might no longer remember, not the sister.

“I don't know.”

“Why keep the ugly parts?”

Lin Mo's hand still ached from the pen. If he wrote only about protecting people, a future reader might mistake every refusal for ingratitude. He couldn't make that reader believe. He didn't want to leave proof that everyone was obliged to like him.

He said too much without pausing. Fang Ji let him catch his breath instead of recording it all.

Yu Mei asked whether Xiao Lu should hear what he was writing. Lin Mo remembered her request: finish a page first. Her past could not simply accompany his into a place she hadn't chosen.

He hadn't always been incapable of waiting for an answer.

After one training session, a man in his group had bent over an untied boot, fingers shaking. Lin Mo reached down and lifted the tongue to hurry things along. The man pulled his foot back, sole scraping concrete. Lin Mo thought he was refusing help until he explained through clenched teeth that his sock had stuck to broken skin.

“Do you know where it hurts?”

He hadn't known. He had recognized an action and assumed performing it faster removed the need to ask. He ended up crouched beside the man, supporting the heel at the angle requested while the sock was loosened. Someone called them to assemble. He wanted to hurry. He kept his hand steady instead.

“What was his name?” Fang Ji asked.

Two names crowded onto Lin Mo's tongue. One might have belonged to the dormitory; another might have come from a caption the Theater attached to an old image. He could picture a face in profile.

“Leave it out. I can't put an uncertain person into an action I remember.”

“And the action definitely happened?”

“That's how I remember it now. That's all I can say.”

He disliked the answer. It made the whole diary vulnerable to a casual question. But certainty wouldn't restore either the hand that had steadied that heel or his splinted wrist now.

Fang Ji noted Name Uncertain without striking out the scene. Lin Mo asked him to add that he had known to ask comrades where they hurt, yet often forgotten to ask at home.

“Don't make that a lesson about the army teaching you to be a good person.”

“It didn't.”

“I know. Otherwise there wouldn't be an umbrella.”

Lin Mo had no reply. Nobody laughed. Eventually he nodded; the bluntness helped him continue.

He remembered coming home after spending time in the service. He had aligned his shoes, checked a window catch, checked the door. His sister had draped a garment over a chair. He folded it and put it aside, then began planning her next day: when to get up, where to go first, how to pack.

He thought he was helping.

She rested a hand on the folded garment.

“I'm not doing that tomorrow.”

“It saves time.”

“I didn't say I wanted to save that time.”

He had drawn turns on the blank back of a sheet, explaining which route was inefficient. She ignored the diagram. Did he only come home to correct her?

He had felt deeply wronged. He rarely got home, and she wouldn't even let him do something useful.

After she went out, he noticed a loose thread protruding from the garment's pocket. It had been left within reach for mending. By folding it away, he had hidden a task she meant to do.

He didn't immediately go after her to apologize. He restored it to the chair, carefully arranging the sleeve as before. As though putting an object back could remove the person who had tried to organize her life.

“Stop there?” Fang Ji asked.

“Why I remember it comes next.”

That evening she sat down to mend the garment without comment. He waited for her to speak, then asked if she needed help.

“Move the lamp this way.”

At last he did something she had asked for.

He couldn't remember the lampshade. He remembered her frowning at the needle, missing its eye several times. Let me do it crowded into his mouth. He kept it there. When the thread went through, she bit her lip briefly, then bent over the seam.

“Worth keeping,” Fang Ji said.

Lin Mo asked him not to write that she had finally forgiven him. They had eaten and talked afterward. There had been no declaration of forgiveness, no embrace suitable for an ending.

“You really want to tie it up,” Yu Mei said.

“I still do.”

He had been choosing memories whose endings might comfort him. Facing a large sacrifice seemed to entitle him to arrange all his smaller wrongs into things already resolved.

He Cen told him not to take the pen back. His left hand was shaking.

They stopped. Fang Ji laid the pen across the notebook. Only after Yu Mei's shoulder eased did she take the chair back to the doorway.

Once connected, Lin Mo said the bottles were still there and no clearing had been attempted. Would Xiao Lu like to hear two family incidents as he remembered them? She could disagree or decline. He didn't need her to certify every word.

“How long?”

“A little over a page. You can hear just the first.”

“Start with that.”

He described the umbrella, including the absence of permission. At the scene by the door, he admitted uncertainty about anyone else being present. Xiao Lu supplied no witnesses.

He thought she had lost interest before she asked what became of the umbrella.

“I'm not sure.”

“Then don't buy me a new one at the end.”

He laughed, hurting his cheek. She noticed the breath and asked whether his mouth still hurt. He said yes without using the pain to escape her remark.

“I nearly wrote it that way.”

“You always think a new one fixes it.”

Did she mean that umbrella, or something else he couldn't remember? He asked whether she wanted her observation included.

“Not now. I don't want to dig everything up because you're writing a diary.”

He agreed and began the story about the lamp. This time she stopped him halfway. She couldn't remember the garment's color and didn't want to search.

“I haven't written a color either.”

“Write what you remember. Don't say I told you that's how it happened.”

“All right.”

He explained that he wanted another copy somewhere, because paper could be lost. He hadn't found a place yet.

“Could everyone read it?”

He wouldn't choose public display. Still, a place in the Theater's remains could not become safe merely by being called private.

“You can keep those two stories. Don't put my voice with them. Or what we're saying now.”

“Only my account. I'll label it as what I remember now.”

She changed the subject. Was Yu Mei standing? Yu Mei said she was leaning against the wall and would sit soon. Xiao Lu asked to end the connection. Next time he mustn't use the umbrella to test whether she still remembered.

Afterward, watching Yu Mei flex her fingers, Lin Mo asked what she needed him to do.

She pointed at the paper. “Wait until I sit before you read. Don't plan my next bit for me.”

He put aside the sentence already waiting in his mouth.

On the page, the umbrella remained broken. Nobody arrived to repair it.
