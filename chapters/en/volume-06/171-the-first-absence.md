# Chapter 171: The First Absence

Yu Mei's hand waited below the narrow screen.

Looking at the smudge beside her thumb, Lin Mo remembered he hadn't asked whether she was cold. There would always be something else he hadn't asked. If every question needed an answer first, he could sit here until the voice port rusted shut.

The original notebook still lay on his knees. He asked Fang Ji to return it to the little room beside the secured clean copy. Fang Ji rose from beside the chair, put it away, and returned to stand behind the wheelchair.

The arm's old latch hadn't shifted. Fang Ji kept his injured palm off the chair. He Cen leaned against the corridor wall; the seatkeeper sat lower, his injured leg clear of anyone's feet. None of them had crossed the gray threshold.

“I'm saying go ahead,” Lin Mo said.

Yu Mei looked up.

“Not another preview. Clear the autobiographical associations of my male self within the scope we checked, establish the continuing connection, and bring her out along the original route. Keep the agreed facts: my name, age, Special Forces, and that she's my sister. Whatever is cleared won't come back because I regret it.”

On sister, he bit the sore inside his cheek. His voice blurred, but Yu Mei waited for him to finish rather than supply the missing sound.

“I know the part left inside has to continue. I don't know what it might feel. I agree to begin. Yu Mei, press it now.”

“Xiao Lu?”

“My permission to be sent out hasn't changed.” Her voice was quiet. “I heard what he said.”

He wanted to tell her she didn't have to keep listening. Yu Mei's hand had already descended, doing exactly what he'd asked. He couldn't pretend now that he had merely been talking.

Execution Started.

The words lit once.

He heard an enamel basin hit the floor. Nobody outside the door held one, yet it seemed to roll beside his feet, striking its rim twice before stopping beneath a bunk.

His legs were whole and strong. His trousers were rolled up; water ran down his calves into slippers. Someone called his name from the washroom doorway, telling him to stop admiring himself.

He'd never considered this an important memory. He hadn't known he remembered the crack in the wall. The mirror hung too low; he had to bend to fit his forehead into it. His young face showed through the steam, a shaving nick beside his mouth.

He wiped his chin, staining his fingers red. The person outside called again. Lin Mo tried to turn.

The water fell silent.

The room remained, including the chipped enamel inside the basin, but he no longer knew why he was there. The mouth in the mirror moved, apparently finishing an answer. Only its silent shape remained.

Pain in his right knee drove him against the wheelchair's back. The door, narrow arm, and Yu Mei's hand crowded into view. The water had receded too quickly for him to keep its last sound.

A moment ago he'd known who stood outside. He'd even known how that person would draw out the last syllable when he called again.

“Who's calling me?”

“Nobody just called,” Yu Mei said.

His mouth stayed open. The washroom door had closed in his mind without leaving him its location. He knew he'd been in the military, knew what communal dormitories and washrooms generally looked like. Trying to return, he found only a picture anyone could draw.

“Something's already gone.”

Fang Ji stepped forward, then stopped. Such aborted gestures used to irritate Lin Mo; he'd thought saying he was fine should reassure people. Now he wanted Fang Ji closer and couldn't think what to ask him to do.

This wasn't in the diary.

They'd spent so long recording three incidents, filling the margins. He'd known they couldn't write everything. Only now did he understand how small an omission could be: someone calling through a doorway, perhaps holding something for him. Someone who'd done nothing grand enough to become a story told repeatedly.

He hadn't even left an uncertain name.

“Get the notebook…” He stopped. When Fang Ji asked which page, he shook his head. The boot after training belonged to another occasion. He couldn't borrow someone from that memory to stand outside this door merely because both involved the military.

“Want to write down what you just saw?”

“I want to remember it first.”

“Then tell me.”

Fang Ji waited, and Lin Mo's chest tightened. Tell him what? The basin was white; the mirror hung low.

But the utterly familiar irritation of being called twice had become only the knowledge that, moments ago, he'd felt it. Having Fang Ji write wouldn't make that voice his again.

He wanted to lash out. They'd come so far; why couldn't someone wait outside the door while he remembered one name? Hearing his own harsh breathing, he realized he wanted to blame the person who was actually waiting.

“It isn't because you're writing too slowly.”

Fang Ji's mouth moved. “I haven't picked up a pen.”

That brought him back to the chair. He studied the cloth across his knees until the anger subsided. Xiao Lu recited no old stories through the port, offered no substitute voice.

“Are you still there?”

“Yes. I don't know what you just lost.”

He nodded, then answered aloud. He needed her here, and needed that admission to remain. If she filled every gap with a sufficiently convincing story, he might lack the strength to tell the difference.

“It was an ordinary morning.”

Even as he said it, he realized he couldn't be sure daylight had come. Perhaps the person outside was in a hurry, perhaps he'd complained afterward about waiting. None of those perhapses could open the door again.

Something scraped at the arm's base, but Yu Mei kept her hands off it. Dust trembled along the latch; she asked Fang Ji to check the wheels. The wheelchair hadn't moved, its brake still lowered. The vibration came from inside the door.

The two parallel lines of light separated. One sank deeper; the other pressed against the door's edge.

Lin Mo's teeth clicked. The sound seemed to come from behind his breastbone. Something rose and fell half a beat behind his breathing. His right hand remained properly secured, yet he felt another hand opening repeatedly inside a cramped space.

He looked down despite himself.

“What is it?” Yu Mei asked.

“A hand.”

“Which one?”

He couldn't answer. Both his hands were visible, but the pressure remained: five fingers he couldn't see pressed against something cold. He couldn't prove they belonged to the structure being left behind. Still less could he call it an empty, unfeeling place.

Fear turned his stomach. There was nothing in it to bring up.

“Someone in there?” He Cen asked hoarsely.

“I can't tell.”

Xiao Lu drew breath through the port. “Something's loosened on my side. It hasn't opened yet. Don't move the arm.”

He didn't ask whether she could see another version of him. Once he'd said that aloud, they might give the unknown a name and presume to speak for its suffering, or agree on its behalf that it should stay forever.

He laid his left hand flat on his leg. His injured index finger touched cloth, producing a sharp pain with a definite location. The other pressure persisted alongside it. He counted four breaths; on the fifth, he forgot why he'd been counting.

A face appeared on the gray door.

His former face, older than the one in the washroom. Nothing grotesque about it, nothing deliberately damaged. Even the faint impatience at the corner of its mouth was familiar. Below it lay a neat collar; below that, the light failed to join up.

“Enough,” the face said.

He stiffened. The voice vibrated against the door's surface rather than emerging from the arm.

“You've proved you're willing. Come back.”

A hand reached out. He recognized the faint mark across its knuckles. Watching it, he could almost remember how the mark had happened. The image held there, like a book opened to the last page with a finger covering the answer.

Yu Mei didn't announce that it was fake. She called his name, then pointed ahead of the wheelchair. The hand cast a shadow on his right footrest, although the hand itself blocked none of the dim light behind the door.

“That's what I can see,” she said.

His throat hurt. Even if the image was false, the face had once belonged to him. Refusing its hand couldn't mean he'd never wanted it back. He envied the effortless way it stood while he needed help simply to keep from sliding out of his chair.

“You can say you want it,” Xiao Lu said.

He turned toward the port.

“I heard it say come back. You don't have to pretend you don't want that just so I can get out.”

“I do.”

The words cost him more than ever before. The face nodded gently, as though he'd finally delivered the correct line.

“But it hasn't returned what I lost.”

He searched once more for the washroom voice and found the same absence. The figure offering its hand was complete, but it provided no way back into his past. He looked away from the scar. His left shoulder spasmed, bringing tears to his eyes.

Fang Ji asked permission to steady the chair's back. He said yes.

The uninjured hand followed Fang Ji, hovering beside Lin Mo's shoulder as if to displace the real help. Lin Mo closed his eyes. Tires made a small sound under pressure. He neither reached for the image nor sent anyone through the gray door after it.

When he opened his eyes, the face remained, but its collar had fractured into little squares. Each showed the same mouth forming the words: come back.

The seatkeeper tapped his heel on the floor. “Over here.”

A thin metal edge curled outward beneath the door, directly toward a small front wheel. Fang Ji bent to press it down. Before his injured palm landed, Lin Mo told him to keep his hands away. A fine brightness flashed along the metal in time with the broken image.

He couldn't know what touching it would do. There was no reason to offer it an open wound first.

“We can move the chair back a few inches,” Yu Mei said. “Without moving the arm.”

She planted her feet and released the brake. Fang Ji briefly steadied the side frame with his left hand. The wheelchair rolled back, jarring Lin Mo's knee enough to make him cry out. The seatkeeper used his good foot to draw his trouser cuff clear, keeping his injured leg away.

Metal scraped upward, scoring the floor white where the wheel had been. Yu Mei reset the brake and tested it before letting go. The arm remained in position.

“Can you still hear me?” Lin Mo asked.

“Yes.”

He'd heard that answer countless times, over telephones, through closed doors, perhaps in places he could no longer recall. All he could hold now was how quickly she'd answered this time. The old collar finished breaking apart, but the metal edge stayed.

“What are you holding?” Yu Mei asked.

He opened his left hand. Nothing. His fingers had remained curled as though around the enamel basin's rim.

He tried to say where the basin had been and stopped halfway. A few describable details survived; the feeling of having looked out from that room was gone. If he kept speaking, he might preserve an afterimage he'd just witnessed as though it were a complete memory.

“Don't fill it in for me.”

Fang Ji swallowed his question.

A short sound came from Xiao Lu's side, like one strand of a long-taut cord releasing. She called him, her voice nearer, so near he almost turned to look behind his chair. Only the same four people remained outside.

“Brother, I can move a little toward you now.”

He answered. The word still touched a painful place in his chest; for the first time, no procession of remembered images of her immediately followed that pain.

The line inside the door advanced an inch. His left hand remained empty.
