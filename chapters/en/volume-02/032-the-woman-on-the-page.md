# Chapter 32: The Woman on the Page

The delivery worker kept hold of the box.

The man’s shoe still pinned the wheel. His open hand hung between them. The tape on his chin shifted with his breathing, an old red edge showing before his tightened jaw hid it.

“They’re waiting out front.”

The hand stayed.

“Check it, then. Hurry.”

He set the box on the narrow bench. Before using his key, the man turned the label beneath the yellow tape toward Lin Mo.

Lin Mo still held the chair leg, body facing the exit. Outside, Wang Hai braced its broken end against the corner.

“One more look under the lid.”

“Look,” Wang Hai said. “But if the trolley goes farther in, don’t climb aboard.”

“Who’s asking him to ride?”

“You aren’t. I’m telling him beforehand.”

Lin Mo steadied himself against the stool and passed the wood out. Wang Hai wedged it beneath the shutter again, right shoulder against the frame.

The flat key entered a hole in the box. The lid rose a finger’s width before the worker pressed it down.

“Just match the number. Leave that layer alone.”

The man stopped turning the key. Beneath the lid, a roll of gray paper filled the outermost slot. Its folded tail showed the same character: 茉.

More than a mistake on the outside label.

“Who put the number on?” Lin Mo asked.

“I move the trolley.”

“Then how do you know which layer we can’t open?”

The worker rubbed a fleck of adhesive from the lid.

“Same instructions every trip. Things fall out, you putting them back?”

The man fitted a shallow metal tray into four old recesses in the bench. He gestured toward it. Somewhere to catch anything loose. Still the worker held the lid.

Wang Hai coughed.

“Keep guarding it like that, I’ll think there’s something alive inside.”

The worker glanced outside. As his hand slackened, the man eased the lid up.

No person. Lin Mo saw no label for Xiao Lu’s channel either. A long, narrow strip lay in the center, wrapped with gray paper. The man unfolded the paper over the tray without inserting the strip into any illuminated socket.

First, Lin Mo’s channel number. Then two neatly printed lines.

[SILENT THEATER / PROPOSED IDENTITY: 林茉]
[FEMALE. PERMANENT RESIDENT.]

Below them, an empty box marked PERSONAL CONFIRMATION. No imprint or name.

The woman on the page had been here a long time. She recognized the light signals, knew where to stand. Her clothes fitted. When her turn came, she didn’t have to ask why she should go.

Lin Mo followed the printed movements downward. His right foot drew inward half a step.

The man held the paper there, unfolding no more.

Lin Mo looked down. His toes faced the bench. They’d been pointing toward the door.

He turned his foot back.

“Is this the next script?”

The worker reached to roll it up.

“They’ll explain out front.”

“It says how long I’ve lived here. That’s in the script too?”

“Proposed. You haven’t pressed anything.”

Lin Mo studied the empty box. Disliking the man was no reason to disregard that answer. The box was empty. No new voting progress appeared beside the bench.

He indicated the strip, then the paper.

“Has this been installed?”

The man shook his head, released a catch, and slid the strip out half an inch. Behind it, a section without a connector. He closed the catch without handing anything over.

Lin Mo’s right hand loosened. The status he could call up still showed forty-four and forty-two percent. His beginner protection hadn’t changed.

The worker pulled a thick pen from beside his apron.

“You want 默? I’ll write 默. Don’t hold up a whole trolley over a character.”

He bit off the cap and crossed out 茉 on the outer label, ready to replace it.

Lin Mo raised his palm without touching the pen.

“Inside too.”

“The name?”

The worker waved the label and read it aloud. “Lin Mo.”

Exactly the same sound. The answering voice rose behind Lin Mo’s teeth, as it always did when someone called him.

He kept it there.

“Mo as in silence. 默.”

“You understood me, didn’t you?”

The pen moved from the label toward the empty box inside. Lin Mo lowered his palm between pen and paper. The worker twisted his wrist, nearly inking his skin.

The man slid the gray paper across the tray, taking the confirmation box beyond reach.

Lin Mo withdrew his hand. For a moment he’d wanted to ask for the pen. Fill it in himself. Have it done.

“Is he correcting it or getting you to sign?” Wang Hai asked.

“I don’t know.”

“Then he can explain with the pen farther away.”

The worker held it upright, point toward the ceiling.

“Didn’t even touch you. All this over one character?”

Lin Mo didn’t smile. Dust from the bench clung to his fingers. If he’d grabbed the pen, he could have left his own print on that paper.

“Read the rest.”

The worker stopped smiling. The man had flattened the sheet, exposing every printed line. Slowly, the pen returned to the outer label.

Lin Mo pointed below PERMANENT RESIDENT.

“That’s already prepared,” the worker said. “Just worry about your name.”

Lin Mo looked at the practiced movements. Hair slipped against his lips. He brushed it away with his right hand. The figure on the page was printed in the same pose.

He almost lowered his hand to do it again, testing how easily it came.

Wang Hai’s shoe shifted outside the gap, striking where the broken casing had fallen.

“Your neck hurt, staring down that long?”

Lin Mo turned. Half of Wang Hai’s face showed through the gap. His injured left hand stayed against his side. His right held the wood; the shoulder bracing the frame was tiring.

“Still not finished?”

“He’s only changing the outside.”

“Put the pen away, then. Save your ink. Ten names outside won’t fix what’s inside.”

The worker needed two tries to snap the cap on.

“I change it, you complain. I don’t, you won’t let me leave.”

“He’s holding the wheel,” Lin Mo said.

The man glanced at him. Lin Mo slowly withdrew his hand from the bench.

“But I wanted to look. I asked.”

The man rolled the paper with the empty box facing outward. He picked up a square clip and set it beside the lid, then gathered the paper’s tail with his fingertips and returned the roll to its slot. The strip remained seated, unconnected to the bench’s cables.

As the worker reached for the box, the man shut it and held it down, showing him the square clip with its two notches.

“Return?”

A nod.

“On your head, then. Don’t ask me to bring you food next time.”

His gaze dropped to the tape on the man’s chin. His mouth moved, but he left the rest unsaid.

The man’s fingers paused. Then he fastened the clip to the lid and returned the key to its cord.

“Who takes it back?” Lin Mo asked.

The worker ignored him and jerked the trolley handle. The pinned wheel held. His elbow knocked the tray out of its recesses, sending it toward Lin Mo’s legs.

Lin Mo reached with his left hand. His shoulder sank. The tray struck his knee and slipped free. He pulled his legs away, bracing his right hand on the stool as a corner hit the floor.

A loud crash.

The unplugged white cable still hung from its ring. The white box stayed dark. On the small screen, Xiao Lu turned a script page without looking up.

The man crouched to retrieve the tray. Freed of his shoe, the trolley lurched forward, scraping the stool and jolting Lin Mo’s knee again.

Wang Hai drew the chair leg outward to clear a passage. He kept his injured hand away from the wheels.

“Lin Mo. Feet back.”

Lin Mo tucked his heels beneath the stool. The worker put the rejected box aboard.

“Want to see? Come on. Follow me. See where I deliver it.”

He stopped at the door, waiting for Lin Mo to rise.

Lin Mo’s right hand already pressed against the stool. The trolley turned away from their way back. Beyond the first bend behind the bench, he would leave Wang Hai’s sight.

He looked outside. Wang Hai waited with the wood, neither shaking his head nor urging him.

Lin Mo settled his weight on the stool and slowly lowered his left shoulder.

“I’m not following.”

The worker snorted and pushed inward. The man neither stopped him nor pursued him. A loose corner of yellow tape, caught beneath the clip, vanished around the bend.

Lin Mo listened to the retreating wheels.

“Still didn’t see who wrote it.”

“Saw who wouldn’t show you.”

The man reseated the tray, pressing its bent corner with a block of wood. Lin Mo reached to help. The man moved the block away and looked at his knee.

“Just knocked it. No cut.”

The man pointed outside.

Clear enough this time.

Lin Mo stood. Xiao Lu still held her script on the second stool, now clear of the light shining straight at her face. He didn’t call to her or ask for the white cable to be reconnected.

From packing paper the box had left behind, the man tore a strip. The tear cut through the end of the number. Below it, the name field was blank. A thick black arrow pointed beside the words:

[SILENT THEATER. AWAITING PERSONAL CONFIRMATION.]

Lin Mo took it by a corner, touching no buttons.

“Does this mean come over, or that I’ve agreed?”

The man pointed to AWAITING, then outside.

Lin Mo tucked it into a blank page of his booklet, separate from the fragment. He turned his shoulder to pass beneath the shutter, ducked, and straightened only when fully clear. Wang Hai removed the chair leg. The shutter dropped, hiding the apron last.

They stood outside a while.

“You really wanted to follow?”

“Yes. They’ve stuck that name on my forehead.”

“On a box.”

Lin Mo touched his forehead before realizing what he’d done. Wang Hai sighed and said no more.

A full-length mirror stood along their route to the rest area. Lin Mo stopped. Thinking his knee had given out, Wang Hai offered the wood.

“Not yet.”

In the glass, a skirt showed beneath his gray jacket. Narrow shoulders. Hair against his cheeks. Some things matched the gray paper.

He raised his right hand. The white wrist scar rose with it. His bruised knee still hurt.

“I’m Lin Mo. I’m a man.”

His voice remained thin. He paused and said it again.

Nothing reversed. The mirror held his present face. His status stayed at forty-four and forty-two.

Wang Hai stood beyond the reflection without reciting for him.

Lin Mo took his pen from the booklet and wrote his own 默 on the back of the packing paper. Beside it he wrote “permanent resident” and crossed those words out.

He didn’t cross out the face in the mirror.

“Remind me tomorrow, when we pass a mirror.”

“To say it, or answer for you?”

“Remind me. Even if I forget, ask me first.”

Wang Hai nodded and handed him the chair leg. Together they returned to the rest area.

At the door, Lin Mo gave his own name, then Wang Hai’s. Sun Ning answered inside. The door opened a little, her right arm still against her side.

“What did you bring back this time?”

He showed her the packing paper, keeping his original documents and medication blister pack with him.

“They’ve written me a past.”

She took its edge with her left hand and turned it over: first his handwritten 默, then the crossed-out words.

“Your writing?”

“On the back.”

She turned it faceup and pointed beside the arrow. He’d concentrated on AWAITING PERSONAL CONFIRMATION and missed the smaller line below.

[DURING THE PERFORMANCE, SPOKEN CORRECTIONS WILL NOT BE ACCEPTED.]

Sun Ning looked up at his mouth.

“Once you’re inside, how will you tell them they wrote it wrong?”
