# Chapter 46: Half a Notebook

Xiao Lu didn't catch the book as it slid farther down.

She watched Old Chen's face. When Wang Hai lifted the strap, she told Old Chen to tuck his chin. His ear scraped coarse cloth; his shoulder dropped. The bag still pulled against his neck. He couldn't get out.

“Cut it. Nobody pull.”

The apron man returned with heavy shears, offering them point down. Xiao Lu kept the loose fabric back with her left hand and took them in her right. Checking where Wang Hai's fingers lay, she opened the blades in front of Old Chen's shoulder.

“Cut here and the bag stays inside.”

He nodded, panting.

Two cuts split the thick strap halfway. Threads stretched taut. Wang Hai supported the cut end. At the third cut, Old Chen pitched forward. Instead of grabbing his notebook, he braced against the doorframe, bent his knees, and ducked beneath the curtain.

Wang Hai gave him space without hauling on him. Old Chen sat on the corridor tiles, feet still against the threshold, face flushed.

Xiao Lu returned the shears, then crouched before Old Chen.

“Can you breathe?”

He nodded. Only after a while could he cough. A red line crossed his neck, with a small scrape near his shoulder. Dust covered his dark skirt.

“Leave the book alone,” she called inside.

The apron man, bending for the fallen badge, straightened. He handed it out first, then cut the cloth from the axle. Bracing the frame, he had the cart attendant push from the other side to release the bag.

The notebook fell with a small sound.

Lin Mo couldn't see where it landed. He didn't summon Wang Hai to lift him or promise from a distance that the book was fine.

When the man pulled it from behind the bottom rail, the cover had torn. Its stitched corner trailed a scrap of bag. Several pages remained caught beside the wheel, exposing ragged edges. He tugged once; scraps dropped onto the track.

“Stop pulling,” Old Chen said clearly.

The man handed Xiao Lu the notebook and spread greasy palms.

“You went in yourself. Told you we were changing the set.”

“You stopped after I said someone was inside.”

“Other end started it. Can't see from here. Curtain's in the way.”

“Now you know the curtain blocks your view.”

He stared without answering. Waiting at the cart's rear, the attendant finally told him to block off the outer entrance too, before anyone else followed a voice in. The man dragged an empty barrier across it, then bent to retrieve the scraps.

Xiao Lu didn't wait for an apology. She laid the notebook in Old Chen's open hands, severed strap and badge beside it.

“He's getting what he can. Sit steady first.”

Old Chen stared at the torn cover without opening it.

The child called once more. His shoulder flinched, but his eyes stayed on the book.

Xiao Lu asked whether the sound could be turned off. The man stepped over the barrier and disconnected a small speaker hung on an inner frame. Dad broke off. The curtain moved, but no crying child followed the voice out.

The attendant had disconnected the cart's speaker earlier. Only now, looking at the separate cables, did Lin Mo know where the second voice had come from. Who recorded it, whether someone had timed it for Old Chen's turning away, he still couldn't tell.

Xiao Lu collected her script and bag, then fetched the tin cup. She opened it without immediately putting it to Old Chen's lips.

“Can you hold it?”

He tried. The rim struck his teeth. Hands shaking, he lowered it between his knees.

“In a minute.”

She waited instead of pouring water into him.

Wang Hai returned to Lin Mo, crouched, and rubbed his right wrist before laying down the wood.

“You'll have to wait to get up.”

“I know. Rest.”

“Not being polite. I really can't hold you.”

Lin Mo looked at his hand and nodded. His knees hurt, but a real wall supported his back. Nobody had to keep lifting him.

Old Chen managed a sip and coughed twice. Xiao Lu moved the loose wrapping away from the rim so the wet cloth wouldn't stick to his chin. Halfway through the movement, she stopped.

“What?” he asked.

“Nothing. Stay still.”

She arranged the cloth but didn't withdraw. Her thumb pressed empty air outside the rim. Lin Mo watched her stare at the spot without calling her name.

Eventually she closed her hand.

“I saw a yellow bowl. A piece missing from its edge.”

Wang Hai looked up. The cup was tin, its rim wrapped in cloth, nothing yellow about it.

“I wanted to turn the chipped part away. Someone was raising it to drink. I knew they shouldn't touch that side.”

“Who?” Lin Mo asked.

She shook her head.

He thought of bowls at home, in hospitals, white shapes crowding together. He couldn't remember a yellow one and didn't offer her the closest match.

She'd noticed his mouth open.

“What were you thinking?”

“Guessing which bowl. Couldn't.”

“Then don't guess yet.”

She returned the lid without turning the cup again, leaving the movement where it had stopped instead of repeating it until a person emerged.

The apron man brought two whole pages, a corner of another, and smaller shreds. More remained beneath the axle. Getting them out meant dismantling the base, which he wouldn't do now.

“When will you?” Old Chen asked.

The man shook his head.

“I'm not staying to wait.”

Nobody stopped him. The man put the recovered paper inside the torn cover and returned the damaged bag. Old Chen tightened the cup lid, packed cup and badge, and wound the severed strap around the bag's mouth. He held the notebook against his chest.

His thumb touched the inside cover. Suddenly he turned to Xiao Lu.

“If you'd caught it—”

She met his eyes.

“To catch it, I'd have had to let the cloth fall over your mouth.”

His lips moved. Nothing came.

“I chose to let you breathe.”

Lin Mo nearly explained that Old Chen wasn't blaming her, then realized he didn't know. Xiao Lu's answer was clear. It didn't need a third person smoothing away what hurt to hear.

Old Chen clenched the cut strap, eventually loosening his grip.

“I told you to leave the book. I said that.”

“Yes.”

“See it torn and I still want someone to blame.”

She pointed out the last scrap beside his knee for him to pick up. He put it inside the cover without asking her to say it was all right.

Only then did he look at Lin Mo on the floor.

“Made you all crouch down with me.”

“I sat down myself.”

Something almost like a smile reached Old Chen's eyes.

“You called me. I thought you were in my way.”

“I noticed.”

Lin Mo didn't add that he didn't mind. His shoulder remained numb; Wang Hai's hand wouldn't recover because of a sentence. Old Chen studied the strap without offering anything nicer.

They rested until Wang Hai could grip the wood again. Old Chen carried rather than wore his bag. Xiao Lu watched ahead for cart wheels. Several times he looked toward the curtain, turning away himself each time.

At Sun Ning's door, Lin Mo announced them all.

“Could hear how slowly you were walking.”

Seeing Old Chen's neck, she paused.

“The verification desk strangles people too?”

“Wasn't the desk.”

Lin Mo described watching the whole session, his pressing pause, Xiao Lu insisting they continue. He didn't hide the cart encounter behind a vague delay. Seated outside, Old Chen took over to explain entering the scenery.

Sun Ning's left hand rested beside her cup.

“Next person tells me they have fifteen years' experience, I'll ask which fifteen years.”

“Falling before doesn't mean you won't fall again,” Old Chen said.

“I don't have time to catch you.”

“I can see.”

She moved her cup left without getting up to make him room. Wang Hai sat outside the frame, wood across his right knee, finally able to release it.

Lin Mo asked about her arm. No better; she hadn't got up while they were gone. He put the water bottle where she requested, did only that, then sat again.

Xiao Lu kept the yellow bowl in her own script. After writing, she volunteered the last sentence: she didn't know who held it or when it was from.

“What do you want to remember this time?” Lin Mo asked.

“What my hand was doing when it came.”

“All right.”

She closed the script and studied him.

“You really sat down.”

“Standing wasn't helping.”

“You've been unable to help before.”

He didn't answer immediately. Her thumb pressed the spine, as though guarding the words from becoming recognition of their earlier life.

“I mean onstage. You hurt me when you grabbed me.”

“I know which time.”

“This time was different.”

His shoulder relaxed a little. She hadn't said she was no longer angry. But she'd let what happened now belong to him without making it account for the past.

She stood and said she was going back to wait. No more old sessions today. Lin Mo wanted to ask about tomorrow. Seeing her already outside, he asked instead whether she wanted Wang Hai to walk with her.

“His hand's tired. I know the way.”

She warned Old Chen not to wear the bag again and said goodbye to Sun Ning, who asked her to move the outside stool a little to clear the doorway. Xiao Lu moved it and left. Helping didn't oblige her to stay.

Lin Mo let his unasked tomorrow go with her footsteps. This time was different didn't answer for the next.

Old Chen opened his notebook. The cover stitching remained, but gaps showed inside. One page retained three characters. He separated whole pages and scraps on his knees, pausing at an oily tear.

“Keep what you can mend,” Wang Hai said.

Old Chen tried fitting two pieces. They didn't join. Lin Mo had expected him to recognize every page's place. Watching him turn one fragment three times, he understood otherwise.

“Remember what you wrote?”

“Some.”

“I can write down today. You tell me. I don't know the earlier things.”

After a while, Old Chen agreed.

Avoiding the pocketed fragments, Lin Mo opened a blank page near his own booklet's back. Old Chen says. The male voice, the similar session name, walking behind the curtain. Old Chen asked him to add that he'd known it might be recorded and still wanted to listen. Lin Mo read it back without omitting that uncomfortable part.

“The child too.”

“Heard someone call Dad?”

“Yes. Don't write my son.”

Lin Mo stopped. Old Chen held the scrap with three remaining characters, his finger moving across the ink.

“I didn't think he'd suddenly appeared in there. Wanted to hear whether anyone said his name next.”

Sun Ning watched without asking why he needed someone else to say it.

“I remember him complaining about the schoolbag. His name, sometimes I get right up to saying it and there's nothing. The harder I try, the worse.”

Lin Mo laid down his pen without supplying a name.

Old Chen looked at the notebook's front. The old loss at the front was separate from today's tears farther back; part of the binding still held.

“You said you never got the front half back.”

“Never did.”

Old Chen pressed his palm against the old gap for a long time.

“Something I didn't tell you earlier.”

Lin Mo left the pen down.

“They didn't snatch that half out of my hands,” Old Chen said. “I handed it over myself.”
