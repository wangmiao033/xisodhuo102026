# Chapter 58: The Cup Kept Its Place

Lin Mo couldn't stand.

He reached the wood beside his left hand. Putting weight on that arm made both scrapes bleed. He let go and drew his feet closer, but his knees wouldn't arrange themselves beneath him.

After a long time crouching, Xiao Lu asked, “Help you, or move aside?”

“Behind my back. Don't lift my arms.”

She went behind him, waiting until his legs raised him a little before supporting his lower back. Shao moved the folding ladder closer. He lowered himself onto its bottom step without looking at the glass, hand pressed over the tag inside his jacket.

Tao blocked the white frame outside the track. Someone told him to clear the way. His reply was hard: source-owner processing wasn't closed. The vacant footprints glowed. Old Chen's face never returned.

“Don't play that voice,” Xiao Lu said.

Tao unplugged the cabinet's local preview. The screen stayed lit, red name still present.

“Silent here. Can't reach the continuation end.”

She nodded without taking one quiet place as proof Old Chen would never be used again. Lin Mo almost asked about the original recording. There wasn't even a body to lay somewhere. Those old voices might still wait for somebody to answer.

Shao asked to see his left arm. He rolled the sleeve slowly, stopping when the tear caught beneath his elbow. She glanced at it. There was water outside. Don't brace doors with that arm again.

“Can we still check this number?”

She removed the source-owner dispute strip and tried reinserting it. The match field stayed dark. Only DEACTIVATED appeared beside it. She withdrew the strip and clipped it beneath this cycle's fault report, giving him no replacement number.

“That's as far as I can take this comparison.”

She'd tried supporting Old Chen herself. Now she could only hang the incident beside the cabinet and make room for another rack. Lin Mo wanted to hate her answer. Demanding she invent another route would only leave them lying to each other before the glass.

He asked her to keep the enforcement result onscreen a little longer.

The tag slipped as he took out his booklet. Catching it on his knees, he removed the receipt too and set both on the ladder. He opened the page with a hundred thousand.

His pen hovered. First Seatkeeper, then currently matched source owner. Xiao Lu read the still-lit physical-severance text word by word. He copied it. At complete, his pen stopped on the final stroke.

“You don't have to make that stroke neat.”

He finished it, dragging ink across the paper.

Then: I raised the inner guard. My left sleeve caught. Old Chen stayed in the source-owner position, waiting for me to withdraw. His right wrist almost refused to hold the pen.

“Put it away,” Xiao Lu said. “I didn't see that part. Tell me when we're back.”

He didn't ask her to certify he'd had no alternative. She'd waited at the outer desk until he called the location. Those first decisions hadn't happened before her eyes.

He returned the tag and receipt to his jacket. As Tao cleared the doorway, Lin Mo asked if anybody knew where removed bodies went. Tao shook his head. No borrowed rumor to send him waiting at another desk.

Outside the passage, Xiao Lu stopped.

“I need to wash my hands.”

Only at the water station did he notice her hands were still half closed. Water struck them. Her fingers opened and closed; she reduced the flow, staring at gray dirt in her palm lines.

He rinsed his forearm too, jaw tightening as cold water crossed the scrapes. The blood thinned. He didn't rub. When the water stopped dripping, he loosely lowered his torn sleeve.

Xiao Lu turned off the tap and stayed there.

“I told him not to try that leg again.”

He remembered.

“Then I wondered if listening to me cost him one chance to get out.”

“It hurt him.”

“I saw. I saw the other side going empty too.”

She pressed her wet hands against her clothes without waiting for him to judge the choice. He almost said she'd done enough. That sounded like another request to put it away. She hadn't asked for a verdict.

“I still want to yell at him. Why stand so long? But he was already trapped when I came.”

“He waited for me.”

“I know. I'm not saying it's all your fault, or that none of it is. Let me think.”

Lin Mo leaned against the water station, leaving her half a step. Eventually she turned and said they should go. Her cup stayed in her bag. She offered nobody a drink to wash this down.

Wang Hai saw the wood first.

“What happened?”

He stood, reaching halfway before noticing blood beneath Lin Mo's sleeve. Sun Ning turned, waiting for the usual names.

“Lin Mo. Xiao Lu.”

He stopped.

Wang Hai looked behind them. Clothes rustled as someone passed in the corridor. Nobody turned through their door.

“Old Chen?”

“He died at the cabinet.”

Wang Hai didn't move. Sun Ning's left hand pressed her seat, but her shoulders didn't rise. Lin Mo heard himself continue: physical severance, before their eyes, his body disappearing piece by piece. No return to the stage.

He was speaking too fast. Wang Hai raised a hand.

“Stop. Sit first.”

Lin Mo sat, wood beside the chair. Wang Hai looked at the bag by his feet and stepped back, nearly kicking its base.

“He asked me to watch it.”

“I know.”

“Stop knowing everything. He said he'd come back for that cup. I told him not to talk about his effects.”

Lin Mo said nothing. Wang Hai bent to lift the bag. His right wrist hurt; he set it down again. The cup touched the floor with a small sound.

“Can't even hold this.”

Sun Ning told him to move his stool closer and sit. Don't fight the bag. He sat, keeping his hand on its mouth.

Lin Mo laid the tag and receipt on a chair where he could see. Wang Hai picked up the tag and covered the channel number, leaving only the small 100% field visible, as Old Chen had when he'd first arrived. Then he released it.

“He gave you this?”

“Through the grille. Asked me to check again.”

“Did you?”

“His number.”

Wang Hai put it back on the receipt without tearing either. Start before entering the cabinet, he said. Not with the final prompt.

Lin Mo described raising the guard inward, Shao's warning, Old Chen keeping his face forward. When he reached telling Old Chen to leave first, Wang Hai asked whether his hand had been free.

“No.”

“How could he leave?”

“Shutter would've dropped on me.”

Wang Hai looked from his wrist's white scar to the newly torn sleeve.

“Then was leave first something he could actually do?”

Lin Mo opened his mouth.

“I didn't give him another way.”

Sun Ning had listened throughout. Had Old Chen known beforehand that he'd die? Lin Mo shook his head. They'd known crossing the limit would be charged to the source owner and the requester could pursue a claim. The severance request appeared after his arm caught.

“Then don't say he'd planned it all from the start.”

“He hadn't. He was frightened.”

He described Old Chen asking them to pull, and his last request that nobody open his notebook. Wang Hai's hand stopped on the bag.

“Only checked everything was there. Didn't turn any pages.”

Lin Mo told him he'd already claimed that, only to be asked how he knew. He repeated the exchange exactly. Anger crossed Wang Hai's face; then he looked down.

“Even then, he had to keep account of whether we got our words right.”

Nobody laughed.

Xiao Lu, seated by the door, joined the account where she'd entered. The grille had risen. Nothing pinned his legs. She'd supported his elbow and the weight had gone. Only that part. No noble reason supplied for the waiting she hadn't seen.

Could they leave the door wider? Sun Ning agreed. Xiao Lu turned her stool toward the corridor, without moving beside Lin Mo.

Much later, Wang Hai returned the tag and receipt to the bag. The damaged notebook lay over the cup. He placed both objects against its outer cover, between no private pages, and wound the severed strap around the mouth as before.

“Here for now.”

He stood and walked toward the door. Lin Mo thought he needed air until he asked which way the cabinet stood beyond Tao's desk.

“You want to go now?”

“See for myself. You say he's gone. I still picture him standing there.”

Lin Mo gave directions. When he mentioned the white frame, Wang Hai stopped in the doorway. Looking at Lin Mo's sleeve, he asked what he could bring back.

“I found no body to bring.”

“Didn't ask you to decide for me.”

“Only telling you what I saw.”

Wang Hai stood with his right hand against the doorframe, fingers slowly loosening. Then he returned to the bag. He didn't say he wouldn't go, only that his legs felt weak. He'd sit a while. His chair scraped the floor. Sun Ning's right shoulder jerked; he stopped until she'd adjusted, then lowered himself.

Nobody divided the contents. Lin Mo remembered Old Chen saying the water was cold again. He looked toward the bag, then away. He'd never again tell this man to drink slowly. An unfinished mouthful couldn't close fifteen years.

Sun Ning asked what had been inside the cabinet.

He opened the booklet on his knees. A cramped word rewritten; the long ink trail after complete. Suddenly he feared they'd see a hundred thousand first and think what he'd obtained could balance a person's loss.

“This is what he waited for me to read.”

He wanted to add Old Chen saved me on the preceding page. His pen stayed raised. The words seemed too small to contain what had happened, yet written too large they might cover those last pleas to leave.

“He did help you,” Sun Ning said.

He looked up, realizing he'd asked aloud.

“He helped, and he was afraid, and he could regret it. Don't erase him asking you to pull just to leave a prettier account.”

Lin Mo laid the pen along the spine. He didn't replace the events with a tidy phrase.

Wang Hai leaned over the page without taking it. Sun Ning asked Lin Mo to read the important lines, her injured arm still supported by her left hand.

Same livestream. A hundred thousand eligible viewers simultaneously maintaining freedom.

He paused for questions. Wang Hai pointed to leaving reduces: if early arrivals left, later viewers had to replace them. Yes. Withdrawing freedom reduced it too; they couldn't keep accumulating in another session.

“Everybody has to choose it?”

Lin Mo indicated not everyone required. The display said unanimity wasn't necessary. Wang Hai almost said that made it easier, then looked at a hundred thousand and swallowed the words. Some could keep watching the spectacle. They still needed others to stay.

Did the number include them? Sun Ning asked.

He pointed out performers don't count and accompanying participants don't count to Wang Hai, then read both aloud. The provisions behind the cabinet gave Xiao Lu no vote through personal connection. She listened without counting herself.

“After it's enough?”

“Verifies this session's departure applicant. Doesn't say everyone leaves.”

He hadn't written that clause earlier. He identified what he'd copied there and what he was adding from the text he'd just read. In the blank space he wrote added from memory, then recorded the clause and application route: Start Broadcast page, Departure Application for This Session.

Wang Hai watched his pen.

“You tried that entry?”

“No. Read-only there.”

“Another thing we need to see ourselves.”

Lin Mo nodded. The broken bag lay by their feet, a corner of the old cover showing beneath its severed strap. He didn't suggest opening it to search. He smoothed his own badly written page instead.

Xiao Lu rose. No more old recordings today. She wanted to sit outside. He agreed without asking her to wait until he recovered. She took her bag to the bench still visible through the doorway.

Sun Ning closed her eyes, then opened them.

“Right now, I want to tell you to stop going.”

Lin Mo watched her.

“But if you go on, doing it for us doesn't mean you don't have to ask. Today I didn't even see where Old Chen stood at the end.”

“Next I only want to find the Start Broadcast page. See if this route can actually be selected.”

“That's next. Put your hand down now.”

He closed his booklet and rested his hands on his knees. Wang Hai sat beside the bag, keeping that small patch of floor occupied.

Inside the cloth, the cup still took up its old place.
