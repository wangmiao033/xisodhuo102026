# Chapter 44: Let Me Be Angry with You

Lin Mo returned his left hand to his lap.

"Go on."

Xiao Lu pressed play. Her recorded hand finished rising, fingertips touching the hair beside his ear. She asked what had happened to him. He hurried to say it didn't matter now. Sitting on the stool, he heard how slowly his attempt to correct that had come, as though he'd yet to grasp her right to ask about him.

The voting field lit. Wang Hai shouted to watch the lights.

At the table corner, Wang Hai stepped back. The broken wood tapped the partition. Lin Mo kept watching. Onscreen, he held Xiao Lu's hand. She asked him to loosen his grip; only then did he. The count had stopped at sixty-four.

Her unfinished question sounded, followed immediately by the host's voice.

Beside him, Xiao Lu didn't repeat the words. She covered her right hand with her left, rubbing its back more and more slowly. He resisted guessing which memory she'd found there.

His recorded self raised his cuff, chasing her gaze with the patch. She tried to pull away. He kept his fingers hooked beside her wrist, so desperate that even releasing her seemed to await an answer.

"You're hurting me."

The small speaker was quiet. From this distance he heard it more clearly.

Xiao Lu separated her hands.

"I remember it hurting. Didn't remember you saying so much before."

He wanted to explain how frightened he'd been of losing her. His recorded self had already explained it: clutching her, bending down, trying to make her see what mattered most to him.

"I didn't listen," he said.

She didn't say it was all right.

He watched himself turn toward the yellow input, catch his sock on the crack. Wang Hai extended the wood. Xiao Lu's shoulder moved beside him. Her right hand left the table as if to stop the man about to strike the microphone.

Halfway there, she stopped herself.

"He's sitting here now," Wang Hai said.

"I can see."

She turned her palm toward herself and slowly curled her fingers. Lin Mo didn't ask why she'd wanted to protect him. One more question like that, and she might feel obliged to prove where every reaching hand came from.

Onscreen, she asked where he was going. He could stop. No need to go down there.

Xiao Lu leaned forward and turned up the volume.

"I don't remember this."

He thought of the gap she'd written down.

"I didn't recognize you anymore," she said. "I could still tell you not to go."

"You still knew my name."

"I've always known your name."

He heard no reproach, but his fingers tightened. He knew his own name too. Knowing how to say it and feeling it belonged to him had only recently split apart before the long mirror.

She retrieved her script onscreen and asked where they'd got to. His silence had been preserved. Nobody had cut it into an immediate embrace.

She frowned at his answer: my sister. Only when her recorded self told him not to answer for her did her brow ease.

He didn't ask what she believed now.

Finally they pressed their separate end buttons. Both microphones went dark. The settlement screen appeared, with the two of them still visible in a corner. Neither had managed a smile.

[END OF SESSION RECORDING.]

Xiao Lu didn't restart it.

"What was I going to ask you?"

Lin Mo watched the motionless screen. He remembered that unfinished you. He'd supplied endings for it: Were you hurt? Why did you come? When can we leave? Each sounded like his sister. None had come from her mouth.

"I don't know. You didn't finish."

"You didn't tell me before. I thought you knew the whole question."

"Only the beginning."

She slowly found the page in her script. He looked away, listening to the pen. Eventually she read her new line aloud: Nothing recorded tells me the rest.

His chest tightened. He nearly objected. Perhaps the thin piece held it. Perhaps another camera, or somewhere in her mind. But they'd watched from beginning to end. Her voice had stopped there. Nobody could have heard the part she hadn't said.

"If something comes back later, I'll have to separate that too," she said. "Whether it's from before, or something I thought of after hearing this today."

"You still want to try?"

"Let me say whether I want to."

He nodded. She capped her pen without offering him the page.

"Want me to tell you what I heard?" Wang Hai asked.

"Did you hear all of it?"

He shook his head.

"Then not now."

He drew back the foot he'd started forward. A damp handprint marked the wood where he'd let go. Lin Mo felt an ache looking at it. They both wanted to finish her sentence, as if that would spare her facing a question with only its beginning left. Yet here she sat between them, her script closed around the gap.

His throat hurt as though he'd said all those words again. Every one had played. None had left him an easier ending to carry away.

"I said it," Xiao Lu told him.

He looked up.

"That word. I saw my mouth move. Heard it. You didn't say it was something I'd agreed to perform."

His left fingers remained pressed against his skirt.

"But I still don't recognize you."

"I know."

"Don't be so quick to say you know. I've just watched myself recognize you and then not recognize you. I'm scared too."

He looked properly at her. She'd sat rigidly upright throughout, as if relaxing might let the person onscreen pull her to her feet. He withdrew his forearm from the table to leave her room to lean.

She only laid out her script.

"For a moment I thought my hand was hurting now."

"Want to stop here?"

"It's finished."

Then she gave a short, unexpected laugh.

"How much longer were you planning to let me stop?"

His breath eased. He didn't take the laugh for forgiveness.

The attendant asked whether they wanted the recording reference. Xiao Lu wrote down the pair of session numbers and how much they'd watched, without copying that form of address. Lin Mo asked to see the unbroadcast collection of the recording pieces. The man indicated the sign overhead.

"Not here. Where they go after collection isn't my business."

Lin Mo let the answer stand. He still had to trace the yellow input's supply himself. This table had shown them more of one session; it wouldn't dismantle the theater for him.

Xiao Lu closed her script and drew her hands off the edge.

"When you paused, were you afraid the rest would upset me?"

"Me too."

"What else?"

He glanced at the dark screen.

"I wanted you to spend longer seeing yourself recognize me. Maybe then—"

He heard what he was saying and left it there, without making it sound like something he was doing for her.

She nodded. The little ease in her face vanished.

"I'm angry with you."

"Yes."

"About just now too. Not only onstage."

He didn't know where to look. He nearly promised it wouldn't happen again, then remembered hurting Sun Ning's arm.

"You can be angry."

"I don't need permission."

Wang Hai shifted. The wood tilted, then steadied. Xiao Lu glanced at him without asking him to speak for anyone.

"What now? Water? An apology? Or are you going to ask whether you can still come and see me?"

Lin Mo stared. He'd been considering the first two. The last had waited on his tongue since the screen stopped.

"The last one."

"Let me be angry with you first."

She took out her own cup and drank. He stayed, without moving closer to wait for forgiveness. Wang Hai sat on the newly available edge of a stool, wood across his knees. They remained quiet until the attendant began gathering the selectors. Xiao Lu tightened her cup lid and put it back in her bag.

Before rising, Lin Mo waited for Wang Hai to find his footing. His jacket hem brushed a table leg. Xiao Lu's hand moved, but she only said, "On your left."

He gathered the fabric, let her pass, and slowly stood.

"Finished?" Old Chen asked from across the corridor.

Xiao Lu said yes. She didn't report a result for Lin Mo. Old Chen didn't guess why she looked unhappy. He made room beside him.

Lin Mo sat to rest his legs. A few steps away, Xiao Lu turned her back to the table and wrote with her script against the wall. He couldn't see. He didn't ask Old Chen to look.

Wang Hai went to put back the stool. Two steps away with the wood, and Lin Mo leaned forward, almost calling him back. Wang Hai stopped himself.

"Two steps. I can see you."

Lin Mo nodded and eased against the wall. Old Chen moved his cup out of the way.

"How much did you hear?"

"Only the bit about being angry."

"She says she called me that. Still doesn't recognize me."

Old Chen looked at his hands before answering.

"Are you asking how to make her recognize you, or how to sit here?"

Lin Mo had no answer.

Loosening his lid, Old Chen said he'd once treated someone's willingness to stay and listen as hope. Anger had reassured him: if they could get angry, surely they remembered. Eventually that person wouldn't even be angry where he could see.

"Counted everything. Left because they were afraid of me. Stayed because they knew me. However I counted, I came out right."

Lin Mo glanced toward Xiao Lu and looked away.

"What did you do?"

"When they told me not to come, sometimes I didn't. Started keeping count in my book. Resisted once. Later I stopped. Afraid I'd reach ten and think I could trade them for a yes."

Wang Hai returned in time to hear the end.

"Charging interest too?"

"Yes. Kept my own books. Always won."

Old Chen drank and grimaced. This time he didn't blame the cold water. Lin Mo began to understand that fifteen years often meant having no better answer either. Old Chen hadn't made his own failures into a debt somebody else owed.

Xiao Lu returned, script against her chest.

"I wrote down what I saw today. It doesn't count as getting my memories back."

"All right."

He didn't ask to read it.

"Next time you only want me to watch one sentence, say that's what you want. Don't wait until your hand's on the button."

"All right."

After a moment, she asked whether his shoulder still hurt. He said yes. She left the past alone and warned Wang Hai about a raised edge on the way back. He'd almost tripped coming here, too busy watching Lin Mo.

Wang Hai looked at his feet without saying he knew.

They rested until Lin Mo could walk, then returned along the outer corridor. Xiao Lu neither took his arm nor hurried out of sight. She stayed on the cart side, stopping at approaching wheels so those behind could gather their own hems.

Another cart stood beside the waiting entrance, a small speaker attached to its front. Lin Mo heard metal scrape, then a man's voice.

"Move your hand. The needle's still in there."

Old Chen stopped.

He didn't call a name as Lin Mo expected. His cup tilted. Water leaked around its loose lid onto the dark skirt.

Xiao Lu turned and asked what was wrong. He stared at the black box traveling beneath the speaker. The cart's handler was replacing pieces in the waiting-sign rack. Someone told them to move out of the way.

The man's voice repeated.

Old Chen stepped forward. Xiao Lu asked where he was going without reaching to stop him. His toe hovered where the wheel had passed, then withdrew. His lips moved, a name perhaps, but none came out. When the handler looked over, he only asked them to wait. He didn't hold out his booklet.

Lin Mo remembered the schoolbag, the pliers, the stuck needle. He couldn't identify the speaker for Old Chen.

"Shall we stop here?"

At last Old Chen set his cup beside the wall. He took out the thin booklet with its stitched corners and opened near the front. His finger rested against a torn edge.

"Is it the time you remember?" Lin Mo asked.

"I don't remember those words."

Old Chen looked up. The woman's voice that came out was rougher now.

"The voice is mine. From before."
