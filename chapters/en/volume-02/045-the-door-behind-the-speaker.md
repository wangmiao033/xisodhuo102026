# Chapter 45: The Door Behind the Speaker

Old Chen's finger stopped at the missing pages.

Lin Mo watched the black box. The person behind the window with the old photograph had used a familiar voice to ask for his hospital receipt.

“I've heard something like my old voice here. Someone was waiting for me to reach in.”

“I know they can fake it.”

“I wasn't saying you didn't.”

The cart attendant slid a replacement into the sign rack and waited for its light. His sleeves were rolled up; black grease marked one cheek.

“Against the wall if you're passing. Scenery coming through behind us.”

“Where's that recording from?” Old Chen asked.

“Old demonstration. Connected the wrong socket.”

He pulled a short cable. The male voice broke off halfway through a word. Without asking Old Chen to prove anything, he kicked the chock from beneath a wheel.

“What happens to the old recording?”

“Comes with the cart.”

Old Chen put his notebook in the bag, retrieved the tin cup by the wall, and tightened its leaking lid. Lin Mo thought he was leaving. Instead, he turned with the cart.

“Can I see the session name?”

The attendant wouldn't dismantle the rack. He tilted the box enough to expose a frayed label over older adhesive. Lin Mo could make out CLOTHES MENDING. A catch hid the rest.

Old Chen slowly set down his cup.

“I performed that.”

Xiao Lu examined the label from the rack's other side.

“You recognize the name or what's inside?”

“The name. Mine was called that too.”

“Could be another with the same name.”

“Yes.” His mouth tightened. “Heard the voice and supplied the missing words.”

He left the catch alone. Lin Mo wanted Xiao Lu to stop questioning him, then remembered Old Chen asking him questions like these. Fifteen years didn't make answering easier.

She moved a stool out of the walkway rather than demand proof.

“What are you looking for?”

Old Chen didn't answer.

Wang Hai stood a little behind Old Chen, still holding the wood in his right hand.

“Mending your son's schoolbag?”

“I performed it here. Don't make those two occasions one.”

They'd heard about a man at home pulling a needle out with pliers. Now there was a performance of it, something an attendant could unplug.

Old Chen pressed his bag.

“They hadn't finished changing me. My voice wasn't like this yet. Put me at a table with a new schoolbag. Reenact what I'd told them. Someone directed everything, where the needle went, where to put the pliers.”

“Why agree?” Lin Mo asked.

“They said I'd hear the child's answer afterward.”

He picked up the cup again. Water dripped from its base, and he wiped it against a dry patch of skirt.

“Did you?”

“Heard a child say it didn't rub anymore.”

He didn't call the child his son.

“I remember being happy. That part's clear.”

“Listen again after that?” Xiao Lu asked.

“Went a few times. Sounded more like him each time. Eventually I couldn't be sure what he'd sounded like during the real argument.”

“And you kept going?”

Old Chen seemed about to offer something useful.

“Wanted to.”

She neither nodded nor shook her head. Her fingers lay flat against the script instead of opening its written pages.

“You're losing all your water,” Wang Hai said.

“Half a cup left.”

“I meant your skirt.”

Only then did Old Chen touch the cold, wet patch. Nobody laughed.

Lin Mo's throat tightened. At the verification desk, he'd wanted to make happiness last longer too.

Old Chen gave him a brief smile.

“Don't look at me like that. Sound sensible telling the story, don't I? Put it in front of me, I still want to hear.”

The attendant told them to move. Xiao Lu gathered Lin Mo's coat hem clear, then stepped against the wall. Wang Hai steadied the carved end of the wood beneath his hand. Left foot first, right following slowly. They'd barely cleared half a person's width before the cart scraped past.

“Wait,” Old Chen said.

The attendant stopped impatiently.

“Could you finish that sentence? You said it was the wrong socket. I just want to know whether there's more.”

“I deliver recordings. Don't take requests.”

A rear wheel bumped over the raised floor strip. The box crackled. Old Chen followed one step and stopped.

Lin Mo raised his left hand to beckon him back. He couldn't find anything to say to go with the gesture. He held it there briefly, then lowered it.

“Sun Ning's waiting. Let's tell her what happened. If you want to return, we can talk about it.”

“Talk about it, or talk me out of it?”

Lin Mo hesitated.

“I can hear the unspoken half too,” Old Chen said.

“I'm afraid you'll follow it alone.”

“That I understand.”

The cart rounded the sign rack, revealing a broad doorway. Its overhead lamp was dark. Heavy curtains hung above wheeled scenery frames. A man in a black apron crouched gathering cables.

The attendant called for him to hold everything while the cart passed. The man acknowledged him and laid a plug beside the frame.

Old Chen set his cup by the wall and straightened his bag.

“Dad.”

A small voice behind the curtain. Not obviously addressing anyone outside. A child's word squeezed through held-back tears, followed by fabric being pulled.

Old Chen's hand missed the lid.

He opened his mouth, but only a short breath emerged. Bracing against the wall, he stared beyond the attendant's shoulder.

“Recorded?” Wang Hai asked.

Nobody could answer. The attendant lifted the cart's cover, apparently oblivious.

“Old Chen,” Lin Mo called.

“I heard.”

“I'm calling you.”

Old Chen looked back. He seemed so clearheaded that Lin Mo couldn't warn him as though he didn't know it might be a trick.

“I know. I'm going to look.”

He pulled the bag's long strap over his shoulder, leaving the cup. Lin Mo seized on a ridiculous hope: someone who'd left water behind would have to return. Yet he too had walked past a perfectly good way back and refused to turn.

“They're moving scenery,” Xiao Lu said.

Old Chen nodded and kept walking. No running, just steps that carried him beyond the cart.

Lin Mo moved after him. Pain caught his hip; the wood tilted beneath his palm. Wang Hai steadied it and lurched toward the wall.

Xiao Lu stepped between Lin Mo and the cart without grabbing his wrist.

“Follow him now and you'll bring Wang Hai down.”

Wang Hai didn't claim he could manage. Lin Mo stopped.

“Do you want to go?” he asked her.

“I'll make sure they see someone in there. Don't crowd in behind me.”

She placed script and bag by the wall, clear of Old Chen's cup, instead of loading Lin Mo's trembling hands.

The child's voice sounded again, farther away. Old Chen lifted the outer curtain. His cloth shoes disappeared into the narrow gap beside the scenery.

Xiao Lu stopped by the cart's front and called to the apron man. Someone inside.

“Which side?”

She pointed left.

A green lamp lit within the doorway. The wheels began moving. The man shouted that the other end hadn't stopped; the rolling noise drowned him out.

An entire false wall slid sideways. The stage lighting was off, its painted windows as gray as old glass. One of Old Chen's shoes reappeared, its toe against the advancing bottom rail.

“Stop!” Xiao Lu shouted.

The man lunged for a red handle and threw it down. The cart attendant dragged his load back, boxes rattling beneath the cover.

The scenery stopped after traveling a little farther.

Old Chen gasped behind the curtain.

Through its raised lower corner, Lin Mo saw the bag snagged on a projecting bolt. Its strap pulled diagonally across Old Chen's shoulder, stretched between two frames. His body wasn't crushed, but the strap held him bent sideways against the false wall.

“Stay still,” the apron man said. “Why'd you go in there?”

Old Chen clutched his bag with whitening fingers.

“Get him out first,” Xiao Lu said.

“Need to move this back. Don't pull in there.”

The attendant cleared space with the cart. Crouching by a wheel, the other man cursed. A loose length of the bag strap had wound around the axle. Pushing backward only jammed the wheel harder; the strap remained taut.

Old Chen said something Lin Mo couldn't hear.

Xiao Lu squeezed between the stationary cart and the wall. Without crossing the transverse track, she raised the curtain to expose his face.

“What did you say?”

“My book.”

His bag had split. The notebook protruded, caught beneath the bolt. His old badge fell beside his shoe. He didn't look down.

“Get your shoulder out first. Leave the bag.”

“It'll fall.”

“I can see.”

His left arm trembled. His right palm still covered the notebook, guarding what remained against another loss.

Lin Mo leaned forward. Wang Hai told him sharply not to push, a tendon standing out at his wrist. Lin Mo took his weight back.

The man who'd taught him to leave gaps couldn't let go. Now all the pages he had left might become another gap.

“I can put my hand underneath,” Xiao Lu said. “Look where I'm putting it.”

She slid her right palm beneath the notebook, avoiding his wrist. Only a finger's width separated them. Between them, the metal was cutting through the bag, white threads breaking individually.

The apron man warned them not to push: the wheel was still under load. He went around to find somewhere to brace it. The curtain lifted with him, and Old Chen swayed.

The notebook slipped lower.

He snatched for it. The strap tightened against his throat, cutting his voice into a tiny breath. Xiao Lu withdrew her hand from the book and held back loose fabric before it could cover his mouth and nose.

She pointed out the threshold and the stationary doorframe behind him. Something to brace against besides the suspended strap.

“The book'll fall,” he managed.

Lin Mo returned the wood to Wang Hai. Keeping his left palm against the wall, he lowered himself to the floor. Knee pain briefly darkened his sight.

“Help her. I'm sitting.”

Wang Hai checked that his back was supported and both feet clear of the track. He propped the wood on Lin Mo's left and went, right hand free.

Lin Mo didn't reach for the book again. He could see Old Chen but couldn't reach him. This time, he didn't try to fill the distance with a body that couldn't stand.

Xiao Lu indicated the strap.

“Lift here. Let him tuck his head down.”

Wang Hai raised it from behind without touching his throat. Old Chen could breathe. He looked at Xiao Lu's freed hand, then at Lin Mo.

Behind the curtain, the child was still calling Dad.

This time Old Chen didn't turn.

“Leave the book for now.”

His right hand slowly lifted from its cover.
