# Chapter 48: An Empty Bowl Can Burn

Xiao Lu stopped looking at the bowl.

“You told me they wouldn't let you put it down.”

The man's hands dipped, then rose again.

“Said it made people more likely to come.”

“That's different.”

“You came, didn't you?”

Lin Mo tightened his grip. The broken wood shifted against the tiles. Xiao Lu neither hid behind him nor asked whom the man imagined her to be.

“I thought you needed help lowering your hands.”

“I do. They really hurt now.”

The blue-aproned attendant sighed. Rest, he said. Stop making it look like torture. At last the man's elbows touched the table. The bowl made a faint sound against plastic. He watched Xiao Lu as though he'd lowered his last reason for her to stay.

She turned to leave.

“Wait. Didn't say you had to be anyone.”

“You're waiting for me to turn the bowl.”

“He said you would.”

He glanced at the attendant, who shook out a cloth without looking up.

“What's wrong with being helpful? Nobody tied you down.”

Old Chen's hand paused beside the mark on his neck. He didn't touch it. He moved aside to leave Xiao Lu a way out.

Lin Mo called her name. The guarded look she gave him tightened his throat.

“If you want to go back, I'll come.”

“And him?”

“He can put the bowl down.”

The man looked angry. He pushed the bowl away, catching it at the edge as though breaking the prop would leave him with nothing.

“I'm not pretending. My mouth's really cut.”

Lin Mo saw the blood on the rim and the split lower lip. A real injury, a story with half left out. Both in front of him, making him want one simpler reason to stay or leave.

Old Chen asked what the man wanted now.

“Someone to sit awhile.”

“At this table?”

No answer.

The attendant tapped the panel. Neither seat had been selected.

“Sit if you're talking. Block the entrance and people think this table's taken.”

Lin Mo asked whether they could leave before starting. Anyone who hadn't confirmed could leave, the attendant said, pointing at the bowl.

“That stays. And don't wait for me to serve you.”

The man's anger vanished.

“You said food when the seats were filled.”

“Not a free canteen. You asked when you came.”

“Are you hungry?” Xiao Lu asked.

He nodded. His hands finally lay in his lap, knuckles curled.

Lin Mo felt suddenly furious, and not only at the attendant. He'd been looking at yellow glaze and wondering about resemblances. This man wanted them to fill two chairs so he could eat a meal that hadn't arrived.

Old Chen nodded toward the corridor.

“They still give out flatbread past the water station?”

“Yes.”

“Been there?”

“Had mine. Ate it. They said hot food here.”

Old Chen swallowed whatever he'd meant to say. He too had walked somewhere he couldn't breathe for a voice that made him feel better.

“Come out for some water?” Xiao Lu asked. “My cup. I'm not sitting at that table.”

After a long time, the man asked whether he could return afterward.

“You'll have to ask him. I don't know.”

The attendant wouldn't hold the place. The man rubbed his knees, stared at the empty chair, then stood. His sweater caught on a nail. He freed it himself.

Lin Mo made room at the door. The wood bumped the bag against the wall. Old Chen steadied its mouth; the cup clinked inside, but the notebook stayed put.

“I'm in the way,” Lin Mo said.

“Take your time.”

The man waited until he was steady before crossing the threshold. Behind him, the attendant replaced the yellow bowl with a white one. The chip was gone. The flowers on the tablecloth hadn't changed.

Xiao Lu followed it with her eyes, then took out her own cup.

The man wiped his mouth with his sleeve and asked to use the lid. There was blood on his lip, and he didn't want to get it where she drank. She filled the lid halfway and waited until he held it securely.

“Jiang Jian,” he said. “Not the surname on the sign in there.”

She gave her name. Lin Mo gave his too, then repeated it when Jiang Jian couldn't hear. Jiang Jian repeated the name as though afraid of getting it wrong. This time Lin Mo knew he was being addressed and nodded.

There were no empty stools outside. The four moved slowly toward the water station. Xiao Lu put away her cup, then picked up Old Chen's bag with his permission, freeing his hand to help Lin Mo. Jiang Jian followed without touching her shoulder.

At the waiting-sign rack, Old Chen coughed and stopped against the wall. Xiao Lu waited. Lin Mo asked whether his neck had hurt at the door too.

“Talk too much and it hurts.”

“Why keep asking questions?”

“He said putting it down meant nobody looked. Wanted to speak for him.”

Lin Mo waited.

“Tell you he's having a hard time. Ask you not to leave so quickly. Then thought I'd better ask what he wanted. Answer for him, he never has to admit he wants company.”

“You hungry too?” Xiao Lu asked.

“Yes. Not enough to need you sitting at that table.”

She produced half a wrapped flatbread, collected earlier at the waiting area. Old Chen asked whether she was keeping it for herself. She broke off a corner, put away the rest, and offered that.

“This much I can spare. Ask for more and I won't have any.”

He took a small bite without praising her as somebody's good child. Lin Mo didn't claim he wasn't hungry either. When she asked, he said he wanted to collect his own share before returning. Jiang Jian pointed out the hatch beside the water station, visible from here. He could collect it without agreeing to take a seat anywhere.

Only after sitting did Lin Mo realize his back was soaked. He propped the wood on his left, right wrist resting on his leg. Xiao Lu returned the bag and sat at the far end, leaving space for Jiang Jian.

He studied the gap.

“Waiting for someone?”

“No. Sit if you want. Give me the lid, though. I need to put it away.”

He lowered the lid onto his left palm and tried screwing it down before realizing he wasn't holding a cup. His ears reddened as he returned it. Without Wang Hai there, Lin Mo almost heard what he would have said. His mouth twitched.

Jiang Jian saw it, smiled briefly, and sat. Xiao Lu took out her cup, screwed the lid on, and left it in her lap.

Xiao Lu still wanted to know why he'd expected her to turn the bowl.

He pointed toward the side door.

“He showed me someone carrying a script and told me to lift the bowl when you came. He said you'd reached out once already, so you'd want to finish what you'd started.”

“Showed you what?”

“You were fixing the cloth on someone's cup. I saw it in the little window back there.”

Old Chen tightened his hold on the bag. Lin Mo had stood beside them while she moved the wet wrapping. Nobody had announced a session or arranged themselves for a camera.

“Who else was in it?”

“Short hair. Cloth around the cup's rim. Didn't show the face long. Then they switched to the bowl.”

Seeing Old Chen's neck, Jiang Jian seemed to realize whom he'd sat beside.

“You?”

Old Chen asked where the window was. Turn behind the side door, Jiang Jian said. The long table in the old rehearsal room now stood across half the doorway.

Lin Mo looked at Old Chen, who stayed seated, lips pressed tight.

“Where I gave them the book was called that. Had a long table. Last time I went, it had moved.”

A matching name couldn't prove they'd found it. Lin Mo remembered the mending box, half its label hidden. He asked where the images came from.

Jiang Jian didn't know. While waiting for a place, he'd been called over to watch briefly. He hadn't followed a cart or seen anyone recording.

Xiao Lu settled her cup in her bag, thumb against its lid.

“What else did he tell you to say?”

“Hands hurt. Don't say the bowl's empty.”

“You told me it was hot.”

He lowered his head.

“Yes.”

She didn't find a kinder explanation for the lie. Nor did he repeat that he'd been hungry. His hands opened on his knees, no longer holding anything up for people to see.

Lin Mo watched her hands. She'd carried Old Chen's bag. Now someone claimed she always had to steady a bowl. Two choices she'd made could be attached to a relationship someone else waited for her to perform.

“I didn't know they were filming,” he said.

“Neither did I. I don't regret helping him drink.”

Old Chen shifted his bag nearer his feet.

“Keeping you from my cup wouldn't help.”

“I didn't suggest that.”

“I know.”

Lin Mo didn't suggest she hide her hands. With a possible camera outside, she'd first have to learn not to help, not to pick things up. He thought of the original paper in his inner pocket. He'd once thought hiding its back meant showing its front didn't matter.

Jiang Jian stood.

“Getting water. Don't save the seat.”

He didn't ask to return. Xiao Lu waited until he began filling his own old bottle, then laid the script on her knees.

“Something I want to ask you. Nothing to do with whether the bowl came from home.”

“All right.”

“Did I always have to look after you?”

His first impulse was no. His sister had been hospitalized. He'd borrowed money, searched for her. But Xiao Lu wasn't asking who had done more, or using Jiang Jian to accuse him.

“I sometimes told her only the good news. She noticed. Had to ask me what was wrong.”

“Did you listen?”

“Sometimes. Sometimes told her she was sick and should worry about herself.”

She studied him without claiming that other woman as herself.

“Sounds tiring.”

“Yes.”

He almost listed the things he'd done for her, then stopped. Xiao Lu took out her pen. After writing for a while, she looked up.

“I'm recording what happened at that table today. Your story, I'll listen to for now.”

“I nearly asked you to turn it again,” Lin Mo said.

She looked up.

“Wanted to see if you'd do it the same way. Didn't say it.”

“Don't use somebody's sore hands to test me next time either.”

He agreed. The words had a particular man attached now, his seat still empty. Lin Mo couldn't turn them into some vague promise.

Old Chen loosened the severed strap around his bag and checked the book above the cup. Without unfolding the torn pages, he settled it securely.

“I used to go to the long table and ask only when I'd get my book back. Eventually I hardly looked at the person behind it. I'd hear the child and follow again.”

“What would you ask now?”

“Who used what I gave them, and where. Who delivered what they showed him today.”

“Think it's the same table?”

“Have to look. Not chase a voice tonight.”

Footsteps approached from the waiting area. Jiang Jian returned with his filled bottle and stopped before Old Chen. He didn't sit. The words seemed to have been waiting in his throat.

“Didn't explain about the man behind that window.”

“What?”

“When he saw you in the picture, he said, Old Chen still has the back half, then.”

Old Chen's hand stayed pressed over his bag.

“He knew you before today,” Jiang Jian said quietly.
