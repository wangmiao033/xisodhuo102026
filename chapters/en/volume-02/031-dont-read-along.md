# Chapter 31: Don’t Read Along

The cable dragged past Lin Mo’s sock. The shutter rose six inches.

He waited for the yellow tape under the trolley to pass inside, then edged along the door. Someone with a long hook caught a corner of the trolley and pulled it toward a narrow workbench.

At the corner, Wang Hai raised his right palm.

Lin Mo stopped. The hand pressed downward. Wang Hai was looking behind him. Only then did he notice the descending shutter brushing his hair.

“Stay down.”

He bent, papers pressing against his chest inside the gray jacket. His left shoulder struck the frame. Trying to pull it back, he tipped sideways and braced his left hand against the wall, inching his socked feet over the threshold.

Wang Hai stayed outside, wedging the chair leg beneath the shutter. He pointed back along their route, then at the doorway. Lin Mo nodded. The carved name faced the gap.

“Past that bench, I can’t see you,” Wang Hai said. “I’m not chasing you through another door.”

“Just to the bench.”

“And if they tell you to go farther?”

“I come back.”

Wang Hai set the wood against the floor, pinned it with his shoe, and pointed.

“Use your hands. I told you to duck and you nearly went forward. I need to understand where you’re going.”

Lin Mo mimed turning, then pointed outside. Wang Hai nodded him on.

The man behind the bench put down his hook. A faded apron, a flat key tied at his waist, a patch of tape on his chin. His Adam’s apple moved. No words came.

Lin Mo lowered his empty hands.

“I want to see what just came back—”

The man raised a finger to his lips. Lin Mo stopped, but the white box beside the bench flashed.

A small screen lit up: the waiting entrance. Xiao Lu sat on the second stool, opening her script, closing it, looking toward the door. The speaker beside her played Lin Mo’s unfinished sentence, so close it might have been spoken into her ear.

She dragged her stool half an inch farther inside.

Lin Mo opened his mouth to warn her. The man covered the white box and shook his head. Wang Hai tapped the shutter, pointing to his own mouth.

Lin Mo closed his.

The man unplugged one cable. The speaker stopped. Xiao Lu hadn’t followed the voice.

He clipped the loose plug into a ring beside the bench, lifted his hand from the box, and slowly exhaled.

Lin Mo waited, then pointed at the screen and himself.

The man indicated the box and a hole beneath the bench. Lifting a thin wooden panel, he showed two cables: one to the screen, one disappearing into the wall, spliced in several places.

Lin Mo leaned sideways to see past his knee. The man immediately lowered the panel and pointed outside.

Wang Hai was still there.

Lin Mo indicated himself, then the bench. Only looking. Taking nothing. His left hand drifted toward his inside pocket, as though he needed a patient’s form to show it was his turn.

Paper brushed his fingers. He withdrew his hand.

The man’s mouth tightened. Instead of reaching for the paper, he pushed the trolley toward him. A wheel caught at the threshold.

Thinking he was being sent away, Lin Mo backed up. Wang Hai tapped the wood. The man shook his head and lifted the yellow-marked cable.

Its plug was caught beneath the frame.

Lin Mo crouched, knees turning inward, left shoulder against the trolley for balance. The man pinned a wheel with his shoe and lifted the frame, but couldn’t reach behind the plug.

Lin Mo stopped his left hand beside it and looked up.

The man nodded.

Without pulling the cable, he smoothed down a loose corner of yellow tape and worked the plug backward through the gap. His right wrist struck a metal brace. Pain jabbed beneath the white scar. He drew that hand away and kept pushing with his left fingers.

The plug dropped into the man’s palm.

As Lin Mo stood, the man briefly supported his left elbow, then pointed from his shoulder to a low stool.

He didn’t sit.

Wang Hai raised the shutter enough to show half his face.

“Sit. You can’t see under the bench standing up anyway.”

The white box stayed dark. No sound reached the waiting entrance. The man moved the ring holding the plug farther inward without reconnecting it.

Wang Hai pointed to his eyes and kept watch.

Lin Mo sat, skirt hanging beside the trolley, toes toward the exit. The man pushed the trolley two steps inward, still in view, and lifted its cover.

It wasn’t empty.

Black boxes lined one side, cables coiled, casings marked with colored strips. Xiao Lu’s channel number lay uppermost. Lin Mo leaned closer to read beneath a folded corner without touching it.

The man removed that box and undid two loose screws. Inside were three narrow slots. One held a sliver. Two were empty.

Lin Mo turned toward Wang Hai, shaped a rectangle with his right hand, and pointed at his pocket.

Wang Hai merely shifted the chair leg.

Lin Mo realized he was indicating where his original payment slip belonged. He patted the outside of his jacket and made a larger rectangle.

“I don’t understand,” Wang Hai said. “You want my box?”

He nodded.

“Show it, don’t give it to him?”

Another nod. Wang Hai set the broken casing outside the shutter, yellow strip facing in. As the wood moved, the shutter slipped. He hastily propped it again, knocking the casing over.

Lin Mo stayed put. The man crouched and turned it right side up through the gap, studying the crack. Then he looked at Lin Mo.

Lin Mo indicated the sliver in the intact box.

“Collected just now?”

The man nodded, keeping his hand on the casing so Lin Mo couldn’t take it.

“Are you looking,” Wang Hai asked, “or trying to haul it out again?”

Lin Mo’s left hand had reached the bench. He caught his own cuff and pulled it back.

“Watching where he puts it.”

“And where it goes afterward?”

“Haven’t seen.”

“Then don’t let your hands get ahead of your eyes.”

Lin Mo placed both hands on his knees. Wang Hai pocketed his casing without offering to trade.

“You want that bit where she called you?”

Lin Mo stared at the sliver.

“Yes.”

“Bring it back, and she remembers?”

“I don’t know.”

Wang Hai waited, right hand bracing the wood. Lin Mo could see the pale factory-badge mark on his chest.

“I wanted to go back to her.”

“You know where she is.”

“I’m afraid next time she won’t even remember today.”

Wang Hai nodded toward the screen.

“She moved her stool. Someone spoke in her ear and she didn’t like it. You saw that from here.”

Lin Mo looked back at the sliver, without addressing the white box.

“I’ll watch which one he installs. Anything I need to ask her, I’ll ask her in person.”

The man removed the sliver and laid it on a soft pad with three grooves. A white point marked its edge, like the flash Lin Mo had seen when Xiao Lu’s voice was collected.

He brought out another. Held together under the light, they were equally wide, but their holes were offset by half a space.

He separated them without inserting either into the original slot, clipping them to opposite sides of the pad. The waiting entrance shrank into a corner of the screen. Stage footage appeared beside the bench.

Xiao Lu stood with her hand above the end switch. Then she looked at the camera and began to smile.

Lin Mo gripped the stool, knuckles white.

The man stopped the footage and waited.

“Play the bit before that,” Lin Mo said.

The man shook his head. He turned a small lamp toward the second sliver, revealing a narrow label beneath it.

[LIVE AUDIO CAPTURE]

The first bore only a proposal number and the word EXECUTION. No name for his sister.

He pressed that sliver into place beneath the image. A light beside the yellow cable came on. Xiao Lu froze with her hand raised. The second sliver remained uninstalled.

Lin Mo rose, heel touching a wheel.

“This goes in first, then she says what follows?”

The man withdrew his hands, showing the empty groove between the slivers. He didn’t nod.

Lin Mo touched the lampshade. The image jumped forward half a frame. Xiao Lu turned toward the camera. His knees bent, trying to carry him back to the mark opposite her.

His left shoulder pressed into air. There was no microphone to hold.

Wang Hai struck the doorframe hard with the wood.

“There’s a trolley at your feet!”

Lin Mo stepped clear of the wheel and retreated. The man removed the first sliver. Half the image dimmed, but the urge to move forward remained. Holding his jacket hem, he slowly sat.

He’d been looking for Xiao Lu’s eyes, not watching his feet.

The man shut the first sliver in a narrow lidded case. The live recording stayed on the pad, beyond Lin Mo’s reach.

Lin Mo indicated the lampshade and opened his hand.

“I wasn’t asking you to play it again.”

The man moved his chin. A fingernail brushed the tape, exposing an old red edge beneath. He lowered his hand.

Lin Mo didn’t ask his name.

“She said that herself,” he told Wang Hai.

“Said what?”

“To let go. Would that piece record it?”

Wang Hai watched the bench a while.

“I don’t know. Are you asking about her words or that light?”

Lin Mo released the stool and pressed his palms against his knees.

“I was holding her. What if this place writes that moment up as her wanting it?”

Wang Hai rested his right shoulder against the doorframe.

“She didn’t want it. You let go. You can say that when you go back. You don’t need it to pick you a nice-sounding bit.”

“I wasn’t picking.”

“Then don’t start.”

Lin Mo didn’t argue. He held out an empty hand toward the closed case. The man left it shut and lifted the pad, settling the live recording flat. Lin Mo watched him keep the two apart.

Wheels sounded outside. Wang Hai secured his broken casing and moved beside the shutter, clearing the passage. A delivery trolley stopped at the corner, its black box still covered.

“New adaptation. For the front,” the delivery worker said. “Don’t mix in the live piece.”

The man shut the narrow case and pushed it back. Lin Mo turned sideways, right wrist against his jacket, offering no help. The strip on the trolley bore his number, not Xiao Lu’s.

The worker saw him and his blue collar.

“What are you doing here? They’ve called twice.”

Lin Mo’s feet turned toward the door, then stopped.

“Called for what?”

“Next performance.”

“I didn’t accept.”

The worker pressed the cover down, fingers over the number.

“Materials are here. You have to go.”

Holding the stool, Lin Mo suddenly pictured a fitting-room seat: waiting to be called, clothes on, name given. Next came standing up and walking. The cloth tie tightened around his waist. He looked up. The stool was still beside the bench.

Wang Hai extended the chair leg through the gap, carving toward him.

“Want to come out? Hold this. Don’t follow the trolley.”

He gripped it. Wang Hai didn’t pull, only braced the broken end against the wall. Lin Mo steadied himself and moved clear of the wheel.

“His,” the worker told the man. “Check it and send it out.”

The man didn’t open the cover. He lifted the loose white-cable plug from its ring and held it beside the trolley lid. Yellow tape surrounded the socket. The plug didn’t fit.

He pointed at the tape.

A strip of white paper protruded beneath it. Lin Mo could see one character: 茉.

He didn’t peel it back.

“Show my whole name.”

The worker covered the paper. The man brought the lamp closer, illuminating its edge.

[林茉]

Lin Mo. The same sound, but written with 茉, jasmine, instead of 默, silence: the character in his own name.

Outside, Wang Hai called “Lin Mo,” meaning the name he’d always had. Lin Mo answered without releasing the wood.

The worker hugged the box to his chest.

“They’ll change it at the front.”

“Who wrote it first?”

No answer. The trolley turned toward the door.

The man pinned a wheel beneath his shoe and raised his empty hand, gesturing for the cover to be opened.

Lin Mo watched him. The man didn’t look back. His palm stayed open before the box, waiting.
