# Chapter 47: The Man Who Stayed Behind

Lin Mo moved the pen before it rolled off his knee.

“Who did you give it to?”

“The man writing scripts. Long table by the window in the old rehearsal room.”

“So you could hear the child answer?”

Old Chen nodded, palm over the old gap.

He'd heard the schoolbag answer once and wanted others. The writer said he needed to know what had happened at home before he could supply what came next. Old Chen handed over the front half: wrong shoe sizes, a window screen he kept putting off repairing, a night the boy cried until morning, when Old Chen slammed the door and left.

“He asked what he couldn't use. I said he could use all of it.”

Sun Ning's cup touched the chair.

“Did your son agree?”

“Couldn't ask him.”

“So you agreed.”

“Yes.”

He didn't explain how desperate he'd been. He tucked an oily scrap into the cover.

Afterward, the child in the performances stopped complaining about the bag. Never mentioned the door. The voice always ended by saying, Dad, I know you meant well. The first time, Old Chen was still sitting there after the lights went out.

“Wasn't what I'd written.”

“Why didn't you ask?”

“Liked hearing it.”

Wang Hai rubbed his right wrist. Lin Mo thought of the word he'd stopped the recording on. Keep that one word and he needn't hear the pain afterward. All Old Chen's extra years hadn't carried him far from that.

“Did you try getting it back?”

“Said they'd separated the pages. Had to gather them. Then the table moved. Someone else behind it. Went a few times. Never found it.”

“Remember why you slammed the door?”

“The boy broke the screen. Fixed it twice; he pushed it out again. Said it was stuffy. Told him I'd deal with him when I got home.”

“And then?”

Old Chen studied the damaged cover.

“Did I buy new mesh? I can picture two versions. One I remembered, one they performed. Think too long and they crowd together. But I slammed the door. When I wrote it, I still knew why I was angry.”

The man who'd refused to stay outside the curtain couldn't be sure he'd fixed a window. The pages hadn't been blank when he'd surrendered them. He'd recorded his own failures.

“They promised to return your child?”

“Promised the answer. I made it bigger. Thought sounding right meant getting a little of him back.”

“Giving them paper let them erase your memory?”

“Nobody said that. Didn't go blank when I handed it over. All the sessions afterward, all that listening, I can't separate what disappeared when. Giving them the book doesn't explain everything.”

Lin Mo loosened his thumb against his own booklet. He'd wanted a simple boundary: keep the paper and this couldn't happen.

“You only said they took it.”

Old Chen's face shifted.

“Would you have listened to my advice if I'd told you?”

Lin Mo had imagined the notebook as something that had survived fifteen years, every missing page torn away by somebody else. Picturing Old Chen selecting pages to hand over made him unexpectedly angry.

“You told me not to use her words as proof for myself.”

“I did the opposite.”

“You didn't say so.”

“Afraid you'd think less of me.”

He looked toward the doorframe, then back, without sheltering behind his new injury.

“I don't want your advice right now,” Lin Mo said.

“Then I won't give it.”

Wang Hai shifted. The wood rolled on his knee; he stopped it with his palm. Somewhere a person coughed, a stool scraped. Admitting a half-truth hadn't taken fifteen years away from Old Chen. Lin Mo needn't immediately decide which parts to trust.

“People you knew were in those pages?” Sun Ning asked.

“Yes.”

“Don't put what I said today in your book.”

He agreed. Lin Mo closed his own booklet. The new page held only what Old Chen had asked him to write about today. He didn't ask Sun Ning to check it.

Eventually she wanted to close her eyes. Lin Mo reached for the water bottle on the floor. Shoulder pain stopped him. Wang Hai straightened, then winced as he lifted his right wrist.

“Bottle's not going anywhere,” Sun Ning said. “Leave it. I'm not thirsty.”

Lin Mo sat back. His coat bunched beneath his waist; the cloth belt pressed against his changed hip. Shifting twice only hurt more.

Old Chen opened his mouth and closed it.

Lin Mo knew what he was waiting for. He stubbornly waited until his leg began to go numb.

“You can help with this.”

“Don't lift yourself yet. Pull the coat hem out.”

He freed one corner. Old Chen suggested bringing his feet closer, pausing, then shifting forward. Wang Hai offered the wood. Lin Mo shook his head. He was only trying to move his weight onto his feet, not stand.

Once he'd done this without thinking. Now the extra weight on his chest and the changed angle of his hips and legs threw off the timing. On the second attempt, he braced his back the old way. His knees trembled.

“Stop there. I used to try doing it all at once too.”

“Eventually got easier?”

“Learned to position the chair. Still forgot the bag strap when I went behind that curtain.”

Wang Hai angled his stool to clear space.

“I moved this one. Don't blame them for moving the furniture.”

Old Chen laughed, then stopped when it pulled at his neck.

On the third attempt, Lin Mo lifted his back a little and settled again. Still sitting, but no fabric digging into him. He wanted to call it progress, say he was adapting.

“My feet didn't slip.”

“Saw that,” Wang Hai said.

Xiao Lu appeared at the door, checking the resting Sun Ning before speaking. She still had her script and bag, but her right hand was empty, its fingertips pressed together until they blanched.

“There's someone outside with a yellow bowl.”

Lin Mo stiffened. She shook her head immediately.

“Don't know whether it's the one I saw. He's inside the side door by the waiting area. A real chip in it. Saw him on my way back. Didn't touch it.”

“Someone invited you in?”

“Said his hands weren't steady. Needed someone to hold the bowl.”

Wang Hai asked whether the session had started. The door said preparation. A panel on the table let newcomers choose a seat. She hadn't chosen or sat down.

“He said he'd held it a long time. I told him to put it down. He said never mind.”

Lin Mo nearly asked what the man looked like. She hadn't called him familiar.

“You want me to come?”

“To stay outside with me. I want to ask whether he really has to hold it. And whether I was so busy recognizing the bowl that I missed something else he said.”

Old Chen gathered his loose pages.

“I know that door. Used to have tables waiting for enough people to start.”

“And now?”

“We'll look from the doorway.”

Lin Mo reached for the wood. Wang Hai held it across himself.

“I need a rest. I'm not going.”

Lin Mo's hand stopped. He knew Wang Hai was tired; he'd told him to rest himself. Hearing it now still felt like losing a door behind him.

“I'll sit with Sun Ning. Take the wood. Doesn't have to be me holding it.”

“Could we wait?”

“Until my hand feels better? Or until I give in and come?”

“Both. Afraid I can't make it.”

“Then say you're afraid. Asked whether they can help?”

Xiao Lu said they could go slowly by the wall. There was a wall by the doorway too, and they could come back if he couldn't manage. Old Chen could steady his left elbow with his right hand, but couldn't take his whole weight.

None of it reassured him as much as a simple let's go from Wang Hai. Lin Mo looked from the bandaged left hand to the aching right wrist. He'd been waiting for the less injured hand to agree for everyone.

“You stay here.”

“Was going to. Bring the wood back. Not finished with it.”

Lin Mo laughed briefly. Wang Hai passed him the carved end without getting up.

“Heard where you're going,” Sun Ning said, eyes closed. “Names when you return. Don't pick up a session ticket for me.”

“Only the side door.”

Old Chen put the closed notebook above the cup in his bag and secured the severed strap around it. He stood holding the bag, the red mark still vivid on his neck.

Xiao Lu asked whether he could walk.

“Walk, yes. No more squeezing into gaps.”

Standing was harder than practicing in the chair. Lin Mo planted the wood outside his left foot, right hand lightly against the seat. Old Chen waited beside his elbow until asked before helping from the left. Xiao Lu cleared the doorway stool.

They waited again once he was upright. Wang Hai didn't hurry them or hide his injured hand.

Outside, Lin Mo looked back. Wang Hai drew his feet closer to the frame, where he could see the corridor while Sun Ning slept. He didn't wave them off.

Before missions, Lin Mo had hated people repeating instructions at parting. Now, leaving someone seated behind him, he'd almost done the same.

“Water's on your right.”

“I see it. Watch your feet.”

Old Chen stayed just behind his left shoulder. Lin Mo paused at floor seams. The short chair leg couldn't bear weight like a properly fitted cane. Xiao Lu passed the corner, then returned within sight.

“You said no more old sessions today.”

“Someone's holding a bowl now. They ask me to watch the past, I won't.”

He nodded, then admitted he'd nearly asked whom the man resembled.

“I know.”

“How?”

“You kept watching my face. Didn't ask whether the bowl was hot.”

Embarrassed, he asked.

“Empty. That's why I don't understand him holding it.”

Preparation glowed above the side door. No long mirror forced them closer. Through the wide opening, Lin Mo saw half a dining room: plastic tablecloth, faded landscape on the wall.

A man of perhaps forty wore a brown sweater. Elbows off the table, he held an old yellow bowl in trembling fingers. Its chipped edge faced his mouth. The base had worn to a gray-white ring.

Lin Mo didn't know him.

Xiao Lu returned to her previous spot without touching the panel.

“I brought someone. What else did you say before?”

The man's eyes went first to her empty hand.

“Asked if you could turn it for me.”

Her thumb curled abruptly.

Behind the table, a person in a blue apron urged her closer. The chip would show nicely on camera. Turn it, sit down, have a meal. Family looked after each other.

She stepped backward against the side of Lin Mo's wood. He steadied it without blocking the door.

“I'm not choosing family.”

“Then we'll wait for the next person.”

The man watched her withdraw. Beneath the chip was a speck of dried blood. His lips moved. Lin Mo realized there was more to his raised hands than keeping the tablecloth clean.

Old Chen set his bag against the wall outside.

“You haven't selected start?”

The man shook his head.

“Why not rest your hands?”

He glanced at the attendant, then the empty chair. Something more embarrassed than frightened crossed his face.

“If I put it down,” he said, “she stops looking this way.”
