# Chapter 49: The Man Behind the Table

“He said that himself?” Old Chen asked.

Jiang Jian nodded. Water sloshed in his bottle. The man was missing a small piece of his left ear and kept holding a pencil beneath his nose.

Old Chen touched his upper lip, then lowered his hand.

“Used to do that. Never noticed his ear.”

“I can take you.”

“Not now.”

Jiang Jian glanced toward the side door. Asked whether he wanted to return to the dining table, he said no, then admitted he wasn't sure. Perhaps the hot food had arrived.

“Not trying to talk you out of it,” Old Chen said. “If you pass the window, tell him I'll be here after eating. He can come to me. Don't promise him my book.”

“What if he tells me to hold the bowl again?”

Old Chen lowered his pointing hand.

“Then don't go. Shouldn't have assumed it was on your way.”

Jiang Jian said he'd left a jacket by the table. He'd ask for it from outside. He could deliver a message, but wouldn't wait at the window for an answer. Old Chen agreed without adding anything.

“Aren't you afraid he won't come?”

“Yes.”

Old Chen hugged his bag and waited for Lin Mo to stand. Nobody asked why being afraid hadn't made him get up first.

The bread hatch was just beyond the trough. Lin Mo queued behind two people and said he hadn't collected his portion. A flatbread slid across, with no question about which table he'd take. His right wrist couldn't turn beneath the wrapping paper. Xiao Lu held down a lifting corner until he'd steadied it.

Jiang Jian stayed behind, struggling to screw his bottle cap on straight.

At the rest area, Lin Mo announced himself, Xiao Lu, and Old Chen. Wang Hai took back the wood and checked its broken end.

“Made it back in better shape than you.”

“Didn't walk here itself.”

“Knows when to accept advice.”

Lin Mo waited until he was seated to laugh. His shoulder still hurt. He told Sun Ning the man had left the table and they hadn't sat down. He described the footage and the window without turning Jiang Jian's claim into a verified meeting.

“Going out again after eating?” she asked.

“Meeting him by the water. We won't follow him inside.”

“Last time you were only looking from the doorway.”

Lin Mo paused with the bread in his hand.

“I want to ask who delivered her footage. If we can't find out, we come back. No following carts.”

“Eat first. Holding bread doesn't count as resting.”

Old Chen rose to collect his portion. Wang Hai suggested leaving the bag unless he planned to grow a third hand. After hesitating, Old Chen set it by his feet and asked him to watch it.

“I'll watch. Won't reimburse fifteen years if it disappears.”

“Just the bag.”

“That I can afford.”

Old Chen laughed himself into a cough and went slowly. For once, Lin Mo didn't imagine the bag held all of one person’s memories. There was a leaking cup inside, and things another person could watch for a moment.

Xiao Lu sat against the wall and took out her remaining bread. They ate their own portions without pretending not to be hungry. Lin Mo took too large a bite. His dry throat wouldn't let it down.

Her hand moved toward his chin, stopping short. Instead she reached for the bottle on Sun Ning's left and asked permission. Sun Ning nodded. Lin Mo drank a little and swallowed carefully.

Xiao Lu was watching where his Adam's apple used to be.

“What were you going to do?”

“Make you spit it out.”

“Did I eat like that before?”

He heard where he'd taken the question. Her hand returned to her lap.

“I saw you couldn't swallow.”

“Yes. Ate too fast.”

After a while, she said something else had nearly come out: a sentence, there on her tongue.

He didn't ask for it. He capped the bottle, told Sun Ning he was returning it to her left, and broke his bread into smaller pieces. Xiao Lu watched him finish two before eating again.

Old Chen returned without asking what she'd remembered. He retrieved his bag and ate slowly. Even afterward, Lin Mo stayed against the chair until his legs stopped trembling. Then he borrowed the wood again.

“I'll bring it back.”

“Stop making it sound like your last request.” Wang Hai turned the carved end toward him. “I'll be here.”

Old Chen folded his bread paper into the bag. If he heard the child, could Lin Mo call his name?

“Called last time. You kept going.”

“Might not listen this time either.”

“I can call. Can't pull you back. Can't switch it off for you.”

“Call. Leave the rest to me.”

It didn't reassure Lin Mo completely. He told Old Chen not to wait for somebody else to expose another omission.

“Can't promise to tell it well,” Old Chen said. “Won't hide behind they took it this time.”

Xiao Lu asked whether he wanted her there. He wanted a straight answer about her footage, he said, and wouldn't decide anything else for her. She packed her things and agreed to the water station.

Jiang Jian was gone. A man in a brown waistcoat sat beyond the trough, a flat case on his knees, pencil behind an ear with a piece missing. He pushed out the neighboring stool.

“Sit down, Miss Chen.”

“Old Chen.”

“You used to correct me.”

“Still do.”

The man held the pencil beneath his nose. Old Chen ignored the offered stool and sat beside Lin Mo, their bag and wood on their side.

“It's you. Told me the table had moved. Couldn't find all the paper. Then you disappeared.”

“Moved again.”

“You were who I was looking for.”

The man glanced at the neck injury and damaged bag.

“Jiang Jian said you would see me. Xu. Call me Master Xu. Everyone does now.”

Old Chen recognized a particular person, and the person admitted receiving his pages. At last there was someone to question besides a table.

“The front half?”

“Separated. Some in old sessions. Some changed hands. Don't have all of it.”

“Said that last time.”

“Last time you asked if I'd gathered it.”

“Today I'm asking which of my words you changed.”

Xu opened his case. No paper inside, just a small box with a speaker: the end of the mending session. He'd have to find the rest.

Lin Mo moved the wood clear of Old Chen's feet and asked what switching it on would do. Xu showed them the playback switch. No seating panel. A portable playback sample, he said, no session running; they could decline. He couldn't alter their bodies here.

Lin Mo didn't take that as a promise about the rest of the Theater. Old Chen asked whether he could stop whenever he wanted. Xu set the box out of reach on the ledge and said he'd stop it.

“Play it.”

Xu explained that later versions had combined several domestic episodes. Same schoolbag on the table, different arguments.

“I know,” Old Chen said. “Listened before. Don't call a recorded performance the sound from my home.”

A male voice scolded a child about the window screen. Old Chen pressed his lower lip inward. A pause, then a child said, Dad, I know you meant well.

Old Chen didn't smile.

“My page said he was hot.”

“Needed something afterward.”

Old Chen described repairing the screen twice. Last time he'd nailed the edges so firmly the window barely opened. The boy put his fingers through the hole, showing him the breeze outside. Old Chen had seen only another piece of mesh to buy. Told him to repair it himself.

“Still remember all that?” Xu asked.

“Today. Tomorrow, I don't know.”

“Writing it down at all was more than most people do.”

“Don't close the door for me.” Old Chen's eyes stayed on him. “Hear that I'm doing well, I can forget about him being hot again.”

Xu's hand rested by the switch.

“So you made him stop minding?”

“It's dialogue.”

“I gave you an argument. You gave me a child admitting he was wrong.”

Xu stopped the sound without looking caught out. Old Chen had asked when he could hear another. Hadn't demanded the child's grievances then.

“No. That's why I had to come and say it myself.”

Xiao Lu looked at the box.

“Did you make me family because I fixed the cup?”

“Tried one movement with the bowl. You didn't sit. Went no further.”

“Who delivered it?”

Xu pointed his pencil toward the rear passage. Ready-made clips arrived with the changeover; he selected usable ones. He hadn't sent anyone to follow them. Asked which cart, he said he hadn't watched the entrance.

Part of his pencil tip broke. He picked away the loose wood and dropped it into the lid. Old Chen asked whether he still performed.

“Not much.”

“Finish writing other people's family lives and you don't have to?”

“Nothing that certain. Nobody takes the parts, I still get called up.”

“So you knew why Jiang Jian wouldn't lower the bowl.”

Xu said nothing. Xiao Lu said he'd put it down, walked out, and wanted his jacket back. This time Xu didn't call it just one movement.

“I'll ask the person delivering them,” Lin Mo said.

“They'll send you back to your own session records. That window doesn't handle where things were filmed. We get material for rehearsal.”

Lin Mo didn't follow that answer into guesses about what lay beyond. This man could at least answer for himself.

“You knew she hadn't agreed.”

“Waiting for her to come and agree.”

Xiao Lu tucked her hands into her sleeves, then brought them out onto the script.

“You waited for the wrong thing.”

Xu half closed the case. If they could finish the old sequence, he could request the uncut sound using its original session number. Perhaps the child's name was there. No guarantee. Better than chasing similar voices everywhere.

He had to return this box. This was today's chance to hear it outside a performance. He'd brought no originals to repay a debt. To continue the scene, they'd have to inspect its terms inside; a nod beside the water wouldn't enroll anyone.

“Then why come here?” Xiao Lu asked.

“If he'll listen, we have something to discuss.”

Old Chen stared at the catch. Lin Mo nearly suggested listening needn't mean agreeing. Beside him, Xiao Lu's hands rested on her own script. He waited for Old Chen to ask.

“In exchange for the back half?”

“Wouldn't need it today. Doesn't all have to come from the book.”

Xu drew the empty stool closer. He looked at Xiao Lu as though he'd finally found someone who could supply the rest.

“Put her in the scene. One Dad from her is worth ten pages from you.”
