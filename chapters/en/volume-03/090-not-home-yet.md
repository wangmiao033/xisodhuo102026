# Chapter 90: Not Home Yet

Lin Mo's hand rested against the wall.

A mortar seam passed beneath his fingers, through the place where Xiao Lu's shoulder had been. Blood began seeping from the back of his hand. He followed the seam down, finding only dust and a broken wire.

“Xiao Lu.”

Nobody answered for her.

He probed the wall's foot with the sound end of the wood, brushing away dust. No door appeared, no loose brick. The cold light had vanished. Wood striking wall sounded exactly as before.

He didn't know where she had gone.

Someone was speaking. He didn't make out the words until the cart shifted backward and his injured knee struck her bag.

“Wait.”

The front attendant stopped.

During that pause, an overhead seam shifted. The gray-costumed woman turned the cart toward the corridor's center. Dust swept across the place Xiao Lu had occupied. A larger fragment struck the cushion's empty corner.

Wang Hai withdrew to the opposite wall.

“This part's giving way.”

Lin Mo stared at the corner. He'd tucked his legs tightly aside to make room for her. They remained there, as though straightening them would occupy a place she still needed.

He retrieved the bag strap from beneath the wood and looped it around his right forearm. The cup knocked softly inside.

“Back to the front. We saw people get out there.”

People had gone beyond holding's other door too, but he hadn't seen where that passage led. At the front, he'd watched people cross the outer doorway without being dragged back.

The attendant cleared a route for the turn, nudging the fallen casing aside with his shoe. The woman steered. Wang Hai watched the inner rear wheel, calling the small adjustments until it cleared the sharp corner.

Nobody pulled Lin Mo away by his injured shoulder.

He gathered his feet inside the board and worked the cushion behind his waist. The white cloth still hung over his left forearm. He pressed it briefly to the scraped back of his hand, then folded the stained section inward.

The wheel crossed a tile seam.

He looked back.

The corner remained, gray with dust. Last to disappear was the short mark his wood had cleared at the wall's foot. Then the corridor hid it.

The shallow console's doorway had sagged. A beam trapped one chair leg. The console tilted outward, dust covering much of its closed audio display.

No voice came from it.

The empty space behind him didn't grow heavier.

The duty light went out as they passed. Beneath it, the backstage attendant who had relayed sound for Xiao Lu moved loose wiring aside. Seeing the cart, he steadied its other edge and asked whether the way ahead remained open.

The front attendant pointed toward the light through the side passage. People had gone that way. They would look.

One dead lamp couldn't pronounce the whole theater finished.

The door to the rest area was trembling too.

Sun Ning had stood with her left hand on the stool beside it. Footsteps passed near the water station. Someone called that the front wall had opened, telling people not to wait at their old marks.

Her right arm hurt whenever she moved it. Holding it against her body, she returned to the inner wall. Old Chen's damaged bag remained there, its broken strap wound securely around the opening.

She drew the bottom closer with her left hand, stopped when something hard inside caught, then changed the angle and gathered it against her waist.

The stool stayed behind.

She followed their earlier route to the water station. An overturned cup rack had spilled water across the tiles, joining scattered footprints. She stopped on the dry side, the bag in her left arm, keeping her injured right hand off the fallen rack.

A young man passing by saw her hesitate and supported the bag from underneath.

“Hold the bottom. Strap's broken.”

He did. She steadied herself against the wall and skirted the water. On dry floor they settled the bag so she could gather it back into her left arm. She nodded. Without opening it, he hurried after a companion ahead.

She continued.

The scrolling directory at the public selection entrance had stopped, folding several names into a fallen display. Part of the white boundary line was gone. No light pursued those who crossed it.

Sun Ning went around by the waiting side. Across the front area she saw a low cart, recognizing Wang Hai beside it by the way he held his right wrist.

“Over here!”

He stopped at the side-passage entrance. Instead of running toward falling debris, he pointed out the clear strip along the wall. The front attendant checked that nothing blocked her feet before returning to the cart.

She reached them with the bag in her left arm.

Lin Mo's face was harder to reconcile with her memory than before he went onstage. But his eyes were looking at her. A notebook's corner still showed beneath his clothes. Beside his legs lay the wood she'd seen him carry, shortened now.

“Lin Mo?”

“It's me.”

His old voice hadn't returned. Nor had the opened door restored the familiar angles forced out of his body.

Sun Ning crouched. Her gaze had already found the bag beside him.

“Where is she?”

He opened his mouth.

The gray-costumed woman kept hold of the cart without answering for him. Wang Hai waited, his injured wrist against his chest.

“She vanished at the corner. I couldn't hold her.”

Sun Ning looked down the passage. Only dust came out. She didn't ask why they hadn't gone back, or replace vanished with an ending she could bear.

She tightened her hold on the closed bag.

“I brought this.”

Lin Mo saw the broken strap around its mouth. No new words appeared. No hand emerged. Old Chen hadn't returned. Sun Ning had brought what he'd left behind.

“Watch the bottom,” Wang Hai reminded her.

She shifted her left arm beneath it.

Knocking came from the outer door. Its attendant was striking the frame with a broken board, calling them forward. The upper edge had dropped again. Several people supported one side while the last injured people they could see approached.

The former sign-holder emerged from a cracked passage, his sign gone. His companion still covered his ears. At the doorway, they released each other's clothes and squeezed through.

Lin Mo didn't know whether everyone left in holding had taken that route. He could see those two and Sun Ning beside the cart. That was all.

No orderly line waited at the door. Someone in a long red costume tried squeezing ahead, caught a hem on the broken frame and yanked twice. A person behind shouted to unhook it instead. Their argument stopped when both ducked a falling panel.

When they stood, the costume had torn. Its wearer gathered the loose edge without returning for another.

The woman waited for an opening, then turned the cart. Someone demanded its passenger walk a few steps. Wang Hai raised his injured wrist and told him to look. Seeing Lin Mo's torn stockinged toes, the stranger abandoned the sentence and kicked a fragment clear of the wheel.

Lin Mo didn't look up to identify him. He was adjusting the cup inside Xiao Lu's bag so its hard rim no longer pressed into his leg. Beyond the door was somewhere people could go. Reaching it still depended on these hurried, fallible people finding a hand to spare.

The cart turned toward the exit.

The black frame lay beside the main stage. Its mirrors could no longer assemble a whole giant hand. High fragments still reflected people bending and reaching, without joining those movements into one command.

He passed the gray transfer door. Inside, the narrow seat lay beneath wreckage. Debris covered the route he'd cleared inch by inch. He couldn't see the whole groove. The short wood fragment, fallen pins and mirror-nail shards stayed behind.

The longer piece lay beside him.

He touched the notebook through his clothes. Its pen remained clipped; old pages, private folds and unfinished blanks pressed against his ribs. He didn't open it on the crowded board.

When his body had reached one hundred and cognitive overwrite had stopped at fifty-four, he'd still remembered his name. There was no panel displaying those figures now. He still knew he was Lin Mo, knew whom Xiao Lu had been calling.

He hadn't heard what she'd tried to say at the end.

He couldn't supply it for her.

The front wheel stopped.

Half a step inside the outer doorway, a torn metal strip rose across the floor. A person could step over it. The low cart had struck it squarely.

The woman stopped pushing and cleared the people behind. The jolt drove Lin Mo's foot into the board's edge. He gasped.

“Don't hit it again.”

The two attendants crouched on either side and lifted. The front rose only a little. The slanted strip caught beneath the platform; lifting farther would slide him off the cushion.

He drew the bag against his abdomen and settled his back.

“Reverse first.”

They retreated half a wheel's width. He passed down the wood and indicated the gap beneath the raised metal. The attendant tried it. A splinter fell from the broken end.

It couldn't support the entire cart.

He laid it flat on the landing side instead, making a small step for the wheel. Both attendants lifted while the woman steered. The front wheel cleared the strip and settled onto the wood.

Its broken end cracked a little farther.

The wheel rolled down. They lifted the rear wheels across, setting the cart on level floor. The attendant retrieved the wood and returned it to Lin Mo's right, with two fresh black wheel marks across it.

The doorway was close now.

Sun Ning went first, guided toward its wider side. She held Old Chen's bag against her waist, protected her injured right arm and stepped across. The brightness beyond swallowed her shoes, then her hem. Lin Mo saw her turn once before he could no longer make her out.

Wang Hai followed. At the edge, he stopped to ask about the cart. The front attendant said it could be pushed through and told him to keep the way clear.

Wang Hai withdrew his half-raised left hand. Holding the door's edge, he crossed.

He called Lin Mo from the other side.

The voice was close. The theater could no longer locate it for him.

The woman looked once at the cart's handle, set both hands on it and pushed. No light beneath her feet urged another repetition. The backstage attendant guarded the rear wheel. The front attendant stepped aside to leave room.

Deep inside, something massive collapsed.

Wind drove dust toward the exit. Before Lin Mo could look back, the cart crossed the outer threshold. Cushion, wood and bag dipped together. He guarded the bag with his right forearm, his left elbow tight against his notebook.

Someone behind him shouted to duck.

He bowed until his forehead nearly touched his knees. The final push came from behind. Wheel noise suddenly receded; the hard support beneath him began to fall away.

He caught no new hand.

Unfamiliar cold air touched his face, smelling of wet concrete and stagnant water. Far away, something dripped. After a pause, it dripped again.

Lin Mo didn't open his eyes.

He tried to call Xiao Lu. His lips barely moved. Before consciousness sank away, he still remembered the half of the cart he'd cleared for her.

She hadn't sat down yet.
