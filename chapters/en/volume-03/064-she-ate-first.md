# Chapter 64: She Ate First

Xiao Lu laid the prop chopsticks flat, then picked them up again.

“These won't separate.”

Their ends were glued together for easy staging. A little pressure exposed a white line in the glue. She stopped pulling.

“I remember splitting them that time. Badly. One long, one short.”

“A splinter?” Lin Mo asked.

She looked at him. He stopped himself.

“I'm guessing. You didn't say that.”

“I don't remember a splinter. Don't add one.”

She set down the joined pair and rubbed a thumb along her other forefinger, as though seeking the uneven resting points. He couldn't supply this from his memory. He remembered his sister watching him with her chin in her hand. He hadn't even noticed the kind of chopsticks.

“The noodles were stuck together. I lifted them, put them down. Didn't think they'd taste good. Then I ate them anyway. I was hungry.”

The background remained frozen with its two people looking at each other. It supplied no answer. A comment demanded the host be reset, but hadn't become a proposal. She glanced at it and returned to her hands.

“When I'd eaten, half the bowl was left. I put the lid back. It wouldn't fasten properly. One edge stuck up.”

His fingers tightened on his knees.

“Don't look at me like that.”

He turned toward the table without entirely avoiding her.

“I remember half a bowl. I hadn't said it.”

“Were you there?”

“I arrived later. We can't force what you remember and what I remember into the same occasion yet. Do you want to hear it now?”

“This time, yes. Only the part you were there for.”

“I remember my sister saving half a bowl for me. I arrived late, ate too quickly, then asked why she hadn't eaten hers while it was hot. She said, Swallow before you yell at me.”

It came out in one breath. He'd carried the words too long. Every one wanted a nod from her.

She didn't nod.

“Why didn't you tell me before?”

“At the water station, you said that sentence and asked me not to tell you yet. I agreed.”

She considered this, pushed the chopsticks farther away and waited for him to continue with the part he'd witnessed.

He straightened, earning a warning from his shoulder. He began again with approaching the bowl. His sister had saved noodles. He'd assumed she hadn't eaten properly and wanted her to take care of herself. He hadn't seen the waiting, or anything before she'd lifted the lid.

“I asked why she hadn't eaten while it was hot. I thought I was looking after her.”

“She'd already eaten.”

Xiao Lu stopped after the quick answer.

“I remember eating. I can't yet tell whether your person belongs to this occasion. But don't take those mouthfuls away from me.”

He nodded, throat tight. If it was the same occasion, there was a part he'd never witnessed before his late arrival: her impatience, her hunger, her decision to pick up the chopsticks herself. He'd treasured the half she saved without asking where the first half had gone.

“Keep them. I didn't see everything.”

He wanted to tell her she'd been right to eat first. Before he could, she asked what he was about to say. He admitted it instead of pretending he wanted water.

“I didn't just learn today to eat when I'm hungry.”

“I know.”

“You sound as though you just found out.”

He rested his palms on his knees, offering no medal for those mouthfuls. Taking back a sentence didn't make her instantly gentle. She shifted the wooden chair sideways and sat where she didn't have to face his gaze directly. At the scrape of its legs, he wanted to steady it. He asked first.

“Already moved it. Next time, wait until I can't.”

He lowered his hand. Xiao Lu returned her attention to the table. Sounding familiar didn't require her to touch the same medicine packaging and answer the same question again. He'd given up a quick way to reassure himself. He could only listen while she kept the uncertain parts too.

She remembered regretting replacing the lid. The remaining noodles had cooled; she might not eat much if she reopened them, but the person still hadn't arrived. She wanted to lift the lid. Having said she'd save the food, she resisted.

“I blamed him too.”

Lin Mo didn't rush to defend himself.

“For always being late?”

“Another guess.”

“Yes. Tell me.”

“I don't know if he was always late. That time, I didn't want to wait, but I didn't leave. I saved the food and stayed angry. Whoever came back should at least know I'd waited.”

She gave a brief, embarrassed laugh.

“Doesn't sound as nice as the picture.”

He looked at its frozen smile.

“More trouble than I remembered.” She raised her eyes. “I have to ask questions. Get told off. In my version, she handed over the noodles and all that remained was how moved I felt. Spared me a lot afterward.”

“Don't decide that means you asked the right question now.”

“I haven't.”

She watched him until he finished.

“A little. I'm taking it back.”

This time she couldn't quite suppress her smile. Then she frowned, as though smiling tugged at the anger she'd only just recovered. He didn't cover it with his own laughter. Both expressions could remain.

Some viewers explained for new arrivals. Others complained the explanation was too long: scroll up. Lin Mo couldn't know who'd carried news of the session where. Attendance kept growing, but the conversation hadn't all become a siblings' reunion.

Someone asked to see beneath the bowl.

Xiao Lu looked up. Her mouth began its practiced smile.

“Welcome, new—”

She stopped, gripping the table. Lin Mo checked the proposal panel. The previous round still showed failed; no new execution line. He didn't cover her mouth.

She waited through the breath and finished herself.

“New arrivals should look carefully. You can't eat this.”

“Want to stop?” he asked quietly.

“I know how to say that. Hosting made it familiar. Not every easy word has to scare me.” She looked at the model. “I want to show them, too. Don't decide everything I know now needs changing.”

He nodded, easing his hand against the wood. She was still Xiao Lu. She knew how to bring newcomers into a conversation. Remembering waiting for someone hadn't invalidated those things.

Could she show the props closer, without a close-up of her? He aimed first at the table edge beside the bowl, showed her the frame, then raised it slowly. Her hands entered. Her face didn't grow larger.

After checking the rim wasn't too hot, she tilted the full bowl. The model noodles formed one solid piece she could lift, revealing the shallow hot-water holder underneath. Steam rose as she removed it. Both hands moved clear at once.

“There is one.” She tapped a yellow piece with the chopstick tips. “Glued on firmly. Take it home and you might pass it down.”

Lin Mo laughed aloud, then pressed his chest. Laughing didn't mean he'd put what had happened to Old Chen behind him. Far away, a cup remained inside a bag. Nobody would return to drink because they could laugh. He'd heard something genuinely funny and couldn't twist himself into another shape at the same time.

Xiao Lu placed the noodle model on the table, ending its pretense of covering a meal. She left the other bowl alone. Could he change the background to an empty table? She didn't want to sit beneath that smile any longer.

He went to the controls, exited the frozen background and chose the empty view from the same prop set. The figures and preset voices left this display. Two bowls remained. No new room opened; no executed memory change vanished with an image.

When he returned, FREEDOM stood at sixty-two.

He sat, suddenly too dry-throated to speak. So much to ask: which table, which day, whether it was the hospital. Questions lined up, eager to squeeze through while she was willing.

“I want to stop a while,” she said.

He looked toward the controls.

“End the broadcast?”

“Stop asking about my past. Let me sit. Don't fill it with someone else's voice.”

He looked back and agreed.

He looked back at the bowl whose noodle layer had been removed.

Comments accused the viewer of pretending to be good. Others said watching didn't require goodness. Lin Mo's fingers curled. He nearly said he didn't want such votes, then looked at the legs he'd only just learned to move. An absolute declaration wouldn't spare him pain.

“I can't make promises for you. Vote next time, and I'll fight it again.”

Xiao Lu saw his hand.

“Hurting again?”

“A little. Angry too.”

She didn't tell him votes were enough. Neither could turn eighty-three choices into a clean speech of thanks. Eventually he lowered his hovering hand to his leg and quietly gave her the number.

She looked without declaring herself ready to continue. After sitting longer, she released the table and glanced toward her bag.

“I want to write down those mouthfuls. Not for the camera.”

Wang Hai offered to take her things out and pass them over. She refused; she'd fetch them herself. He left the bag unopened on the waiting-side stool.

Before rising, she looked back at Lin Mo.

“When you told her to eat, was she really angry?”

“Yes. I thought she was just tired of hearing me talk.”

“Maybe more than that.”

“I know now it might have been.”

His agreement didn't suddenly reveal her old name. He didn't ask her to call him her brother again, turning the difficult truth beside the bowl into an ending he could claim.

She took two steps and stopped.

“And that paper.”

He raised his head.

“You told me someone signed and gave everything she could pay. I've heard what she agreed to. Now I want to know what she thought before agreeing.”

“I didn't see all of that.”

“Then don't say she had no regrets before I can find out.”

She looked at the two bowls, speaking softly.

“If it was me, I might have been frightened. Angry. I might have wanted to change my mind. I want to know whether those parts get to stay.”
