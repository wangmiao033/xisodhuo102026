# Chapter 88: Put Your Hand Here First

As the gray door moved outward, Lin Mo first saw Wang Hai's shoes.

They stood well beyond the doorway beside the waiting bench. The front attendant bent forward with unwrapped white cloth in his hands, without entering.

“Bottom row's down. One more.”

Lin Mo looked. The teeth nearest the door had dropped into the floor, but a final section still stood at an angle, pointed toward his ankles.

EXECUTING RELEASE remained lit on the panel.

Applicant: Lin Mo.

No missing votes waited beneath it. What a hundred thousand had accomplished was withdrawing the lock, section by section. He held the seat leg, neither reaching for End nor asking anyone to recount.

The last section sank.

A long fracture sounded overhead.

The main anchor had broken in the previous strike, but the door-foot and side retainers had held the fallen frame under tension. Now the last restraint released. Without that opposing pull, the side beam rolled outward. The empty wrist shell struck another beam.

Mirror panels fell in succession.

Farther away, the black stage frame's wheel base cracked. A tearing sound traveled through the walls. Thin bands still bearing lit position numbers snapped free behind the broken mirror, struck the floor, and went dark.

The theater could no longer maintain its stages around an applicant permitted to leave.

It began to collapse.

Beyond the black frame, someone holding an empty tray suddenly lowered their arms. The tray struck the floor. They flinched, waited a breath, then stepped across the mark they'd been guarding.

A second step followed.

Another performer, caught in repeated bows beside the stage, knocked against their own knees. Straightening, they steadied a neighbor and pointed toward a passage newly exposed behind the frame. Its barrier had detached along the top. A draft carried frightened voices through the gap.

None of their names had joined Lin Mo's application. Broken restraints gave them places to put their feet.

Someone shouted from the passage's far end that the door was clear. A leaning beam had torn open the outer doorway. A duty worker checked beyond it for obstructions, then beckoned.

The tray-bearer picked up the fallen object, ran several steps carrying it, then left it against the wall and went through the theater's outer door empty-handed.

Nothing dragged them back.

Seeing them turn and wave, those still waiting beside floor marks followed. Someone crouched to retrieve a companion's lost shoe. Another pushed past impatiently, then stopped to help support a panel hanging across the opening. Lin Mo saw only those few. Dust hid the farther rooms. Before he could count, another piece fell overhead.

“Away from the frame!” Wang Hai shouted.

He braced on the bench and stood, retreating two short steps. His left palm hurt under pressure; he rested his forearm against the wall instead. His wounded right wrist stayed against his body.

Inside the door, Lin Mo dragged the long wood closer.

An exit didn't enable him to stand. Glass covered part of the distance ahead. He swept twice. Small pieces moved; a larger shard revolved and pointed toward him.

The attendant crossed the sunken teeth, crouched inside, and slowly pushed it aside with his shoe. A pale scratch scored the shoe's upper. He kept pushing until the glass reached the wall, then cleared the narrow route.

“Where can I support you?”

“Behind my waist. Not the left shoulder.”

White cloth went beneath his left palm. One hand supported his lower back. The other protected the notebook's projecting corner without taking the troublesome object away.

Lin Mo pushed with his heels, braced his palm heel through the cloth, and moved clear of the seat leg. The wood lay across his knees. After each movement he dragged it closer.

Light from outside reached his face. He stopped again.

His right leg shook too hard. The angle he'd just learned couldn't hold indefinitely. The attendant waited until he settled his foot, then helped him across the threshold that could no longer close on him.

Wang Hai didn't tell him to get up.

He braced the waiting bench with his forearm while Lin Mo eased sideways onto it. His feet stayed on the floor, the long wood across his thighs. A gray-red patch marked the cloth beneath his left hand.

The judgment room's side-camera light wavered.

Its picture tipped, then a falling black frame blocked it. The seat microphone carried the impact out to the stage, then went silent. The attendant's screen turned gray. The door didn't close again over the release already executed.

“Xiao Lu's in holding,” Lin Mo said.

“Side passage. Past the backstage duty light, then two turns. The inner door's loosening too.”

The familiar gray-walled staff passage lay exposed between stage and backstage. Dust sifted from its entrance. Lin Mo couldn't judge how long crawling there would take.

The attendant called down it for something that could carry him.

The person left at Xiao Lu's console had abandoned the dead connection. Hearing, he repeated the call. Farther along, Xiao Lu held the observation-room doorway and heard that the injured person waited on the front stage.

“My brother.”

The gray-costumed woman had already retrieved her empty cart.

No lighted mark pulled its wheels now. She turned the handle herself, aimed its narrow end toward the corridor, and asked what could cushion it. The holding attendant laid a fallen seat pad on the low platform and pressed it, checking for sharp objects.

Xiao Lu reached for the handle. Her palm slid across it before finding a grip.

She followed the woman out. Her bag remained on her left shoulder, cup and script seeming heavier with every step. Passing the baskets she'd moved herself, she tried to push one closer to the wall.

Her fingertips passed through a little section of wicker before stopping against a thicker part.

She withdrew her hand and looked toward the cart.

“Get him first. I'll be slower.”

She followed a little farther, reaching the first corner nearer her old station before stopping against the wall. Looking back, the woman left the holding attendant with her and continued. Around the corner, past the duty light, along the open passage: the cart reached the front stage.

When she explained where the cart had stood, Lin Mo realized she was the person Xiao Lu had mentioned, the woman who hadn't been able to stop pushing.

She placed the cart beside the bench and crouched to hold a wheel. The front attendant supported his lower back while he shifted slowly from bench to padded platform. With white cloth wrapped around his left palm, he gave a final push and brought his legs inside the edge.

The wood came too, laid on his right. Nobody stood it upright to pretend it could still serve as a crutch.

“She's waiting at the corner,” the woman said. “Something's wrong with her hands.”

His face lifted sharply.

The wheels were already turning. The woman protected the broken wood near the edge while the attendant cleared the way ahead. Wang Hai followed slowly along the wall. At the duty light he stopped to let two people from a side station pass before continuing.

Lin Mo saw Xiao Lu's empty console.

The chair remained pushed underneath. No papers lay on the surface. The voice field was closed. She'd left this place herself. He let the empty chair fall behind him and watched the corner approach, fingers tightening once around the wood before loosening.

Xiao Lu sat against the wall just before the first turn.

The holding attendant crouched beside her, trying to support her right elbow. His hand touched her sleeve, but the arm inside became alternately light and heavy, as though part of it kept failing to settle into his palm.

Hearing the wheels, she looked up.

“Ge.”

Lin Mo leaned forward. Pain caught his hips and knees together. The woman stopped the cart beside her and steadied it.

“What happened?”

Xiao Lu removed the bag from her left shoulder. Its strap slipped through her fingers. She cupped the bottom with both hands and brought it toward the cart. Lin Mo extended his right forearm without bending the injured wrist, letting the whole bag settle beside his legs.

“It warned me when I released the binding. What I had left wouldn't hold me up for long.”

A gray seam in the wall showed through her knuckles.

“When did this start?”

“Before registration. Only the fingertips then.”

She pressed her palm to the floor and tried to move closer. Her body shifted a little; the support beneath her hand vanished. She settled against the wall again.

“Where's your cup?”

“In the bag.”

He opened it, touched the script's stiff edge, and saw the index-wrapped receipt tucked to one side. Leaving the papers alone, he took only the screw-top cup. His shaking wrist couldn't turn the lid. The woman loosened it and put the cap on the cart.

Xiao Lu could still accept the cup.

Holding it in both hands, she swallowed a little water. It tasted cold and faintly metallic. Lin Mo watched her throat move before asking whether it helped.

She waited, then shook her head.

He looked at the attendant. The man still held out the hand that had tried to support her elbow. He could find cloth and water here, he said, but the release terminal had already warned her that holding couldn't replenish what she lacked.

Light made her ear look thin. Lin Mo could hear her breathing. He couldn't know how much of her each breath kept present.

“Come with the cart. We'll find someone outside.”

He didn't promise a solution. She nodded and searched for a place to set her feet, just as he'd searched for a heel position in the judgment room. Her wish to leave was so concrete. Even her bag was already aboard the cart she meant to share.

He closed its mouth with the back of his hand, settling the papers inside. He refused to let this small preparation become the packing of her effects.

The cup wall was sinking through her fingertips.

The woman caught it underneath and lifted it away. Water spilled onto Xiao Lu's clothing, leaving a dark patch. Floor lines showed through the patch's lower edge. The water couldn't make her outline solid.

“Leave the things for now,” Lin Mo said.

She didn't need to accept the cup again to prove she was still here.

A heavy crash came from holding. Its inner door had been wrenched open by the leaning wall. People supported one another through the new gap. Someone called that another passage continued beyond it and told those behind to slow down.

The voices couldn't lift Xiao Lu.

Looking toward the route, she pressed her fingers against the floor again. Only her clothing moved. Lin Mo took the cloth from beneath his palm, folded a clean corner outward, and laid it across his right hand.

“Put your hand here first.”

She reached.

Through the cloth he felt a little warmth, as though she'd barely touched its edge. He kept his hand flat, afraid to close the injured wrist. His left hand left the cart to support hers from underneath.

Xiao Lu moved half an inch closer.

The cloth slowly sagged.

His hand hadn't moved. Hers remained before his eyes. No weight held between them.

He looked up sharply.

She hadn't withdrawn or told him to leave. Watching his face, she tried to curl her increasingly transparent fingers into his palm.

“I'm here.”

Lin Mo reached again.

This time his fingertips passed through her hand.
