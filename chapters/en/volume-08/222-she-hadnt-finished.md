# Chapter 222: She Hadn't Finished

“When didn't I wait?” Lin Mo asked.

Xiao Lu sat on the repositioned chair, silent. Outside, Zhou's chopsticks touched his bowl. Downstairs the workmen checked their tools. Life hadn't cleared a sufficiently quiet space for her.

“When you decided to get me out.”

He looked at his supported right hand. He knew the outline, what he'd written and left behind. On paper the decision was clear. Facing somebody who said he hadn't waited, that clarity suddenly seemed terribly thin.

Did she blame him for bringing her out?

Her brows drew together.

“You've changed the question again.”

He closed his mouth. It was too convenient. He had turned a complaint about being left out into a demand that she pronounce on the outcome. Knowing the outline of what he had done did not entitle him to choose the question she was trying to answer. If she said no, her anger would lose its footing. If she said yes, she'd seem obliged to reject being alive.

She turned slightly towards the half-open door. She'd wanted out. Wanted to live. She was glad he could sit here. None of that prevented anger. After waking, she'd had to learn, bit by bit, what he'd left and lost. There'd been no chance to argue or ask about another way.

“I know there might not have been another way. But I didn't even get to finish saying don't.”

His throat dried. He couldn't use the diary's voice to defend every minute, or declare that earlier self entirely wrong to comfort her. Two people stood on the far side of that decision, not a rescuer and a successful result.

“I don't remember the whole process.”

“I'm not asking you to recite it.”

She touched the table, withdrew, and clasped her knuckles. There was no phone to grip now.

“Sometimes I want to ask: if you could decide I should live, can I make one decision for you?”

He didn't answer.

“You looked outside, and I wanted to block the view. You wanted your phone, and I wanted to check who it was first. If I knew a little sooner than you, perhaps I could stop it.”

She shook her head.

“I know holding your phone doesn't make you safe.”

His finger pressed against the spoon. He almost promised it would never happen again. Instead he asked whether she wanted water.

She looked at him. He admitted he'd nearly guaranteed something impossible. Changing the subject to water was another way of avoiding her.

“I still want some.”

The cup was within reach. He pushed it slowly across, without using his injured right hand underneath. She drank and set it nearer herself. Neither apologized for its handle pointing away from him nor asked to have it back.

Could he repeat what he thought he'd understood, rather than change her meaning again?

She nodded.

“You aren't denying you wanted to live. You're angry that once I'd decided, all you could do was bear the consequences.”

Her eyes reddened without spilling over. There was more. Since returning, he kept trying to say the right thing. Giving choices. Telling her not to do things for his sake. She understood the kindness, but sometimes even staying seemed to require proof that she'd chosen independently, free of concern for her brother.

He looked towards the book. That afternoon he'd said she could come if she wanted; she'd meant to ask whether he needed help carrying things. An unnecessary assurance could send her a long way from the place beside him.

“If you want to stay, I can say thank you. You needn't remove every bit of worry first.”

She didn't say that settled it. Trying to break a raised splinter off the chopsticks she was using left a shallow mark on her finger. He almost took them away, then asked whether she wanted another pair.

Yes. She laid the splintered pair aside.

Zhou brought replacements without asking how the conversation was going. Whose dinner needed reheating? Both. Lin Mo asked him to leave the soup; it would be difficult to hold if too hot.

After he left, the plastic bag rustled faintly.

“Will you think I'm ungrateful?” Xiao Lu asked.

“No.” He answered quickly, then admitted his first impulse had been to defend himself. He couldn't pretend he'd listened well throughout.

She studied him as though testing whether this, too, was an answer designed to manage her anger.

He added nothing. Moving the wet receipt away from her notebook, he realized she'd brought the thin book onto her lap.

His name wasn't on the cover.

“Is that about you?”

“Yes.”

“I won't look.”

Her hand rested on it. A brief smile appeared.

“I haven't said you can't.”

He couldn't smile back. Everything he said tonight seemed to go astray. She shook her head. Not everything. Opening the notebook, she showed him one page, covering the others with her palm.

Places and scattered times. Printing, binding, how long to walk. He couldn't read the lowest line. She explained: she wanted to try doing something.

While printing the notice at the general store, she'd heard that old sample books needed sorting. Occasionally somebody helped for two hours. She hadn't accepted, only asked when they might discuss it. Back here, she'd almost mentioned it several times, then seen him writing today's record and stopped.

“Why?”

“I was afraid you'd agree too quickly.”

This time he waited.

“You'd say go, don't stay for my sake. As though staying meant I was doing something wrong.”

The small handwriting recorded a journey that might hurt him, or get her pushed away by him. Times had been written and crossed out. He didn't reach for another page.

“What would you like me to know now?”

“I want to ask. I haven't decided to do it. Wanting to go doesn't mean I don't want to look after you.”

“I'll want you here.”

She looked up.

He'd grown used to her chair, to recognizing who made a cup rattle. He wanted to keep that sound. Yet when Zhou was here, some things didn't require her to remain. Wanting her back and making it impossible to leave were different.

“I don't know whether I'll always separate them clearly. Tonight I'm trying to tell the truth.”

She closed the notebook without handing it over. The part she had shown him remained enough. He could remember the scattered times without needing to inspect what came before or after them. She had brought him into one thought, not surrendered everything she might write next.

What did the two hours involve? He asked. She opened it a little again. Different sizes of samples were mixed together. Blank backs that could still be used needed separating; punched or stapled sheets went elsewhere. She'd only heard an explanation. She hadn't tried, didn't know the required speed, or whether it amounted to a regular job.

“What about pay?”

“I hadn't reached that.”

He nearly told her not to go without asking about money. Her glance stopped him. She hadn't agreed to work unpaid. Her inquiry had simply not reached his warning yet.

“Ask tomorrow, then. You ask. I can help with anything unclear, without telling them you can do it.”

“You don't necessarily have to listen.”

“Right.”

She drew the page towards herself and added a few words. He couldn't see them. For once, not seeing needn't mean exclusion. He was seeking something he could do too, he said. Helping with a notice wasn't a job. If she found work first, she needn't wait for him.

Her pen stopped.

“Will it feel as though I'm telling you to get better faster?”

He considered it. Perhaps, for a moment. Money disappeared quickly; other people's plans might make him impatient. But that feeling wouldn't be the meaning of her work. She needn't stay home to protect him from it.

She didn't promise merely to look and hurry back. Finishing her note, she fitted the pen cap backwards, removed it and tried again. He remembered the complaint about shoes, the earlier self who could decide too quickly who'd put something in the wrong place. He still lacked the room around that memory. Here, at least, he could see a small thing being done again.

Would soup reach the notebook where it lay? He indicated an empty part of the table. She moved it herself, leaving the pen handy. They didn't name the movement or declare it proof that everything would henceforth be negotiable.

The reheated dinner arrived. Everyone made room; hands paused in the small space without upsetting the water. Xiao Lu tasted a mouthful and laughed, drawing air over her tongue. Hot. Zhou said he'd warned her. She knew. She'd wait.

Lin Mo ate too. A vegetable stalk defeated his left hand. After one attempt he asked Zhou to separate it with serving chopsticks. Xiao Lu had already raised hers. Seeing Zhou take over, she returned them to her own meal without apologizing for not helping. Lin Mo needn't manufacture another difficulty to reserve a task for her.

The conversation hadn't sweetened the rice or healed his wrist. He could stop talking without proving indifference, or continue eating without pretending everything was resolved.

Later, Zhou arranged the transfer. Xiao Lu stayed where Lin Mo could see her and asked where to help. Zhou was already supporting him; she needn't add another hand there. Please move the coat. She did, then stepped aside while they transferred slowly.

Once his leg was secure, his back was wet. The breath he'd held emerged raggedly. Xiao Lu fetched a towel and asked. He nodded. She handed it to Zhou instead of insisting on wiping him herself.

When Zhou went to clear the dishes, she sat beside the bed without bringing her notebook.

“Sometimes I think if I'd known sooner, perhaps less would have been lost.”

Lin Mo watched her hands. He couldn't return missing years and knowledge one item at a time. Nor did he want an unrealized earlier warning to keep punishing her awake tonight.

“I don't have an answer either.”

“I know.” After a pause: “I still think it.”

She needn't promise to stop just to reassure him. She didn't relax immediately, but her shoulders gradually left their ready-to-rise posture.

Tomorrow, could they ask when Zhou was available, then visit the store? Yes, ask about his plans first. She stood and put her notebook back on the table.

At the door she turned.

“Saying I'm angry doesn't mean I don't want you as my brother.”

His throat moved. He didn't ask her to call him that again.

“I heard you.”

She didn't add that he'd been forgiven. Taking the cup outside, she poured herself more water. He heard the quiet stream and knew she was doing something for herself.

The door remained open. A draft came along the corridor. Lin Mo asked Zhou to adjust the window gap, without calling for his sister first.
