# Chapter 224: The Call Couldn't Answer for Her

The phone woke Lin Mo that afternoon.

An unfamiliar number. Not the hospital's previous contact, he established before answering. Zhou was hanging a towel outside. Xiao Lu had gone downstairs for salt; afterwards she'd finish arranging the trial shift.

The caller asked for Lin Mo, introduced himself as Xu, and said a friend had mentioned his remarkable story.

Sleep left him.

“Which friend?”

“Qin Yue. The photographer.”

Xu's voice was bright. In the background, objects were being moved around what sounded like a large room. Qin Yue had mentioned recent events. They might make a personal documentary, with payment, not simply take his time.

At documentary, Lin Mo's left finger touched the new paper beside the bed, then stopped.

“Did Qin Yue give you my number?”

Yes, quite casually. Lin Mo neither accepted the claim immediately nor supplied his address. Send a message introducing yourself and the proposal, please. He'd check with Qin Yue first.

“I'm not selling anything.”

“I didn't say you were.”

Xu laughed. Of course, no misunderstanding intended. Many people would be interested in experiences like theirs. Ideally, family who'd shared them could participate. More authentic.

Where had family come from? Xu paused. Qin Yue had mentioned a sister accompanying him. They'd naturally need to hear the details from the people themselves.

Lin Mo's thumb pressed the volume button. The voice leaped louder. Hastily reducing it pulled at his supported wrist and made him inhale. Bad time? Xu asked.

“For now, that's enough.”

He ended the call.

Zhou appeared. Who was it?

“A stranger. Says Qin Yue introduced us.”

Lin Mo explained without turning potential payment into income, or a laugh into proven malice. He asked for the charging lead, then messaged Qin Yue: had he given the number to someone called Xu?

Eventually Qin Yue replied. Yes. He thought they might need work. He'd only mentioned a little. Not an inquiry into the incident's cause, just their experiences. It should be fine.

Lin Mo stared at should.

Ask before giving out his contact details in future, he replied. He'd investigate this himself. Qin Yue mustn't agree to recording or filming for either sibling. An okay came back, followed by an assurance that Xu was polite and payment negotiable.

Lin Mo didn't argue about politeness and reliability. Putting the phone down, he tried to sit straighter and discovered the pull in his wrist remained. Would repositioning the support help? Zhou asked.

Yes. Steady the right arm first, without making the left shoulder bear his weight.

Halfway through, an argument rose outside over a delivery blocking the entrance. Words crossed the courtyard wall and became an indistinct let her out.

His back locked. Breath wouldn't pass his throat.

Zhou stopped moving him. Look at him. Where did it hurt?

Lin Mo shook his head, then corrected himself: there was pain, but he meant the words. He could still see the towel, bed rail and window gap. The voice came from outside. A familiar urgency nevertheless climbed his back, as though hesitation would invite somebody at the door to decide for him.

“Don't move yet.”

Zhou held the support steady without pinning his hand. After several breaths the argument ceased; wheels rattled away. Lin Mo located his left foot. His right leg remained supported. He hadn't set it down to escape a sentence.

Only when he could continue did Zhou settle the cushion more comfortably.

“Call Xiao Lu?”

“No.” Too quick. He added that she was merely buying something downstairs. Someone was here. Nothing required her to hurry back. A clear worsening meant following the clinician's instructions about seeking help, not waiting for his sister to solve it.

Zhou nodded without treating this as a declaration that Lin Mo no longer needed her.

Xu's message arrived. A brief introduction and a proposal for a short film about lived experience. Talk first, no camera; discuss further if interested. No amount or distribution destination.

Lin Mo wanted to refuse. Half-sentences cut apart, his former body forced before a lens: even imagining somebody saying once more was unbearable.

But the proposal concerned two people.

He could refuse for himself. He shouldn't fold his sister's answer into it. He wrote that he did not currently consent to recording or filming. If Xu wanted to continue, specify purpose, conditions and payment. Family members would decide for themselves. No photograph, diary page or hospital document accompanied it.

After sending, he discovered he was waiting for Xiao Lu.

Not for water. He wanted everything neatly settled before she spoke, sparing her worry and himself the exposure of those difficult minutes. The impulse troubled him more than the call.

She returned some ten minutes later with salt and a little roll of adhesive tape. The store's goods were muddled today; finding things had taken a while. Handing Zhou the salt, she saw Lin Mo's face.

“Has it been hurting?”

He nearly said he'd just woken.

Instead he explained the pull in his wrist, the adjusted support and being startled by words downstairs. He could speak now; he hadn't blacked out. She put her bag down and looked to Zhou, who added only what he'd witnessed, without guaranteeing that everything was fine.

“Why didn't you call?”

“You didn't need to hurry back.”

Her face tightened. He asked her to sit: there was also a call to explain. She could listen standing. He described Qin Yue's referral, Xu's mention of family and his reply. Would she like the original messages?

She reached. He handed over the phone.

Reading decide for themselves, she stopped.

“You already don't want to discuss it, do you?”

“I don't want filming.”

“I mean you don't even want to listen.”

This time the phone genuinely belonged in her hands for the purpose he'd offered it. He couldn't reach back merely to follow her reading. Yes, he admitted. He'd wanted to refuse immediately, but hadn't refused for her.

She dimmed the screen. She didn't yet know the offer or requirements. Asking shouldn't feel like betraying her brother because cameras frightened him.

“I don't think that.”

“Your face looks as though you do.”

Anger arrived. His face might carry a reaction to past events without issuing instructions. She couldn't judge his every silence for him.

She put down the phone, her voice hardening too.

“Then don't decide that knowing you were distressed will stop me doing things.”

He stared.

He'd told her, hadn't he? But there was the answer he'd nearly given about waking. Rather than supply her with an infallible ability to read him, he asked why she'd said it.

She'd seen Zhou clearing the cloth used with the cushions. Meanwhile Lin Mo had turned away, as though waiting for his face to settle before speaking.

One observation couldn't prove every thought. It had caught this one.

He laid his left hand on the cover. After a while: “I did want to wait until I felt better to tell you.”

“I'm not asking for an immediate report about everything.”

She'd gone out that morning and returned. If each return offered only already sorted, with even pain inferred from somebody else's movements, those two hours would become an invisible blank. She'd fear more, not less.

Zhou put the salt away without intervening. Watching the cupboard shut, Lin Mo realized they'd turned each other into consequences. If he admitted fear, she must stay. If she learned he'd hurt, letting her leave must have been wrong. Yet perhaps she wanted to hear what happened without immediately being assigned a repair.

“I can tell you about the day when you come back. Not make you responsible for all of it.”

She didn't guarantee that would always work. She drank water and asked whether Xu had replied again.

Not yet.

Sitting, she opened a blank page, not the trial-work notes. Write the questions first: destination, actual price, whether both must appear together. Authentic experience couldn't stand in for details.

Why would Qin Yue think they needed this work? She asked.

Lin Mo showed her the referral message without supplying Qin Yue's private motives. She pointed at might need. Yes, they needed money. They hadn't asked anybody to offer their experiences for sale.

He agreed. She returned the phone and saved Xu's contact on her own. Opposition stirred again in Lin Mo. He began warning her not to give their address. She hadn't sent anything, she told him. She was saving a number.

“I know you're worried. But wait until I'm actually about to do something before asking why.”

He nodded, keeping the warning to himself without pretending complete ease. Zhou brought back the salt, asking how the packet opened. Finding no tear notch, Xiao Lu fetched scissors beside the trial-work paper. She put that paper away, cut the salt packet, then returned. The unfamiliar call hadn't replaced every other task.

The store had clarified the hour for the morning after next, she told Lin Mo. Zhou had confirmed he could remain home then. She still wanted to try. Hearing tonight's offer wouldn't automatically cancel those two hours.

Lin Mo admitted he'd almost made them alternatives: sorting paper or a potentially better-paid film.

They didn't know the amount, she said. Nor did she want every possible job laid before her brother to determine which made her the right sort of person.

“Then tonight we only ask about tonight.”

“What would you like me to help with?” he added.

“Read the message slowly. I missed a line while I was angry.”

He read it sentence by sentence. At talk first, no camera she stopped him. Keep that line. If talking later required recording, the conditions would have changed.

He agreed without congratulating her on learning caution. He needed those words visible too, rather than guessing every intention through fear of a lens. Neither of them had to decide whether Xu was a good person before asking what he proposed. A clear answer might still be something they disliked. At least it would be an answer, rather than the brightness of his voice or Qin Yue's assurance standing in for one.

Xu replied as they finished: could they talk for ten minutes that evening? Lin Mo turned the screen towards her. She could listen without agreeing to participate. He would discuss conditions too, not demonstrate old events.

They sent that meaning together. It contained no story for him to use, no scene to arrange, and no promise that either of them would say yes after hearing the terms. The evening appointment was only a conversation.

Afterwards she tucked the paper outside her notebook and taped a loose corner. The tape stuck to her fingertip. Two shakes failed to remove it; she slowly peeled it off with her other hand. Lin Mo couldn't help smiling. She raised the hand, told him not to laugh, then laughed first.

It didn't dissolve the argument. It let him rest against the cushion. His wrist still ached. His sister still wanted to hear the price. Neither had become the other's answer.
