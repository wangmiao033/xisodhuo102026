# Chapter 217: This Time He Said It Himself

Water remained on the lunch-box lid.

Xiao Lu wiped it before stacking it. By the time she finished, Lin Mo had lost his opening. I saw a hand might sound as though somebody else had appeared in the shop.

“It wasn't another person standing there,” he began.

She sat and waited.

“More like I was there. Holding wood. It was hot, or something nearby was hot. I can't separate those.”

He lifted his left hand to demonstrate the grip's width, then stopped. His shoulder wouldn't tolerate that for long; his right hand couldn't attempt it. Xiao Lu moved an empty cup without offering anything to grasp.

“You needn't act it out.”

“I know. Words aren't coming easily.”

He could recount plenty from the notebook. Turn to the relevant page, and its sentences stayed. This hand had no page number. The feeling was so close he couldn't find a place from which someone else might understand it.

He studied the knuckles visible beside his brace.

“It used to be wider here.”

She followed his gaze and nodded.

“Do you remember the wood too?”

“I didn't see that happen.”

Softly, but immediately. Something reaching outward in him met empty space. He'd known she hadn't been there. Wanting somebody to receive this moment had briefly pushed that knowledge aside.

“Right. You can't identify that for me.”

She didn't withdraw her attention.

“I remember your hands. I can't remember for you what you held that day.”

He lowered his hand. He nearly asked how he'd held a cup before, then saw his own water: half full, handle facing left. If she described an old habit, he might copy it immediately and mistake the familiarity of imitation for something returned.

“Don't tell me too much of the old stuff yet. I might confuse them.”

“Not ever?”

“Just today. I'll ask when I want to know. You can tell me things you want to tell, too. Don't guard every sentence for me.”

Her brow eased. Even asking someone to be silent briefly could leave them uncertain where waiting ended. He couldn't make her calculate whether mentioning a memory over water would hurt him.

Between her two earlier statements lay a small space. He no longer had to choose between everything being true and nothing meaning anything. She could recognize the brother she'd known without being required to authenticate a room she'd never entered.

A plastic stool scraped outside.

The heated wood came closer again. It didn't obscure this room. He could still see the cup and containers, while something heavy and wet seemed to lie across his chest, his shoulders supporting it, his collar hot and stifling. He didn't pursue the sound. He said he was still here, not dizzy, his vision not going dark.

Xiao Lu moved her feet clear of the leg support. Should she call Zhou?

“Not yet. Don't hold my hand down.”

She hadn't been doing so, but agreed.

There was a lower voice in his mind. Yet the words he'd spoken at the shop had emerged in his current voice. Those weren't the same thing. Nobody had heard his former throat return. Even he couldn't treat the internal sound as a recording.

But the refusal wasn't something printed on a page.

He couldn't recover everything that followed or a complete face. He knew that in that instant he hadn't wanted to comply. Something had demanded it. He'd held the wood and refused.

“Maybe what I remembered wasn't how I looked.”

“What was it?”

“What I meant.”

She didn't immediately smile or celebrate. He was grateful she didn't turn this into a recovery anniversary. Fear lived in it, anger at being cornered, pain he still resisted examining. Calling all of that progress would make his suffering then seem designed to produce good news now.

He said it again, without maybe.

“In that moment, I didn't want to.”

The words didn't broaden his wrist. Xiao Lu rested her hands on her knees.

“I hear you.”

He waited and found he didn't need her to ask how much more he'd recovered. Nor could this clarity establish that everything missing waited somewhere, retrievable through another fright. The fragment might fade. Shapes might eventually prove mixed with other things. He didn't want those possibilities to take the refusal away too.

“Are you disappointed?”

“About what?”

“That you weren't in it.”

She watched him, lips pressed together.

“I want to be. But I'm not the next installment you owe me.”

It wasn't an effortless answer. She pinched a small fold along her trouser seam, then released it. Her not demanding anything didn't entitle him to decide she felt no hurt.

“I want that too.”

He didn't promise she'd be next. Steam faded over the soup. Neither filled those seconds with a memory that hadn't arrived.

From farther away, Zhou asked whether they still needed the bowls. Xiao Lu rose to collect them. Lin Mo almost told her to stop fussing; instead he asked whether she'd finished her own portion.

“Yes. That's yours left.”

“Keep mine. I might want it later.”

She agreed. Her agreement did not mean every earlier unhappy moment had been settled between them. She was simply taking away the containers that were empty and leaving the food he still wanted. This wasn't a receipt settling every old hurt. Asking correctly now didn't cancel the times the notebook showed him not asking.

He slept briefly that afternoon.

Not to pursue dreams. His wrist ached, he'd been told to rest, and Zhou arranged the supports. When he woke, the light had shifted. Xiao Lu washed cups in the other room. The wood hadn't continued into his dream, but hadn't wholly vanished afterward either.

He established where today had reached: shop, hospital, lunch, talking with his sister. He remembered that sequence, even her wiping the lid, without retrieving it from paper. The gaps in his past remained. Today hadn't drained through them.

His phone was to his left, nearly discharged. Zhou brought it and put the cable on the table; Lin Mo didn't reach toward the socket on his right. He opened a blank document for Qin's promised note.

He deleted the first sentence twice.

An anomaly made the machine malfunction sounded like an established cause. Cutting affects bodies turned one observed association into a universal rule. He described what he'd encountered instead: temporarily jammed paper, Zheng Lan unable to lower her left arm freely, ordinary power disconnected. Qin had described disconnecting it; the plug was visible. Lin Mo hadn't witnessed every moment from switching off onward.

Zhou didn't polish those sentences into official-sounding language.

Lin Mo typed slowly with his left fingers, resting between paragraphs. For longer sentences, he asked Zhou to type his dictation rather than guess. Liang and the technician had checked the rear mechanism. Lin Mo hadn't watched the return operation to completion. He'd left his observation position; later events belonged to the respective witnesses' accounts.

“Will this sound as though you did nothing?” Zhou asked.

“I suggested not releasing the paper first.”

“Include it?”

“Yes. Why I thought so, too. I'm not removing myself.”

They separated his suggestions and their basis into a short paragraph without identifying whom anyone should thank. Recommendations to preserve evidence and keep equipment unused applied to this scene, not instructions for Qin to test the machine himself. Nothing authorized reopening.

Reading it through, Lin Mo noticed Zhou had typed already where he'd dictated at that moment. Zhou leaned closer, laughed at his misreading of the small text, and corrected it. They reread the remainder without making this a judgment on his care. Then Lin Mo exported and sent it.

No photograph of Zheng Lan. No family phone numbers. No account of Lin Mo's old room.

While they waited, the screen dimmed. He didn't ask Zhou to stand watch or wake it every few seconds himself. Xiao Lu finished washing and asked where to put a dry cup. Zhou went to find space. The room hadn't stopped to await acceptance of something Lin Mo had written.

He spread his left fingers, their pads sore from typing. He'd worried the report would crowd out what he'd told his sister. Instead, the two things had happened alongside each other: an account for the scene that required explanation, and something he kept here. Neither had to displace the other.

Qin acknowledged receipt eventually. Could he forward the safety conclusion to customers collecting photographs?

It wasn't a safety conclusion, Lin Mo replied. He could explain that the equipment remained isolated for investigation, not rename limited observations and circulate them as a guarantee. Qin said he understood. Then he transferred two hundred yuan, labeled site observation.

Lin Mo checked the amount and accepted it.

Transport still needed checking against Zhou's actual receipts. The hospital detour wouldn't automatically become Qin's expense. One obligation was clearer now. Zheng Lan's examinations hadn't thereby ended, nor had the trapped photograph become ordinary paper.

It was still light when Zhou offered the old notebook.

Lin Mo thought, then declined for today.

He asked Xiao Lu for a fresh sheet, not one torn from the old pages. He wrote the actual date at the top. His left-handed first line slanted. He could have asked someone with a steadier hand to copy it, but legibility was enough. The uneven line still contained the words he had chosen. He didn't start over.

“Today I remembered a feeling of holding wood. I remember that I didn't want to.”

Today's identifiable event and the incomplete past occupied separate sentences. He didn't write memory restored or insert his sister into a scene she hadn't witnessed. Had he mentioned the wood's color earlier?

“No.”

“Then I won't add one.”

She waited for the ink to dry. Watching her hands, he said he'd like to keep this piece himself rather than copy it into the index shared with others.

She returned it where he could reach.

“Then keep it.”

He didn't slip it into the old notebook as though it had always belonged there, or into Old Chen's sealed private fold. It went with his present-day records, its date visible. This page had come into existence today. It could hold a small piece without having to prove a whole person complete.

Xiao Lu went to switch on the outer room's light and asked whether they were reading tonight.

Lin Mo watched the doorway she'd crossed. No heat from the old room, no sudden return of years together. Just the small sound of a cup being replaced where it belonged.

“Yes. Food first. We'll stop when we're tired.”

She answered without turning to determine whether that sounded like the brother she remembered. Lin Mo settled against the supports. His wrist still hurt. His voice was still his current voice. But nobody else had spoken the sentence on the paper for him.
