# Chapter 229: Don't Close the Door for Me

Xiao Lu came for the coat she'd left inside.

Lin Mo was awake. Zhou had arranged his nighttime supports and was looking for tomorrow's socks in the outer room. Beyond the curtain, someone wheeled a bicycle into the courtyard, its bell catching with a short ring. Seeing his open eyes, Xiao Lu asked whether he still hurt.

“Better than earlier. Not entirely settled.”

She nodded without demanding a more reassuring number. Coat in hand, she remained. He thought she might discuss the trial work, then resisted guessing the conversation into the shape he expected. He looked back from the doorway and waited.

“When you remember, will you do it again?”

He understood: act without waiting for her. He didn't reduce the question to whether he'd go out tomorrow. That would be easy to answer. Tonight he couldn't even transfer independently. She meant a later time, when he might hold choices again.

“I can't promise I won't want to decide for you.”

Her grip tightened on the coat.

If he simply said never again, he explained, danger might let him dismiss that promise as something there wasn't time for. He didn't want to give her a beautiful guarantee to sleep on. They needed to discuss what could no longer be omitted.

She put the coat on the chair and sat.

Zhou asked from outside whether to leave the light. Yes. He could sleep if tired; Lin Mo would call for help. Zhou had a few things left to do. He didn't urge reconciliation or come in to sit as judge.

Looking at her hands, Xiao Lu spoke about a door she kept remembering. She didn't specify its location or ask Lin Mo to see it. Sometimes a closing door brought a swift thought: He's already gone through. I'm too late again.

The door before him remained the lodging's chipped door. No mirror or counting screen. His inability to see her remembered doorway didn't make her description an exaggeration.

“When the power went off and I took your phone...”

She stopped. Describing that seemed harder than describing earlier suffering. He remembered her refusing to let go and his anger at losing an ordinary possession. She'd feared a call could summon him away; a message missed by her might mean he'd already arranged everyone's next move.

“So I held on.”

“I didn't like it.”

She nodded without immediately explaining the depth of her fear. Her hands opened against her knees. She wouldn't deliberately withhold his phone again. If she noticed panic, she'd try to say what frightened her first. She couldn't promise instant success, but her good intentions couldn't cover what she'd done.

Lin Mo watched her.

He'd expected this conversation to end in forgiveness or continuing anger. Instead she was naming a wrong of her own without trading it against his. The wrongs weren't identical, nor did speaking cancel either of them.

Another bicycle bell. She glanced back but stayed seated.

If he wanted to accept another potentially dangerous site visit, he said, he couldn't just announce an outing. He needed to explain where, what dangers he knew, who would accompany him, and when to contact one another. Anything affecting her directly couldn't be accepted on her behalf. He paused. Did this sound like another set of orders?

“You can begin with what you want to do. Not with what you've already decided.”

He considered the change. It sounded small until he imagined making it himself. Beginning with a decision let him prepare every reason in advance and present them as a finished structure. Beginning with a wish left something open. She might ask a question he hadn't anticipated, or point to a cost he'd been quietly assigning to her. He would have to remain in the conversation long enough to hear it, rather than treat speaking as the last duty before departure.

He revised it: describe the possible job first, then work things out. She could disagree. He couldn't treat disagreement as evidence that she didn't understand. Discussion might still leave different opinions; neither could permanently bar the other's choices with a word.

“So you can still go.”

He didn't evade it. His decision was his responsibility, but he couldn't hide costs that would fall on her and return with thanks afterwards. If leaving required her to care for something alone, he must ask whether she'd accept it. Closing the door didn't complete a fair division of work.

After a long silence she said she didn't want to make fear into a lock. Yet being told she could stay behind made her wonder whether, without someone to stop him, he'd use himself up again.

His chest ached.

He wanted to hold her, wanted to prove something immediately. He couldn't force his injured body forward. Resting his left hand by the bed, he offered it if she wanted, without requiring contact. She finally held only his unburdened fingertips.

“I didn't come to hear that I should stop caring.”

“I don't want you to stop.”

Sometimes telling her to pursue her own life also contained a wish to push her away from pain, he admitted. If she couldn't see, he needn't acknowledge needing her. But needing her and ordering her to remain were different. He could ask for company while leaving room for refusal.

The phone vibrated. Xu had sent another line: perhaps there could be future work using only the sister's voice.

Lin Mo made the screen visible to both and asked whether she wanted to read it now. She glanced over. She'd refused this occasion and didn't want another discussion tonight. He neither issued a permanent refusal for her nor urged her to consider it. Through his own phone he said invitations concerning her must go to her directly, not through him.

Why reply at all? She asked. Silence would also have been possible, he said. He wanted to close that indirect route, rather than let Xu think the brother was easier to persuade. She didn't inspect every word. Afterwards he put the phone away with its sound still on for calls he might genuinely need.

“You wanted to refuse for me again.”

“Yes.”

“I could hear it.”

He was embarrassed. He hadn't said anything; how? He'd drawn breath as though preparing a long sentence. It was a guess, she added, possibly wrong. Correct this time, he said, but ask next time rather than treating each inhalation as an order.

She finally laughed. Must they report breathing too? Lin Mo said Zhou would move out first. Hearing his name, Zhou called from outside. Both said nothing, sounding suspiciously guilty. He looked in, saw less tension and returned to his socks.

The laughter was brief. Lin Mo didn't hurry to declare the argument over while it lasted. Her eyes were still red. The next sentence could hurt again. But they had managed one joke neither was forced to accommodate.

She released his hand after a while. Her fingers were warm. He didn't turn the contact into a ceremony of reconciliation. What did he want now? She asked.

He wanted her to consider tomorrow's trial as her own choice, neither canceling because of their quarrel nor going to prove fearlessness.

“I still want to go.”

“All right.”

She produced the shop's written note, not her whole private notebook. Two hours tomorrow morning, payment specified. No cutting machine. Stapled material belonged to the assistant; she'd sort sample books. If asked to do something else, she'd inquire rather than accept everything because she'd arrived.

Recognizing the assistant's written duration, Lin Mo asked whether she could bring lunch on the way back. Not necessarily; she might be tired. He blinked, then said they wouldn't assign that. He and Zhou would order separately.

“That needs asking too.”

“Yes.”

A two-hour trial didn't need to become a turning point in a whole life. She might sort incorrectly, dislike the effort or return saying she didn't want it. Her present willingness still deserved time without guaranteeing success. Lin Mo could wish her well without making success the price of having been allowed to leave him.

They discussed Zhou's time. Xiao Lu considered asking him to postpone his nap. Lin Mo stopped her: ask for the necessary period, not his whole day as reassurance. She asked aloud whether the morning remained convenient.

He'd be here then, Zhou replied through the doorway. In the afternoon he'd arranged tea with someone. Already mentioned; don't forget the tea tomorrow.

Both said they remembered. Their simultaneous answer made him laugh. No need to sound like a roll call.

A night breeze parted the curtain. Watching Xiao Lu close it, Lin Mo momentarily felt that she should be smaller, quicker. He recognized that hearing old stories might have supplied the image. He wouldn't demand she turn around to prove it.

When she sat again, he mentioned an uncertain old impression. Did he want to describe it? Not pursue it today. She didn't look wounded. Tomorrow she'd listen if he wanted; if not, he could keep it.

“Are you frightened I'll forget you?”

After a long silence, she nodded.

“And that when you remember everyone, you'll still think all I need to do is wait.”

Quieter than anger. Calling her sister couldn't answer it completely. He asked her to point out when he began finishing her sentences. No need to wait for complete recovery or a painless body. He might dislike hearing it. His displeasure couldn't make her words forbidden.

She might speak harshly too. He could say which words hurt, he answered, without canceling the whole matter. Neither smiled. It wasn't a clever new rule, only something they might use clumsily tomorrow.

Zhou came to inspect the supports and suggested dimmer light. Xiao Lu moved aside. Lin Mo asked him to wait while he returned his left hand to comfort, then they adjusted together. She watched without racing to perform every movement first.

Settled again, he saw her take the coat and pause by the door.

“I'm going to bed.”

“All right.”

She didn't ask once more whether he was truly fine. He didn't invent a task only she could complete to prolong the conversation. The familiar gap remained in the door; the outer light went dark and a chair moved softly.

Later, his aching wrist needed a different angle. He called Zhou. Xiao Lu didn't stir. Lin Mo didn't know whether she slept and didn't ask Zhou to check. He described what he needed, waited until the movement was secure, then thanked him.

The water caught a trace of light. He made sure it was reachable. Zhou put away the supporting cloth, returned outside and set down his phone. Lin Mo didn't call again merely to keep the room occupied.

No one had locked the door. Tonight it was simply a door left ajar. Tomorrow someone could go through it, and someone could return.
