# Chapter 230: I'll Tell You When You're Back

On the eighteenth morning, Lin Mo heard Xiao Lu getting up before he heard an alarm.

Something rustled in the outer room. He first thought she was looking for her phone, then recognized paper. The shop's note went into her pocket, came out, was folded smaller. Carrying breakfast past her, Zhou asked about water. Already in her bag.

Lin Mo didn't repeat the inventory.

A little of yesterday's shoulder pain remained. Zhou helped him settle with the usual supports. He described the actual discomfort rather than hiding it on her first trial-work morning. Nor did he require her to witness every adjustment before leaving. She ate, waited until he'd finished speaking, then asked whether yesterday's arrangements still held.

“They do.”

She swallowed the last mouthful. Checking the sorted material might take a little time after the two hours; she'd message if delayed. Lin Mo reminded her that they would order lunch themselves, not wait for her to buy it. Zhou confirmed, keeping the arrangement from becoming something only the siblings understood.

Her stool caught the bag strap as she stood, nearly pulling over the bottle. She crouched to free it and laughed at herself. Only going nearby, yet preparing as though for a long coach journey.

Lin Mo almost laughed too, then remembered last night's question and felt his chest tighten.

“I hope it goes well. You can tell me if it doesn't.”

She acknowledged it without demanding reassurance that he wasn't expecting failure. Morning air entered with the open door. She left it in the usual position behind her and went.

Zhou gathered bowls. Lin Mo checked the time, left the phone audible and asked for the book. He wouldn't spend a whole morning guarding a screen, or silence it to demonstrate indifference.

He read slowly.

The traveler still had farther to go than he'd expected. At a passage about wet clothes beneath eaves, a bamboo peg flashed through his mind. No clear color, but familiar pressure against a fingertip. He paused. He'd just read about hanging clothes; everything following that needn't be recovered memory.

“Tired?” Zhou asked.

“Something occurred to me.”

He steadied the book without immediately taking paper. His strongest desire was to call Xiao Lu: this fragment might concern home. He imagined her answering quietly over a stack of paper, asking whether he hurt. No, he'd say, memory. Then she'd have to decide whether to return before it disappeared.

His hand stopped on the phone.

Did he need her now? Not whether he could tell her later, not whether she'd want to hear. What required her immediate return? Zhou was here, water accessible, no sudden physical change. The peg didn't require his sister's instant naming in order to have occurred.

He asked for his current record. Beneath the date he wrote that reading about clothes had brought an unclear peg, possibly mixed with the reading itself. His left hand began trembling; he stopped rather than force an image into completeness by adding lines.

Should Zhou record more detail? No. Lin Mo had said only this much. Zhou capped the pen without supplying old habits to improve the picture.

Later, someone called from below.

The name belonged to another tenant and resembled his. His head lifted anyway, shoulders tightening. Zhou checked the window and reported only that the caller wanted their neighbor. He didn't inspect the entire courtyard to certify every sound. Lin Mo waited until the neighbor answered and went down, then released his grip.

He still startled. Last night's conversation hadn't canceled fear that reached his body before thought. Afterwards, however, he could return to reading without summoning Xiao Lu to witness his calm.

In the outer room, Zhou divided tea into a small tin for his afternoon visit. His friend complained that he always came empty-handed; today there'd be less to complain about. Had Lin Mo taken too much of his time? Zhou looked at him for a while.

Sometimes it was tiring. But tea this afternoon was arranged. No need to decide whether he should have come at all while he was packing it.

Lin Mo lowered his eyes and acknowledged the point.

How readily an ordinary remark became debt. Zhou hadn't pretended care was effortless or demanded immediate repayment. The lid screwed shut; the afternoon remained waiting. Lin Mo asked whether an earlier lunch would help. They settled a time without answering for Xiao Lu before she returned.

Near the end of two hours, the phone lit.

She was checking the last stack's quantity, would be a little late, and didn't need collecting. The assistant was there. Lin Mo replied that he'd received it. He removed Take your time before sending. Perhaps she wanted to finish promptly; he needn't prescribe the right emotion from another room.

He read the message to Zhou so he wouldn't wait at the door. They would order their own food, then ask what she wanted if she hadn't eaten. Lin Mo chose rice he could manage with a spoon.

When the door sounded, Xiao Lu wasn't carrying a mountain of work.

She set down her bag and looked at his face. He told her about briefly startling at a shout, recovering afterwards, and no obvious worsening of the shoulder. Zhou knew. He'd also recorded an uncertain image, which could wait until she'd rested. She listened standing, then sat and let out a long breath.

“My hands ache.”

Her fingertips looked rubbed red, without breaks in the skin. He didn't touch them. She inspected both palms and said gloves hadn't made constant sorting familiar. Zhou brought water. After drinking, she asked about lunch.

They hadn't chosen for her. She considered, decided on congee rather than prolonged chewing, then selected it on his phone and paid. Her portion was hers; no need for him to compete to cover it.

“You received the trial pay?”

“Yes. The two hours written down.”

The extra checking wasn't another batch of labor. The assistant had inspected what she'd already sorted, then they'd settled. She'd checked the amount received rather than accepting praise as payment. Lin Mo didn't ask for the figure. She could tell him if she wished. The promised money had actually arrived.

“And later?” Zhou asked.

Uncertain. She'd mixed two similar kinds once. Fortunately they hadn't entered the customer's sample books. She'd sorted them again, slower than an experienced worker. The assistant might contact her when more work came, without promising continued employment.

The hardest part, she said, wasn't the error. It was having a neatly arranged stack pushed back with the words that it wasn't right. Her face had burned; she'd almost told the assistant to do it themselves. Then she found two pages the customer still needed. Blank backs didn't make them waste.

“I thought they were finding an excuse because I was slow.”

What had she done? Spread the stack out, asked which marks meant it must be retained, and tried again. A mistake didn't make everything unfamiliar disposable. Nor had she agreed to stay indefinitely to prove herself after the arranged two hours.

She took an empty glove wrapper from her bag. Lin Mo thought she wanted him to judge its suitability; she was retrieving the settlement paper caught beneath it. After another check, she put the paper away. The shop's gloves remained there as required. She'd carried the wrapper accidentally.

“I can take it back to throw away, or throw it away now.”

She spoke as though finally finding something that needed little thought. Lin Mo moved the little rubbish box closer instead of deciding for her. She discarded it and let her shoulders settle.

She said nobody had asked how her brother was injured or why she was only now seeking temporary work. Most of her morning's words had concerned which stack belonged where.

A small vacancy opened in Lin Mo, then lightness. In a shop he hadn't entered, she'd been someone capable of sorting wrongly and learning again. His injuries didn't have to follow her name everywhere.

He nearly said everybody struggled at first. True perhaps, but it would cover the particular difficulty she'd described. He asked which papers had confused her. She produced two scraps the assistant had permitted her to keep and showed their subtly different backs beneath the lamp.

He couldn't distinguish them immediately.

She pointed out the marks. Thickness and transmitted light could help, but identifying marks came first; no guessing by feel. Following her finger, he found a faint letter at a corner.

“There.”

She nodded without laughing at his ignorance. For a moment they looked at the same small mark, neither of them an expert on it merely because they had survived other, much harder things. He could ask where it was. She could show him something she had learned that morning.

“It took me a while too.”

She returned the scraps to her bag instead of making the table a classroom. They didn't certify lifelong independence. They represented something she'd spent two hours learning. Tired, with aching hands, she still wanted another opportunity, provided time and money were clear again.

When lunch arrived, she ate congee outside while he and Zhou had their own food. Voices passed between the rooms. No ceremonial celebration was required for this first payment. The congee was thicker than expected; Zhou suggested warm water. She tried it and found the consistency better.

Before leaving for tea, Zhou checked the supports and water with them. Xiao Lu accepted the help she could actually provide, not an improvised transfer into the courtyard. Lin Mo didn't require an outing merely because the mood had improved. He waved with his left hand and reminded Zhou to take the tea.

The door closed, leaving the siblings together.

Leaning back, she asked whether the image remained. A little, he said, though writing it might have made it seem clearer. He offered the current note without inserting it among the old diary pages.

She remembered clothes pegs at home, but couldn't identify this one. He accepted that without pressing her to try harder. Returning the paper, she said they could still talk about it another time.

“Read the old notebook today?” he asked.

After considering, she chose the story instead. She'd spent the morning looking at paper corners. She wanted to hear something that didn't require sorting.

He smiled and asked her to bring it nearer. She secured her own scraps first, then found the marker at his morning's place. Start there? She wanted the missed half-page too.

So he turned back half a page. She poured water and chose a seat where she could hear without blocking the table. He waited until her cup was secure before beginning with the traveler beneath the eaves again.

The person returning didn't have to catch every step he'd taken alone. He could tell her where his morning had reached, then read slowly from the place she wanted to hear.
