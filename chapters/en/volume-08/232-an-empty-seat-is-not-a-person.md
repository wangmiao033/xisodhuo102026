# Chapter 232: An Empty Seat Is Not a Person

Qin Yue did not hand his phone to the man holding the sign.

Lin Mo heard him ask the man to lower it. Then the camera dropped. A low stool appeared by the door. Qin Yue leaned the phone against the wall behind it, adjusting it twice until the inner doorway and the nearest row of seats remained in view.

“This is as close as I can get.”

The man in gray trousers was still bent over. His surname was Shao, he said. His fingers were going numb. Could someone find scissors?

Ms. Luo supported his forearm without pulling. Her staff badge swung at the picture's upper edge.

“Don't cut it yet,” Lin Mo said. “I can't see how it's holding you.”

“Then get a better picture!”

Qin Yue explained that this was a private call, not a livestream. The camera showed his hand and the seat.

“The hand's fine. Not my face.”

Qin Yue kept the camera low and crouched to describe what he could see. One end of the strip had lifted; the other lay flat against the seatback. The indentation in Shao's skin aligned with the seat number.

Shao abruptly seized the loose corner with his free hand.

Paper tore. The half bearing the seven fell to the floor. He screamed as the remaining scrap curled upward, pressing his hand lower against the chair.

Luo shifted her support. Lin Mo told them to stop, then realized how vague that sounded. He had not meant Shao should stop bracing himself with his legs. He asked Qin Yue to make that clear.

Qin Yue retrieved the torn piece by its edge, without pressing it against Shao's skin.

Shao leaned after it. Luo warned him that she was bracing her elbow against the chair; she couldn't withstand another sudden pull. He stopped, apologized, and asked to call home.

A woman waiting outside helped retrieve his phone. Shao gave her the number without reciting it toward the video. Lin Mo heard him tell his wife he was at Minghui, his hand was hurt, and medical help had been called. He paused to listen, then said he wasn't alone. People were helping him. After asking her not to bring their child, he had the woman end the call and leave his phone on the chair edge where he could see it.

“Tearing it made it tighter,” Shao said.

Lin Mo studied Shao's shoes. He was off the cushion. The folding seat had sprung up, yet the paper held his hand. An empty seat had not released its occupant.

A quiet chime came through the loudspeaker.

[Do not omit a registered seat.]

This time the words appeared low enough on the screen to read. Lin Mo asked Qin Yue to preserve the torn piece and not replace it with a new ticket.

“What did you do when you arrived?”

“Signed my name. Took a ticket. Like everyone else.”

“Did they give you a second one?”

“I came alone. Why would I need two?”

Luo said the tickets came in two parts. She turned toward the box by the door; deprived of support, Shao cried out. She returned immediately.

Qin Yue asked where to find it. Following her directions, he brought the entire box with its clip from the lowest shelf of a small cabinet, keeping it apart from loose tickets on the table. The shallow transparent box appeared beside the stool.

Lin Mo tried to angle his head for a better view. His left shoulder burned; the lettering blurred.

“Is the phone too low?” Xiao Lu asked.

“My shoulder hurts. I need to lean back.”

She moved the stand left, waiting for his hand to settle before turning the screen. She didn't pull his body into position.

“Please hurry,” Shao gasped into the brief silence.

“I'm listening. Qin Yue, read it to me.”

White and pale yellow strips filled the box, their long edges perforated. Luo explained that the organizers had supplied joined pairs. Visitors took the white ticket; the yellow counterpart stayed behind. Someone had told her to put those into the replacement-seat clip so vacant places could be reconciled automatically.

“Who?”

“The one inside with the black lanyard. I don't know his name.”

Was that the man with the sign? She hadn't seen who held it. Lin Mo asked her to check the printing on the yellow slips instead of deciding they must be the same person.

With her hands occupied, Qin Yue turned them over.

[Next Seat]

He found Row Three, Seat Seven, matching the torn white piece. A fresh red diagonal crossed small lettering that said “replacement pending.”

At Lin Mo's request he read the fields below. Shao's surname appeared on the current-session side, then again in the continuation field on the back. A later session that day had been entered, with the same seat number.

Shao had signed only once, at the entrance. Nobody had mentioned a later session.

Luo explained that matching numbers on paired tickets were normal; they identified which halves belonged together. The continuation name fields had been blank when she received them. Qin Yue moved the box nearer the camera and turned up the yellow slip, indicating the continuation field with his thumbnail. Lin Mo could make out Shao's surname and a time written across the printed line, in a thicker hand than on the front.

“They've signed me up for that too?” Shao asked.

“I didn't make the red mark,” Luo said.

Qin Yue put the torn white piece on the outer chair edge, where Shao could see it. The other half remained attached to the seatback. Nobody pulled it. The marked yellow counterpart lay in the box, away from his hand. Shao asked again why his name was on it.

“I never said I'd attend another session.”

“We've kept your place. There's no need to register separately,” someone said behind the inner door.

Only black shoes appeared in the picture. A hand reached for the ticket box; Qin Yue blocked it and asked what the place was being kept for.

“Seats have to continue. Otherwise the system thinks someone's absent.”

“He's injured,” Luo said.

“He can leave when this round ends.”

“When?”

There was no reply. The shoes withdrew. Paper shuffled rapidly beyond the frame. Unable to tell what was being handled, Lin Mo asked Qin Yue to keep watch over the box in front of them.

“Isn't there another seven?” Xiao Lu pointed.

A second pale yellow slip lay beneath the thick clip. Qin Yue loosened it and withdrew the unnamed ticket. Same seat. Luo identified it as an unused counterpart from an earlier batch. They had replaced that batch before opening; two short strokes at the lower left marked the old tickets as void.

A joined pair for Seat Nine lay at the bottom, also marked void. Its reverse already carried “Next Seat,” but no name or current registration mark.

Lin Mo asked Qin Yue to move that discarded pair to a clear surface, checking whether the clip merely held it or the paper itself had stuck. The pair came free without resistance and settled on the stool. The empty folding seat beside them didn't move. Qin Yue did not put either slip against a person.

The result gave them enough to continue separating paper. Nothing more.

“Don't throw the live one away,” Luo said.

She shuddered at her own description of a named ticket.

“Keep everything,” Lin Mo said. “Keep his white ticket and its counterpart visible. Separate anything that hasn't been used.”

Qin Yue asked whether they should change Shao's seat number.

“No. And don't have someone sit there to try it.”

An empty seat couldn't take his place.

Xiao Lu offered Lin Mo his cup. A swallow did little for his tight throat. Reclaim your past self: the advertisement's promise returned, like something lost long ago being held up at a distance. He knew it was only a promise. For an instant he still wanted to ask what they intended to give back.

Shao groaned. Lin Mo put down the cup.

Following Luo's directions, Qin Yue moved the void tickets and unused counterparts to the other end of the box, separating them with its existing divider. Shao's named yellow slip stayed in the clip. As the thick bundle loosened, words flickered on the screen.

[Row Three, Seat Seven: Replacement pending.]

The indentation in his hand remained.

“Still hurts.”

Qin Yue's breathing had grown heavy. He asked what came next. Lin Mo first wanted to know whether he could still use the outer door.

Qin Yue stood, walked out of view, and called back from farther away. The exit was open. Two people were already outside, waiting for medical help.

“You can go too,” Lin Mo said.

Qin Yue returned and crouched into view.

“I know. She can't support him much longer. I'll take over.”

They explained the change to Shao and settled his arm before touching the tickets. With a hand free, Luo lifted the yellow slip to examine it, leaving it secured in the clip.

“This man isn't taking a replacement seat,” she called inside. “Stop putting him down for the next one.”

Someone said the records wouldn't match.

Under the red mark was a small serial number matching the white ticket's. Qin Yue pointed to the discarded slip already separated at the far end of the box. Its seat number matched, but its serial didn't. Luo had sorted by seat alone, accidentally mixing the batches.

Along the yellow slip's bottom edge, she found a sticker covering print. She lifted only an unfastened corner. Qin Yue read the exposed words: Cancellation of a continuation booking required the current-session stub to be checked against the original register. Adhesive concealed the rest. Lin Mo told him not to tear it.

“Where's the register?” Luo called.

Nobody produced it. Shao asked whether he had to sign again.

“Don't sign anything we haven't read. We can stop this ticket being used to fill another place. The later entry still needs checking against the original.”

Luo laid the sticker back down. She didn't substitute fresh paper for the named slip.

Lin Mo asked her to leave the discarded ticket where it was. Removing that couldn't withdraw Shao himself; nor should she erase the named ticket. She took only Shao's yellow counterpart from the replacement clip and laid it beside the torn white piece, both serial numbers visible.

“His current session is still here,” she called. “Come and check it. Don't add another.”

The pending notice disappeared.

[Current registration retained.]

Shao moved his hand.

It lifted a few millimeters from the chair edge, then a finger's width. Qin Yue did not haul him upright. Luo asked whether the pain had worsened. Shao's collar shifted into view as he shook his head.

“I can move it.” His voice shook.

Lin Mo asked them to leave the papers in place. Shao slowly drew back his hand. Red marks remained, his fingers still reluctant to bend. With Qin Yue supporting his forearm, he edged into the aisle but went no farther.

Luo called for medical help to see him first. Farther back, someone else asked to leave. Another pair of shoes turned toward the aisle, then stopped. Lin Mo did not tell them to tear their tickets or announce that everyone could go.

Shao tried slipping his hand into a pocket, withdrawing it as soon as his fingers touched the fabric.

“Am I all right now?”

“It's swollen. Let the medical people check.”

Qin Yue pointed out the retained-registration notice. Shao read it, his voice unsteady, and asked them to preserve his original ticket. They mustn't use his seat number to agree to anything else for him.

The projection turned white.

[Next: Complete the missing attendance.]

Metal struck metal in the lobby. At the edge of the picture, a hand pulled the moving leaf of the outer door toward its frame.

Qin Yue stood and shouted. The hand stopped on the handle.

Lin Mo watched the separated tickets. The old equipment remained sealed at the photography shop. Here, in another building, someone was insisting on putting departing people back into their places.

He told Qin Yue to keep the accessible passage open and not follow anyone farther inside. Then he turned to Xiao Lu.

“Has Old Zhou called back?”
