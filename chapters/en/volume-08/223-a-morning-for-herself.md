# Chapter 223: A Morning for Herself

In the morning Zhou draped damp socks over a chair and regretted washing them the night before.

Lin Mo, eating congee, asked whether he needed to go out. He'd meant to buy a few things, Zhou said, though not necessarily first thing. Xiao Lu stood outside with her notebook. At not necessarily, she immediately offered to ask at the store another day.

“I haven't said I'm changing my plans.”

She stopped.

Zhou spread the socks near the window. Tell them how long she needed first. Nobody should have to guess, then compete to be least urgent. She only meant to ask about the work. Walking and talking ought to take under an hour. Any trial would need arranging separately.

A small circle had appeared beside a time on last night's page. She seemed to have prepared her questions already, yet stood ready to close the book again.

“I can go after half past nine,” Zhou said. “Can you be back before nine?”

For questions, yes. If they asked her to start immediately, she'd come back to discuss the timing rather than keep him waiting. They could arrange a trial after his return, he said. Everything needn't be settled today.

Lin Mo asked about his usual morning transfer. Zhou would help first, finish breakfast, and check again before leaving. Listening, Xiao Lu put the notebook in her bag.

Her new hair tie was loose after two turns, too tight for a third. Twice she tried before starting again. Watching her wrestle with it, Lin Mo wondered whether last night's admission that he wanted her here had become another reason she must stay.

He didn't pursue her with further explanations.

She fetched her water bottle, then couldn't find its lid. All three looked. Beside a food box. She washed and tightened it, turned the bottle upside down to test for leaks, then packed it.

At his bedside she asked whether he needed anything bought.

“No.” He glanced at the tissues. “Those are nearly finished, but Zhou's getting them. No detour needed.”

She nodded without leaving.

They both knew this was where come back soon could fit. Her hand wound around the bag strap. He said a message would do if the conversation ran longer. He might not see it immediately; urgent matters should go to Zhou by phone.

“Have I just scheduled every minute for you?”

“No. I need to let you know the timing anyway.”

“Then go.”

This time the words didn't seem to send her away. At least her feet didn't hesitate. She checked for yesterday's cable, found the maintenance equipment gone, and went downstairs normally.

Zhou left a gap in the door for air.

With one person absent, the room sounded different. A plastic bucket scraped downstairs. Lin Mo could hear his own breathing. He reached for his phone, found no message and put it down.

“She just left.”

“I know.”

Too sharp. He explained that he wasn't checking her location; he wanted something to do. Zhou offered the book.

Lin Mo wanted to read. He was also afraid something might return while she was absent, leaving nobody to confirm it.

The thought stopped him. After hearing her last night, he'd still hoped to reserve a morning for himself, keeping his sister beside any fragment of the past that happened to surface. Rather than turn this discovery into another punishment, he supported the book and looked above the marker.

“Can't find the place?” Zhou asked over the washing-up.

“Found it. I read this bit slowly before.”

He read aloud, quietly. A traveler asked directions at a fork and received conflicting answers. He tried to follow who said what instead of making every crossroads a symbol of himself.

Zhou asked whether the man had gone back again.

“Not yet.” Lin Mo checked two lines. “He's only saying he might.”

Zhou laughed at having missed it. Lin Mo repeated the sentence without testing whether he'd listened properly. At the page's end, his throat was dry. He stopped for water.

The phone lit up.

Xiao Lu's name. His finger moved too quickly to open the message. Zhou brought it nearer. The clerk was still busy; could she wait ten minutes?

Lin Mo checked the time rather than immediately approving. Had Zhou's half-past-nine plan changed? No. He replied that the earlier arrangement held; if she couldn't get back before nine, let them know. No questions about who else was in the shop or where she stood. He could imagine asking them, could even imagine calling the questions ordinary concern. They would not tell him whether the conversation was useful to her. The one answer they needed now was about time, and she had supplied it without being pursued. He left the reply at that, although leaving it alone still required effort.

After sending, he still wanted to look again.

Zhou offered the remaining congee. Yes, please. Lin Mo moved the phone far enough away not to press on the book, close enough to reach if it rang. Turning it off entirely wouldn't prove trust.

Later, running feet shook the door. Lin Mo looked up, heart tightening, then heard the neighboring child call for his father. He neither sent Zhou outside nor phoned Xiao Lu. The tension stayed for a while, passing down with the rest of his breakfast.

She returned a little before nine.

Calling from the entrance, she put her shoes away before approaching. He didn't ask why she'd sent no second message. She didn't list every storefront she'd passed. She took out a sheet of paper: the store needed sample books sorted every few days, beginning with a two-hour trial.

“Paid after?” Zhou asked.

“The trial is paid too, by the hour. I asked him to write it down.”

The clerk had written a duration and amount. Her notes beside them recorded how payment would work if she stopped early. Lin Mo didn't read out the number and decide whether it was worthwhile. Did the work match her understanding—sorting samples, not operating equipment?

“No paper cutter. Stapled sheets go separately. I'm not supposed to pull staples out with my fingers.”

She'd asked about gloves. They had some; she could inspect them later that day. There was no commitment to regular work, just one trial to discover whether it suited.

Paper cutter sent a pull through his wrist. He looked down.

Seeing him, she quickly said this meant ordinary sample books, not Qin Yue's machine.

“I know.”

She nevertheless drew the sheet a little closer to herself.

Every pain might look like a veto. He couldn't pretend the words hadn't affected him to make her feel free to go. He separated the two: hearing them recalled the photographer's shop. It told him nothing definite about this one. He hadn't visited. He could neither certify it safe nor declare that something would happen.

“Do you want to look yourself first?”

Instinct said yes. He could inspect a door, equipment, the person talking. Things he knew how to do, instead of lying here hearing other people's plans. But transferring, traveling and entering an unfamiliar shop couldn't be erased by calling it a quick look.

“I want to. That doesn't mean you need my inspection before you can try.”

Zhou took down his shopping bag. Nearly time. Lin Mo realized how long they'd spent around the paper. Please put it away for now. Zhou's return time should remain as planned, not shift whenever anxiety appeared.

Before leaving, Zhou checked the water and supports. Xiao Lu listened for where help might be needed instead of competing to assume every task. After the door closed she looked between the paper and Lin Mo.

“What do you want to say?”

He considered. Was this the clerk who'd printed their notice?

“Yes. Being the same person doesn't mean we know everything about him.”

Lin Mo smiled. She needn't anticipate that objection for him. He'd only wanted to place the person.

She smiled too, loosening her fingers from the page. The sheet still contained unanswered questions. They could leave those unanswered for a few minutes while Zhou did his shopping. Lin Mo looked at the small amount of space between her hand and the paper, then returned his attention to what she was actually telling him.

They replaced the book with the simple description she'd brought. Could he read it? Yes, without viewing the other notebook pages. One line was vague: afternoon, no exact hour. She admitted she'd forgotten to ask and would check when the clerk was free.

Turning the sheet over revealed last year's price list on the back. While waiting, she'd watched somebody arrive with missing pages for printing. The clerk hadn't guessed what belonged there; he'd asked for the original file. Sorting paper had sounded simple until she'd noticed the difference between discardable piles and customers' uncollected work.

“Still want to try?”

“Yes. Let me understand it first.”

He heard first without translating it as reluctance. Her nail lifted a trace of old adhesive. She stopped before damaging the sheet she needed to keep. Tucking it outside her notebook separated it from her personal writing.

He'd been searching for a sufficiently solemn permission to give her. These two hours also required smaller judgments: whose paper, which appointment. They weren't wholly a statement about her brother.

“Shall I call for you?”

“No. I'll ask.”

“All right.”

He didn't offer his phone as a demonstration of support. Hers was in her bag. She sent a message, set the screen face down and asked where he'd reached in the book.

He showed her the marker.

“Only half a page?”

“Zhou mixed the characters up. Explaining took a while.”

“Which of you wasn't reading properly?”

“Possibly both.”

He read the passage again. Xiao Lu guessed the next line incorrectly at the question about directions. He let her discover the answer instead of correcting her too early. Her oh made the little stretch of morning without her into an ordinary thing he could tell her afterwards.

The response arrived at the end of the page. She read it: the trial could be the morning after next. She didn't press send immediately.

“What else do you need?”

“To check those two hours with Zhou when he gets back.”

Lin Mo nodded. The urge to say go on, go hadn't won the race this time. There was a real arrangement to make, not a test he could pass by sounding generous. Zhou had shopping of his own to finish. Xiao Lu had a question of her own to ask. Lin Mo could remain part of the practical answer without becoming the person who issued permission.

A fruit seller called outside. Should they buy sweeter oranges next time? Xiao Lu asked.

“Finish these first. Don't throw the sour ones away because I said something yesterday.”

She lifted a remaining segment, tasted it and pulled a face.

“This one really is sour.”

He laughed until his chest tightened, then stopped. She noticed without extinguishing her own smile. They waited for the discomfort to pass before dividing the last segment.
