# Chapter 226: She Said No Herself

After breakfast, Xiao Lu changed her shoes by the door.

She took her phone and a blank sheet. No diary, no altered photograph. Her notebook wouldn't fit in her coat, so she left it where it was and asked Zhou whether lunch remained as arranged.

It did.

Remembering Xu's appointment at the printing counter, Lin Mo asked whether she wanted a bag. For one sheet? If Xu offered a stack, she needn't accept all of it. Lin Mo stopped looking for a bag.

She expected to be brief, then would check the shop's trial-work equipment and procedure. She might come back early if the conversation went nowhere.

“I'll be here,” he said.

Her hand paused on the handle. He added that he had no outing planned, and Zhou would be here. He wasn't requiring her return at a particular moment to keep him in place. She nodded and left, her soles making two short sounds over the threshold.

For a long time he listened.

Other feet on the stairs were indistinguishable from hers. His phone held no new message. Zhou asked about narrowing the open window; the breeze kept lifting paper corners. Yes, Lin Mo said, realizing he'd been staring at a dark screen.

He didn't send, Have you arrived?

The sound remained on, the phone within reach. When Zhou considered going downstairs for something, Lin Mo reminded him of their arrangement. Zhou tapped his forehead. New clothes pegs could wait until afternoon. He set the broken peg by the door. Lin Mo eased a little. Demonstrating trust didn't require dismissing the assistance he actually needed.

This wasn't an examination passed by resisting a call. He still wanted to know where she was, still feared a change of meeting place. But she'd said she wouldn't enter Xu's car. The shop was nearby; Zhou knew it too. Repeating everything couldn't manufacture another layer of protection.

Zhou returned from taking the bowls away with a tangled charging cable. It had caught under a cup. Watching him coil it, Lin Mo suddenly felt a remembered tug: someone winding a wire into too small a circle, his own impatient hand pulling it free.

Only the force in his hand. No face.

“Wait.”

Zhou stopped. Lin Mo told him to continue. Nothing wrong with the cable; something had occurred to him. He immediately wanted to tell Xiao Lu. His finger reached her name and paused. The urgency resembled an invitation that might finally justify bringing her back.

He set the phone down and asked for today's paper.

Writing left-handed soon tired his shoulder. He recorded the coil and his apparent tug, adding neither person nor location. A question mark remained beside them. Zhou's present charging cable hadn't become an old object merely because a fragment arose beside it.

Afterwards, the pen box was on the far side of the table.

Lin Mo reached, felt his shoulder pull and stopped. Leaving a pen there was harmless, yet he hated having to call for this too. Xiao Lu had been gone barely minutes; already he had a succession of little needs, as if collecting evidence that she shouldn't have left.

He laid the pen across the page.

Zhou came for water and asked why his hand wasn't resting comfortably. The pen. Zhou moved the box, without asking why he hadn't called earlier or declaring things difficult in her absence.

“Could it stay here?”

That blocked the water. They tried another position, settling near the wall. Not within effortless reach, but a capped pen could rest on the small table until the next change of position. Lin Mo tried replacing the cap. His fingers fumbled; the pen fell on his lap. Zhou waited for him to ask before retrieving it.

Clumsy minutes. No astonishing old skill returned, no military experience made his left hand deft. But the water remained accessible and the pen needn't roll away each time. They could improve something without Xiao Lu witnessing it.

Noodles or rice for lunch? Zhou asked. Noodles, Lin Mo decided, then left her choice for her return. He selected one of two accompaniments instead of waiting for her to guess.

Only afterwards did he realize he'd stopped counting footsteps outside. Fear hadn't disappeared; a pen and lunch had occupied part of the morning. He moved the cable note away from possible soup splashes. Her departure hadn't taken away his right to have experiences of his own.

As he prepared to read again, Qin Yue called.

Had the man made contact? He was good at this sort of content. Perhaps their experiences could help people. Looking at his question mark, Lin Mo asked exactly what Qin Yue had told him.

Qin Yue slowed. Only that they'd had difficulties, that the brother was injured and the sister stayed with him. No hospital details. Lin Mo couldn't verify completeness over the telephone. He stated the boundary: ask before introducing people; don't pass numbers around or promise appearances.

“I didn't promise.”

“Then don't describe us as willing to talk.”

Qin Yue agreed, then asked whether the observation statement could remain in the shop's records. For its original purpose, Lin Mo said, not renamed a safety conclusion. He finished without mentioning where Xiao Lu was.

Zhou had heard enough to ask whether another trip to the photography shop was coming.

“No.”

Too quick. Lin Mo explained that no new site visit had been requested or accepted. The refusal didn't erase the shop. Zheng Lan and the isolated equipment still had unresolved matters. This call supplied no new progress on either.

A vegetable seller called beneath the window.

By the third line of his book, Lin Mo was back at the first. Outside, Zhou struggled to thread a needle for a button. More light? His eyes were the trouble, Zhou grumbled, finally magnifying the task with his phone.

“Were you waiting for me to suggest something?” Lin Mo asked.

“For a button?”

Lin Mo laughed. He'd been hoping someone would need him, preferably for something with an immediate visible result. His sister had gone alone; Zhou could even solve the needle. The vacant place first felt like a floor being withdrawn, not freedom.

He didn't make that feeling into a message for her.

He had no wish to turn the morning into evidence of how little she needed him now. Her ability to ask questions at a counter did not erase what had happened between them. Nor did his inability to follow her mean that every useful part of him had been lost. He could see how readily both thoughts offered themselves, each making a much larger story out of one absence than the absence could bear.

Zhou went on with the button. The thread caught once, and he loosened it rather than pull until it snapped. Lin Mo watched without suggesting a better method. The ordinary delay was strangely difficult to permit. Nobody was asking for an answer, nobody was failing to notice a danger he alone understood. A man was mending his clothes while another man waited for his sister. That was all the room required of them for the moment.

When she returned, the button was sewn. She carried two folded sheets and dust on her shoes. She washed her hands, drank half a cup and only then did Lin Mo let himself ask.

“I didn't agree to be filmed.”

Relief came embarrassingly fast. She saw it and paused. He explained that worry didn't mean only refusal counted as the right answer. She set the sheets down. They could discuss that later; first she'd tell what she wanted to tell.

According to her, Xu had come without aiming equipment at her. Asked about filming only her, he returned to the brother-and-sister image: viewers needed a relationship immediately understandable. Without reenacting reunion? At least an entrance shot, he'd insisted. Otherwise the piece didn't work.

“I said, then it doesn't work.”

Her level voice didn't match the white pressure of her fingers on the cup. She didn't perform perfect composure for Lin Mo. Xu had continued persuading her. She'd stood and moved to the counter to ask about her own work rather than sit through more.

“Did he leave?”

“He wasn't there when I came out. I didn't follow him.”

That couldn't promise no future contact. It didn't require her to pursue a man she'd refused either. She would consider any later message on its merits; a meeting didn't mean owing him another.

Lin Mo asked about the papers. One was Xu's prepared outline. She'd taken it because several statements were wrong. The other was a sorting sample, unrelated. He asked her to separate them so he wouldn't confuse them. She slipped the sample into her own notebook.

“Tomorrow's two hours are unchanged. Sample books and reusable backing paper. No machinery. A trial, not a promise of a permanent place.”

“Paid?”

“The two hours written down yesterday. I checked again.”

What if the task changed on arrival? She'd ask. Stapled material stayed with the shop assistant; she wouldn't pull staples bare-handed to prove competence. Refusing one offer today didn't require demonstrating moral purity through another tomorrow.

“You don't actually need me to teach you,” Lin Mo said.

She didn't rescue the statement. He heard how easily it could request reassurance that she did need him. He completed the thought.

“I feel a little left out. You came home with it settled, and I realized I'd been waiting to protect you once more.”

She sat for a long silence.

“You can listen.”

“Yes.”

“But don't make every time I go out into a failure of your protection.”

He looked at the leg support. No new strength had appeared there. He couldn't accompany her today, and couldn't dress that limitation as a grand act of letting go. He remained worried, he said. He was learning not to assign that worry to her.

She turned the outline so he could read it, without offering her decision for approval.

A large heading led into an opening: after suffering, two people had chosen to face life anew. The second paragraph said they'd jointly made the hardest decision of their lives. A blank followed for details to be supplied during the interview. No quotations, no interview date, yet jointly was already installed.

Lin Mo suddenly wanted to stop reading.

“I said that wasn't right,” Xiao Lu told him. “He said it's a standard opening. It could be changed afterwards.”

“Afterwards?”

“I brought it back because I don't want it becoming something we've supposedly approved.”

He held the corner with his left finger instead of tearing it. Thin paper trembled beneath him, from breeze or hand. Nothing printed there proved the past. But those words had found precisely the place he'd been walking around.

He asked her not to fetch a pen yet.

“Let me finish reading this sentence first.”
