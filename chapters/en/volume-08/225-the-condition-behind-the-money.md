# Chapter 225: The Condition Behind the Money

Xiao Lu set the phone a hand's breadth from the water.

“I want to ask him myself.”

Lin Mo looked at the message arranging tonight's call. The man wanted to make a record of their experiences and offered payment. He used such light words: a chat, a little while, a few things to say. Asked where the finished film would appear, he'd said they could discuss it.

“You want to do it?”

“I want to know what he's buying.”

He nearly said there was no need. She heard the beginning and waited with her finger above the number. He changed it: he didn't want a stranger filming his recent experiences. This time he was speaking for himself.

Zhou was dismantling a damaged cardboard box outside. Should he shut the door? No, Xiao Lu said, but please don't join the conversation yet. She didn't want everyone in the room mistaken for willing participants. He sat farther away with his scissors.

Only when the call connected did Lin Mo notice his leg tightening. He asked Zhou to straighten the rolled cloth beside the support without pushing against his right foot. Xiao Lu waited, then introduced herself as the sister mentioned earlier. Xu greeted her warmly. He was preparing questions; perfect timing.

She explained that her brother was listening. Tonight was about terms, not recording their story or answering interview questions.

“We don't make that sort of sensational material,” Xu said.

Lin Mo wanted to ask whom he represented. She asked first. Xu made short films for various accounts. Which ones? That depended on who wanted the result. Having material made it easier to negotiate.

“So you can't tell me where people would see it?”

“There are plenty of platforms. Reaching people is good.”

She glanced at Lin Mo without inviting him to take over, then asked whether Xu would film her alone, speaking only about herself. A pause. A single person would have less impact. Their appeal was the two of them finding each other again.

Lin Mo's fingers curled beneath the table.

Xu hadn't mentioned the Theater or deleted memories. Qin Yue had described injured brother and accompanying sister, people who'd endured something difficult. A few rough facts were enough to make a saleable shape.

Xiao Lu asked how much.

After promising to treat them fairly, Xu named a figure. It exceeded Lin Mo's observation fee. He didn't check his balance, but hospital journeys, meals and rent all crowded into his mind.

She asked what the amount covered.

“Half a day. Two cameras. Nothing complicated. You enter, see him, call him your brother. Then sit and explain how you got through everything.”

“I came through the door today already.”

“We aren't making security footage. We need the relationship to be clear. Just act naturally. Real emotion.”

Lin Mo looked up. Xu wanted his face visible and the wheelchair in good light. Xiao Lu asked whether he wanted the injuries shown. Not necessarily. They could roll sleeves up as far as comfortable and cut unsuitable material afterwards.

“He hasn't agreed.”

“Let him hear the proposal.”

“I've heard,” Lin Mo said. “No filming my injuries. No acting out a reunion.”

Xu immediately offered adjustments. No need for a final refusal yet. Lin Mo stopped; he wouldn't turn his sister's call into his own argument. She asked whether the price remained the same. Xu said it would need recalculating.

“So that's what the money is for.”

A little laugh. Stories needed images. Who would watch someone talking at a wall? Downstairs a door slammed. Lin Mo drew back against his support. Xiao Lu didn't step in front of him; she asked whether further filming would be required.

Usually a couple of extra shots, Xu said. Until it worked.

“And if I don't want it released afterwards?”

“We can't spend time and money for nothing. Decide beforehand.”

She stopped asking about the figure. No agreement tonight. If he still wanted to explain, they could meet tomorrow at the printing counter in the nearby general store. Not their home, and no filming. She wanted to confirm gloves and the paper-sorting procedure for her trial work anyway. Xu would be passing in the morning. Ten minutes, with a page of proposed questions.

When she hung up, Lin Mo couldn't hide his surprise.

“Why meet him?”

“He kept interrupting. I'd like to be clear in person.”

“You know the conditions now.”

“I can refuse in person too. Meeting doesn't mean I owe him a story.”

He worried about Xu cornering her alone. She darkened the screen and asked whether he feared her being deceived, or feared that hearing the money might leave her interested. He couldn't separate them enough to choose the more flattering answer.

“Both.”

“Asking the price doesn't mean I'm selling your injuries.”

He hadn't said that. But he'd already written an ending for her question. Money might make her yield. Worse, he'd felt its pull himself, and wanted the door closed quickly.

“I calculated too,” he admitted.

She looked up.

“What it would pay for. In my head. You weren't the only one.”

She moved the phone for Zhou's bowls of soup. He didn't ask whether they'd secured a deal. His own bowl was hot; theirs needed a moment. Lin Mo touched its side. The sum represented actual journeys, actual bills, not abstract greed.

Xiao Lu didn't want considering earnings to feel like wrongdoing against him. Her paid trial at the shop was arranged; it didn't promise lasting work. Xu had at least offered money openly. She wanted the whole offer, rather than someone deciding she needn't even ask.

After a sip, she asked whether selling a voice alone, without a face, would be different.

Lin Mo thought of his present throat, of trying to lower it and retrieve something old. Xu only needed a voice to fit inside a film. Any pitch could be cut against another face. He still didn't want it. If she asked, she should establish who could hear it and what changes could be made.

“He didn't answer who.”

“No.”

“You kept watching me while I asked.”

She put down her spoon. Watching his expression made her anticipate his anger and miss Xu's words. She didn't want every discussion of money to involve checking whether her brother thought she was becoming a bad person.

He looked at his hand. What felt like worry inside him might be only a hardening face to her. She shouldn't have to translate every muscle into kindness.

“Next time I'll listen. Speak when something concerns me. Not stare while waiting for your answer.”

He needn't deliberately face the wall either, she said. He smiled ruefully. He could overdo things. She didn't join the smile; remember the unanswered questions, she said, rather than announce afterwards that he'd known all along.

Zhou snipped a raised cardboard corner. Lin Mo's attention followed the sound. For an instant, Xu's promise to cut things afterwards seemed capable of removing part of his own sleeve. The thought didn't make the scissors anomalous. He flattened his left hand and waited.

“I felt bad when he said he'd cut things,” he told her. “Not because you asked the price.”

She watched him, then drew her bowl closer.

“You could have said that sooner.”

He acknowledged it without asking her to apologize. She added soup and finally stopped studying his face over the rim.

“But he wants me to call you,” she said. “Then walk in and do it again.”

Lin Mo knew how often that word had been trapped and interrupted. He wouldn't attribute all her present disgust to trauma, as though an ordinary person couldn't resent this request. What did she want to do tomorrow?

Ask whether anyone else had received the proposed questions, then refuse the reenactment. If Xu persisted, leave. She wouldn't bring him home. She would sit near the shop counter, not enter his car or sign for Lin Mo.

It wasn't a certificate of safety. Lin Mo still wanted to go. The dull pain around his leg support reminded him what accompanying her meant: Zhou, transfers, transport. His fear couldn't require everyone to make that journey.

“Would you tell me how it goes?”

“Yes. Not repeat every sentence.”

“All right.”

She asked whether Zhou could stay tomorrow morning. He could; his own plans were in the afternoon. But could they decide breakfast before everybody became hungry? Xiao Lu wrote a reminder on packaging paper, without opening her private notebook.

Watching her, Lin Mo said he had no right to forbid her from describing her own experience. He wouldn't be filmed, and no one should speak for him. Those decisions needed separate space.

She murmured agreement without immediately forgiving his interruptions.

He had wanted money to be a simple thing: an honest task, an agreed sum, a bill paid without owing anyone more. Yet the offer had reached straight into the space between them. He could reject what Xu wanted from his body. He could not make that rejection into a test of whether his sister loved him properly. Nor could he pretend that asking a price had cost him nothing merely because she had been the one to say the question aloud.

Xiao Lu did not offer him a reassuring answer to any of this. She was still eating. Her spoon scraped lightly against the bottom of the bowl, and when Zhou moved a cup she waited for room before setting hers down. The evening had ordinary tasks left in it. They would have to finish those together without requiring their disagreement to be neatly finished first.

After dinner she cleared the table. He offered the empty box beside him. No need yet, she said, then reconsidered: that one would help, provided he didn't reach across the water.

He moved slowly enough for the bottom to scrape. She waited instead of snatching it. The light box still took several seconds. Handing it over, he wanted to promise he'd be able to do more when she returned tomorrow.

He didn't.

The phone lit with the meeting time and a line about the power of their story. Don't miss this opportunity. Xiao Lu showed Lin Mo the location, then confirmed that they would discuss terms only, without filming. She put her phone in her own pocket.

A horn sounded and receded. Downstairs the shutter descended, leaving a small lamp lit by the entrance.

Lin Mo asked Zhou to help change his position. Xiao Lu stood beside them, touching a cushion only when Zhou asked her to hold it. Lin Mo winced during the movement. When she asked where it hurt, he answered honestly instead of performing ease to prove that tomorrow could proceed.

Settled again, he watched her go to wash the cups. The breakfast reminder remained beside the door. Beneath it was no line saying her brother had approved.

He studied that empty space and slowly withdrew the extra warning he'd been about to add.
