# Chapter 228: The Words Left Unsaid

Towards evening, Lin Mo asked Zhou for the old notebook.

He wasn't trying to prove that his afternoon admission had been too harsh. Fragments kept rising: shoes by a door, a hand gripping something. He wanted to distinguish what he knew from reading, before waking in darkness and confusing it with experience.

Zhou placed the diary at a readable angle, leaving Old Chen's unopened private fold alone. Nearby, Xiao Lu trimmed an eraser, her work sample beneath her own notebook. How much would he read today?

“One short passage.”

“Want me to join you?”

She could listen if she wanted, without certifying each sentence. She gathered the shavings rather than immediately leaning over.

The page edges had grown fuzzy. His left fingertip caught a corner; he asked Zhou to lift it first, then held it down himself, leaving the right wrist secured. Beside an existing index mark lay a few lines not assigned to a particular location.

One said: Tell her when she wakes.

Nothing followed to explain what.

He studied the heavy strokes. They looked like an addition made in fear of forgetting, but that was a present impression of handwriting, not proof of old urgency. No room or alarm appeared on the page. It didn't say whether he'd spoken afterwards.

“Xiao Lu. Do you know what this refers to?”

She leaned close enough to read, then sat back. Not the particular occasion. He prepared to continue, but she put down the eraser.

“You said things like that quite often.”

He didn't ask whether he'd always been terrible. That would require her to comfort him before finishing her complaint. He moved his hand aside and waited.

When they were children and someone knocked, he'd tell her to sleep; tomorrow he'd explain. Later it became after a meal, after she finished something. Delays weren't all bad. She did get tired, did sometimes not want to hear. But some tomorrows arrived after everything had been settled. She received only the outcome.

No old room appeared to Lin Mo. He couldn't reliably arrange the periods she described. He heard his sister, sitting here now, describing what she remembered.

“I'm not proving this page. It reminded me of that.”

She recalled wanting a light moved because it didn't reach the corner of her table. Had she bought a desk lamp afterwards? He asked. No, don't hurry towards a similar ending. She'd wanted the existing light moved. He'd told her to wait for his return, fearing she'd fall while reaching high.

“Did I move it?”

“Yes. But you came back later than you said. I'd finished writing by then.”

She wasn't calling the late return deliberate. The light eventually stood where she wanted; he'd remembered. What angered her was not knowing how long to wait, working while listening for a key outside.

Lin Mo looked at their present lamp. A man's back on a stool seemed about to emerge. He stopped himself supplying colors. She'd mentioned reaching high; he'd supplied the stool. Neither page nor sister had described one. He explained the distinction, keeping his imagined picture from replacing her actual account.

She said he needn't report every success or failure at distinguishing memory. She knew it was difficult. He could listen to a story without turning it immediately into an examination.

There was impatience in her voice. He wanted to explain that he was taking her seriously, then saw her grip the eraser. Seriousness could occupy all someone else's speaking space too. He left his question mark beside the page and stopped asking what the lamp looked like.

She described dragging her chair into better light, her leg cramped against the table, discovering an impressed line on the paper when she'd finished. She'd told him the next day. He'd said she should have worked in the outer room. Lin Mo could almost predict the question about why she hadn't said so sooner.

She didn't supply it.

“I was waiting for us to move it together, as you said.”

The eraser had one crookedly shaved edge. He couldn't use the eventual repair to cancel her hours waiting in dim light. He acknowledged hearing her, without requesting a version better suited to proving her brother's love.

The difficulty lay precisely in how ordinary it sounded. A light moved late, work already finished, a sentence about what she should have done instead. There was no great villain for him to oppose in that room. There had only been a child waiting for the person who said he would return, and a brother whose useful answer arrived after the waiting was over.

Cooking smells drifted up from downstairs. Zhou partly closed the window and moved some clothes. After he passed, Xiao Lu said she'd heard plenty about waiting inside too. Wait for the right words, a vote, someone to recognize her. When her brother finally came, she'd thought at least they'd know together what the next wait was for.

Lin Mo's chest sank.

He remembered hot wood, not her face in that scene. Incompleteness made it easy to push difficult things back into not remembering. He almost said he wasn't the same person now.

“You're allowed not to remember,” she said first. “But things didn't only happen when you remember them.”

He closed his mouth.

A tear fell on her trousers. He reached for tissues; pain caught sharply in his left shoulder. Xiao Lu stood immediately, hand already moving towards his right-side cushion, so practiced that their conversation seemed never to have happened.

“Not there.”

She stopped.

“I reached too far. Left shoulder.”

She looked from tissues to face. He feared she'd think her words had caused it. He tried explaining, but hurried breathing sharpened the pain and broke the sentence.

Zhou asked whether it differed from previous pain. Lin Mo took time: it had caught during the reach, without a fall, impact or weight on his right leg. Pause with the usual supports; if it didn't ease or worsened, contact the doctor. He wouldn't pretend comfort just to keep arguing.

Zhou asked Xiao Lu to clear the table and helped adjust him in the familiar way. She lifted the diary, uncertain where to put it. Empty chair, Lin Mo said. Not with her notebook. She obeyed and remained beside the table.

He kept the sequence clear when he could speak again: he had reached, felt the pull and stopped. Her anger had not moved his arm for him. Nor did saying this remove the need to treat the pain seriously. He needed help now, and that need could be spoken without making it a judgment on what she had just dared to tell him.

The sharpness subsided somewhat, not instantly. Zhou should stay nearby. Lin Mo wouldn't try reaching again. Xiao Lu placed tissues on his left as though completing an interrupted duty.

“Those were for you.”

She stared without taking them.

He completed the broken explanation. “I hurt myself reaching. You're still allowed to be angry.”

More tears. She wiped them with a sleeve.

“When I see you like that, I can't say anything.”

He didn't instruct her to be strong or suggest leaving it all for another day. Another day resembled the diary too closely. Did she want a pause, or to finish before going out? He could listen, though he needed to lean back, not sit upright facing her throughout.

“Can you really hear me?”

“Yes. If pain stops me following, I'll say.”

She sat closer, then moved the chair back. He let her choose instead of requiring the best position for caring for him. Zhou sat outside with the door not fully closed.

She said her worst fear wasn't a single concealed decision. It was that more memory would restore his conviction that only he knew how to save people, while she remained someone placed after the outcome. She could be angry, he could explain. But if every explanation ended with her own good, refusing that good looked like ingratitude.

The closed diary held reasons for risks, other people's costs, much that wouldn't return intact. He couldn't retry every old decision. The words in front of him, however, couldn't remain a side issue.

“I was afraid you couldn't bear it.”

She gave a small laugh through tears.

“And once you'd done it, I could?”

He had no answer.

She didn't demand an immediate cleverer one. No more diary today. No questions about her own affairs for a while either. She wanted the outer room. Zhou was here; she wasn't abandoning Lin Mo alone.

He nodded, then heard himself apologize.

She stopped at the doorway. He didn't append a long indictment of himself or ask for reassurance. The brief apology remained without purchasing her return to the chair. She took her own notebook and went out.

The movement was small and final enough for this moment. He could hear her in the next room, but hearing was not permission to call her straight back. He let the apology stand without sending another sentence after her.

Zhou waited before checking the pain. Easier than before, still aching. Warm water arrived on the left. Simple dinner tonight, no further trips.

The cup's warmth brought Lin Mo back to his hand. He wanted to call himself useless, dependent even during an argument. Remembering how Xiao Lu had been silenced, he stopped. Condemning himself completely would again require someone to pick him up.

“Thank you. But don't tell her not to take it to heart.”

Zhou hadn't intended to. He didn't always know what to say either. Sitting outside and responding when called had been enough work.

Could he take tissues to her? There were tissues outside, Zhou said. Let her take them herself. Lin Mo recognized another attempt to arrange reconciliation without actually asking.

Cooking smells faded. Voices carried over bowls downstairs. He left the old diary closed. Tell her when she wakes still lacked context. He wouldn't add his preferred ending.

A cup sounded outside. Xiao Lu's footsteps paused, then continued to the basin. She hadn't returned to announce forgiveness or left the lodging. He asked Zhou to adjust the light so he could see his water. At dinner, they could ask whether she wanted to eat together.

When Zhou asked, she wanted to wash her face first. Lin Mo didn't hurry her, or keep dinner cooling while waiting for an emotional verdict. He ate what he could; her portion stayed covered.

She took her bowl in the outer room. Should the small table be moved? Zhou asked. Today she'd stay there. Lin Mo swallowed his mouthful. Through the partly open door he heard her eating, without calculating how much anger had therefore disappeared. Afterwards she washed her own bowl. Brief running water, then quiet under the light.

He didn't call her back. The quiet could remain as she wanted it.
