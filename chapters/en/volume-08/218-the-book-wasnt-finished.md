# Chapter 218: The Book Wasn't Finished

Halfway through dinner, the outer room went dark.

Xiao Lu stopped in the doorway, bowl in hand. Lin Mo heard Zhou tell her to stay still, then a chair scraping. His left hand had already closed around the armrest, but his right leg hadn't followed it downward. That hesitation kept his foot from taking his weight. It also let him hear voices outside and someone upstairs knocking on a wall, asking whose electricity had gone too.

“I'll get the torch,” Zhou said.

Lin Mo asked him to light Xiao Lu's feet first. They'd left a basin there; she shouldn't trip trying to find the table. Once the beam appeared, she stepped clear, set down her bowl, and said it seemed to be the whole building.

Seemed. Nobody hurried to turn that into reassurance.

Zhou went to the entrance and returned with the landlord's answer: the outside distribution equipment was being checked. No restoration time yet. Lin Mo asked him not to touch the switches inside. His phone gave enough light to see his food. Nor did he want ordinary electrical trouble to erase the reason his back and shoulders had tightened.

“I thought it was starting again.”

Xiao Lu sat down. What was?

“After the lights went out, somebody would call me.”

He couldn't say who. He hadn't actually heard a voice. The expectation had arrived first, like a strap fastened but not yet pulled tight. Watching the half-table lit by the torch, he felt he ought to hide his unfinished food. Otherwise someone would come and tell him he wasn't allowed possessions here.

Xiao Lu didn't clear his meal.

“Nobody's calling now. Are you keeping that to eat?”

He looked down and nodded. She resumed her own vegetables. Chopsticks touched porcelain softly. Zhou connected a power bank to the phone without shining the beam into his face.

Several minutes passed before he released the armrest.

He tried the chopsticks once, lost his grip on the rice, and returned to the spoon. The wood from that morning hadn't reappeared. His right wrist hadn't become able to grasp. This mouthful depended on the left hand he'd slowly learned to use. It needn't wait for the old one.

Outside was still dark when they finished.

Xiao Lu suggested skipping their reading. Torchlight tired the eyes. Lin Mo almost agreed, then noticed the book beside her knee, brought there in anticipation. Did she want to read herself? He wasn't asking whether she would keep him company.

“I want to finish this part.” She opened it at the paper marker. “We haven’t finished this bit.”

He couldn't remember every word of the preceding passage. He reread the lines above the marker to recover what the traveler was doing. Zhou helped arrange the light. They tried several angles, moving it when reflected glare reached Lin Mo's eyes. An empty food container finally raised it enough to light the page instead of his face.

“We stop when your eyes are tired,” Xiao Lu said.

“You already said that.”

“I can say it twice.”

He smiled and held the edge with his left finger. An unfamiliar character tripped him; she corrected his pronunciation. Thinking she'd read ahead, he asked. No, she only knew that word. She didn't know where the person in the story would go either.


Someone knocked softly twice.

A child from upstairs had brought a rechargeable desk lamp. Did they have a spare cable? Zhou searched with the door open. Seeing the wheelchair, the boy stopped speaking. Lin Mo heard him ask whether somebody's grandfather was inside, then saw half a face peering around the door.

“Not a grandfather,” Zhou said. “What connection does it take?”

The boy offered the lamp. Lin Mo didn't reach for it; Xiao Lu examined the base. Holding it to the light, she said their cable wouldn't fit. Don't force it. The boy insisted his father always managed. She held the two connectors together to show the difference in width.

“This one not fitting doesn't mean your lamp is broken. Ask whether you still have its own cable.”

Could he borrow their torch instead? Xiao Lu looked at Lin Mo before agreeing. He began to say take it, then the page grayed in the dark. He heard himself hesitate.

“We're using this one. Ask Zhou about his smaller one.”

Ten minutes, Zhou said. He'd need it afterward; have an adult return it. The boy ran off nodding. Xiao Lu sat again without congratulating Lin Mo for keeping something he needed.

“When I was little, I thought people could always fix things if they wanted to help.”

“Not now?”

“I still get impatient. This light makes me want to rub my eyes, for example.”

She didn't turn that into a lesson about how accommodating sisters should be. He noticed she'd tilted the book too far from herself so he could see. She moved her chair; he didn't strain his shoulder following the light. Zhou steadied the container underneath. Finding an angle that didn't require either reader to keep twisting took some time.

The small torch came back punctually, brought by the boy's father. Thanking them from the doorway, he said they'd found the original cable. Xiao Lu acknowledged him and returned to their traveler. He remained in the rain. The commotion outside hadn't carried him into the next chapter.

They continued together.

The character was making a slow detour around a wall fallen in the rain. Lin Mo reached a mention of eaves and stopped. He hadn't seen another room. It was more as though an unspoken complaint had found a place on his tongue: Why have you left your shoes here again?

Xiao Lu waited, thinking he couldn't see.

He didn't read the complaint aloud. There were no shoes on the page, no sister standing at a door. First he showed her the line he'd reached. The other words weren't printed here; they'd just occurred to him.

“Write them down?”

“Not yet. I want to see if I can finish this sentence.”

He did. His voice tightened between rain and shelter without becoming its former lower sound. Xiao Lu moved the marker to the next line. She didn't urge him to assemble the interruption into a story.

After another short passage, he lowered the book.

He recalled the sensation of a body passing through a doorway. The frame had been closer to his shoulder than it would be now. A shoe struck something; he looked down impatiently. It wasn't a picture detailed enough to describe colors. He couldn't even guarantee the doorway belonged to their home. But the entitlement in those words lay so close to the umbrella and clothes in the notebook that he wanted to put it somewhere he already knew.

“I might be mixing in things I've read.”

Xiao Lu neither agreed nor denied it.

“What did you want to say?”

He repeated the complaint about the shoes. Only afterward did he realize how much he feared hearing that he'd always been like that. It would give him corroboration while taking away the courage to ask whether anything else was possible.

Instead, she asked whether he wanted her recollection or only wanted her to listen.

After considering, he said she could tell him whether he'd said it. She needn't reconstruct a whole day.

“You did. More than once. Sometimes the shoes were yours.”

He stared at the light on the book, unable to answer.

She wasn't laughing at him. He almost laughed himself: how readily that former voice had blamed someone, without checking whose shoes lay there. Then his chest grew heavy. Would recovering himself mean recovering only the parts that had made other people unhappy?

He asked.

Xiao Lu pushed the marker farther between the pages so it wouldn't fall.

“Not only those.”

“You don't have to pick something good for me now.”

“I haven't picked anything.” She looked up. “Let me finish first.”

He closed his mouth. Zhou was putting bowls into the basin. Xiao Lu waited through two splashes before continuing.

She remembered many things about him. They weren't a list she could produce at any moment to decide whether somebody was good or bad. Tonight she'd wanted to finish the book. Asked suddenly for a verdict on the brother he used to be, she didn't know how to answer without having it heard as consolation.

“I'm not saying I won't talk. But every time you ask, you immediately tell me which answers you don't want.”

His finger stopped on the spine.

He'd thought he was giving her room. Don't select flattering memories. Don't add details to prove anything. Don't protect him from disappointment. One sentence after another could also enclose a shrinking space, leaving her afraid of answering wrongly whatever she said.

“How do you want to answer this time?”

She didn't use the opening to pour out years of accumulated grievances.

“Finish this part of the book first.”

He watched her briefly and nodded. Raising it where he could see, he resumed with the traveler setting off after the rain. On the second page he coughed. Reaching for water nearly disturbed the marker; Xiao Lu held it until he'd replaced his cup.

Then the lights returned.

Both narrowed their eyes. Zhou switched off the torch, then the harsh overhead light, leaving their usual smaller lamp. Somebody upstairs shouted with relief. A television started. Lin Mo waited several seconds. No further command came.

“The basin's still by the door,” Xiao Lu reminded them.

Zhou saw it and carried it away. He didn't declare the room permanently safe because its electricity worked. Would they like the torch left on the table? Yes, Lin Mo said, so they needn't search next time. Then he asked whether that would take Zhou's own nighttime light.

“I have a smaller one.”

“Then leave this here.”

When they reached the end of the passage, Xiao Lu closed the book first.

Lin Mo still wanted to ask about those shoes. The question reached his tongue; instead he asked her to mark their place. She marked where they'd finished rather than turning back. Tonight had advanced a little. A doorway unexpectedly remembered didn't require starting it again.

Zhou prepared his nighttime supports. During the transfer Xiao Lu watched the leg rest from the other side, moving it only when Zhou said she could. His right leg took no weight. His left shoulder pulled painfully; after settling he needed a long moment to unclench his teeth. The brief ease of reading hadn't spared him this.

Did he want the new sheet of paper? Xiao Lu asked.

Not brought over again. He'd make a note when awake tomorrow. Tonight he wanted to remember what she'd said rather than make her an on-call source of old answers. That sounded like another rule imposed on himself, so he didn't announce it. He only told her she could go to bed without waiting until he was entirely comfortable.

She gathered the book but stayed.

“I wasn't unhappy that you remembered something.”

“I know.”

She looked at him.

He corrected himself. “I might not have known then. I've heard you now.”

Only then did she carry the book out. At the doorway she nudged a slipper she'd left crooked. Its sole brushed the floor. She bent and straightened it herself, without naming the action after anything in their past.

He watched without asking her to repeat the walk.

Once in the night, headlights through the curtain woke him. First he thought of Zheng Lan's arm refusing to lower. Then he found his cup on the left. The water was cool, its handle still turned within reach. He drank and replaced it without touching his wrist brace.

The complaint about the shoes remained. He didn't know whether tomorrow would bring more. Nor did he want to supply an unseen coat while half asleep. Beyond the door, Zhou turned over. A while later Xiao Lu coughed softly. Lin Mo listened without calling anyone to guard that piece of his past.

When his breathing slowed, he tried saying his name in the dark.

It was still his current voice. This time he didn't try to lower it.
