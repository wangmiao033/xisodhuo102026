# Chapter 5: The Returns Window

The passage behind the cupboard barely held one person. Lin Mo edged sideways, catching the chair leg against a wall. He switched hands, felt the broken end touch his injured wrist, and switched back.

A few steps ahead, his old jacket slid through a square opening into a metal basket. His trousers followed, belt still threaded through the loops. He reached for them. Someone dragged the basket away.

“Once it's returned, leave it.”

A woman sat behind the opening, hair pulled tight, glasses on her nose, a gray apron over her clothes. Without looking up, she unfolded his trousers and checked the pockets.

He pointed. “Can I have the belt?”

“Came in with the trousers.”

“Rule three says I can keep personal effects.”

She looked up.

“Why didn't you take it off?”

“Didn't think of it.”

She paused halfway through folding them.

“Come back next time you remember something.”

“The belt.”

They looked at each other. His cheeks still twitched; the forced smile hadn't entirely loosened. He lowered the chair leg a little.

She unthreaded the belt, wound it twice, and tossed it into a tray beside the opening.

“Your sort's the worst.”

He caught it. “What sort?”

“People who read the rules.”

He pocketed the belt and produced the handover record.

“Then could you look at one more thing?”

Her gaze stopped near the blue stamp.

“Got the jacket?”

“Yes.”

“What's missing?”

“Don't know.”

“Find out first, then come back.”

She picked up a white shirt. He left the record in the tray.

“I want to know who sent it.”

She turned the shirt's left sleeve inside out and snipped a loose thread.

“This is the returns window.”

“Anything coming in must have a record.”

“Doesn't mean it's mine to show you.”

“That's your stamp.”

“I didn't stamp it.”

She put the scissors in a drawer and examined the blue collar showing above his gray jacket.

“You arrived wearing that?”

He didn't answer. Dust covered half the sign above her window. Clothing Recovery was still visible. No camera beside it, only a dim red light in the corner. He couldn't tell how long it had been on.

She lowered her voice.

“Should've asked before the round. They love it when you finish the whole process, then come chasing answers.”

“Why do you sit here?”

She dragged the basket toward herself.

“You keep shoving clothes in. Someone has to collect them.”

He held the tray in place.

“It's my sister's jacket.”

She stopped pulling.

“Pills hidden in the pocket lining. A patched sleeve. I know whose it is. Now the original holder's restricted information, but the notes know I've just hurt my wrist.”

He extended the injury through the opening, catching his breath as cloth touched skin. She frowned.

“Take your hand back.”

“Just tell me who sent it.”

“I've seen it. Pull your hand back.”

He stepped away. She opened another drawer and tossed him a small roll of clean cloth.

“Pad the cuff. Don't drip on my window.”

The burn wasn't bleeding. Sweat from his chin had run over his hand into the tray. She saw it too.

“Sweat either.”

He wiped the back of his hand with a corner, avoiding the wound. As he started winding the cloth tightly around his wrist, she caught its end with the scissors handle.

“Just padding. Cut off your circulation and you'll tell me there's something wrong with what I gave you.”

“Wasn't blaming you.”

“You stuck your arm right in my face. Who else?”

He loosened it and tucked it inside the cuff. The seam no longer touched the injury. Turning his wrist still hurt, but less sharply.

She wiped the tray, dropping the used cloth outside the basket, away from the old clothes. As he settled his sleeve, he heard her searching a ledger.

A number was sewn on her apron. 7.

“What should I call you?”

“Seven.”

“I'm Lin Mo.”

“I can see.”

She pointed at the chair leg. He turned the carving toward his palm. Seven had already lowered her head. The ledger was thick, its page edges black from handling. Following the record number, she turned several pages and tapped a line.

“Jacket came in first. Notes were added later.”

“When?”

“After you entered the corridor.”

He retrieved his paper. Both events appeared on one sheet, as though they'd happened together. He stared at the blank space between the intake time and the notes, realizing he'd kept treating them as one thing.

“Who added them?”

She covered the lower half of the ledger.

“Through the custody channel.”

“She was backstage?”

“Plenty of people can use that channel.”

“Can I?”

“Got something to deposit?”

He looked up.

“Can I send something to a specific person?”

“Recipient identity first. Then we check deposit eligibility.”

“What eligibility?”

Seven shut the ledger.

“You've only just collected something. Already sending things out?”

He gripped the gray cuff, the pills pressing against his thumb through a pocket. If something could come in, there was at least one route. He'd have to see whether it could carry a message back.

“Let me see that page.”

“No.”

“Copy a number for me.”

“On your sheet.”

“The source number.”

She glanced at the red light.

“You've got the jacket. Everything else belongs at another window.”

“Where?”

No answer. He set down the chair leg and carefully opened the payment receipt. His writing had smeared. Wang Hai, on the last line, was still legible.

Seven stared at it.

“You came with him?”

“He didn't enter nine.”

“Did you call him?”

“Yes.”

She drew a plastic bag from beneath the basket. Blue work clothes. A towel still wrapped the left sleeve, blood dried dark red into it.

He reached out. She pinned the bag beneath her arm.

“Not yours.”

“Where's he?”

“I only collect clothes.”

“Did he go through the return slot?”

“It's not that big.”

He looked up. She shifted the bag, uncovering a card. Blank photo, blank name. A small piece was missing from the plastic clip.

“Came to me like that.”

He pressed a fingertip against Wang Hai's name on the receipt. The man had said he could pronounce it, but didn't know whether it was his.

“Could we write him another name?”

Seven glanced at his handwriting.

“Where?”

“On that badge.”

“Stick paper on it. Call it Wang Hai. Go ahead.”

“Would that get it back?”

“Write a name on my desk and I have to send it out as a streamer?”

He said nothing. She tapped the card against the window's edge.

“Number still reads. Once the name's gone, you can't search by it. Want to find him? Keep this.”

She put it in the tray. Factory name and employee number remained on the back, with a fine barcode newly added below the number. He placed it against his receipt.

“You're allowed to give me this?”

“Not the clothes. Card isn't clothing.”

“Then before—”

“You reached for the bag.”

He put the badge away and looked past her. A long black coat hung over her chair, like the one in 09. Its unusually long sleeves reached the floor. She moved the basket to block his view.

“Anything else?”

“Source number.”

She scraped a nail over the blue stamp. He waited.

“Don't come smashing my window if you're wrong.”

“I won't smash your window.”

She looked at the screw in his chair leg. He rested it beside his leg.

“I didn't hit it before, either.”

She opened the ledger, took out a thin sheet, and laid it over his record, pinning it beneath the tray.

“Old deposit receipt. You can check it. You can't take it.”

Its number shared a prefix with the jacket's intake number. His name appeared beside it. A field underneath read Linked protection.

He bent forward.

“Protection comes through here too?”

“It's linked.”

“I never bought any.”

She moved the ledger closer, revealing small print.

> Payment method: Paid on another's behalf.
> Linked individual: Lin Mo.
> Original account status: Invalid.
> Balance: 0.

His finger stopped at the final line.

“Is that money?”

She rotated the receipt and covered the name field with the ledger.

“Where do you see a currency?”

None. No yuan, no numerical payment amount, no payment time. Just a solitary zero in the balance field.

“How can an invalid account add notes?”

“The custody channel didn't close with the account. Outstanding handovers can proceed normally.”

“Anyone can use it?”

“I said plenty of people.”

She pulled the receipt back. He held one corner with a finger, without snatching.

“Whose account is this?”

The red light flashed. Seven released the paper, staring at the corner beneath his finger. He lifted his hand. The light returned to its dim glow.

She tucked the receipt into the ledger.

“Want me to hand over the entire page?”

“I didn't know that would happen.”

“Now you do.”

He pulled his zipper higher. Pills and ID inside, handover record still intact. Looking hadn't made it blank. He watched the ledger, her hand flat on its cover.

“Could you tell her I got the jacket?”

“I don't have a recipient identity for whoever you mean.”

“Doesn't her receipt count?”

“It's old. Find out what identity they're collecting under now.”

He unfolded his record to the blacked-out name, pressed a fingertip against the unchanged strip, then turned the page toward himself again.

“Where can I check the account?”

After a pause, Seven took out a gray card.

“Next round.”

Only a name on it.

> SILENT ROOM

“I can check there?”

“Clear it first. Then you get your settlement. Clothes, names, protection, everything you've collected goes through the same settlement record.”

“Why didn't anyone tell me at the start?”

She returned the blue clothes and towel to the basket.

“If they'd told you, would you still have seen that jacket in the cupboard?”

He folded the handover record. Without his sister's jacket appearing first in 09, he wouldn't have picked up the blue outfit so quickly. Show him something, make him chase it. They'd planned every step.

“Were you a streamer before you collected clothes?”

Seven kicked the hanging sleeve farther beneath her chair.

“Finished?”

“You wore that coat?”

Her hand stopped on the ledger. After waiting a moment, he pocketed the gray card.

“Thanks. For the belt.”

She resumed cutting threads from the white shirt. He took the chair leg toward a thick curtain at the passage's end. Behind it, someone moved a chair.

“Lin Mo.”

He turned. Seven pointed to her number.

“You can ask about things that aren't marked confidential. When the light comes on, stop chasing people's names.”

“Yours too?”

He took out the badge. Its chipped clip caught the paper's edge. Rather than pull, he supported the receipt with his left hand and eased the card free. Seven put down her scissors and waited for him to clear the tray before moving her ledger back.

“Don't rub the writing.”

“Already smeared.”

“Still readable. Keep it that way.”

He refolded the receipt with Wang Hai's name inside and placed the badge outside the fold. Now he didn't have to press the card downward. His fingers could relax.

She withdrew the tray.

“Seven's enough.”

He nodded and lifted the curtain. Overhead light revealed six chairs and a blank screen. Someone sat on the right, hands flat on his thighs, fresh gauze around the left one.

The blue work clothes were gone. He wore an unmarked gray top. Hearing the curtain, he turned. Lin Mo touched the badge in his pocket.

“Wang—”

The man shook his head violently. The screen lit.

> Rule 1: No sound is permitted during this round.

Lin Mo swallowed the rest of the name.

The man stared at him and pointed to his empty chest.

Lin Mo took out the badge and held it up.
