# Chapter 30: The Other End of the Echo

Xiao Lu never finished the sentence.

Her fingers still hovered above the end switch, nails casting small shadows. When the light came on, the shadows stopped moving. She turned to the camera with the smile Lin Mo had just seen on the little screen.

“Welcome back to the performance.”

The voice came through her microphone. The white sound box flashed with it. The speaker at the side stayed silent.

Lin Mo raised a hand before her eyes.

“Look at me. What did you just call me?”

“New audience members, please confirm your seats.”

She looked at him; her words went toward the camera. Her lower lip trembled. Her teeth met, but the next sentence squeezed through.

“The interactive segment will—”

He took her hand. Still cold. The thumb that had brushed his knuckles curled into her palm, as though trying to hold something.

“Stop. Don’t speak. You just asked what happened to my voice.”

Her gaze moved from his face to his gray jacket. When she frowned, he turned the cuff toward her.

“Here. The mending. You’ve seen it.”

She didn’t touch it. Her right hand tugged. Thinking she wanted the switch, he moved her hand toward it, fingers still closed beside her wrist.

“Not that,” she said quietly.

He leaned closer.

“What?”

“You’re hurting me.”

He released her so abruptly that his sleeve slipped past her fingers, baring his white scar. She brought her hand to her chest and slowly rubbed where he’d held it.

The execution bar was still moving. He checked his own status: Physical modification, 44. Cognitive overwrite, 42. Beginner protection remained. Those words shone steadily, as though nothing had happened.

He turned back. Xiao Lu wasn’t him. That light didn’t reach her.

“We said those things ourselves,” he told her. “Not from a script. You asked whether I’d eaten. About the pen.”

Her eyes paused briefly on his left pocket.

He left the pill pack inside. Producing it might only give her another answer to copy. He kept both hands where she could see them.

“It’s okay if you can’t remember. Just don’t follow what it says.”

“This performance uses live dual-channel audio,” she said.

A tear slid to the corner of her mouth. She wiped it away, staring at her wet fingertip as though she didn’t understand the water.

His left shoulder dipped. Already he was turning toward the yellow connection. Pull the cable, and perhaps this sentence could stop halfway. Perhaps she could finish the one before it.

He took a step. His sock slid over the break beside the stand and snagged at the toe.

“Don’t come this way!” Wang Hai shouted.

Lin Mo grabbed the stand. His left arm failed to brace him properly; his forehead nearly struck the microphone. Wang Hai didn’t cross the line to haul him by the neck. He extended the chair leg over the railing with his right hand, within reach.

“Stand still first. Which cable?”

“Yellow.”

“You can’t reach it from here. Get down there, and she’s alone on camera.”

Xiao Lu hadn’t moved from her spot. The back of her hand touched her damp cheek as she turned halfway toward him.

“Where are you going?”

No host’s cadence. His fingers stopped on the wood.

“I want to make it stop—”

“You can stop.” She pointed to his switch. “You don’t have to climb down.”

He slowly returned the wood and edged back along the stand. The progress bar reached its end. The yellow light dimmed. The next segment appeared on the prompt screen.

Xiao Lu looked down without reading aloud. First she retrieved her script and tucked the sliver deeper between its pages.

“Where were we in the performance?”

He didn’t answer.

She looked up at his face. Something went blank in the space between them.

“Was that wrong?”

“Do you remember who I am?”

“Lin Mo. You’re performing with me.”

He waited.

So did she. At last she looked at the hand he kept at his side.

“What else?”

He opened his mouth. The sentence was short. He’d already said it once. Say it again, and perhaps he could force it into her ears. But she stepped back first.

“What did you call me?”

His breathing boomed through the microphone. He turned aside, keeping it from the audience.

“My sister.”

She didn’t immediately shake her head. She checked her script, then the composite image now relegated to the sidebar.

“Did I agree to play that?”

“No.”

“Then don’t answer for me.”

His right hand touched his pocket and fell away. He wanted to tell her she’d said that before, to use one surviving sentence as proof the whole person hadn’t gone. She stood watching him cry, hesitantly hugging her script closer.

“All right.”

She pointed to the end switch.

“I want to stop now.”

He pressed his. She checked his confirmation light and pressed hers, without letting him guide her hand.

Both microphones went dark. The screen announced the joint performance had ended. Audience members still refused to leave, demanding a replay of what she’d called him.

She looked toward the speaker.

“I said that?”

He was about to nod, but she had already taken half a step toward the side where recordings were collected. She stopped, gripping her script.

“They’ve taken another piece.”

Her voice was quiet. Outside the lights, he didn’t use that piece to call her back.

Two shallow steps led offstage. He misjudged the height; his knee gave as his foot came down. Wang Hai braced his left elbow with his right shoulder, leaving the injured wrist alone.

“Watch your feet.”

“I am.”

“Then see them.”

Holding the railing, Lin Mo placed his foot on the step again. He lifted the jacket hem from his knees. A loose thread hung where the metal had caught his sock. It hadn’t reached his skin.

Wang Hai didn’t ask whether it hurt. Only after both steps did he return the chair leg.

Xiao Lu came down the other side, script in her arms, heading toward the waiting entrance. Lin Mo called to her. She turned without approaching.

“Where are you going?”

“To sit in the back. I haven’t accepted another performance.”

Inside the open door, empty stools lined the wall beneath a light.

“Can I come find you again?”

She considered him.

“If you have questions, ask. Don’t play a recording of me calling you something and say it means I agreed.”

He nodded.

“I won’t this time.”

She took the second stool from the door, script across her knees. Almost the pose from the composite footage. But he saw her drag the stool back, out of the light shining in her face.

He didn’t follow.

Around the corner, Wang Hai produced the broken black casing. The narrow yellow strip still clung to its back. He let Lin Mo see, then pocketed it.

“The lights didn’t come on together.”

Lin Mo looked up.

“Yellow first. Then she stopped. White didn’t flash until she spoke.”

“You saw?”

“The order, yes. I don’t know what happened inside. Don’t turn that into ‘pull the cable and save her.’”

Lin Mo propped the chair leg against the wall. He didn’t sit. Voices carried through from the stage, no longer distinguishable. Only now did he notice his fingers still curved as though holding her hand, unable to straighten.

Wang Hai loosened the water bottle’s cap and offered it.

“Drink.”

Lin Mo shook his head.

“Not telling you to get over it. Your lips are cracked.”

He took it. The first mouthful sat on his tongue before he swallowed. It didn’t wash down the thing in his chest. It only let him speak.

“She remembered.”

“I heard.”

“I didn’t tell her to call me that.”

“I was there.”

He stared at the dent his grip had made in the bottle. He couldn’t go further without asking why not sooner, why hadn’t they pressed at 59, as if erasing her choice could buy a different outcome.

Wang Hai waited for the bottle back.

“Sun Ning first. You promised to tell her.”

Lin Mo retrieved the chair leg.

“Then I’m going to the collection and dispatch room.”

“For what?”

“To see where the yellow connection’s stuff comes from. I can’t keep waiting for it to reach her before finding out which recording it is.”

“It carried sound before. You don’t know if this was the same. You didn’t see inside.”

“So I look first.”

“And if there’s another box like this?”

Lin Mo looked at the bulge in Wang Hai’s pocket.

“I don’t smash it. I watch who installs it. Where they put it afterward.”

Wang Hai held a right hand over the pocket.

“This one’s already broken. Breaking another won’t open a door.”

“Ge,” a voice called in the distance.

Lin Mo turned instantly. His feet stayed still. The front-stage speaker played it again, the ending clipped short. Wang Hai reached toward his sleeve and stopped without pulling.

Only after the third repetition did Lin Mo turn back.

“Let’s go.”

Wang Hai hadn’t agreed to go with him. Back at the rest area, the door was shut. Lin Mo knocked twice and gave his name, then Wang Hai’s.

Sun Ning answered. She opened the door a little, bracing it with her left hand. Her right arm stayed at her side. The sealed bag lay on the chair behind her.

She looked past them.

“Didn’t bring her?”

He shook his head.

“She called me her brother. Then she forgot again.”

Sun Ning didn’t ask for the exact words. She moved aside, showing him the empty chair. After a moment, he sat. Wang Hai left the water by his feet and leaned against the doorframe.

“She remembers things we did today,” Lin Mo said. “Not letting me answer for her. Performing together. Just not that I’m her brother.”

“You’ll have her do it again?”

“No.”

Only after answering did he realize he’d still been considering the next question, the next phrase to test. The chair leg pressed against his knee. He turned its carved side toward himself and stopped looking for questions.

Sun Ning watched his hands.

“They can play the past. But deciding whether to reach for each other just then, that moment had to be yours.”

“They took that too.”

“So you never did it?”

He looked up. She didn’t wait for his agreement. With her left hand, she shifted the sealed bag toward the chair back, clearing space beside the table.

“I’m not going. My arm still hurts. If there’s something you want me to keep, tell me something I can remember.”

He handed over neither pills nor original paper. He recounted the things they’d said without reading the screen. He didn’t omit her asking him to release her hand. When Sun Ning asked about an unclear detail, he repeated it without making himself sound better.

After listening, Wang Hai glanced down the corridor.

“When’s the next trolley?”

“The recordings have to go back once they’re collected. An empty trolley was outside the waiting entrance when we came offstage.”

“Chasing another one?”

“Not the delivery end this time.”

Wang Hai took out the casing and pressed along the yellow strip.

“I check the way out first. Tell me before you grab anything. With my left hand like this, I can only cover one side.”

“You don’t have to—”

“I know. I’m not offering to die with you. I don’t want to hear myself speaking someone else’s words one day without knowing who did it.”

Lin Mo offered him the chair leg, carving beside his own palm, broken end outward.

“Then check the way out.”

Before shutting the door, Sun Ning told them to give their names when they returned. They agreed and followed the paper-delivery route. This time, at the fork, Lin Mo stopped until Wang Hai checked the door behind them.

The dispatch room’s shutter wasn’t fully lowered. White boxes flashed occasionally inside. A steady pinpoint shone beside the yellow connection. An empty trolley stood by the door, loose cable tangled underneath, a little yellow tape visible in the darkness.

Lin Mo didn’t crouch where the old recordings came out. He moved to the return side of the trolley, keeping his shadow clear of the doorway. At a corner within sight, Wang Hai pointed back: the route was open.

Someone inside spoke.

“This performance’s batch. Send it back.”

The wheels moved.

Lin Mo watched the cable pass his feet. This time, he meant to follow it in and find the connection through which they’d changed back someone who had just recognized him.
