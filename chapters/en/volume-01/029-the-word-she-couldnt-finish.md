# Chapter 29: The Word She Couldn’t Finish

Lin Mo checked the prompt screen first.

Blank. Xiao Lu’s upright script still hid the composite face. No one had turned a page.

“Why do you ask?”

“Your hands have been empty.”

He opened his right hand. The microphone stand had left a red mark across his palm. The white scar lay beside his wrist.

“Empty hands mean I haven’t eaten?”

“No.” She pushed her microphone away, then drew it back. “I don’t know. I just thought you’d say you had.”

“Which answer do you want?”

Her eyes changed. He swallowed the rest. Again, he’d put a question of hers on the scales, waiting to weigh whether it was what he wanted.

“Sorry. I’m not asking you to guess.”

“Then answer.”

“I hadn’t eaten before I came in. I’ve had water since.”

Her lips moved, but she said nothing. He remembered the unsent reply on his phone, three characters left there before the elevator doors closed. Standing here, he couldn’t even be sure he hadn’t sent it.

“Her last message told me to eat too. I asked whether she’d eaten. Don’t know if it went through.”

Xiao Lu stepped half a pace toward him.

“What did you say?”

He repeated it, leaving out “last.” She watched his mouth as if unable to fit the voice to the face.

“Don’t rush to answer. They might have seen that message too.”

“I’m not answering a cue.”

She pressed between her eyebrows, covering part of one eye. He almost told her not to, then asked whether she wanted to stop instead.

“No. You mentioned pills.”

He opened the left pocket. The pack remained inside the lining.

“Here.”

“Don’t take it out yet.”

She spoke so fast they both fell still. Someone shouted a complaint. Lin Mo let the pocket close, ignoring them.

Xiao Lu looked at her fingertips.

“Did you write on it?”

“Yes.”

“The pen didn’t work.”

His right hand clenched.

“It wouldn’t write. I scratched three lines. Have you seen that footage?”

“What footage?”

He didn’t answer; she didn’t pursue it. Her right hand traced a stroke in the air, nail clear of the stand.

“You pressed down, but nothing came out. Then held it up to look. You said you’d just bought it. It couldn’t be out of ink.”

He remembered the pen, fishing it from his jacket, holding its cap between his teeth, plastic digging against them. He didn’t remember saying that.

“Then the ink spilled. I wiped it with a tissue.”

“It didn’t spill.”

Her hand stopped.

“You didn’t wipe anything. You hadn’t finished the last character. You scratched beside it until you could go back and finish. The cap was still in your mouth.”

He swallowed. A blue sleeve, a bedside, a black dot on a tissue: he’d seen them so clearly a moment ago. Reaching for that dot, he could only recall lying down, someone supporting his wrist, a tissue moving beside the bed.

A different time.

He didn’t claim he’d sorted it out. He shook his head.

“I might have mixed up the ink. Go on.”

“I might be wrong.”

“Then leave it open.”

“You won’t take it out and check?”

Through his pocket he felt the hard plastic edge. Three scratches could prove he’d made them, not how she knew. He’d seen them himself in footage someone had chosen to show him. Producing the pack wouldn’t suddenly make this room honest.

“When you’ve finished.”

Watching his hand, she slowly lowered hers.

“You said the writing was too small. You were worried it wouldn’t be clear.”

“My handwriting’s always been bad.”

“Not bad. You’d filled the space. The first two characters were squashed together. Hardly any room after them.”

He took out the pack, writing toward himself. After meals. Half a tablet. He’d crossed out a mistake on the second line; the last character, “tablet,” was crammed alongside. He shifted his fingers to expose its broken strokes.

Xiao Lu didn’t reach for it. He looked at her, then turned it for her alone.

After several seconds, her brow remained furrowed.

“How do I know this?”

He couldn’t say, Because it’s you. The half tablet lay motionless inside the pack, broken edge against the clear shell.

“That screen was blank while she spoke,” Wang Hai said.

“How do you know she didn’t see it somewhere else?” someone asked.

“I don’t. I saw that screen was blank.”

He pointed with his right hand, then rested it on the railing. His cuff brushed the wood. He offered no further testimony.

Xiao Lu looked away from the pack.

“Are you satisfied now?”

Lin Mo stared.

“I got a few things right, so you can forget everything I got wrong. I still don’t know the hospital floor. Still don’t remember what was beside the bed.”

“I didn’t say those don’t count.”

“Your face did.”

He put the pack away. The joy straining to escape wouldn’t go with it. It lodged in his chest, making each breath shallow.

“I’m happy because you’re speaking for yourself.”

“You thought I’d learned it by heart.”

“I’m still afraid of that.”

Her gaze stopped.

“How do you want me to prove it?”

“It’s not all yours to prove. You have to recognize me too. If you can’t, I can’t hold up a piece of paper and say you’ve lost.”

“Then why did I stand my script up just now?”

“To hide the face they gave you.”

“Who’s ‘they’?”

He glanced toward the side, pointing to neither host nor masked spectator.

“I don’t know who made it.”

Her shoulders lowered a little.

“You saw. Don’t tell me later I never used to be afraid of things like this.”

He had no ready answer. The past he was so eager to recover had cornered her. Even standing a script before a screen needed to go on record, or later it might not count.

“I’ve never seen you on a stage like this before. You get to say whether you’re scared.”

She pressed the script closer to the screen. He didn’t help until she reached toward him. Then he moved the empty stand blocking its side. Both people remained in frame; the prerecorded figure no longer showed around the pages.

She watched him finish before taking her microphone.

“I’m scared I’ll open my mouth and hear someone else again.”

“I’m listening.”

“Not just to what’s right.”

“To what’s wrong too.”

Someone clapped once.

“Good choice of words. Fail to recognize him, and you lose.”

The masked spectator. Two fingers braced the paddle, unable to hold it straight; the other hand steadied it.

“Don’t change what I said,” Lin Mo told them.

“You keep changing things. She was doing fine as host. You show up, it’s all about the past. Can you eat the past?”

“They haven’t fed me in the present either,” Xiao Lu said.

Laughter rose. Neither of them laughed. Her own voice seemed to nudge her; she looked toward the pocket hiding the pills.

“Had you eaten then either?”

“When?”

“When you were writing.”

He tried to remember. His stomach seemed to hold the sourness of coffee, but seeming wasn’t certainty.

“I can’t remember. I might have told you to eat first.”

“You pushed things over to me. When I asked, you said later.”

She stopped. Her hand slid up the stand, fingertips beneath the microphone.

“You always said later.”

There was no paper to produce this time, no writing on its back to check. He couldn’t find a sufficiently distinctive memory to make that “always” belong to them.

He remembered a nurse turning the bed list over. No such person. He remembered smoothing a damp payment receipt, as though flattening it enough could bring someone back into that empty box.

“I went looking for you.”

Her fingers stopped.

“Someone else had the bed. Your things were gone. Later, I saw you paying here. Not with ordinary money.”

“Paying what?”

“Everything you could. So they couldn’t reduce me to zero after I came in. They said your account was closed. Your relationships outside had been removed too.”

She shook her head faintly, less a denial than a refusal to let the whole weight settle on her at once.

He didn’t explain the protection’s details. He wanted to ask whether it hurt, when she’d decided. What came out was the least useful question.

“Why didn’t you tell me?”

Tears filled her eyes without falling.

“If I told you, you’d keep asking.”

His hand slipped from his pocket.

He’d heard those words at the end of the footage. He couldn’t pretend otherwise, couldn’t count hearing them twice as fresh evidence. But she was looking at him now. The picture hadn’t taken her away when she finished.

“See?” she said. “You’re still asking.”

“This time’s different.”

“How?”

“I want to ask where you want to go from here.”

Only afterward did he realize he’d left out “escape.” He couldn’t point her to an exit, only two working microphones. He had no right to promise a path he hadn’t found.

“Stop or continue. Want me farther away, or listening. That’s what I’m asking.”

She studied him, then looked at his right wrist.

A notification sounded. The masked spectator’s paddle was lit. A proposal to restore the host identity entered the voting panel. Recipient: Xiao Lu.

“Sixty percent,” the female voice reminded them. “Upon approval, the corresponding memories will be synchronized.”

“End together,” Lin Mo said, pointing to their switches.

She looked at the uncounted votes, then back.

“I know where it is. One more thing.”

He slowly withdrew his hand, leaving her free to disengage whenever she wanted. His own switch was within reach. Pressing it alone couldn’t end the performance for her.

“What happened there?”

“During a performance.”

She raised her hand and stopped short of him.

“Can I?”

He nodded.

Her fingertips settled beside the white scar without pressing the injury. Her hand was cold. Instinctively, he started to close his around it, then stopped so she could draw away.

“Your voice…”

“They changed it. My face too.”

She searched his features like someone looking for an old window in an emptied room. He didn’t show his ID or angle his face to resemble who he’d been.

“You don’t have to match what I remember. I’ve changed too.”

Suddenly she gripped his fingers.

He stepped half a pace closer, stopping where she could see him. The joy in his chest hurt beyond recognition. His words went out between the microphones. He didn’t check what the screen made of them.

“You’re my sister.”

Her lips parted, closed. A small breath escaped, as though she’d finally brought long-held words to her tongue and feared they’d become someone else’s once spoken.

“Ge.”

Brother. One syllable. He couldn’t answer at once. The lights blurred.

Her other hand touched his cheek, pausing at the strand of hair by his ear.

“What happened to you?”

“Never mind that.”

Too quickly. He corrected himself.

“I’m not saying you can’t ask. I’m still here.”

She nodded. Tears fell. Her thumb brushed his knuckles as though wiping his tears instead. He wanted to say he wasn’t crying. His throat left no room for anything so pointless.

Wang Hai’s voice cut in.

“The light!”

The proposal had entered its final countdown. Lin Mo turned. Only Xiao Lu was named: restore her host identity for this performance and synchronize the corresponding memories. The 60 percent threshold remained. Approval jumped from 59 to 61.

The masked spectator lowered the paddle.

“There. She recognized him. Can’t cry all night.”

Lin Mo reached for the end switch, fingers coming to rest on his own. Xiao Lu turned too, but her confirmation light stayed dark. He still held her hand.

“Loosen your grip.”

He released her immediately. She reached for her switch, but the screen had stopped changing.

> Approval: 64%. Proposal passed. Executing.

A light came on above the yellow connection beside the stage. Xiao Lu’s hand stopped halfway. Her lips moved again.

“Ge, you—”
