# Chapter 22: The One Who Paid

“I paid for my sister,” Lin Mo said.

The timer counted down from six minutes. Xiao Lu didn't ask his sister's name. She moved the microphone aside and waited for him to steady himself.

“Before she disappeared?”

“Yes. She was still there then.”

“And afterward?”

“Someone else in her bed. The nurse couldn't find her.”

He pressed his inside pocket. A corner of the receipt poked against the gray cloth, but he didn't take it out for the camera. He edged his left foot back half an inch, away from the microphone.

Xiao Lu pointed to the adjustment knob. “You don't have to stand closer.”

He loosened it with his left hand and swung the microphone toward him. The base scraped the tiles. Someone behind them shouted to stop moving things.

“I asked you,” he said. “Have you been to that hospital?”

“Five forty,” Wang Hai called from the companion seats.

His right hand rested on the timer sheet, his left tucked away. He wasn't pointing Lin Mo toward an answer. Through the open rest-area door, Sun Ning sat facing the stage, her right arm against her waist.

Xiao Lu's lips moved. He waited until she'd lowered her hand from the stand.

“I've seen the beds.”

“Where?”

She glanced backstage. No screen, only a black curtain reaching the floor. A shadow passed beneath a small light beside it.

“Performances. Hospital rooms.”

“Where were you standing?”

“The door. Sometimes beside the bed.”

The knob dug a ring into his palm. He released it, but his right wrist had struck the stand. Pain ran beneath the white scar; the padding slid toward his cuff.

“Bed six?”

She shook her head. “They didn't let me see the number.”

A masked spectator rapped his voting board and raised it, pointing at her with his uninjured left hand.

“We've seen the clip. Stop pretending.”

“I said I didn't see the bed number.” She didn't turn. “Replay the clip if you want. That counts as your voice.”

The woman announced that this session recorded their live answers. Old footage could not answer for them. The spectator lowered his board. Comments rolled past.

Xiao Lu pulled her microphone back.

“My turn. How did you get here after you couldn't find her?”

“I heard her voice.”

“At the hospital?”

“On my phone. A link she sent.”

“You saw who sent it?”

He opened his mouth. She sent it caught behind his tongue. He looked away from her name tag.

“Her account. I didn't see her press send.”

She nodded and turned to the next section without putting her own name beside the account.

“What did you hear?”

“She told me to go to the eighth floor.”

“She said it? Or was it text?”

White light from the elevator doors crowded his vision. He remembered his phone, the ringing on the stream, the break in his sister's cough. But the floor number had appeared below, typed by a viewer.

“Text. A comment.”

Someone laughed behind him. He didn't say she again. His fingers found the tear in the receipt and pressed it flat.

“The voice brought me there. They wrote the floor number.”

Xiao Lu tucked the question sheet into the stand's clip to stop it lifting in the draft.

“Would she recognize your face now?”

His hand stopped at his pocket. His ID lay beside the receipt. He pinched its hard edge without drawing it out.

“I didn't look like this before.”

“I didn't ask about before.”

He raised his face. The front camera drew closer; light caught his hair. Xiao Lu straightened her stand without pushing the camera away.

“She hasn't seen this face.”

“How will you get her to recognize you?”

“Talk about before. If she doesn't remember, start with what she does remember.”

Her fingers curled against the script.

“What if she remembers differently?”

He released the ID. “You said you hadn't seen the bed number. I've kept that answer.”

She pushed her answer column toward him. She didn't supply a number.

Someone shouted that they'd never recognize anyone at this rate. Wang Hai pointed at the countdown.

“In a hurry? Give him another minute?”

The spectator turned his board away. Wang Hai lowered his hand.

Xiao Lu tapped the page margin with her pen.

“I've performed parts where I get someone to come in. Cough at the start. Don't show my whole face.”

He leaned forward, hair brushing the microphone. Pushing it aside, he asked which performance, too quickly.

She capped the pen. “Several. Coughing doesn't make that particular one mine.”

He stopped, his socked toes still inside his mark. She stayed where she was.

“In one, the desk was narrow. I sat behind it, holding down a sheet of paper so it wouldn't curl.”

“What paper?”

“A payment receipt. I didn't know who it was for.”

He withdrew his hand from his pocket. He wanted to ask about the stamp, the back, whoever took it. His finger tapped the microphone instead. The amplified knock drew her gaze.

“Making me guess again?”

“Which side faced up?”

“The printed side. I didn't turn it over.”

“Four ten,” Wang Hai called.

The masked spectator's board lit up. Beside Lin Mo's face appeared a new proposal.

> Identity: Lin Mo, someone from the ward.

Lin Mo guarded his pocket with his left hand and lowered his right.

“Being in a ward makes you someone from it?”

The spectator held the board to the light. “Visitor, patient. What's the difference? She remembers the receipt. Don't you want to keep performing?”

Xiao Lu raised her script, its opening instruction to question each other still visible.

“You submitted an identity proposal. Don't change my questions.”

The woman announced the terms. Approval of 60% would enact it, limited to Lin Mo's hospital-room identity display this session. Xiao Lu's answers would remain unchanged. The corresponding performance records would be synchronized. Their remaining time would stay the same.

Wang Hai stood, knocking his chair against the railing. He steadied it without reaching onto the stage. His bandage caught a shirt button; he freed it with his right hand and tucked it into his sleeve.

“An identity display comes with memories?”

“Record synchronization is included in the proposal.”

Lin Mo pointed at the preview opening beneath the label. He wore hospital clothes, hands on his knees, the receipt by the bed.

“Is that what you want me answering about? Being in bed?”

“More familiar, isn't it?” said the spectator.

“If I lie down, whose questions fill these six minutes?”

A front-row board lowered. Someone farther back shouted not to listen. Lin Mo turned the microphone toward his mouth, bracing the stand's base with his left hand.

“I stood at the payment window. Put me in bed and you won't find out who I saw taking the money.”

Xiao Lu's pen stopped. Lin Mo waited for the result.

The hospital-room identity passed with 61%.

The camera rose to his chest. The gray jacket covered his blue collar, but onscreen a white collar with blue stripes appeared. He pinched it. Still his blue shirt. He raised the zipper; the onscreen collar rose too, still striped.

Xiao Lu raised a hand. “Your clothes haven't changed. Don't take them off.”

He stopped at the zipper. Trolley wheels rattled over a threshold through the speakers. His knees drew together, waiting for a bed beneath them. His feet found only floor.

Onscreen, he lay down. Someone drew a blanket to his waist. He raised his right hand to stop them and struck the stand with his injured wrist. Pain loosened his fingers.

“Lin Mo. Three minutes.”

He turned toward Wang Hai and the factory badge showing at his chest. He opened his mouth to ask him to lower the foot of the bed.

Sun Ning called from the rest area. “You haven't sat down. You've been standing.”

He pressed his left hand to his skirt's waistband, the broad cloth cinched outside his jacket. No blanket. His socks brushed the mark. A tile seam pressed into his sole.

Xiao Lu moved her microphone so he could hear clearly.

“The question just now. Which side faced up?”

“The printed side.”

“That was my answer.”

He shut his mouth. She waited for him to release the zipper and turned the sheet toward him.

“Your receipt now. Where did you put it when you got it?”

“My jacket's inside pocket.”

“And now?”

“Still there.”

She tucked the lifting page back into its clip and wrote printed side in her answer column. Nothing about a bedside table or pillow.

He reached toward it, but the two marks were too far apart. He withdrew his hand. The page hadn't become a hospital room with the screen. He slid his left foot back, then stopped to ease the sock wrinkled beneath his toes.

“Don't look for that receipt,” she said. “Use the one you've got.”

He took it out left-handed. The tear caught on the pocket seam. He carefully worked it free.

The original cashier's stamp emerged as the screen filled with a close-up of the bedside receipt. The same red stamp on both. His trembling paper tapped a button. He turned it over. Four digits beside the tear, with the character for male he'd added beneath.

“Don't open it for them,” Wang Hai said.

He folded the back inward, leaving the original date visible. Holding it before his eyes, he read the date aloud without handing it to Xiao Lu.

Onscreen he still lay in bed. He began to remember a nurse tucking the receipt under his pillow. He remembered turning on his side so he wouldn't crush it in his sleep. His shoulder tilted. He braced his left elbow against the stand before his body could follow.

He straightened, the cloth tie biting his waist. When he released his elbow, the stand swayed. He caught the microphone as it swept past his mouth.

“I was standing when I took the receipt.”

Xiao Lu clipped her pen to the script. She didn't confirm his statement for him.

“Who gave it to you?”

“The person at the payment window.”

“Their face?”

The opening had been too low. He'd watched the hands; lifting his head would have scraped the panel. Change pushed back, paper on top, fingers sliding it toward him. Unpainted nails.

“I didn't see clearly.”

Her hand dropped from the microphone. He pocketed the receipt, its date at the fold. Onscreen, a hand turned the bedside paper over.

He stopped following it.

“When you sat behind the desk, who did you give the paper to?”

She looked from his pocket toward the black curtain.

“I wasn't allowed.”

“Who wouldn't let you?”

“The performance said to wait until someone reached inside. Far enough, then I could push it out.”

“Two minutes,” Wang Hai said.

She turned back to her microphone. Her voice rasped; she paused.

“I never got to see a face. The opening was down below. When the money came in, hold it down. The paper couldn't go with it.”

His hand touched the bottom of his pocket. He left the receipt there, but the panel above the payment window seemed to press lower, hiding whoever sat behind it.

Xiao Lu extended her hand to the edge of her mark. She wasn't reaching for his paper.

“After you paid, did you reach inside again?”
