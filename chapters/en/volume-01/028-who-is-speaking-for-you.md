# Chapter 28: Who Is Speaking for You?

Two microphones stood side by side, an unlit indicator beneath each.

Lin Mo passed the chair leg to Wang Hai outside the accompanying performers’ line. Once Wang Hai had a firm right-handed grip, he confirmed his own channel, staying on his side of the central gap. Xiao Lu checked her name and confirmed hers. The six-minute timer from the previous performance, already spent, was gone. The screen showed only “End together.”

“We can still choose not to answer,” she said.

“Yes.”

“You too.”

He nodded. She withdrew her hand first. He approached the left microphone, but the right indicator went dark.

Xiao Lu didn’t swap places. Checking the mark by her feet, she dragged her stand back. Its metal feet scraped the floor. The sound came from the left speaker.

“Where’s mine?”

“We recommend recording separately for a combined broadcast,” the female voice said.

The overhead picture had split in two. Lin Mo’s half was moving; in hers, Xiao Lu sat with her script across her knees. Footage from the waiting area had been moved into the present.

“She’s standing right here. You have to put her back in that chair?”

“The recommended format does not compromise the completeness of the exchange.”

Xiao Lu waved a hand before her face. Her onscreen self didn’t raise a hand. Instead, she slowly smiled.

Someone whistled.

“Like that one better. Other one’s too mean.”

Xiao Lu turned the microphone toward herself.

“I’m asking you. Not her.”

Lin Mo followed her gaze to the sound indicator. It stayed dark when she spoke. His flashed with the audience’s laughter. He reached to pass her his microphone, but she held the stand at its base.

“Don’t. Then you won’t have a voice.”

“Together or apart, they find a way to shut you up,” Wang Hai said from outside the line. “Efficient equipment.”

Lin Mo let go. Wang Hai stayed where he could see both microphones and the thick cable to the side door. The white floor line marked the distance they’d agreed on.

A thin pain ran across Lin Mo’s sole. He shifted his weight without calling Wang Hai in.

Two options appeared: retain live dual-channel audio, or use separately recorded audio combined for broadcast. The highest vote would decide. This choice involved no identity rewriting.

Xiao Lu tapped that line with her nail.

“Vote on this first. We don’t start until both sides work.”

“Both channels have been confirmed.”

“I agreed to start. Not to have someone speak for me.”

Lin Mo turned his microphone so the camera could see her beside the seated version onscreen.

“Want to hear her answer? Let her. If you only want the edited version, you’ve already seen it.”

“How do we know you didn’t plan this?” someone shouted.

“We did,” Xiao Lu said.

Lin Mo turned. She closed her script and set it by her feet.

“We agreed either of us could refuse to answer. After his questions, I get mine. That’s all.”

A brief hush, then people slapped armrests, demanding she ask the hardest question first. The masked spectator whose fingers wouldn’t close sat to the right, voting paddle against their knees, facing her.

“Let her speak, then. See how long before she admits it.”

Lin Mo’s throat tightened at those hands. He didn’t take up the suggestion.

The result appeared: live dual-channel audio, 68 percent; separate recordings, 32.

The right indicator lit. Xiao Lu said, “Hello,” her voice ringing briefly from her own speaker. Before Lin Mo could speak, a gentle voice came from the side of the stage.

“I’m just glad you’re back.”

His left knee bent. That familiar dinner table seemed to be waiting behind the camera. He held the stand and stayed upright.

Xiao Lu put a foot in front of her script.

“That wasn’t me.”

“I know.”

“You looked.”

Bowls still clinked behind the small speaker’s black grille. Lin Mo angled his microphone toward it. The sound fed back into itself with a piercing shriek. He turned it away at once.

“That’s still playing. Turning off our live audio wouldn’t stop it, would it?”

Wang Hai pointed with his right hand.

“Its light stays on. Even when neither of you speaks.”

Both fell silent. From the side came the full sentence, “Sorry to keep you waiting,” in a tone Lin Mo had never used.

The audience laughed. Some enjoyed the botched edit; others shouted for them to pick up their cues and stop spoiling the mood.

Lin Mo gripped the stand until his knuckles whitened. Xiao Lu pointed to the preview switch.

“This only turns off mine. Press yours too.”

They pressed together. The tableware fell silent. Both halves of the screen finally showed the people standing there.

A little emptiness opened between them. No table. No smile waiting for its reply.

The camera slid between them, framing her, then him. Onscreen, his extended hand looked as though he meant to take hers. He brought it to his side.

“How are we supposed to ask anything like this?” Xiao Lu asked the camera.

“Close-ups help capture authentic reactions.”

“I want to see when he opens his mouth.”

She moved sideways into his view, keeping her distance. The camera swung after her. Its support pressed onto the floor cable with a thump. Lin Mo lifted a foot to nudge it clear.

“Don’t step there,” Wang Hai called. “Something’s loose underneath.”

Lin Mo stopped, left foot suspended. Trying to set it back, he couldn’t find the patch where he’d been steady. Xiao Lu moved right with her stand, making space. He stepped into it and found his balance.

“What’s the penalty for breaking it?” someone shouted.

“He hasn’t touched it,” Wang Hai said. “Do they penalize bad eyesight too?”

Laughter surged from the back. The heckler swore and fell quiet. Wang Hai wasn’t smiling. He motioned downward. A corner of the cable guard had cracked. A shoe could bridge it; a sock would snag on the metal edge.

Lin Mo went around the break and dragged his microphone where both of them could remain in frame. Xiao Lu held the stand’s base, keeping it from pulling the cable along.

“Here. Don’t touch the stand when I’m speaking. I won’t touch it when you are.”

She tapped her microphone; the right speaker sounded. After she withdrew her hand, he tapped his. Onscreen, both stood with their hands on their respective sides.

Someone complained that the repairs were taking forever.

“In a hurry for answers?” Xiao Lu asked.

“Yeah.”

“Then let me get to the questions.”

Lin Mo laughed too briefly for the microphone to catch. When she looked over, he stopped.

“Not laughing at you.”

“I know. You rushed me too.”

He took his hand from the stand, leaving her room to ask.

The composite face remained in the sidebar. Xiao Lu tried covering it with her hand, then propped her script against the little screen at her feet. She didn’t tear out a page or hold it up for the audience. She simply kept the image out of her own sight.

Only then did Lin Mo notice her trembling thumb. Not every silence meant she knew what to say next.

He held his question back.

“Closer,” he said.

She immediately stepped away.

“You said you wouldn’t tell me how to move.”

“The microphone. Closer to you. They can’t hear at the back.”

After a look at him, she lowered it. He dragged his own backward. Its base struck his foot; he hissed.

“Yours doesn’t have to move.”

“Got it.”

Her mouth twitched, then tightened. He didn’t seize on that flicker to recognize someone from the past. He waited until she settled.

“What do you want to ask first?”

Her gaze followed his hand from the stand to its habitual place against his jacket’s inner pocket.

“Answers hidden in there?”

He let go.

“My ID. Things I don’t want everyone here seeing.”

“What am I supposed to trust, then?”

“I can show you privately. The pill pack’s here too. But not the writing first. If you read what I wrote and then I ask whether you remember, we won’t be able to tell.”

“You’re afraid I’ll read it out?”

“I’m afraid I’ll hear you read it and think you remember.”

He opened the left pocket enough to show where the pack lay, then released it without exposing it to the camera.

“I might remember the words wrong too. Don’t change your answer to match.”

She looked down at her script without retrieving it. “Pills” had appeared onscreen, followed by guesses: mental patient, unpaid bills, faking illness. Lin Mo closed his jacket. He owed them no explanation.

“Boring,” said the masked spectator. “Show us nothing, and how do we know what you’re recognizing?”

“I haven’t recognized anyone,” Xiao Lu said.

The voting paddle shifted.

“You will. Don’t choose for yourself, and they’ll choose for you.”

A new panel appeared. Proposals were open for changes to identities, relationships and associated memories in this performance. Identity and cognitive changes required 60 percent approval. Passed changes would not be reversed if one participant withdrew.

Lin Mo stared at “associated memories.” They’d read the terms before. Here, the words seemed different. He stepped toward Xiao Lu. She raised a hand to cover her microphone, not to stop him.

“I saw.”

“We can still end together.”

She glanced at the masked spectator.

“Will ending this make the recording they have stop being my voice?”

“That’s not what I meant.”

“I know. But I haven’t asked yet.”

He stopped. Wang Hai leaned against the railing, right hand around the chair leg, its carved side inward.

“Ask me, I’ll say leave,” Wang Hai said. “Ask her, listen to her answer.”

Xiao Lu waited for Lin Mo to face her again.

“I want to finish this round. Do you?”

He looked at the newly lit right-hand indicator.

“Ask.”

She uncovered the microphone.

“What will you do if I never remember?”

The familiar answer, I’ll find her, reached his tongue. He swallowed it. The dry air scratched his throat.

“Keep looking.”

“For me, or for someone who can answer you?”

“For what she left behind. For the things you tell me too. You not remembering can’t mean the person speaking now doesn’t count.”

“And you’ll still come asking?”

“If you’re willing.”

Her toe touched her script. She moved it aside.

“Every time you say ‘willing,’ you look like you’ll fall apart if I shake my head.”

Someone laughed. Lin Mo neither joined in nor looked.

“I’ll try not to stare.”

“I didn’t say close your eyes.”

He turned back. This time she looked at him first. Neither found easy footing in what she’d said.

“I’m scared of being wrong,” he said. “And scared you’re right here and I’ll walk away. Both at once. What I actually do doesn’t sound as good as what I say.”

Her grip on the stand slowly loosened.

“Would you blame her?”

“What?”

“The person you’re looking for. If she never wanted you to come.”

His left shoulder suddenly felt heavy. He seemed to stand outside the footage again, watching someone pay everything for him before erasing herself from his life.

“I’d be angry.”

She looked up.

“Why look for her, then?”

“To be angry in person. She can yell at me too. This place has spoken for us enough. I don’t want it playing our last words as well.”

Beyond the railing, Wang Hai glanced down at his factory badge and said nothing.

Lin Mo waited. The proposal panel flashed, but their prompt screen stayed blank. Xiao Lu studied his jacket, from collar to pocket, then his open right hand.

He left the pill pack where it was. He didn’t remind her it was his turn.

“What is it?” he asked softly.

She frowned, as if a long-delayed sentence had finally arrived.

“Did you eat before you came?”
