# Chapter 107: White Paper Doesn't Settle the Debt

Zhao Yun arrived carrying a little spoon.

Tissue wrapped its handle, one corner damp. She stood beside the bed before noticing she'd brought it with her, then hurriedly pocketed it.

“Dad wants to feed himself. Says somebody already did it last night. No need today.”

Lin Mo looked at the small shape beneath the fabric. “Can he hold it?”

“Dropped it twice.”

Her smile faded quickly. Her father was brighter than yesterday. He complained about thin porridge and asked if they'd turned off the gas at home. But the hand that had held seventeen couldn't grip a spoon. The doctor had examined him; they needed to keep observing. Nobody dared hold his fingers and make him squeeze.

Lin Mo's breakfast waited on its tray. The care worker turned the spoon handle outward, preparing to leave them to talk. He asked Zhao Yun to stop her.

“I'll eat now. Sit and talk. I can hear you.”

Zhao Yun pulled up a chair. Watching him tilt his head back for the porridge, her eyes reddened. He finished the mouthful and waited for the spoon to withdraw.

“Don't look at me like that. I was already injured.”

“I know.”

She looked down and unwrapped the tissue in her pocket. A tooth mark dented the spoon handle, old or newly made.

“He asked your name. I told him Lin Mo. He said to remember, so I wouldn't end up saying thanks to that woman.”

Lin Mo's lips moved.

He wanted to say brother. Man. Remembered the silences after speaking on the sixth floor yesterday. Zhao Yun was trying to remember the person who'd saved her father. She didn't yet know how much those words weighed.

“Lin Mo is fine.”

She typed it into her phone and showed him. Both characters correct.

Her father had sent another message: don't rush to take the last ticket next time.

“He was watching yesterday,” she said. “He couldn't talk much. He could still see.”

Lin Mo looked toward his right leg, the gray mark under the sheet. He'd been so intent on getting the old man out that he hadn't noticed whether those eyes still followed him.

“Tell him I'll try not to next time.”

“He said don't.”

“I heard.”

Her laugh finally made a sound. Someone called from next door. She stood, chair legs scraping softly. Lin Mo asked her to give the spoon back instead of hiding it from her father.

“If he wants to try, let the doctor say when. Hide it and he'll blame you for losing it.”

She nodded and left with the spoon.

The next visitor was the workman's wife.

She brought nothing, her coat collar turned inward, one hand clenched around her husband's examination paperwork. His surname was He. He installed equipment and had come to collect documents for a relative. On his way out, he'd seen someone being dragged and gone in to help.

“They've treated the wound. No weight on it today,” she said. “He's still asking if he can work next week.”

Lin Mo didn't tell her it would be fine. He could still see the man hopping beyond the yellow line. The narrow cut went far deeper than a photograph suggested.

“You tell him to look after himself. He won't listen to me.”

“He might not listen to me either.”

She looked at Lin Mo's supported left arm and sighed.

“True. Same problem, both of you.”

That reminded him of Old Chen, always tucking his cup and notebook against his chest. At the end, he'd hoped to inch his way out too. When it really hurt, he'd asked people to stop. None of them existed only in that last brave moment. People hearing the story could so easily leave out everything before and after.

Mr. He's wife explained why she'd come. The hospital was compiling an account. Her husband couldn't remember whether the shoe or the pole had fallen first. Could Lin Mo help establish what happened, so the paperwork didn't reduce it to a fall?

“The shoe first. He wasn't hurt falling while he helped. He tried to step outside the yellow line. Something held his leg.”

“Can they put that in?”

“That's what I'll tell them.”

Her grip tightened. Her eyes reddened.

“Good. Don't let them say he had it coming for sticking his nose in.”

“They won't hear that from me.”

She stayed, turning over the papers. Several phone numbers covered the back. One had been heavily crossed out.

“He wanted to call the foreman himself. I told him to wait. Two sentences and he'd be promising to come tomorrow.”

“Did you call for him?”

“No. His work. He needs to tell them. I'll sit beside him and listen.”

Rubbing her nose, she looked up at Lin Mo.

“I'm not saying he shouldn't help people. But yesterday the first thing I saw was that shoe. Full of blood. He told me not to worry. At least they'd got everyone out. I nearly hit him with it.”

Lin Mo couldn't quite picture her lifting the bloody shoe, but understood why she needed to say it. Anger after the danger passed often belonged to the person who'd have to live beside the wound.

“He pulled the old man back,” he said. “Someone helped him out afterward too.”

“I know. He kept telling me last night. You sitting there, telling him not to cross again. When he gets to what happened to his own foot, he skips it.”

Lin Mo lowered his head. His recordings said little about his left arm falling limp either. The sign turned. People escaped. As though a good enough outcome could reduce pain's share of the telling.

She folded the papers and let out a long breath.

“I didn't come to blame you. Just needed to talk to someone who was there. When he tells me, I open my mouth and want to swear.”

“Swear a little, then. Don't throw his shoe away. He might still want the pair.”

She stared, then laughed through tears.

“How did you know? He just said the sole's still good. Wash it and it'll do.”

Lin Mo laughed carefully, sparing his shoulder. He promised no lovely ending for them. But the shoe left beyond the line could mean something besides injury again: something one person couldn't bear to throw away and another wanted to hit him with.

She thanked him. At the door, she turned and asked him to send word through the nurse if he needed anything brought in. He thanked her without keeping someone who'd just taken charge of one injured person to care for another.

After breakfast, he dictated what he'd witnessed to the staff member compiling the account. Asked for a signature, the nurse examined his hands and recommended retaining the spoken account first, using whatever procedure he could manage. Lin Mo had the sentence about Mr. He read back. The trapped leg hadn't become a slip.

Near noon, Xu and Tang appeared at the observation-room door.

Tang carried an ordinary plastic box. Lin Mo's chest tightened until he saw the blue sides, the lid labeled Tools.

Seeing his face, Tang left it outside.

“It's not the one downstairs. Brought it to show you the construction. Didn't think—”

“It's all right. Leave it outside for now.”

Lin Mo didn't want it beside the bed, or his reassurance mistaken for an invitation. Tang brought in only a drawing of the box's side.

“The one downstairs isn't molded in one piece. Rigid panels clipped into a frame. Its base cracked once. We patched it with a narrow strip. That's why one side keeps trying to spring up now it's on its side.”

“Could it turn itself upright?” Lin Mo asked, looking at a heavy line in the sketch.

“Hasn't yet. Someone knocks it, or the frame distorts, who knows?”

They couldn't leave it there forever hoping nobody would touch it. Guards changed. Floors needed cleaning. People passed through every day. The barriers hadn't erased that corner from the world.

Xu placed his phone where Lin Mo could see.

The morning photograph showed the red stroke more clearly. Tang had shone light from different angles. It remained on the plastic, never on the black fragment underneath. Where box and scrap met, a narrow bright gap showed.

“Didn't put anything in,” Tang said. “Could see with the torch. Black scrap's under the shutter. Box rim rests beside it.”

He opened another photograph from behind the counter.

The main black sheet remained on the sign's support. A tear ran down beside the spindle, as if something had pulled it open. The fragment outside the shutter had fallen from there. They stood at different heights, without any connecting thread someone could simply cut.

“Two separate places,” Lin Mo said.

“Drew them separately for the guards too,” Xu said. “Don't want someone told to hold the paper down thinking the scrap on the floor is enough.”

A thin cover behind the sign had rusty edges. The support and cover Liang had described last night now had distinct shapes. Tang's light caught a missing corner along the cover's bottom, showing darkness inside.

“Comes off from behind, without touching the spindle. We're leaving that for now.”

“You want to dismantle the box first?”

Tang nodded. “So it can't stand up again. Keep the side that showed the number pinned down. Lay the other panels flat, one by one. Everything stays there.”

Lin Mo studied the drawing. Last night he'd thought only that the box mustn't move. Tang was considering how, since it couldn't move, to give it one less thing it could do.

In the theater, too, he'd depended on what others knew. A wedge's thickness, removing a drive catch, supporting an injured person: reading a few lines couldn't replace those skills.

“I can listen over the phone.”

Tang turned the drawing over. “Listen, yes. How I use my hands is up to me.”

Lin Mo nodded.

“If something's wrong, stop according to what you see.”

“Without waiting for your permission.”

Blunt. Heat reached Lin Mo's ears. He was still gathering responsibility for everyone's decisions into his own hands. It sounded responsible. In an emergency, it might make someone wait for words he couldn't speak in time.

“Right. Don't wait for me.”

They didn't rehearse everything beside his bed. Tang needed another maintenance worker to check the tools' reach. Xu needed the space behind the barriers cleared. The nurse said Lin Mo needed rest; he couldn't stare at a screen from breakfast until afternoon.

“We'll call at one thirty,” Xu said. “Sleep. We'll tell you if that changes.”

After they left, sleep didn't come immediately.

Mr. He's wife had asked them not to call it a fall. Once a person was wheeled away, words could make pain so tidy. Injured. Taken for treatment. Appropriate care arranged. The gash in a leg didn't read those words. Work would wait. Someone else must feed you. You must learn again how to wash without touching the wound.

A paper cup stood on the sill, softened by moisture. The care worker asked whether to throw it out. Yes, he said, then felt he'd answered too quickly. He watched her crush it into the ordinary rubbish bag.

Nothing happened.

He lay back and closed his eyes. He couldn't make every cup and box stand guard for that black paper forever.

By the afternoon call, he'd slept a while. The nurse raised the bedhead and set his phone securely on its stand.

Tang waited outside the line. Another maintenance worker stood to one side. Tools were positioned, a clear path left for retreat. Xu named everyone present, then pointed the camera toward the floor.

The overturned clear box filled the screen. Under the light, the tiny red stroke lay quietly beside the scratch.

“Can you see?” Xu asked.

“Yes.”

A broad board entered the frame and pressed firmly against the box wall. Plastic gave a faint click.

The red stroke stayed dark.

Tang's tool waited outside. He didn't rush into the gap. Only when his colleague confirmed the board was secure did he crouch and begin releasing the first clip.
