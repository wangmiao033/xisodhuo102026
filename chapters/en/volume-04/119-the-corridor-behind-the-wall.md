# Chapter 119: The Corridor Behind the Wall

As the pale board loosened outward, everyone in the basement stepped back.

Lin Mo's wheelchair stayed still. Yuan gripped its handles, watching beneath the board. The cleared retreat ran straight to the original iron door, with the narrow stairs beyond.

Thirty days after the window incident, Lin Mo had returned for his second visit.

The storage man had brought a repair worker to inspect the surface. It was hardboard with old paint, fastened over an existing opening. It wasn't load-bearing, and no working pipes or wires ran through it. Its remaining fasteners could be removed from outside without drilling through.

Lin Mo explained that he'd been rescued from this basement and didn't know what lay behind the opening. He wanted only the confirmed ordinary covering removed. Stop at anything abnormal. Don't follow it inside.

The worker looked between Zhou and the wheelchair.

“Are you looking for someone or something?”

“Where someone went,” Lin Mo said. “Whatever's inside may hurt people.”

The worker didn't agree to explore. With the storage man supporting the board from one side, he removed the exposed fasteners. The last old paint split with a faint crack. The board tipped outward from the top.

No bricks fell out. Cold air came first.

It brushed Lin Mo's left foot, smelling of dry dust. Water still lay beside his chair; the pipe still dripped. None of that dampness came with the draft.

“To the side,” the worker said.

They rested the board securely against the wall. The thin black receipt stayed on the old left edge of the opening. It hadn't adhered to the board or peeled away with it. Its writing still faced outward. Below, a narrow iron strip bordered the opening.

Level ground lay beyond.

Where Lin Mo had seen only a wall crack, he could now see tiles. Worn squares extended from the entrance, unlike the basement's concrete. They continued past the flashlight's reach toward a turn farther in.

The worker straightened and asked whether there was another room behind it.

“Shouldn't be this long,” the storage man said.

“What about next door?”

“I collect rent there too.”

He went out through the original iron door, leaving the flashlight with Zhou. From the passage beside the stairs, he checked the neighboring storage room, staying on a call with Yuan.

“Nothing here but stacked goods,” his voice said through the speaker.

Lin Mo watched the unknown corridor. Following the tiles' direction should put anyone inside beside the man on the phone. He asked him to leave the goods alone and photograph the room from somewhere he could reach by the existing path.

The picture showed an intact brick wall, sealed sacks, and a low slope overhead. Beneath the stairs. An adult had to bend to approach it.

Before Lin Mo, the corridor was tall enough to enter upright.

“Knock on the wall this side,” the storage man said.

Lin Mo didn't send anyone with a tool. Zhou tapped twice with his knuckles on the real-world doorframe. The same sounds didn't immediately emerge from the phone. After a pause, the speaker reported hearing them, muffled.

The frame could carry sound across. The opening's depth didn't fit.

“Come back,” Lin Mo said.

“I'll look a little farther over.”

“Don't find another way. Leave through the door you used.”

After a silence, footsteps sounded. The storage man returned, his expression changed. Outside the original iron door, he looked at the lit squares, then back along his route.

“The wall's still there.”

Zhou stayed with the flashlight. Lin Mo followed its beam. The new opening beside the iron door didn't lead deeper into the basement. It entered the wall they'd descended beside. Beneath an ordinary staircase, a straight corridor couldn't be this tall, with a turn several meters away where a person could still stand upright.

He didn't say Inner World.

“See that tile with the broken corner?” he asked Yuan.

“Yes.”

“How many behind it?”

Yuan counted to the third. The fourth lay in shadow. He stopped there, saying only that the distance was much greater than the wall's thickness.

The worker picked up his tools and retreated to the original door.

“I'm not taking anything else apart.”

“Nobody's asking,” the storage man said. “Go upstairs.”

The worker didn't leave. He pointed at the wheelchair. If they were going, everyone should go together. Yuan assured him that returning Lin Mo to the original stairs was his responsibility. He wouldn't leave him below.

Lin Mo listened without arguing.

The light crossed the receipt's lower end, revealing the words previously concealed.

Transferred into: Inner-Layer Storage.

Below, a line still referred to a receipt association with Core Backup. A black crack interrupted its end. No route, no door number. Lin Mo asked Yuan to photograph it without moving closer, then read it on the phone.

This receipt had already acknowledged that transfer record. His arrival hadn't made Xiao Lu stay behind this wall, waiting for him.

“Where's the inner layer?” Zhou asked quietly.

“I don't know yet.”

Lin Mo's gaze had already moved past the phone. A route led inward, a route that didn't belong to this basement. Waking on the concrete that day, he'd found no door he'd come through. Now an ordinary board had concealed depths he couldn't reconcile with the building.

He wanted to go forward.

His left foot pressed lightly against the floor. The chair didn't move. Yuan had locked the brakes.

“We agreed we'd look from here today.”

Lin Mo released the pressure.

“I want to see the threshold.”

“I'll light it for you.”

Yuan had his colleague take the handles, then moved obliquely alongside the opening. The iron strip bowed slightly in the middle. Its outer edge rested on concrete; a black gap crossed its inner edge. Narrow as it was, a small caster might fit into it and lodge.

Lin Mo remembered the metal lip that had blocked the low cart before the front stage's outer door. Someone had laid broken wood there to get him across. That piece wasn't here. Nobody knew what supported this iron edge.

“Don't step on it.”

Yuan kept his feet outside.

The storage man brought a straight wooden batten left from moving goods. On the real-world side, Yuan pressed its end to check for rot, then slowly extended it through the opening, testing only visible ground.

It struck the first tile with a hard sound.

He lifted it and moved it slightly farther. As its end passed the broken-corner tile, the wood dipped.

Yuan stopped without yanking. He asked his colleague to brace, then backed away slowly. The batten scratched fresh lines across the iron strip before returning to the basement.

Fine dust coated its tip. A new slanting scrape had shaved away part of the underside.

“Does the floor slope down there?” he asked.

“I don't know.”

Lin Mo had seen the wood dip. A tile could have shifted; there could have been a void beneath it; something inside might have pulled it down. He asked Yuan to put the batten aside without trying again.

Nobody had established that this path could hold a person.

The storage man raised the light. It still couldn't reach around the turn. Something dark and soft hung there like a torn curtain, its edge unmoved by the basement's draft.

The theater had often used black fabric like that. Lin Mo couldn't identify a room from it. The front stage had collapsed. Similar-colored cloth couldn't reconstruct a familiar route.

He looked at the chair's small front wheels.

They'd measured the opening. The wheelchair would fit with room on both sides. But fitting through a frame said nothing about each tile's ability to support it. All his practice in the living room wouldn't level a sinking joint.

“Time to go,” Yuan said.

Lin Mo kept his head down. He wanted them to push him to the iron strip. Just a little farther, then lift the front casters. Beyond that, they could deal with the route as they went. That was how he'd survived the theater. He hadn't waited for guaranteed safety.

Yuan's hands remained on the handles.

This man hadn't survived the theater beside him. He'd agreed to take him downstairs, stay while he looked, and bring him back. Lin Mo knew how much that unspoken little farther would add to the agreement.

“Back to the original door,” he finally said.

They withdrew slowly. At the turn, a pebble rolled beneath Lin Mo's left sole. His foot slipped. Instinctively reaching for the hand rim, he struck his braced wrist against the armrest and jerked back in pain. Yuan stopped while his colleague righted the leg support.

Breathing hard, Lin Mo saw that he was still two steps from the unknown opening.

The worker had reached the stairs. Hearing them, he returned and asked if they needed help lifting. Yuan asked him instead to hold the original door clear of the wheels. He put down his tools and braced it with a foot, without urging them to hurry.

The man who'd insisted on stopping was still keeping Lin Mo's retreat open. His urge for another look finally subsided.

A mistake this small could strand him inside if nobody was there to catch him.

“Did you damage your hand?” Yuan asked.

“No. Brace is still in place. Give me a moment.”

They waited before turning him. He didn't hide the collision until he got home. Later, at his examination, he described it accurately. The doctor's restrictions on his wrist and shoulder stayed the same. A new passage in a basement didn't change them.

Before they left, the storage man asked about the board.

“Don't let people come to look,” Lin Mo said. “Stop bringing goods into this room.”

“Needs something across it.”

They didn't push the board against the unknown opening. Zhou brought two ordinary barriers and placed them well back on the real-world side. The basement entrance would be locked, the storage man retaining the key. Lin Mo couldn't promise barriers would stop anything inside. They could at least stop an unsuspecting person bringing down a box.

The storage man would contact people who dealt with hazardous premises and explain what they'd photographed. Lin Mo sent him the images without asking for secrecy to preserve his own access.

“If they can seal it, let them.”

Zhou looked at him.

“Don't you still want to go in?”

“Yes. But somebody passing through can't go first.”

On the stairs, Lin Mo didn't turn toward the corridor. As the transfer chair rose slowly, he heard the iron door shut below. An ordinary click of its lock.

At street level, Yuan pushed him toward the vehicle and asked whether he wanted the painful wrist checked. Lin Mo said yes. A follow-up appointment was already part of the return journey.

Before the vehicle door closed, his phone sounded.

The storage man had sent one final photograph. Barriers stood on the concrete. Behind them, locking an ordinary door hadn't turned the opening back into a wall.

Lin Mo enlarged the broken-corner tile farther inside. Dust covered it. Beside the batten's fine scrape was a semicircle pressed by the toe of a shoe.

He hadn't seen anyone go through.

He asked Yuan to call the photographer. Had he crossed the iron strip? No, the man said. He'd stayed outside the barriers. His own soles were still wet.

The footprint inside had dry edges.

“Go upstairs,” Lin Mo said. “Don't go back for another picture.”
