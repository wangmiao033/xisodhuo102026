# Chapter 109: Pin Down What Can Rise

The next day, Lin Mo didn't have to wait until half past one.

Xu called after breakfast. They had a different plan. Tang was there too, wearing another work shirt, a drawing of the box spread before him.

Lin Mo asked about his arm first.

“Can hold my chopsticks. Better than yours.”

“Good.”

“That's all you're asking?”

“What else? Want me to make you lift it for the camera?”

Tang laughed, then pushed the drawing into view.

Yesterday they'd intended to dismantle the whole box while pinning down the face that had displayed the number. Today every panel had its own restraint. None relied on another panel for support. The old reinforcing strip was heavily circled. Beside it: Secure first. Release clips afterward.

“No cutting one side loose and letting another spring up. We hold every piece before separating them.”

Lin Mo studied the black shape at the bottom of the drawing. The detached scrap beneath the shutter, distinct from the main sheet on the sign.

“This stays.”

“Yes. No tools underneath it.”

Xu raised the camera. They'd extended the barrier and left an empty strip beyond it, putting the work beyond casual reach. Someone else watched the sign's spindle from behind the counter. Service at the old window remained suspended, shutter down.

Farther away, a cleaning trolley passed. Its operator waved to Xu and followed the diversion. Yesterday he'd had to explain that route over and over. Today someone took it without being told.

“No need to keep the video running,” Tang said. “Xu can show you as we finish each section. Stop bracing yourself the whole time.”

Lin Mo nearly asked them to leave the sound on. As though hearing every clink was necessary for the work to count. It wouldn't give him another hand.

“As long as everyone doing it can stop whenever they need. Call if anything changes.”

Xu sounded surprised but agreed.

Afterward, the room seemed enormous. Lin Mo watched the dark screen, a finger resting where the answer button had been. The unseen minutes wouldn't become safe merely because he wasn't watching. But neither could everything the others did depend on permission traveling through him.

The nurse came to change his dressings.

His finger wound had begun to close, though he still couldn't put force through it. When she uncovered his right knee, he caught the sheet, remembered the finger, and slowly let go. She waited for his breath before continuing. The doctor came to reassess his shoulder, asking where it hurt before having him attempt a small movement.

He couldn't reach as far as before.

The pain had eased somewhat since yesterday's prolonged tension. That didn't give him an arm he could freely raise. He needed continued support and help even with short transfers. He mustn't push himself out of a wheelchair with his hands.

“Could I go out in one for ten minutes?”

“Where?”

“The staff passage downstairs. Not inside the yellow line.”

“Yesterday, a phone call was enough to leave you in that much pain.”

Lin Mo didn't argue. He explained: something now held in place might relate to what he'd experienced before. He wanted to look from behind the counter, accompanied, without touching equipment.

“First, let's see how you manage a transfer and sitting today. Having a good reason doesn't make your body ready.”

“All right.”

The care worker straightened his trousers and steadied his back and waist while he sat on the bed's edge. He placed his feet, keeping the right leg where it could tolerate the position rather than using it to bear the weight the staff supported. Moving forward, he instinctively reached for the mattress. His wrist support touched the sheet. He stopped.

“Wait for us,” she said.

They adjusted, and two people helped him into the hospital wheelchair. Arm supported, feet settled. The transfer took longer than his entire explanation of why he wanted to go downstairs.

He lasted only a short while before needing the bed again.

“That's enough today,” the doctor said. “We'll reassess tomorrow morning.”

Lin Mo looked at the nearby door. He didn't ask for another attempt. Even if he insisted on reaching the counter, someone would still have to get him back.

Xu's first photograph arrived after he was lying down.

One corner of the box had been separated. Independent restraints held the panels, with spaces between the wooden supports. The face that had displayed twenty remained downward; it could no longer brace itself against the other sides. The exposed scrap under the shutter looked unchanged. It hadn't followed the plastic away.

A short message accompanied the image: This section's secure.

Lin Mo studied the pieces of wood, each taking its own load. Tang had repaired that box. Familiarity had made him overlook something yesterday. Today the same hands were having to dismantle that familiarity and work through it piece by piece.

The second update took much longer. A short video this time.

A clamp had caught on old adhesive. Instead of pulling harder, Tang changed position to cut into the ordinary glue from the side. No hands appeared in the frame. Cloudy adhesive slowly stretched into a thread, then settled onto the plastic. The red stroke stayed down.

Lin Mo typed Good, deleted it, and wrote Received. Those seconds had already passed. He couldn't approve the next movement on the workers' behalf.

They called again before noon.

The box was apart.

It hadn't become a heap of fragments to sweep away. Clear panels remained where they'd been worked on, each held flat by broad strips. The red-marked face pointed at the floor. Two sections of the frame had been cut, preventing it from supporting the original box shape. No voice. No small red light standing above the tiles.

Feng shone a lamp sideways, pointing out the gaps to Xu. Paper under the shutter; plastic beside it. Nothing packed into another storage box.

“Those panels can't be reused as scrap,” Lin Mo said.

Tang's hand entered the picture, pointing at the warning already fixed nearby.

“Written down. We're not handing them out for lampshades.”

Lin Mo smiled. His shoulder didn't immediately punish him this time. He didn't test it with a second laugh.

Next came the main window.

They left the original wedge in place. Tang added an independent mechanical stop on the outside of the sign bracket. Disturbing one restraint wouldn't let the spindle snap back. A solid barrier blocked the public approach, without touching the glass or pressing against the small scrap beneath the shutter.

Service Suspended still faced the hall. Nobody looking for the wrong department could casually reach into the opening now.

Xu showed Lin Mo the rear of the sign.

The thin access cover Liang had mentioned sat behind its supporting plate. Its screws were separate from the sign's spindle. Rust had eaten away one corner. Tang first braced the edge of the original support, then unscrewed the cover from the staff side.

He paused after each screw. The main black sheet remained attached. It didn't lift or follow the cover. One edge was still caught along the old support beside the spindle, its split running down.

Removing the cover exposed a larger rust hole in the supporting plate.

Light reached a thin dark-gray surface behind it, the back of the black sheet. Pale fibers frayed along an old split. At first it looked water-damaged, although thicker than ordinary paper.

“Were there letters here before?” Tang asked.

Liang leaned into the edge of the shot. She stayed behind the counter and didn't reach in.

“The old inventory number wasn't inside. I didn't add that layer.”

Xu shifted the camera upward. The phone hunted for focus. Pale, short marks did show on the dark surface, tangled with old adhesive. Change the lamp's angle, and glare swallowed them again.

Lin Mo's breathing slowed.

He had seen that sort of arrangement. Letters set apart from the straight ruled lines used on hospital forms. A narrow margin at one side, as though meant to fit into some sort of reading rack. Recognizing a shape wasn't enough to name the indistinct strokes.

“Don't wipe it.”

Tang stopped, cloth halfway out of his bag.

“I was wiping the lamp.”

Heat rose in Lin Mo's ears. “Right.”

Tang cleaned the lamp cover and aimed it again without making a joke. Light entered through the rust hole. The paper stayed attached, as though they'd finally discovered an existing crack behind a thick wall.

“Could I look again tomorrow?” Lin Mo asked.

“You're not asking to come now?” Xu said.

“Doctor won't let me today.”

“Following orders for once,” Tang remarked.

“I wasn't planning to crawl downstairs.”

Lin Mo explained the arrangement. Reassessment in the morning. If permitted, a hospital wheelchair through the short corridor to the rear room. The front would remain closed. Nobody would bring the object out to meet him. Xu would speak to the responsible staff. Being able to sit wasn't enough if the route overlapped with work at the scene.

Lin Mo agreed. Tomorrow's hope didn't have to become today's demand.

The afternoon report came after further observation. No panel had risen, twenty hadn't reappeared, and the main sign remained at Service Suspended. People still used the diversion. They hadn't boxed in the opening behind the removed cover. Supports held the old plate from its open sides, preserving a viewing angle from the rear.

Zhao Yun stopped by. The black mark on her father's hand had faded a little more. He still couldn't reliably hold his spoon. Lin Mo told her to report the change to his doctor. He didn't count the improvement as proof of anything he'd dismantled. Mr. He, the injured installer, still had to keep his foot raised. Silence downstairs wasn't a reason to test his weight on it.

In the evening photograph, someone pushed a child past the outer barrier. The child pointed. The parent shook their head and took the other route. Nobody took a ticket or stopped at a summons.

Before changing shifts, Xu visited with a new drawing of the site. He pointed out the two locations: the main sign and the separate scrap on the floor.

“Could I have a copy?” Lin Mo asked.

“I'll photograph it. We keep the original.”

Xu thought he was worried. Everyone on watch had seen it, he added. Lin Mo explained that he was glad. If he fell asleep tomorrow night, the place wouldn't depend on one unanswered call to remain guarded.

Xu considered him, then smiled.

“It was never your shift.”

“I understand that now.”

“Then eat. I've got to get home too. I'd rather not explain that I was waiting for permission from someone who can't hold his own bowl.”

Lin Mo told him to go. Xu waved from the door without adding another promise about tomorrow. The two marked locations remained on the phone. His footsteps receded.

Lin Mo returned to the photograph of the hall and watched it for a while.

He remembered the dream in which his sister said the cup would break. He couldn't recover her face from it now. He remembered only that he'd finally stopped pressing the lid.

Tomorrow's reassessment details lay beside his phone. He asked the care worker to take out his notebook and open it to an earlier page. Old Chen's name was still there, written in a steadier hand than his recent left-handed entries.

He didn't ask her to turn farther.

That indistinct surface hadn't yet earned the right to reopen every old wound. Tomorrow he would look himself, bringing what he remembered, and the body that someone else would have to help, very slowly, out of bed.
