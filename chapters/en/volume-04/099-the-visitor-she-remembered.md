# Chapter 99: The Visitor She Remembered

Across from the waiting area, a consultation-room door stood ajar. A table, three chairs, an empty water container in the corner.

Lin Mo lifted the photograph from his open notebook. Its edge caught beneath the pen clip. He loosened the clip instead of pulling. His sister's hand still hovered, blurred, in the air. He laid the photograph over the sleeved receipt, covering the patient's name from passing eyes.

The escort checked the time and asked whether he needed the bathroom.

“After she comes.”

Then he remembered the water he'd drunk, the way his leg had shaken last time he'd waited too long.

“Actually, yes. Could you tell the nurse we'll be right back?”

The short trip took more than ten minutes. He kept listening for his name. When they returned, the young nurse nodded.

“Nurse Zhou isn't back yet. Her colleague has passed on the message.”

She asked to check the original receipt again. He eased it from its sleeve and watched her work at the station.

Last time he'd spread that paper on a counter in this hospital, he'd wanted someone to admit his sister had existed. Rain had softened its corner. A white-haired man occupied bed six. Her cup, slippers, and half an apple had preserved no place for her.

“Is that an old admission you've found?” he asked.

“An index entry matching the admission number. We'll check the details with Nurse Zhou.”

“Not whoever's in bed six now?”

“Different patients use the beds. We didn't search by today's bed number.”

He nodded. His chest remained tight. Once, this same receipt had found nobody. Now someone was bringing him a person who might remember.

He couldn't tell when the difference had appeared.

He wanted the nurse who had turned him away too, but remembered only her hair falling over her badge as she bent toward the screen. Insisting this young woman was the same person would create another unreliable piece of evidence.

He opened his notebook. His new left-handed writing crowded beside the old. He added: Admission index found today using original number.

Beneath it: On the day she disappeared, this receipt failed to locate her.

He drew no arrow between them.

The temptation was to join them anyway: then and now, something taken and something returned. His hand could make a line in a second. The paper would hold it as readily as anything else he wrote. But a mark could only say what he thought. It couldn't tell him what had happened while he was unable to see.

A treatment trolley jolted over the doorway strip. A small box slid toward its edge. Lin Mo moved forward instinctively; pain caught his shoulder. He stopped. The nurse steadied the box herself.

“Thank you. Don't get up.”

He hadn't helped at all.

The escort moved his chair closer to the wall. Lin Mo adjusted his right foot on the footplate. His trousers brushed the dressing, sending pain upward. He didn't compare it with the theater anymore. Both kinds belonged to his body. Neither needed to earn the right to hurt.

Someone brought a bag of steamed buns. The smell of scallions reminded him of breakfast he'd once bought for his sister. Fine, she'd said. He'd eaten the remaining half and discovered how salty the filling was. Next morning he'd changed shops without telling her. She hadn't asked, merely pushed a second bun toward him before he cleared everything away.

He'd never thought such things required remembering.

The young nurse pointed down the corridor.

“Nurse Zhou. Over here.”

A woman in her forties approached, her hair tucked beneath her cap, a spot of water on her cuff. She said something to a colleague before coming to the front of his chair.

“You're looking for your sister? She was on the old ward?”

“Yes.”

He held out the photograph too quickly. His shoulder pulled. She took its edge and waited for him to release it.

She looked at his sister first, then the person beside her. Her eyes moved to Lin Mo's face.

“I remember her.”

His hand stayed in the air.

“Look carefully. Don't say it just because I've told you.”

“We'll check the records too.” She brought the photograph closer. “She made a cardboard sleeve for her cup. Complained that you wanted to replace everything.”

He remembered.

“Fold the bottom inward. Secure it with tape.”

“You wanted to test it. She said you'd pull it apart. I added two strips.”

As the nurse turned, he saw the small mole beside her ear. He hadn't given a stranger the face he needed. This woman had stood at that bed, explaining how to hold a cup.

“It didn't come loose,” he said.

“Good.”

Only the cup. She hadn't promised anything about the later days she didn't know.

His sister had deliberately left its crooked side facing him. It worked, she'd said. What if the water spilled? She'd shaken it, making him reach out. Her laughter became a cough. He forgot the cup and tried to help her.

Nurse Zhou had told him to slow down. Support her first.

Now she waited for his hand to steady before returning the photograph. A few inches of paper passing between them. Someone willing to wait until he could take it.

He took the photograph back and lowered his hand to his lap.

Paper had no voice. A person had just said that his sister had eaten here, worried about him here, waited for him in a particular bed.

He hadn't learned where she was. His eyes were already burning.

He'd prepared for a different answer. Too many patients, the nurse might say; she couldn't remember. Then he would tell her about the repaired cup and offer details until someone agreed to think again.

He didn't need those words now.

“You remember me too?”

“Yes. You're much thinner. With the injuries, I wasn't sure at first. The photograph helped.”

“You haven't mistaken me for someone else?”

She looked at the two people sitting close together.

“You said your name was Lin Mo?”

His name came easily. It wasn't identification read aloud or politeness to a stranger. Someone by that name had visited, sleeves wet, unable to stop worrying even about a cup.

And that visitor had always been the woman in the photograph.

He touched the fastening of his splint, then let go. He'd wanted to be recognized. Now what recognition gave back lay so close against what had been taken that he couldn't accept one without the other.

“Some things I remember aren't the same as yours.”

“Which things?”

“I need to start with my sister.”

“Shall we go inside? People keep passing.”

The escort wheeled him beside the table and applied the brakes. Nurse Zhou asked whether he wanted the man to remain.

“Would you wait outside?” Lin Mo asked him. “I'll still need your help afterward.”

“I'll be right there.”

Lin Mo asked the nurse to close the door. He gave her his original identity card and the receipt. Sitting at an angle to him, she asked his sister's name, basic details of the admission, and his own name.

He answered without supplying a new name for her.

The nurse checked the number, then went briefly to the computer next door to consult the indexed entry. When she returned, she still held his originals. She didn't make him tell the story from the beginning again.

“This receipt belongs to her admission. The family contact is Lin Mo. The end of the telephone number matches the one you gave me.”

A slight pause at the relationship.

“Elder sister.”

“You remember an elder sister bringing her meals?”

“I remember you.”

Too natural an answer. She hadn't avoided looking at his face to be kind. The short-haired woman in the photograph wasn't another person he should go looking for.

“What did she call me?”

“Jie, usually. Sometimes your name when she was impatient. I don't know whether you argued about it. Once she said you could manage anything outside the hospital, but in here you couldn't even decide whether the water had cooled without asking her.”

He'd heard that complaint.

His sister had asked how her brother could enter a ward and suddenly stop understanding words. She'd said the water wasn't hot; he still had to test the cup himself. In the nurse's memory, the complaint circled the same cup. Only the person it concerned had changed.

“I'm her brother.”

Nurse Zhou glanced at his splinted wrist, then his face.

“I can call you Lin Mo.”

“It isn't about which word sounds nicer.”

“All right. Tell me.”

He held the notebook's stiff cover. Where could he begin? With the person who had been in bed six that morning, or the receipt that found nobody later that day? The male identity card he'd held beside a mirror? The woman's face this nurse recognized?

“I remember being a man. I can see what my body looks like now. The photograph changed. Old handwriting changed. But the person bringing her meals was me. I remember.”

The nurse didn't begin guessing at a diagnosis.

“I can't explain those things. I can establish who you're asking about and tell you what I know about that admission.”

For a moment he listened to the corridor through the closed door. A voice called for someone. Wheels passed. Nobody outside knew that a family remembered in two different ways occupied this little room. He couldn't make the hospital stop while he found words large enough for it. He didn't have to make this woman responsible for every other answer he'd received, either. She was still sitting opposite him. She had offered the part she could tell.

He watched her hands. Short nails, skin pale from washing. She wasn't holding a pen poised to mark his answer wrong.

“All right.”

He turned the photograph over. His sister's writing faced the space between them, its last strokes rising. The nurse glanced at it without touching.

He wanted to ask again whether she remembered his sister calling him Ge. He left the question unspoken. She'd told him what she remembered. Asking harder wouldn't bring her recollection closer to his.

He put his identity card in the inner pocket of the cloth bag. Its corner touched the new phone. The screen woke, displaying today's date.

He'd kept placing this visit just after those three hours when his sister vanished. He hadn't counted every intervening day in the outside world.

“I was away,” he said. “I don't know how to explain that part. Before I left, I came here asking. Someone said bed six wasn't hers.”

“I may not have been on duty. I can't answer for that person.”

“But you can find her now.”

“Yes.”

She pushed back the receipt, pointed to the final digits of the admission number, and read his sister's name once more. He confirmed it carefully. Photograph, receipt, family contact, a cup repaired beside a bed: all belonged to the same person.

Nurse Zhou didn't send him to another counter.

“Tell me what you need to know first.”

He pressed his palm against the receipt's old crease. The words he'd written in the theater remained on its reverse, faint ink showing through the thin paper.

“Where is she now?”
