# Chapter 92: The Ink Mark Was Still There

“I'm a man.”

The woman at the stairhead lowered her phone as though she hadn't heard. Zhou opened his mouth. Before he spoke, emergency responders were asking people to clear the way.

A woman in uniform crouched beside Lin Mo and introduced herself.

“Can you tell me your name?”

“Lin Mo.”

She asked where it hurt most, whether he could breathe, when he'd lost consciousness. He answered until the last question.

“I don't know how long it's been.”

“That's all right. You woke up here?”

“Yes. Other people came out with me.”

She asked Zhou. He'd only seen Lin Mo; some partitions remained unchecked. She asked the people upstairs to pass that on to whoever dealt with the site, then returned her attention to her patient.

She didn't promise to find everyone he couldn't see.

He flinched as the blood-pressure cuff tightened. She stopped and explained, checking whether that part of his arm could take pressure. He nodded. She loosened the bag strap while her colleague supported the bottom, keeping it in view as they settled it against his abdomen.

Using their equipment, they carried him up the narrow flight. Zhou still braced the iron door. The street was painfully bright. Cartons stood waist-high beside a truck. Someone in slippers retrieved oranges from beside a wheel.

Nobody was waiting for him to perform.

He named his sister's hospital.

“Can you take me there?”

Asked why, he said his sister was there. The responder checked his readings and explained that they would go to the nearest emergency department equipped for his injuries. Contacting family could follow. He wanted to argue, but a movement of the stretcher sent pain through his calf.

“Let them treat you first,” Zhou said.

Lin Mo nodded, holding the other hospital's name in his mind.

Before the ambulance doors closed, he saw Zhou ask where they were going and type into his phone. The promise had become an ordinary, achievable thing. Whether anyone would come asking after him remained unknown.

The emergency doors opened automatically.

He passed a row of waiting chairs. An old man holding a food flask stood, realized Lin Mo wasn't whom he wanted and sat again. A nurse took the handover. Curtains closed around the bed. The noise remained, but his body was no longer exposed to the street.

“Ms. Lin, we need to remove—”

“Call me Lin Mo.”

“All right. Lin Mo. There's blood and dirt on your clothes. We need to examine your injuries. I'll tell you before we do anything. Is that okay?”

He nodded.

His fingers couldn't manage the broad cloth belt. The harder he tried, the more its end caught in a fold. The nurse waited, then offered to help. He moved his hands aside.

The gray coat came off first, its left sleeve eased away while someone supported his injured shoulder. The blue garment and skirt followed. A sheet covered him throughout.

The stocking had stuck near his calf wound. The nurse showed him the scissors and waited for permission before cutting.

Watching the fabric split, he remembered all the times somebody had waited for him to undress willingly. Only when the scissors stopped did he realize he'd stopped breathing.

“Does that hurt?”

“Not there.”

She lowered them. “We can wait.”

He turned his face away and counted the two pairs of shoes below the curtain. Then he said she could continue. He didn't have to explain why a pause mattered. She moistened the stuck fabric and gathered the cut pieces into a small bag rather than throwing them away.

The nurse put his old white cloth into the small bag too. With clean dressings against his skin, he no longer had to search for a corner unstained enough to cover a wound.

He asked to keep his clothes.

“All of them. These go together. Your personal bag stays beside you; we won't open it.”

The coat, blue garment, skirt and belt went into a belongings bag. He retrieved his notebook first and set it beside the pillow, pen still clipped. The coat's inner pocket hadn't been emptied. Only then did he let the nurse close the larger bag.

The hospital clothes were tied loosely enough to spare his bruised waist.

A doctor asked about falls, crushing, blows to the head. Had someone hurt him?

“Yes.” It emerged more quietly than he'd expected.

“Can you tell me who?”

He thought of the hands inside the mirrors, the stage he couldn't find again. None of the names he knew supplied an address where police could knock.

“I can't explain yet. Can we deal with the injuries?”

“Of course. Tell us when you're ready.”

The doctor recorded his answer and continued. He asked Lin Mo to move his toes, checked for numbness and felt around the swollen wrist. When his shoulder hurt sharply, the doctor stopped. Before examining his chest and abdomen, he explained each step. The nurse stayed.

Lin Mo stared at his hand outside the sheet.

“My body…”

The doctor waited.

“You see a woman's body?”

“The physical findings are female. Are you concerned about previous records, or a recent change?”

“A change. I wasn't like this before.”

“Have you had related treatment? Any previous medical records we could consult?”

“No treatment like that. I have my ID.”

The doctor didn't demand he prove his sanity or make his reluctance an explanation for his injuries. He checked that Lin Mo understood the questions and could express his wishes. They would address his injuries and dehydration first. The changes could be discussed in detail once he was stable.

Lin Mo accepted the order.

But as the doctor turned, he called him back.

“I'm not making a joke.”

“I understand this is distressing.”

“When I say I'm a man, I don't mean I can't see my body.”

This time the doctor watched him closely and let him finish. Clutching the sheet, Lin Mo said he needed his sister. She knew him. If he could find her, he wouldn't have to prove everything from the beginning to strangers. He spoke too fast, coughed and lost the rest.

The doctor moved a cup within reach, without yet clearing him to drink.

“We can use your name. The findings stay what they are; your account can be recorded as you give it. First, let's get you through today safely.”

Lin Mo released the sheet. He hadn't found someone who believed everything. At least he didn't have to surrender himself to keep the bed.

Tests proceeded one by one. The waiting took longer. Outside imaging, relatives quietly discussed who would go home to feed a cat. Lin Mo suddenly wondered whether his sister had water. The thought struck without reason. He drew her bag closer. The cup remained inside, its lid tightened for her by somebody else.

Back in the treatment area, the nurse irrigated his wounds. As the grit came away, the pain grew distinct. He clenched his teeth until she told him he could make a sound. Then he released the breath he'd been holding.

Results arrived gradually. The examinations hadn't revealed an injury requiring immediate surgery. His right wrist still needed immobilization, his shoulder protection. The soft-tissue injuries to his knee and leg couldn't bear unrestricted weight. Contaminated wounds, dehydration and exhaustion required treatment. Given the unclear loss of consciousness, overnight observation was safer.

“Can I leave tomorrow?”

“We'll reassess tomorrow. Getting out of bed isn't the same as walking home alone.”

He wanted to say he had someone to find. Looking at his supported wrist, he changed it.

“I need to contact people.”

The nurse could lend him the department phone when he had a number. If nobody could collect him, he should say so early, so they could work out what came next. She offered no promise about missing people, but put the call button within reach.

He asked about payment. He had no phone and couldn't say how much money he could produce.

First they would verify his identity; then they could establish the charges and payment options. His immediate treatment would continue. He asked for an itemized paper bill. She said the care would be documented and he'd receive one.

He'd taken so many bills through hospital windows for his sister, counting out money. Now he lay while a stranger explained. He disliked having to look up, disliked admitting he couldn't provide a definite answer.

As she adjusted the dressing on his hand, he finally thanked her.

“Call if you feel unwell. Don't fall out of bed trying to save us trouble.”

He tested the button's position with his unsplinted hand before setting it beside the pillow.

Once permitted, he drank slowly. The warm water showed him how thirsty he was. He wanted to save half, then realized he could ask for more here. He finished it.

The nurse returned to complete registration.

“Did you bring identification?”

“Inner pocket of the gray coat.”

She set the belongings bag beside him and asked him to point it out. With his better hand, he found the coat and its inner pocket. Paper brushed his fingers first: the old payment slip. He left it there and eased out the card behind it.

It was cold against his palm.

The nurse switched on the bedside light. First he saw his name.

Lin Mo.

The characters were unchanged. Nobody had added a grass radical or substituted something thought more suitable. He looked below them and stopped at a single word.

Female.

He turned the card over, then back.

“This is wrong.”

The nurse didn't take it. “It isn't yours?”

He stared at the photograph.

The person sat very straight, lips set, visibly unwilling to remain before a camera. But the jaw had narrowed. The features belonged with his present body. The broad, hard-edged face was gone; even its incongruous solemnity had been preserved for a different appearance.

“It's mine.”

She waited.

He remembered receiving it. More importantly, he remembered taking it out inside the theater and checking that it still showed a man. He'd put corresponding little marks on the card, payment slip and notebook so he could distinguish a change in memory from a change in the things he held.

He tilted the card toward the light.

The little ink mark remained.

He rubbed it. It didn't come off. It occupied the place he remembered, surviving the replacement photograph without preserving the word beside it. His hand grew cold.

“Could the computer have entered it wrong just now?”

He knew the mistake in that question before he'd finished. The nurse hadn't scanned his card. Today's computer couldn't have changed the photograph inside his coat beforehand.

“That's printed on the ID itself,” she said gently. “If you believe it's wrong, you can check earlier records afterward. I'll use the document for today's registration, and note your concern.”

“Don't write that I remembered wrong.”

“I'll write what you said.”

She added a sentence and showed him. He read every word, then allowed her to continue. The machine sounded. She checked the name on the screen and returned the card.

He didn't ask again about the sex field.

He laid the ID beside the notebook. One was an old possession brought through from the other side. The other held what he'd written of his experience. Both pressed into the sheet, their weights perfectly compatible.

He couldn't make them accounts of the same person's past.

Outside, somebody asked who was staying with him overnight. The nurse said he hadn't reached family yet; leave the chair. Its legs scraped across the tiles and stopped beside his bed.

Looking at the empty seat, Lin Mo said the name on the card aloud.

His name still recognized him.

So did the ink mark he'd left to identify the original.
