# Chapter 116: An Address to Return To

As Tang lay flat on the mat, Lin Mo asked him to take off his keys first.

They hung before the shutter's bottom track, barely two fingers from a clamp securing the transparent panel. Tang looked down, rolled over, and pocketed them. Feng caught the tilting lamp stand and gathered its cable behind his feet.

“The piece on the floor this time,” Lin Mo said. “Leave it where it is.”

Six days after the injuries at the old window, the barriers remained. Two benches blocked the direct approach; people coming for service went around. Lin Mo's hospital wheelchair stood outside the benches, the care worker at its handles. His right leg rested on a cushion, his left forearm on its support. The doctor had approved this one visit. Then he had to go back.

Tang set his phone sideways on a small stand, pointing beneath the shutter. Without putting it inside, he enlarged the image until the half-character for please filled the screen.

The scrap lay against the floor beside the flattened transparent panels. The old red marks faced down, secured on every side. Farther in, the bottom track's shadow hid the paper's edge.

That was what Lin Mo needed to see.

Yesterday's continuation mark on the main sheet had only a corner and short horizontal stroke left. It followed Xiao Lu's transfer record toward an acknowledgment of receipt they hadn't found. This scrap lay closest. All night, he'd imagined joining the edges and finally learning where to go next.

“Can you get any lower?”

Tang lowered the camera. Floor tiles immediately blocked half the view.

“That's it. Farther in, I'd touch the panel.”

Lin Mo stared into the darkness. The care worker drew his chair back half a wheel's turn. Only then did he notice he'd been leaning forward, his right knee almost against the bench.

“Look from back here,” she said.

Feng changed the light's angle. The half-character brightened, its last stroke running toward the floor through the torn edge. The crease beside the main sheet's continuation mark turned upward. Side by side on the phone, the visible edges didn't fit. What the track concealed remained unseen; nobody could settle that part.

Tang sat up, brushing dust from his palms.

“You need the back?”

“Maybe. Maybe it's the wrong piece altogether.”

“We'd have to move it to look.”

A crutch shifted nearby. Mr. He stood beyond the barrier, his right foot off the floor, his wife supporting him. They'd been heading for his follow-up appointment when they saw the wheelchair.

Lin Mo looked at the injured leg. Mr. He pointed at the clamps without asking what they'd found.

“Can it still spring up?”

“It's pinned down,” Tang said. “We're leaving it that way.”

“Good.”

His wife urged him not to keep standing. He nodded, but his crutch slipped out of position. Feng steadied a chair against the wall so he could sit. His foot hung above the floor, his lower leg swinging gently. Only after he pressed a hand to his knee did his face ease.

“Still have to stop at the factory this afternoon,” he said.

“What for?” his wife asked.

“Explain my jobs. They've been covering for days.”

She looked at him without arguing. Lin Mo returned to the phone. Beneath the track, the darkness hadn't changed.

“Put the camera away.”

Tang looked up.

“If we can't see it, leave it. Don't loosen those clamps for something I want.”

Feng withdrew the lamp and coiled its cable. Tang put away the stand, then took a wider photograph from the same spot. The floor, which Lin Mo had reduced to one corner, expanded again to include shutter, benches, and the two fastening rods nobody could touch.

Lin Mo nodded as Mr. He got up. Maneuvering around the wheelchair, the injured man said that if he'd known there'd be so many hospital visits, he'd have bought shoes he could get off more easily.

“Still time,” his wife said.

“You'll have to choose them.”

Lin Mo waited until the crutch disappeared around the corner before asking to go to the rear room. Half his visit was already gone.

The crack in the paper was silent.

Zou checked the front and rear fastenings, then laid his phone on the empty table. The main sheet remained on its old support, a piece missing from the end. The iron frame below hadn't changed. No gray had gathered in the crack.

Lin Mo gave his name and waited. No answer.

“Did he speak last night?”

“Asked once whether there was a window here,” Zou said. “Nothing afterward. We didn't keep calling.”

Lin Mo glanced around the windowless room. Outside, someone dragged an empty box across the floor. An old needle sound stirred in the crack, then stopped. He didn't call again.

Tang opened yesterday's full photograph. Beside the half-mark, two faint characters remained. While they'd concentrated on the missing edge, these had sat at the margin.

Return transfer.

No direction or address followed. Lin Mo asked for the whole photograph again, confirmed that the words lay against the continuation line, then had a close-up saved.

“Return it where?” Zou asked. “Another window here?”

“I don't know. We can't follow the hospital's returns procedure. The hospital didn't print this.”

He rested against the back of the chair. The backstage receiving room, the outer door, the final wall crowded into a continuous passage in his mind. Beyond that outer door, he knew only one place in the real world.

A basement.

He'd woken there without the cart or the person who'd pushed it. His sister's bag had remained in his arms. Something from the theater had crossed that door with him and reached the same concrete floor.

He explained this to Zou without calling the basement an entrance.

“I want to go back and look.”

“Do you know where it is?”

“The ambulance collected me there. They should have recorded the address.”

“And if there's nothing?”

“I come back. This paper hasn't said it's there.”

Tang pocketed his phone and crouched to check he hadn't disturbed the support while gathering the cable. Watching his back, Lin Mo suddenly wished someone would forbid the trip. A clear refusal almost seemed easier than having only a place worth trying.

The care worker checked the time.

“Ask upstairs. You've been sitting long enough.”

He agreed. He took nothing from the framework, only photographs on a phone. As the chair backed toward the door, a short breath came from the paper.

“Old Chen?”

“Finished… asking?”

“For today. I'm going upstairs.”

Silence, then the old sound of a thick needle striking wood. Lin Mo waited for it to pass before moving. He didn't know whose questions Old Chen meant, or from when. He didn't give him return transfer and ask him to find a route.

Back beside the bed, his right leg shook badly during the transfer. The care worker steadied him while someone else settled his leg on the mattress. He caught his breath, then asked for his hospital papers. She waited until his color improved before pulling the bedside table over.

They showed his arrival time and treatment here, but no collection address.

He'd expected to open them and find it. Disappointment sharpened his voice as he asked whether a page was missing. The care worker looked up, her hand still on the papers.

“These are all the ones you brought.”

He closed his eyes briefly.

“Wrong question. Please help me find out where to ask.”

That afternoon, a staff member checked his name, admission time, and identification. They could help contact the transport service about its records. His description of a basement wasn't enough to produce an address. He answered each question, then ate and had his dressings changed. His phone couldn't remove the waiting.

By evening, the collection point had been verified.

He recognized the street and building number. The woman on the stairs that day had stood with her phone, checked the old number on the door, and repeated it. Afraid somebody might return and fail to find him, he'd tried desperately to remember. Now he needed another piece of paper to put the words together.

He asked the care worker to write the address separately, then asked the hospital to pass on his number. If Old Zhou, who'd helped that day, was willing to call, he wanted to ask whether he could return.

Near the evening rounds, his phone rang.

“The one on the stairs?” a man asked.

“Yes. Lin Mo.”

“I remember. Why are you looking for this place again?”

Lin Mo explained that he wanted to check something, not send Zhou downstairs to search. Zhou moved aside for a reversing truck and waited out its horn. The place was still there. Someone had cleared the clutter from the top of the stairs, but the fallen rack below hadn't moved.

“Lost something?”

“Something might have been left. It might also be dangerous.”

“Those pipes need fixing. Water everywhere.”

“More than water. If you see a door that shouldn't be there, or hear someone calling names, don't go in.”

Zhou paused.

“How did you get hurt that day?”

Looking at his wrist brace, Lin Mo said he still couldn't explain where he'd fallen from. He knew someone had hurt him in a place other people couldn't see. He didn't ask Zhou to accept that account, or hide why he wanted to return.

“Just help me confirm the doorway. I'll bring people when I go inside.”

“Get yourself better first,” Zhou said. “Call before you come. Don't sit down there waiting to be found again.”

Afterward, Lin Mo asked for the address to be placed on top of his medical papers. Then he asked about wheelchair-accessible transport. On the phone, he gave the departure point, destination, and who would accompany him. When he mentioned stairs, the operator stopped him.

“We can take you to the door. We don't carry people down steps.”

“Only one flight.”

“Any number. You can't brace yourself with either hand. What happens if something goes wrong?”

Lin Mo didn't explain how he'd once managed stairs. He remembered Zhou's outstretched hand, being unable even to call out by the third step. Going down was only half the journey. Whatever he found, someone had to bring him back.

“Is there a service for stair transfers?”

The operator gave him a number to try. First, ask the hospital whether he could leave, then get clear pictures of the stairs' width and turns. Lin Mo had the number written beneath the address without booking.

The care worker put down her pen.

“Were you thinking of tomorrow?”

He looked at the photograph on the table.

“You could barely sit up when we got back.”

“I know.”

“Knowing that and arranging a safe trip are two different things.”

Anger rose. She'd turned pages and helped with calls all day. Now that he finally had somewhere to go, she put this in his way. He almost said he could find someone else. Then he saw her hand stop, the water cup still out of reach.

That hand owed him nothing.

“Don't book yet,” he said. “Tomorrow I'll ask the doctor what I need to be able to do.”

She brought the rim to his mouth. The water had gone cold. He asked for warm water instead of saying it was fine. For now, getting anywhere under his own power began with asking clearly for the help he needed.

When the doctor arrived, he'd put his phone aside. He explained the narrow stairs and the assistance he'd need in both directions. The doctor didn't approve the trip. First, he wanted a full account of today's pain.

Outside, it was dark. His sister's photograph lay faceup beside him. Looking at her unsteady hand, he remembered the relief an ordinary address had given him.

He still had no way through.

At least a car could take him to the next place he needed to look.
