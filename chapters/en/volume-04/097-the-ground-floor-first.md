# Chapter 97: The Ground Floor First

The switchboard answered while Lin Mo was still checking the last digit.

A recording offered several services. His finger moved before it had finished, then stopped. He listened through the options and selected an operator, leaving the phone flat on the desk.

The woman who answered named the hospital.

The name matched his receipt.

“I'm trying to ask about someone who was a patient there.”

“Do you need a copy of the records, or are you looking for a department?”

His prepared sentence stopped. Where is my sister wouldn't become something she could answer merely because he chose one of those options.

“The department first. I have the original payment receipt.”

She asked him to read the ward name, then checked that he was reading it from the payment document. He repeated the printed words without looking elsewhere for an answer better suited to the present.

A keyboard clicked.

“That ward has been reorganized. Don't go straight to the location on the old receipt.”

“Where should I go?”

“I'll transfer you to the nursing office so you can check the arrangements. If we're disconnected, call the switchboard again.”

He noted the extension. Music began. He nearly lifted the phone to his ear, afraid his sister might speak quietly somewhere inside it. His shoulder tightened. He left it down and raised the volume instead.

Nobody called him Ge.

Another voice asked what he needed.

Lin Mo explained again, including the full printed ward name. He couldn't assume that saying it once meant this person had already heard.

“I'm a relative. I'm trying to find one of the nurses who looked after her. I can't get in touch with her.”

“We can't give you a patient's information over the phone based only on a payment receipt. Can you come in?”

“Yes. But I'm injured. I'll be in a wheelchair.”

“Come through the main entrance and ask at the ground-floor information desk. Don't go around to the old ward. Show them the receipt and ask them to contact the nursing office. We can help connect you with the former ward's staff.”

He drew a little box and wrote ground floor.

“Tomorrow morning?”

“You can come and ask. What we can find will depend on the documents and circumstances. Please don't take this to mean we've already located the person.”

He sat with his pen held still.

“I understand. So far, I only know where to go.”

She asked about his identification, the patient's hospital documents, and evidence of their relationship. He had his own identity card and the payment receipt. He couldn't immediately produce the rest.

“Bring what you have and explain. Viewing or copying formal records requires the proper checks. A receipt doesn't automatically entitle you to collect them.”

“I don't need the whole file first. I need to know who I can ask next.”

“The information desk can contact us. Start there tomorrow.”

He repeated the location, extension, and documents. She corrected one detail: he didn't need an ordinary outpatient appointment before approaching the desk in the main entrance hall. He crossed out registration, once, leaving the word readable beneath the line.

The call hadn't told him where his sister was.

He thanked the person anyway.

A number and a short duration remained in the call history. He didn't press it again. Calling twice wouldn't complete an identity check that hadn't happened the first time.

In the notebook he wrote: Reached someone. Confirmed route. Patient not looked up.

The handwriting wandered. Its meaning didn't.

He had spread this same receipt before a nurse once before, the paper softened by rain. A gray-haired man had occupied bed six. The man's relative had stopped turning the bed crank to watch a stranger enter the room demanding another name.

That man had needed care too.

At the time, Lin Mo had had nothing left to spare for him. He'd gone from one counter to another, hearing that his sister couldn't be found. Finally, an on-screen comment had told him to go to the eighth floor.

He had gone.

He wrote eight in the margin, then paused.

An old entrance wasn't necessarily somewhere his sister still waited. This time there was an operator he could call again, a route beginning at an ordinary front door. Wanting to see her couldn't be enough to send his injured body toward a floor he didn't even know existed.

He left eight where it was and wrote beside it: What I remember happening.

Then he circled tomorrow's ground floor.

His knee had begun to swell with the long sitting. He pushed the notebook farther onto the desk and shifted as he'd been taught. Halfway through, his shoulder caught. He waited for the pain to ease. Nobody was there to steady him. Nobody was judging the time he took either.

By the time he was comfortable, his fingers wouldn't hold the pen properly.

The escort service was saved on the phone.

He called, explained tomorrow's destination, and confirmed that collection would still be from home.

“From my door. Can you come at nine? The hospital's main entrance. I need help reaching the information desk inside.”

Nine was available. The person asked about waiting and the return journey. Lin Mo didn't leave those until later. He requested separate explanations of the round trip, wheelchair assistance, escort inside, and charges beyond the agreed waiting period.

Since he couldn't write steadily, he listened first. Where necessary, he asked to hear something again.

More than today's cost.

He looked at the empty food container, calculating what assistance he might remove. If they left him outside, who would push the wheelchair? If he dropped the return booking, how would he get home afterward? Every deleted item quietly transferred a piece of the journey to his injured wrist and shoulder.

He shortened the initial waiting period instead. Beyond that, they would contact him before adding time. Help to the desk and help home both stayed.

“If you change escorts while I'm waiting inside, tell me who's coming.”

“We'll arrange the handover. Can you keep your phone on?”

“Yes.”

He glanced at the charging cable.

“This time, yes.”

The confirmation message arrived. He checked nine o'clock, the addresses, services, and costs against what he'd heard. Receiving a message didn't make every detail correct.

He saved the nursing-office contact separately from the transport service, using names long enough to distinguish tomorrow.

A car would come.

His sister wasn't found. Yet his body seemed at last permitted to stop. Tiredness gathered behind his eyes.

He set an alarm for a short rest and placed water and phone on his left. He kept the splint on and didn't force his injured leg straight to lie flatter. Someone outside was chopping food in irregular bursts.

He fell asleep listening.

Daylight remained when he woke.

First he looked at the chair. Xiao Lu's bag rested against the wall. Nobody had taken out the cup. For an instant he'd thought he heard its lid being unscrewed.

A tap was running next door.

He silenced the alarm without calling her name, drank, then phoned the food shop. Dinner, and a question about the earliest breakfast delivery. He didn't want tomorrow's doorbell to find him staring at food he hadn't touched.

Around eight was possible. They couldn't promise the exact minute.

“Something simple I can carry. Call when you arrive. It takes me a while to reach the door.”

He put the money within reach, so he wouldn't have to search his wallet in the morning.

Packing didn't mean emptying every old file into his bag. The uniform photograph, the school form, the thick unopened envelope couldn't answer questions by their accumulated weight. His identity card came first. Then he unfolded his sister's receipt and checked the original before returning it to the notebook.

The photograph slipped partway out.

His sister was beside him, one hand blurred because she hadn't lowered it in time. He meant to leave it on the desk. His fingers stopped.

If someone asked what she looked like, this side could still answer.

He put it back without scraping away or tracing over the changed word on the reverse. It couldn't prove he was her brother. It still held her face. He couldn't discard that half merely because the other hurt.

His dressing instructions and discharge papers went where they could be reached easily. Tomorrow's escort might need to know which arm not to pull, when pain meant he couldn't continue.

Where proof of their relationship should have been, a space remained on the desk.

He listed the missing documents separately. He didn't write ready against them. Paying for her care again and again had never given him the right to decide everything for her.

An old transparent sleeve protruded from the drawer. He removed its expired notice and wiped the dust away. There was a small split at the opening; he checked that it wouldn't catch the paper, then put the receipt inside and returned it to the notebook.

Rain had soaked through his coat that day. The more tightly he'd gripped the paper, the softer its corners had become. It might not rain tomorrow. Still, a receipt that had traveled so far with him shouldn't have to survive every failure to take care.

He wanted to know where she had gone.

Facing that desire while admitting he still lacked things was harder than he'd expected.

He photographed the receipt in case the original slipped somewhere. The first image tilted; its numbers blurred. He laid the paper flat, supported his elbow, and tried again, enlarging the ward name and numbers to check them. The photograph was a reference, not a replacement for what lay on the desk.

He returned to his question list.

There was something else to practice saying.

“I came before. They couldn't find her.”

He said it once toward the desk, then added, “I'm her brother.”

He remembered her dividing an apple beside her bed, giving him the larger piece. When he noticed, she'd refused to exchange them. He was the one walking everywhere. She was just sitting there. Unable to argue her out of it, he'd bitten into the apple and reached behind her to raise the pillow.

No telephone number was needed to remember that. He didn't know whether tomorrow's nurse would recognize the person beside the bed. He knew whom he was asking about, and why he had to go.

His voice remained the voice he had now.

No other man emerged from his throat to speak for him. No one arrived to pronounce those words correct. He listened to the two short sentences and wrote them together. Tomorrow he could decide which to say first.

Dinner arrived. Again he asked for it to be left on the stool inside the door. Opening, collecting, returning: each had its own pauses. Afterward, he gathered the packaging without moving Xiao Lu's chair to make room for the phone.

The sack of old clothes stayed where it was. The thin blister pack remained in the old coat's inner pocket. He didn't use medicine whose history he couldn't trust in place of the instructions given at discharge.

The light gradually faded.

He put the cloth bag within reach beside the bed, made sure the keys were secure, and connected the charger. Tomorrow's alarm allowed time before collection: time to eat, time to turn slowly at the door.

One final check of the booking. No new calls.

Only the few newly saved names occupied the screen. His sister hadn't returned. No one called to him from beside the chair holding Xiao Lu's things. He left the ringer on and lay down, leaving a little light near the door.

Tomorrow, the ground floor first.

This time he knew who had told him.
