# Chapter 117: Not Down the Stairs Yet

The moment his left foot touched the floor, Lin Mo knew he'd stood too soon.

The person supporting his waist hadn't secured their hold. The wheelchair wasn't beside him yet. He tried to shift his weight back, but his right knee buckled first. The care worker caught him from behind; someone else supported the injured leg and helped lower him onto the bed's edge.

He hadn't fallen. His chest still hurt enough to make him sick.

“Stop,” the doctor said.

Lin Mo stared at the bed leg's shadow before looking up.

“I didn't use my right hand.”

“You didn't wait for them to be ready, either.”

His wrist remained properly braced, his left shoulder supported. He'd meant to prove he knew how to protect his injuries. Instead, he'd moved ahead of everyone helping him. The person holding his leg had knelt beside the bed, knocking a knee against its metal foot. Lin Mo saw them rub it as they rose.

“Sorry.”

The doctor waited for his breathing to settle, then examined what the movement had strained. He didn't permit another attempt or lift the day's restriction because Lin Mo needed to reach a basement.

“Getting into a vehicle doesn't mean you can get downstairs afterward. You said stairs were the only way. You'll have to come back up.”

“I can hire people.”

“Transfers still hurt. They still tire you out. That's enough today.”

Lin Mo swallowed his reply. His sister was in there. Who could guarantee another day's delay wouldn't hurt her? But this doctor could answer only for the body before him. Lin Mo didn't even know whether she was trapped somewhere that measured time in days.

The care worker straightened his clothes, touching his sweat-soaked back.

“Don't lie down yet. Lean back for a while.”

He obeyed. On the table, his phone lit with a request from the transport service: stair photographs and current advice about leaving home. He asked her to reply that they were postponing. He didn't leave out the failed transfer.

By noon, the pressure in his shoulder had stopped building. Over lunch, he kept watching his phone. Asked what he was waiting for, he said photographs of the basement. The care worker didn't ask what difference another minute would make. She slowed the spoon and waited for him to swallow.

Old Zhou sent three pictures that evening.

The first showed the entrance from the street, its old number clear, loose boards against the wall. The second looked down a narrow, straight flight, a handrail along the left wall. The last was also taken from above, revealing the rusty iron door below and a patch of pale wall beside it.

Nobody had gone down to look for paper on his behalf.

The transport service said they'd need to measure the stairs in person. Pictures weren't enough to promise a transfer. They could retain a provisional plan, subject to his reassessment. He wanted to ask for the earliest date, but didn't type the words.

This was the seventh day since the incident at the window.

The days afterward didn't suddenly accelerate. He woke, took medicine, had dressings changed, asked for someone to support and shift his leg. He waited for his shoulder's pain to become bearable. Sometimes he woke from a nap certain Xiao Lu had called, only to find the person delivering his meal asking whether he wanted soup.

He asked them to leave it.

Every mouthful felt like something more he owed her. Yet refusing food meant he couldn't finish the afternoon's brief practice. Once, he did skip a meal. His vision darkened during the transfer, and they had to lower the bed again. The cold food was reheated. Sitting up, he ate more than half, one mouthful at a time.

After the hospital cleared him to leave acute observation, he arranged care at home and transport for follow-up visits. His returned rental wheelchair didn't reappear. The hospital chair stayed there. He paid a new rental fee for a lower seat with proper support for his right leg, had it delivered, and had a professional adjust it to fit him.

Xiao Lu's bag still sat on the chair beside the wall.

The care worker asked to move the chair and give him more turning room. He said no, then noticed how close his left side was to the table corner.

“Don't open the bag. Put the chair over there, away from water.”

With it moved, he could turn. Nobody had forgotten who'd left the bag.

On day fourteen, he first practiced reaching the living-room doorway.

The person who'd assessed him secured his right leg and kept his left arm supported. He could try moving the chair slowly with his left foot on level ground. His right wrist must stay off the hand rims; his left shoulder couldn't correct his direction. Someone held the handles, ready to stop him.

He lowered his left foot and pressed his sole against the floor. The chair moved half a palm's length.

On his next push, the left wheel turned farther than the right, angling him toward the table. He instinctively began raising his left arm. Pain stopped it halfway. Behind him, the care worker applied the brakes and returned him to his starting place.

“Don't rush the turn. Look at both wheels first.”

He looked down, seeing how much room the chair needed on what he'd considered a straight path.

The second time, he stopped before the table corner and asked for help turning. Then he pushed with his foot. At the doorway, a raised wooden strip caught the front casters. His sole scraped the floor, but the chair wouldn't move.

He stopped pushing.

“I can't get over this.”

“You need help with it. We'll stop here today.”

The strip was lower than his shoe's sole. His sister had complained about dust catching there whenever she swept. Once, he'd pried it up with a screwdriver, then pressed it down again rather than finish the job. Now his wheels rested against the part he'd left uneven.

“Make a note of that.”

“Of what?”

“A car can reach the door. Doesn't mean I can get inside.”

The care worker photographed the threshold with the wheels in view instead of writing down his words. He checked the picture and asked her to save it.

That evening, he resent the basement entrance photograph to the transport service, asking them to include the iron door's width and threshold. Measuring the stairs alone wasn't enough. They said they'd check onsite, then reminded him that they covered only a verified, real-world route. He couldn't arrive and add an unseen basement level.

“I don't know if there are other rooms,” he said. “If anything's wrong, we come back out.”

After a pause, they asked what wrong meant.

He described waking there and the injuries at the hospital's old window. He had no explanation a stranger would immediately believe. He told them the risks he knew and gave them room to refuse.

They didn't make a booking that night.

Two days later, after inspecting the site, the service confirmed they could use suitable transfer equipment on the ordinary stairs. They would take him to the cleared space just inside the original basement door, with two workers for both journeys. If the site differed from the photographs, they could cancel at any point.

He accepted those limits.

On the afternoon of day twenty-one, the care worker set a small water bottle across the living room for a practical exercise: reach the table, then come back. Arriving wouldn't make the bottle follow him.

He brought the chair alongside, steadied it with his left foot, and waited for her to lock the brakes. Trying to tuck the bottle against his side with his right forearm, he knocked the brace against the table. His fingers could move, but when the bottle slipped, his wrist began to tighten reflexively.

“Let it go.”

The bottle rolled onto its side, water circling inside the clear plastic. He didn't chase it.

“Something this small.”

“We can pick it up.”

“I meant me.”

She stood the bottle upright where he could see it. Watching his hand, he felt his anger drain into exhaustion. Going in, getting water, coming back: each step could leave him stranded halfway. Where he wanted to go, there might not even be a table, much less somebody to unscrew a cap.

“Can we fit something on the chair to hold it?”

She contacted the wheelchair supplier and found a suitable lightweight side pouch. They tested it with an empty bottle. Too low, it brushed the wheel on turns. Farther forward, it blocked his left foot. After three adjustments, they settled on the rear side of the seat, the bottle's mouth exposed.

He practiced touching it without lifting. Drinking still required someone to hold it steady, but he wouldn't need to trap it against his injured wrist while moving.

They skipped one practice run to reorganize what he'd carry. Finding a place for water didn't mean he could live independently. He asked to keep all the assistance planned for the basement trip, including the waiting time he'd hoped to cut.

That evening, a strap hung from Xiao Lu's bag on the nearby chair. Worried it might catch in a wheel, he asked the care worker to tuck it away. She laid it on the seat without opening the bag. Watching, he admitted he'd always complained about his sister taking so long to get ready.

“And now?”

“If she could sit there packing her things, I wouldn't mind being late.”

He lowered his gaze and repositioned his left foot. The bottle knocked softly inside its pouch. It stayed put.

By the end of the third week, he could propel himself a few meters across the level living room and slowly back. On his first backward attempt, his heel had struck the frame, nearly trapping his leg. After help adjusting his position, he'd learned to watch the wheels instead of trying to finish on one breath. Turns often still needed assistance. Bed-to-chair transfers still needed two people.

His right wrist remained braced. His left shoulder could neither lift high nor support his weight. The finger wound was closing, but bending and straightening still pulled; the doctor told him to keep protecting it. A knee that no longer constantly seeped wasn't ready to stand on stairs.

He listed these changes for reassessment rather than simply saying he felt better.

On day twenty-seven, he completed a full assisted transfer at the hospital.

The chair was positioned and braked. He waited for support at his waist and back before putting his left foot where directed. His injured leg followed slowly. Once on the seat, he settled his weight before they adjusted the footrest. He didn't rush or conceal his pain.

The doctor asked how long he could sit and who would care for him afterward. He showed the return transport and home-care arrangements. He'd stay only briefly, wouldn't walk the stairs, and wouldn't move anything himself.

“I can't assess the safety of that building,” the doctor said.

“I understand.”

“I'm approving a short, assisted outing based on your condition. Keep protecting the shoulder and wrist. Come back if the pain gets bad.”

He nodded.

Waiting for his ride outside the consulting room, he saw Xu, passing on his way to work. The guard noticed the new wheelchair, checked that its right footrest didn't wobble, then asked if he was returning to the old place.

“Tomorrow.”

“The window downstairs is still closed. Nobody's moved the paper.”

After a moment, Lin Mo asked about Old Chen.

“Some days, nothing. Yesterday he said, ‘No schoolbag.’ Couldn't hear the rest.”

“Don't push him.”

“We know.” Xu pointed at his phone. “If he wants something remembered, we'll keep the original recording.”

Lin Mo thanked him. Xu had a shift to start. After he left, Lin Mo pressed his left foot to the tile and moved the chair a little.

He stopped. A gurney crossed ahead, carrying a patient. Its wheels drowned out the transport worker's new message: pickup tomorrow morning, then the agreed address.

Lin Mo asked the care worker to acknowledge it and return the phone to his chair's side. He waited for the bed to pass.

When his ride arrived, he told the escort that removing the water bottle would make securing the chair easier. They put it in the vehicle's bag, then supported his waist and back. He waited to rise, already looking toward the road beyond the lobby.
