# Chapter 100: A Year Ago

Nurse Zhou drew her hand away from the receipt and looked at him.

“Your sister died.”

Lin Mo didn't move.

“A year ago. Here. I was on duty that day. After the resuscitation, the doctor confirmed we hadn't been able to save her. I was there.”

A trolley struck something outside. Someone said, “Careful.” Someone else answered.

He understood those words. The words across the table wouldn't settle. He saw a chip in the paint at the desk's corner, the photograph curling upward. He reached to flatten it, then forgot what his fingers were meant to do.

“You mean she was discharged?”

“No.”

“Transferred somewhere?”

The nurse shook her head.

“She died. We've checked who she was. I'm not answering you about someone else.”

He searched the receipt for its date. Halfway through the digits, the beginning escaped him. He lifted the paper closer. His wrist struck the desk, sending pain beneath the splint.

He'd used his right hand.

He let go. The paper fell back.

“Don't lean on that arm.”

He had moved away from the backrest, one foot halfway off its support. She asked before helping. He nodded and let her steady the chair while he settled himself. He hadn't even stood. Cold sweat gathered with the pain in his knee.

She moved the photograph where it couldn't fall, then stepped outside to fetch half a cup of water. She put it within reach of his left hand.

“Dizzy? Any discomfort in your chest?”

“Let me sit a moment.”

“All right. Tell me if you feel unwell.”

The water circled twice in the light paper cup, then stilled. His breathing couldn't find that rhythm.

A year ago.

He hadn't spent that year beside her bed, or counted its days waiting in his flat. He remembered rain at the hospital, an empty chair on a screen, lift doors that hadn't opened. Those things stood so close behind him he could almost reach the wet receipt in his pocket.

Someone had placed an entire year between them and this room.

He took out the new phone. The date was unchanged. He'd seen it, set alarms, booked today's car. He hadn't used those numbers to measure how long his sister had been gone.

“This receipt belongs to that admission?”

“Yes. The admission number matches.”

“The date you're talking about. Can you check it again?”

She didn't tell him to accept it. She went next door to confirm the date in the archived index with the nurse on duty, then returned and read it slowly.

He opened his notebook and managed one character.

“Slower, please.”

She repeated it. With his left hand, he wrote the uneven figures and had her check each one. Beneath them he copied today's date from the phone.

About a year separated the two.

That didn't tell him how to count his days in the theater. The figures established what this hospital recorded. They couldn't travel that other distance for him.

“You saw her? Her, herself?”

“Yes. I cared for her. I recognize the person in your photograph.”

“On the original ward?”

“Yes.”

No uncertain embellishments. No vague afterward in place of the location he'd asked about. An answer could be so short, with nowhere inside it to misunderstand.

“Did she call for me?”

The nurse paused.

“I didn't hear her. I can't tell you whether she did when I wasn't beside her.”

His eyes hurt too much to keep open.

Not that she hadn't called. Not that she'd called and he hadn't answered. He couldn't even obtain a complete sentence to show him precisely what he should regret.

“Where was I?”

“I don't know where you were at every moment that day. Your name in the family-contact field can't tell me you were always there, or always away.”

He turned the phone facedown.

He wanted to call his sister. The impulse was faster than explanation. His finger searched for its familiar place before he remembered the new phone had no such place. His restored number could reach the world. It couldn't call somewhere and give this sentence back.

The nurse didn't touch his phone.

He remembered her last ordinary message: eat before you come. She'd been lying here, or he'd believed she was, writing something that could always be answered later. Whether he'd eaten, how much he'd eaten: they could discuss it next time. There were bills to pay, medicines to collect, tests to ask about.

All those urgent things had passed. The least urgent question remained unanswered.

He didn't even know whether Have you eaten? had reached her. If she'd died here a year ago, where did that illuminated conversation belong? If Xiao Lu was his sister, returned to him, why was there someone sitting across this table willing to bear witness to her death?

He couldn't choose whichever side hurt less and use it to erase the other. Holding both, he felt the small space he occupied growing too narrow for his body.

Beyond the door, an announcement called a number. As the last syllable faded, he finally drew breath. Letting it out tightened his throat.

“When I came looking, they said there was no such patient. This same receipt. Now there's even a date.”

“I can't infer why they didn't find her then from what we can see today. Write down what happened during that inquiry. You can include it when you ask for the original records to be checked.”

He nodded. It wasn't reassurance, but it didn't declare those three hours nonexistent. He couldn't pull a reason she'd never witnessed out of her mouth.

“I saw her afterward.”

His voice was quiet. The nurse turned a little toward him.

“After I left this hospital. Not a photograph. Not someone telling me she was alive. I touched her hand. She knew me.”

He stopped, pressing his tongue against his teeth, trying to make the next sentence sound like his own.

“She called me Ge.”

The word had no echo. The room was ordinary, its walls close. Words were spoken and heard. They didn't return from somewhere else transformed into a command.

“You mean you saw her recently?”

“For me, it was after I left here. I can't match the day to your dates.”

He wouldn't make the account easier to believe by adding a time he didn't know.

The nurse listened without turning what he'd said into a dream.

“I can tell you what happened here. I don't know how to explain what you're describing. A few sentences can't let me decide whom you saw.”

“I know it was her.”

There was strength in the words, then his throat closed.

Xiao Lu standing before him was closer than the photograph. Her eyes when she'd called him brother. Her hand leaving his, as though there were still words she needed to give him. He hadn't caught them. Through the door, awake, water, treatment, home, a replacement phone: he'd done each thing necessary to reach this room.

He'd thought those small tasks, joined together, would make a road to her.

At least to someone who knew where she was.

A tear struck the notebook. He hurried to move the photograph. His right wrist couldn't help; his left hand still held the pen. Everything crowded together. Unable to speak, he pressed his hand over his eyes. His shoulder shook once, and the pain made him swallow his breath.

Nurse Zhou put tissues beside him. She didn't wipe his face.

He released the pen. The first tissue soaked through. He folded the second, only partly used, into his palm, as though one small thing still needed completing.

“I didn't come here to be given her death.”

He shook his head before she answered. She didn't owe him a different outcome. She had stood beside that bed too, doing what could be done.

“I know you didn't make her—”

He left the sentence unfinished.

“I know,” she said.

He sat a long time. The water cooled. He drank a little. He didn't demand that she withdraw her words. He didn't agree to think of his sister only as the hospital recorded her, either.

“Can I keep what you told me?” He indicated the notebook. “I'm afraid I'll get home and remember only one sentence.”

“Yes. Make clear what I told you and what we checked. A complete medical file requires a separate application. This conversation isn't a copy of that file.”

He nodded. She hadn't become an entire archive by sitting here. He didn't need to force every question to its end today.

He wrote her surname and the ward where they'd met. Her statement that she'd been present that day. The date they had checked. He put Told by hospital before the entry and showed her.

She corrected one phrase: hadn't heard, not hadn't said.

He crossed it out and wrote it again.

Then he turned the page.

Xiao Lu. Lost contact at the corner.

The old sentence remained. He didn't add death after it. Slowly, beneath it, he wrote: She knew me. Called me Ge. After her hand left mine, I didn't see where she went.

Not because this page had more authority than the hospital's record. Because he had lived that part. He couldn't leave it out on her behalf.

Both pages would stay.

Nurse Zhou asked how he was getting home. He explained the escort waiting outside and checked that their return car was still booked. He didn't want to discuss his body, but told her where he was injured and what hurt after sitting. She read his discharge instructions and reminded him to seek help if things worsened, rather than attribute every physical change to what he'd heard.

He agreed and finished the water.

Before leaving, he asked where to continue an inquiry into that admission. She wrote the contact for old medical records; that office would confirm the documents required. He kept the slip without asking her to abandon her work and stay until every answer was found.

“I may come back.”

“If you have things to check, ask.”

The escort entered without first asking what he'd learned. Lin Mo returned the receipt to its transparent sleeve and tucked the photograph between neighboring pages. Identity card in the inner pocket. Wallet, keys, new phone, water bottle. He closed the notebook and kept his hand on its cover while the chair cleared the table.

At the nurses' station, a colleague called Nurse Zhou. She nodded back at Lin Mo, returned to her place, and accepted someone else's record.

He didn't ask her to come farther.

In the descending lift, he watched the doors. He didn't press eight. It was an entrance he remembered taking. It couldn't answer what he was carrying downstairs now.

In the main hall, the escort parked him out of the draft and went to arrange for the car to pull up where he could transfer safely. Through the glass, morning light lay across the ramp. It wasn't noon yet.

The cloth bag rested securely on his knees. His phone's ringer was on. At home, Xiao Lu's bag waited on the chair, the cup still inside. The old clothes remained in their sack. Today's words hadn't automatically turned those things into relics.

He opened the notebook. Beneath the two accounts that would not give way to each other, he wrote a question.

Then where is the Xiao Lu I held?

He didn't cross out her name. He didn't supply an answer.

Beyond the glass doors, the escort was still speaking to the driver. Lin Mo clipped the pen in place and kept the notebook close, waiting for the car.

The question would go home with him.
