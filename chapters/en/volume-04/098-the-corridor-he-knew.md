# Chapter 98: The Corridor He Knew

The car stopped outside the hospital's main entrance. Lin Mo saw the ramp first.

It had been resurfaced. The crack was gone, but the steel handrail remained. He used to come up that way carrying a flask, his other hand pressed over the receipt in his pocket. Once, in the rain, a loose lid had let soup run down his trousers. He told his sister he'd brought a smaller portion.

Halfway through, she asked, “You had some too?”

He remembered how quickly he'd said yes.

The escort secured the wheelchair beside the car.

“Here we are. Take your time.”

Lin Mo moved to the edge of the seat. His splinted wrist couldn't pull against the door; his left shoulder couldn't help either. Once the escort had steadied him, he set down his better foot and managed the few necessary steps. By the time his injured leg was settled, sweat had gathered at his temples.

Knowing the way hadn't spared him any of that.

The clean cloth bag rested on his lap: notebook, identification, wallet, keys, new phone, discharge instructions. A small bottle he had filled with drinking water at home occupied the side pocket.

“The ground-floor information desk first.”

Machines occupied the space where the newspaper counter had been. The shortcut to the inpatient building was marked Outpatient Treatment now.

“That way?” the escort asked, following his gaze.

“We used to go there. Let's ask first.”

Last time, he'd been able to walk from the nurses' station to the bed himself. A white-haired man had occupied bed six. His sister's slippers were gone, along with the half-peeled apple he'd left that morning. Even the receipt issued that day hadn't helped. No such patient.

Afterward, her voice had come through the phone.

He had a new phone now. The old conversation hadn't returned with it. He touched its outline through the bag.

“If someone says they can't find her, don't wheel me away yet. I need to ask which records they checked.”

“All right. We move when you're finished.”

Two people were waiting at the desk. He took the transparent sleeve from his notebook, eased out the receipt, and unfolded it across his uninjured thigh, turning the hospital's details toward the counter.

“I called yesterday. They said to bring this and ask the nursing office where the old ward went. I'm looking for my sister.”

The woman didn't send him to register for an appointment. She checked the department and made an internal call, explaining that a relative had an earlier inpatient receipt. She wrote a floor number, crossed it out, and asked again.

“The sixth floor of the old building, or the present inpatient building?”

Only after confirming did she hang up.

“The successor ward is on six, in the building behind us. There's a connecting passage and a lift. The nursing office has asked them to help you find someone from the former team.”

“Can you see where she is now?”

“I can't access inpatient histories here. This gets us to the right department. They'll be more useful than trying every counter.”

She returned the paper.

“You're her elder sister?”

His left hand tightened over the receipt. There was no challenge in her question. A nod would save half a minute.

“I'm her brother.”

She looked up. Someone behind him moved closer. He heard the small click as she settled the telephone in its cradle.

“My name is Lin Mo. You can put relative.”

She checked his name against the identity card, crossed out the unfinished description, and wrote his name instead.

“All right. Give them this. I'll show you the route.”

He took back the card. Female remained visible. He hadn't overlooked the word when he'd answered.

The passage was brighter than he remembered. Beyond the glass, fresh paving covered the space where meal trolleys used to gather. He recognized a curve at the foot of the wall and knew there would be a small window around the corner.

It was still there. Two water dispensers had replaced the bench beneath it.

He had waited here during her tests. She used to tell him to sit down, save his legs for getting home. Once she'd said it while supporting herself against the wall. When he went to help, she gave him her empty cup to hold first.

She hadn't wanted both hands taken away.

He asked the escort to stop.

“Leg hurting?”

“Just some water.”

He drank from the water he'd brought rather than reaching for the dispenser. After a moment, he said his sister used to wait for him here.

“You came often?”

“Often.”

“That should make it easier to find your way.”

Lin Mo looked at the new signs.

“The way, yes.”

Before they entered the lift, he checked the directory. Once the chair was still, he pressed six with his left hand. A woman holding a child kept the door open for an older man. He thanked her. She said it was nothing.

Lin Mo watched the changing numbers. His fingertip rested against the notebook's stiff edge until they stopped.

“Ready?”

“Yes.”

On six, he smelled food being reheated. Disinfectant came later, farther down the corridor. The nurses' station was in the wrong direction. The rooms had different numbers. A man carrying clean clothes stepped aside for the wheelchair, brushing his own knee with the bag.

Lin Mo used to be the person standing aside.

The reception nurse found the message forwarded from downstairs. Neither her face nor the surname on her badge was familiar.

“I haven't been on this ward long. Some of the former team still work here. Let me look.”

She checked the department and admission number on the receipt. Did he remember the bed?

“Six. By the window. I came looking once before. They checked and said she wasn't here. Today I want someone who cared for her, someone who can check the old records.”

He remembered a pipe outside the window, the call-button cord that barely reached.

The nurse didn't try entering those details into the computer. She established where the old ward's work had been transferred, then explained that the original rooms had another use now. Their bed numbers had changed too.

“There was a nurse with a small mole by her ear,” he said. “She showed me how to arrange the pillows.”

The woman considered this without pretending to recognize anyone.

“I can ask. Have you brought a photograph? That will help more than the bed number.”

He opened the notebook. His sister's upward-slanting writing appeared first: elder sister.

He could have left the inquiry at a name and an admission number. The photograph would put him before another person's eyes too, confirming the face he wore now.

“Just look at my sister.”

He turned it over.

The nurse laid it flat, keeping her fingers away from his sister's face.

“This one?”

“Yes.”

“May I show the former nurses? Here. I won't take it away.”

He nodded.

His sister's hand was blurred. Memory wasn't perfect either. He couldn't be certain of the light in the room that day. But he knew she'd stepped crookedly on a slipper as she ran back to the chair. He knew he'd reached out to steady her.

His hand hadn't made it into the photograph.

The nurse called an internal number, gave the old department and the patient's name from the receipt, and asked which team had been responsible. Listening, she turned to him.

“Was the nurse's surname Zhou?”

He didn't agree immediately. He tried to recover the face, the badge hanging crookedly from a pocket. His sister drawing out that surname, coaxing the nurse not to make her take a nap.

“Nurse Zhou. Yes.”

Once a cup lid had broken. His sister folded a broad sleeve from cardboard to protect her fingers from the hot water. He said it wouldn't hold. Nurse Zhou brought tape and showed her how to fold the bottom inward.

His sister lifted the cup.

“See? You can improve things. You don't have to throw everything away.”

What returned now wasn't a secret that could prove what had happened to him.

Three people beside a hospital bed, working out how to hold a cup of water.

The nurse finished the call. Nurse Zhou still worked at the hospital and had returned to this nursing team. She was helping with a procedure on the other side of the ward. A colleague would tell her that a family member from the old department was waiting with a receipt.

“Does she remember my sister?”

“I haven't spoken to her myself yet. You can ask her about the cup when she comes. We have confirmed she was on that team.”

He looked at the nurse. He didn't insist she retrieve an answer from someone else's memory.

At least the surname belonged to a person he could find today.

The escort checked his phone, then leaned closer. Their booked waiting time was nearly over. The driver needed to know whether to stay.

“What would it cost to stay longer?”

The escort explained the extension and the return journey separately. Going home meant paying for another trip if he returned tomorrow. Staying meant waiting without a promise that Nurse Zhou would be free in a few minutes.

His wallet was in the bag's outer pocket. He didn't reach for it yet.

He could leave his number. Someone could call. It would cost less, and he wouldn't have to keep sitting in a corridor where every remembered detail had a difference beside it. He even had a truthful reason ready: he'd found the right place; his body had done enough.

“Are you feeling worse?” the escort asked.

“Sitting's all right. I need to shift my leg.”

They did that first. He leaned against the backrest and waited for the tightness to ease before asking the escort to arrange the extension.

“I'll wait this time.”

After calling, the escort confirmed the extra waiting time. Their return car would stay. Lin Mo kept the new expense in mind and put away his wallet.

The nurse showed them a place beside the ward entrance, clear of the treatment trolleys and out of the draft from the lifts. Nurse Zhou would pass the station on her way back. They wouldn't need to search from room to room.

Lin Mo asked to face the counter.

The nurse returned the photograph with the receipt. It hadn't aged further or brought back a word from his sister. He laid it faceup in the notebook and returned the receipt to its transparent sleeve beside it, so he wouldn't struggle to find them again.

Someone came out and called another patient's relative to collect a meal. Lin Mo lifted his head, then looked down.

He didn't know that person.

No one on this floor had recognized him yet.

The escort sat beside him and lowered his phone's volume. Lin Mo remembered how his sister used to save a place while he paid the bill, folding her coat small so he'd have somewhere to sit when he returned.

He drew the cloth bag farther onto his lap, leaving the empty chair clear.

Down the corridor, a treatment trolley stopped. A drawer opened and closed.

He didn't stand.

This time, he knew whom he was waiting for.
