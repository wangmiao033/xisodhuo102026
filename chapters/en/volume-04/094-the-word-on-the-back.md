# Chapter 94: The Word on the Back

It took four days to get home.

On the second morning, Lin Mo could stand with someone beside him, but couldn't reach the door. On the third, he learned to place his feet before rising, sparing his wrist and shoulder. The rehabilitation therapist wouldn't let him force his weight through an ordinary cane. His arms were injured too. Moving the load elsewhere wasn't a solution.

He thought of the length of wood he hadn't brought back. He stopped arguing.

The therapist told him to count to three before getting up. At two, habit made him push forward. Pain interrupted the movement. She steadied him at the waist and waited until he was seated again.

“Have you done much physical training?”

“I was in the army.”

“Then we need a different goal. Not one more repetition. Every repetition without an accident.”

He looked at the tape marking the floor. In the past, when told to go faster or hold a little longer, he'd always found a way to force a little more of his body into the order. This person was waiting for him to say he was ready.

“Again,” he said.

“You can also say you've had enough.”

After the third attempt that day, he did. He didn't lose his next meal. Nobody recorded the pause as a failure.

Long distances meant a wheelchair. The few steps between bed and chair needed practice, especially the turn. Once, reaching for a falling cup, his knee wavered. The nurse caught the cup first. He steadied himself and listened to it being set down.

“We can replace a cup.”

He nodded and practiced again. At night he followed the written instructions for shifting position, explaining where to support him when he asked for help. His wrist remained immobilized, his shoulder hurt, and his leg managed only short distances. The pain was beginning to separate into things he could address. He wasn't healed. But now he could make a plan.

Going home involved questions.

How would he get inside? Steps? Distance from bed to bathroom? Food? Follow-up dressings? He gave the address and drew a floor plan. There was an elevator, but a low threshold at his door. He'd hardly thought it worth mentioning. The nurse circled it.

“This counts now.”

He drew it properly. He admitted he didn't know what he'd find inside. There was nobody to collect him, and he still had no phone. A discharge coordinator contacted an outside assisted-transport service. Travel, assistance, and help settling in were priced separately. She didn't choose the most expensive option for him.

He asked for the figures in writing. She explained that the service covered only what was booked, not round-the-clock care. If home proved unsafe, the arrangement would have to change. Leaving him at the door wouldn't do.

He circled two hours of help after arrival. Vehicle for the journey, wheelchair to the door, then a check of the few routes he needed indoors. The next day's dressing change and transport were booked too. He worked through the costs and removed what he didn't need.

Money became heavy again.

On the fourth day, a staff member wheeled him to the cashier. He checked the itemized sheets on his knees and established what had already been settled and what he owed himself. His bank card came from the wallet. Its worn patches were unchanged.

So was the PIN he remembered.

The terminal paused, then printed a receipt. Payment approved. Relief came first, followed by the need to check what remained in his account. None of this expense had vanished. Dressings, food, a replacement phone: everything would come from the same place.

He kept the receipt with the transport quotation and set aside cash for immediate fares and meals.

Before discharge, the nurse explained when to return: a worsening wound, significantly more pain, renewed unsteadiness. The dressing and review appointments were written down. He asked her to read them out, then repeated them back.

“Don't spend your time at home proving you can manage.”

“I know.”

“Knowing and doing are different.”

He managed a brief smile. “I'll do it.”

Clothes needed sorting out too. He was keeping the old ones, but their bloodstains and torn edges couldn't go back against the wounds. A hospital convenience counter sent up loose clothes and soft, nonslip shoes in the requested sizes. He saw the prices first, chose the simplest set, and paid.

He still needed help supporting his injured leg while putting on the trousers. The nurse turned away while he adjusted the waistband. His fingers rested on the ordinary elastic for a long time. No voice announced how much memory the outfit would cost. Nobody waited to judge its fit.

“Done,” he said.

The hospital clothes stayed behind. The large belongings bag held his old clothes and the cut fabric. Xiao Lu's bag sat separately on his lap. Notebook, wallet, and keys went in a newly bought cloth bag where he could see them. Only after checking all three did he let the escort wheel him out.

At a familiar street corner, he straightened in his seat.

The barber downstairs was still there. The price board beside the door hung differently. He remembered the chair catching as it turned, the clippers touching his nape, the barber telling him to lower his head. Their moving vehicle flashed across the window. He couldn't make out who sat inside.

“That entrance ahead?” the escort asked.

“Yes. Stop above the slope. It's closer.”

For a moment, his own answer surprised him. He still knew the way, even if this body didn't.

The elevator doors closed slowly. The car shuddered before stopping at his floor. He reached for the chair's armrest, then kept the weight off his injured wrist. One attendant pushed him out. The person assigned to help him settle in followed with his bags.

A scrap of old adhesive remained on his door.

The key resisted slightly. With his left hand, he turned it to the familiar point, then eased it back. The bolt clicked. He stayed still until the attendant asked whether he needed help.

Then he opened the door.

The air smelled shut in.

“Anyone here?”

His voice traveled through the small rooms. Nobody appeared. He heard none of the coughing he had learned to listen for.

The aide offered no explanation. Instead, she checked the floor. With his permission, she moved a curled mat and a chair narrowing the passage. He explained how the window opened and directed where to put water, paper, and medication so he could reach them from bed.

He asked her to place Xiao Lu's bag on a clean chair by the wall.

“Anything breakable?”

“A cup. No need to open it.”

She supported the bottom as she set it down, then let go.

The two hours weren't all spent tidying. Following the hospital instructions, he traveled from bed to bathroom, rested at the door, and rested again on the way back. The bed was a workable height. Getting up still took time. The kettle was too heavy, so he had the aide fill two lightweight containers and leave them within seated reach.

“Can you heat dinner?”

“I'll eat something that doesn't need cooking. Work out the rest after tomorrow's dressing change.”

He gave her cash for food he could eat that night and the following morning. She returned with the change and receipt. Two meals weren't a lasting arrangement. He wrote that down beside replace phone.

A door opened across the hall. The neighbor saw the wheelchair and stopped to ask what had happened. Lin Mo recognized the face, though he couldn't remember their last conversation. He gave his name, explained the injuries, and asked whether he could knock to borrow a phone in an emergency that evening.

“I'm home today. Knock hard; I don't hear much from the back room. I'll be out tomorrow, though.”

“Tonight is enough. Thank you.”

This wasn't someone who would always be waiting. He remembered the limit. He confirmed the next day's pickup with the transport attendant: come to the door and ring. Without a phone, saying they'd call before setting off wasn't a plan.

Before leaving, the aide watched him take another drink and checked that he could call for help and open the door. She left the service slip. His left-handed signature was a little better than it had been in the hospital.

The door closed. At last he was alone.

Sitting on the bed, he heard someone taking in a laundry pole outside. Slanting sunlight reached a pale stain near the table leg. His sister had spilled glue there. They'd worked at it with wet cloths late into the night until she said to leave it. A mark to remember it by.

The table remembered that small accident.

He waited for the pain to ease, then moved to the table and sat.

The drawer wasn't locked. Nor was it tidy. An unopened advertisement lay among old receipts. A dried-out pen rested across the top. He stopped his hand from rummaging and took only the photograph envelope near the edge.

Its flap had been folded twice, not sealed.

He remembered taking the top photograph. After he'd come home from the army, his sister had complained that he always avoided the camera. She propped her phone against a cup and made him sit. As the timer ran down, she hurried back beside him. Her hand was still moving when the picture was taken.

He'd disliked the result. She had it printed anyway.

“You're not the blurry one,” she'd said.

Lin Mo turned the photograph over to look.

Her raised hand was there. So were the table corner, the cup, her face turned toward him. He sat beside her with his back too straight, as though answering a roll call.

But beneath the short hair was a woman's face.

He studied the collar. He'd worn that faded shirt so often. It was the same one in the picture, its stripes folding in the same place. The shoulder seam fell on a different shape, as though the person he remembered had never occupied the shirt at all.

He turned to the back.

His sister's lines of writing always crept upward. The last would crowd the edge and curl back. Here they did exactly that. He knew the inscription with his eyes shut. She'd held it up after writing it and asked whether he could deny the evidence now.

Proof for my big brother: you didn't dodge the camera today.

He brought the photograph closer.

The first part read: Proof for my big sister.

The rest was unchanged.

His thumb found a small crease at the edge. He'd made it putting the photograph away, and she had scolded him. This hadn't become a new piece of paper. It retained the damage he'd caused. With every recognizable detail intact, it had changed what they called each other.

He remembered Xiao Lu's voice saying Ge.

It didn't come from the photograph. It remained in his memory: the breath in it, her eyes on him, and the words afterward that he hadn't been able to hear.

On a blank page he recorded the day of the photograph. The cup, the countdown, the slipper she had knocked crooked on her way back. Then he wrote the remembered inscription and the visible one on separate lines.

He could make the words the same size.

He couldn't make them equally true.

Outside, someone called a child in for dinner. Cooking smells drifted along the hall. Lin Mo unwrapped the food left within reach and took a small bite. He needed to eat. Tomorrow there would be more to examine.

After a long time studying both sides, he set the photograph beside his notebook.

He didn't hide the writing underneath.

There were still so many unopened drawers. For the first time, he understood that more than his present body had changed.

The past was already waiting at home, ready to call him something else.
