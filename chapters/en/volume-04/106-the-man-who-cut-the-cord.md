# Chapter 106: The Man Who Cut the Cord

Paper rustled on the other end of the call.

“The photograph was taken before the move,” Xu said. “I'll ask who last handled the sign.”

Lin Mo studied the intact plastic cord, knotted at the end, hanging from the sign's foot. The half he'd caught with bloody fingers hadn't yet been severed.

“Call when you find out. Don't send anyone in looking for it now.”

Xu agreed. Lin Mo kept listening, then realized he was waiting for someone who could answer everything immediately. He let that invisible thread go and ended the call.

The care worker returned with his dinner.

“Food or water first?”

“A little water.”

She held the cup, letting him decide when to stop. His eyes kept straying toward the phone. She turned its lit screen toward him and brought the tray closer.

“I'll tell you if it rings. Dinner won't eat itself.”

He opened his mouth for the spoon. Rice fell onto his chin. He tried to reach up, stopping as soon as his shoulder moved. She wiped it away without making his embarrassment into something to praise him for enduring.

By the last few mouthfuls, he'd stopped watching the phone.

Tang called after dinner. That was the name of the maintenance man who'd held the sign down at noon. He'd changed his shirt and borrowed Xu's duty phone. Running water echoed behind his voice.

“I cut the cord. Three days ago, when we moved the counter.”

Lin Mo settled against his pillows. “Why?”

“It tethered the sign to the counter, so nobody could take it. Got tangled around a box during the move. Almost pulled the sign off. I cut it.”

He paused, as though examining it again.

“I pushed the catch down too. The spindle was loose. Touch the sign and it flipped. All that rattling while we shifted boxes. The counter was coming out anyway. Who'd need it?”

“Which side did you leave facing out?”

“Service Open. Same as before.”

Someone laughed in the corridor, abruptly loud. Lin Mo stared at a fold in the gauze over his forefinger's second joint.

“The window had closed.”

“I know. Notice posted, shutter halfway down, keys handed over. Nobody said it'd keep calling numbers.”

Tang wasn't asking forgiveness. Lin Mo could hear an ordinary small task tightening a man's voice. Cut a cord. Stop a sign rattling. Return to hold it down with both hands while four people escaped.

He remembered breaking his sister's cup lid. Tighten it so it wouldn't leak. Such a simple reason.

“The black paper?”

“Didn't see it when I cut the cord.”

“Did you check the back?”

“No. Pressed the catch from the front.”

Silence followed. Tang didn't turn that into a claim that he'd definitely seen it clean. Lin Mo didn't use the old photograph to supply the missing glance.

“A woman who worked at that window took the photo,” Xu said. “She's coming tomorrow. It was for a repair request. Crooked spindle. They wanted it dealt with too.”

“How long between the photograph and cutting the cord?”

“The same morning. She can give you the details.”

Lin Mo closed his eyes. The thin sheet still had a way in that nobody had witnessed. The photograph preserved the cord, not the paper's arrival.

“I'll find the scissors,” Tang said nearby. “Something might still be caught—”

“Tomorrow,” Xu interrupted. “You haven't even eaten.”

A bag landed. Someone handed Tang a boxed meal. He protested, already snapping apart the chopsticks.

“I've just eaten too,” Lin Mo said.

“Hunger wasn't your problem. You nearly pulled your own arm off.”

Lin Mo had no ready answer. He looked at the pillow supporting his left arm.

“Tell me before you move anything tomorrow. I can't come. You shouldn't go alone either.”

Tang mumbled agreement, apparently through food.

Xu described the cordon. Traffic diverted, guards changed once, another small hardwood wedge securing the spindle. Service Suspended still faced outward. The overturned box hadn't moved. Cleaning staff had been told not to bag the broken pieces on the floor.

“The spectators?”

“Gone. One asked if we were filming a show. Told him to ask his mother whether she'd like to take a number here.”

Lin Mo laughed. Pain stabbed his shoulder.

Hearing his sharp breath, Xu changed tack. “Save the jokes. Get some sleep. Someone's watching tonight.”

Before hanging up, Lin Mo asked Tang to record a brief account of cutting the cord and fastening the sign. No need, Tang said. Xu had written it down; he'd come and tell Lin Mo himself tomorrow. Lin Mo didn't insist on collecting every voice in his phone. A man leaving his name and promising to return was already a way forward.

The duty phone called back shortly afterward.

The former clerk had intended to explain tomorrow. Hearing about the injuries, she'd called Xu tonight. Her surname was Liang. She'd taken the repair photograph.

“I included the sign because it went crooked whenever you turned it. The number on the glass was loose too. After sending the picture, I kept fetching documents for the last few people.”

“The window was still open then?”

“That morning, yes. We moved at noon. No service that afternoon.”

Lin Mo studied the tidy old photograph. A sign without black paper; nothing to show that the photographer would next reach in and turn it for somebody waiting.

“When did you turn it last?”

“Before the move. Somebody rushed over. I found his documents, then locked the side door and returned the key to the office.”

She paused, seeming to understand.

“I might have left it at Service Open. But the notice was up, the ticket machine switched off. The number display had been broken for ages.”

“Today it kept calling after we unplugged it.”

A heavy breath.

“I know. Xu told me. I'm not saying you heard wrong.”

He didn't ask why she'd opened the window again. That last arrival might have taken half a day off, or traveled a long way. She'd fetched another document. Tang had cut an awkward cord. Neither had left room for what followed.

Liang had found an earlier close-up, showing a rust hole beside the base. The repairer had asked whether to replace the whole piece or add a washer. She hadn't answered, then forgot.

“There's a thin iron support beneath the sign, and a little cover behind it over the screws. I can't tell where your paper is attached. Tomorrow I'll bring the old materials list so they know what belongs there.”

“No patient documents.”

“Just the desk and sign.”

She asked if he was the injured person in the wheelchair. When he confirmed it, she fell silent briefly.

“I'll visit the elderly gentleman too. We should have taken that ticket machine away with everything else.”

Lin Mo looked at the photograph of the overturned clear box.

“Don't think about hauling anything away today. Tomorrow, just tell us what you actually moved.”

Afterward, he asked the care worker to add Liang's name to the recording title. The little entry field prompted a question about which character. He explained that it had the wood component underneath, remembering Old Zhou carefully pronouncing his name on the stairs. He hadn't yet seen any altered old papers then.

People were still willing to leave their names. He held on to that, without asking her to promise she would never forget.

He put the phone aside for the night round.

The nurse checked his fingers' temperature and asked about new numbness, then examined the dressing on his leg. The gray nineteen on the side of his right leg was paler than when he'd left the window. Some surrounding skin remained red. She documented it and took a clinical photograph instead of repeatedly rubbing it with alcohol.

“Will it fade?”

“We'll keep watching. Tell us if it hurts or darkens.”

He nodded. That patch of gray answered nothing about his sister. It gave him no extra eye downstairs.

The nurse lowered the bedhead. Today's treatment, observation, and assistance had added to his expenses; the day's charges hadn't yet been collated. He asked for an itemized list tomorrow, and how to arrange everyday care if he still couldn't get up alone.

“We'll work through it in the morning. Sleep first. Ring if you need someone tonight.”

He looked at the bedside button, remembering the steps he'd practiced at home. Everything arranged before discharge, and he'd used it for only a few short days. Eating, answering a call, sitting up. Today half of it belonged to somebody else again.

“I didn't make my injuries worse on purpose.”

She paused while moving the blanket clear of his dressing.

“I didn't think you did.”

Something small lodged in his throat. He hadn't said it entirely for her. Knowing he couldn't lift his hand, he'd still reached for the catch. Given another chance, he didn't know whether he'd find a different way. People had crossed the yellow line alive. He could no longer drink without help. Both were true tonight; neither canceled the other.

After the nurse left, he asked to keep the little bedside light on.

His notebook lay in the cloth bag, pen clipped to its cover. The “big sister” on the back of his sister's photograph hadn't changed back. He wouldn't ask the care worker to spread everything out again because he'd suddenly grown frightened. Just move the bag within reach.

His fingers rested on the cloth as he fell asleep.

In his dream, a box wouldn't close. He pressed the lid. His sister spoke from the other side: Don't push so hard. You'll break the cup. He wanted to ask where she was. When he woke, all he had were the words don't push so hard.

Someone coughed in the observation room. A companion murmured that the water was still hot. Wait a moment.

Lin Mo didn't call for Xiao Lu. He looked at the ceiling, then his immobilized right wrist, waiting until he knew what still belonged to the dream. The sky wasn't completely dark. The phone showed five-something in the morning.

Two new photographs from the duty staff.

The first had been taken from the same position during handover. Shutter, barriers, overturned box: no obvious movement. The second was lower. Light swept across a plastic panel, revealing the old scratch inside.

Lin Mo enlarged it.

Twenty wasn't glowing.

Beside the scratch, however, a dark-red stroke remained, thin as a scraped remnant of paint. When the box fell sideways, that stroke had gone down with its wall, close to the floor.

He remembered nineteen leaving him at noon. The paper had turned white first. The number had lingered. Something ceasing to shine didn't mean it had released the place it had used.

He sent a message asking the day shift not to move the box as though it were ordinary plastic. His finger tapped slowly. One wrong character took effort to delete.

He didn't immediately call afterward.

The people who'd watched all night needed to hand over. He needed enough people awake before anything else could happen.

Daylight gathered by the window. Against his pillows, Lin Mo remembered Tang saying that once he pressed the catch, the sign finally stopped rattling. They'd heard a minor nuisance being fixed.

He looked at that stubborn red stroke and wondered which ordinary thing, today, they would believe they'd finished dealing with.
