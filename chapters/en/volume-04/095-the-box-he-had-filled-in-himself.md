# Chapter 95: The Box He Had Filled In Himself

The bread had been in his mouth a long time before Lin Mo remembered to swallow.

He moved the photograph away from the crumbs. Beneath the desk was a deep drawer for papers he seldom needed. He knew what was in it. His fingers rested on the handle.

If those things were unchanged, perhaps the photograph had some other explanation.

And if they weren't?

A drying pole knocked against the wall outside. He pulled. The drawer opened a little, then caught on a corner of stiff brown paper. He almost used his right hand to wrench it free. Pain beneath the splint stopped him.

The dried-out pen on the desk helped instead. With its barrel he pressed the paper down, then eased the drawer open. Paper and mothballs. An old document envelope lay on top, its fastening string wound carelessly around the button. He recognized his own impatience in that knot.

He lifted it onto the desk and needed two attempts to undo it.

His discharge papers were near the bottom. He looked at the photograph before the name. A straight uniform collar, close-cropped hair, the same reluctance to smile. The face had changed. There was no break where the stamp crossed from photograph to paper, no second layer beneath it.

He unfolded the accompanying registration sheet.

Sex: female.

The period of his service, his discharge, the things he remembered doing: all remained in their places. Reality had kept that part of his life and supplied someone who had apparently belonged in it all along.

He remembered his first winter uniform fitting firmly across his shoulders. Men talking quietly after lights-out, beds creaking when someone turned. Washing after training, water dripping from his chin onto his boots. He had never needed a document before he could know who stood inside those memories.

The woman in the photograph wore a uniform too.

He looked at her for a long time. She had done nothing wrong. But he couldn't surrender his past merely because the paper was calmer than he was.

He laid the military papers beside the photograph from home and continued through the envelope.

A school registration form remained in a transparent sleeve, over certificates and results. His youthful handwriting was unmistakable: straight strokes, endings that ran a little long. The last stroke of his name touched the printed box. He had been in a hurry. A groove almost pierced the right side of the page, where a pen had refused to work and his efforts on a scrap above it had damaged this sheet too.

All those small faults survived.

In the box for sex: female.

Handwritten this time.

He raised the sleeve, then lowered it. Even the pressure of his own pen supported the changed answer. He examined the papers underneath, the photographs and personal details. Different ages, yellowed pages, ink still dark on others. None preserved the man he remembered.

There was a spare photograph inside the sleeve, left over from the application. He had cut it crookedly and stuck it there to keep it safe, then decided against peeling it off in case it tore.

The student in it was a girl.

What was he supposed to do with that afternoon? He remembered sitting among a row of boys, waiting to be called. The boy ahead kept rocking his chair into Lin Mo's knees. Eventually Lin Mo had steadied the chair back and asked him to stop. The boy had turned, looked at his face, and apologized.

The hand holding that chair had not become smaller in his memory. The afternoon had not played itself again to accommodate the photograph.

He returned it to the sleeve. He couldn't make either past persuade the other. For now, he would have to keep both.

The desk was full.

He remembered sorting these papers with his sister at the other end, stacking sleeves by size. She complained that he crumpled important receipts into his trouser pockets, then couldn't find them.

How could he lose something he was carrying?

She had looked up without answering and pulled the very paper he wanted from beneath a pile.

The hand he'd reached out with had been wider.

He spread his present hand beside the school form, stretching his fingers as if he could recover that width. His shoulder hurt. Slowly, he curled them back.

Nobody in the room was making him continue.

One thick envelope remained. He caught its edge, then felt the cold hollowness of his stomach. One bite of bread. That was all he'd eaten. He pushed the envelope back and drank, then finished his food, catching the crumbs in the wrapper so they wouldn't fall into the drawer.

Afterward, he left that last envelope closed.

Ten more sheets of paper couldn't answer his sister's telephone.

He gathered the records. The handwritten female stood sharply under the lamp. He held his pen beside it. Two strokes would cross it out. There was room, if he squeezed, for the word he wanted.

Instead he lowered the pen to his notebook.

He recorded what he'd examined, then began describing the barracks after lights-out. His left hand grew too tired to hold the pen. He stopped, leaving a long space beneath the unfinished memory. That space meant he couldn't write any more tonight. It didn't mean he couldn't remember.

He turned to earlier pages. Dust from the other place remained in the paper's seams. Everything he'd written to prevent himself from forgetting was still there: what someone had said, where he'd refused, where he'd given ground. Coming home hadn't made it into a tidy, admirable story.

His finger stopped beside Old Chen's name.

He couldn't telephone Old Chen. Nor could he send his address to Wang Hai or Sun Ning, whose whereabouts he didn't know. Surviving the door meant he could keep writing. It didn't entitle him to report anyone else safe.

He slipped the photograph into the notebook, clear of the identity card and hospital receipt.

The document envelope also held an old mobile-service slip. He generally threw bills away, but this had stayed with the original SIM packaging. On the back he'd written a hint for the service password, not the password itself. His telephone number was printed on the front.

He checked its beginning and end, then copied the complete number.

The registration copy said female too.

This time he didn't linger. He needed a line that could reach another person. At least the paper told him which company to ask.

He turned toward the door, thinking of the neighbor's telephone, then stopped. The offer had been for an emergency tonight. He still couldn't confidently recall his sister's complete number. Borrowing a phone would only return him to that unhelpful recorded message.

He placed tomorrow's transport sheet on top and wrote himself a short reminder: After the dressing appointment, replace the phone.

Before bed, he nudged Xiao Lu's bag farther onto its clean chair. It remained closed. Through the cloth, the cup touched the chair back. He left his palm there, imagining her reaching for it when she returned.

Then he wondered why he was arranging even that for her. She might want to sleep. She might want to put everything down and carry nothing.

“You decide,” he said.

Air moved through the window gap.

He woke twice in the night, once when turning pulled his shoulder, once for no reason. He found the water beside the bed, then made the slow trip to the bathroom as he'd been taught. Halfway back, he waited for his leg to stop trembling.

On the desk, lamplight caught the photograph's edge protruding from his notebook. He didn't go to look again. Near dawn he finally slept, waking with a crease from the pillowcase pressed into his cheek.

The doorbell rang on time.

He called that he was coming, finished arranging his soft shoes, and moved to the door. Yesterday's escort waited without hurrying him and placed the wheelchair where he could turn safely.

“How was the night?”

“I could get up. Had to rest halfway.”

“Any falls?”

“No.”

He let out a small breath. Nobody had stayed with him, and he had made it through.

He took his notebook, identification, and wallet. Xiao Lu's bag stayed on the chair; the sack of old clothes remained untouched. He looked back before locking the door.

At the dressing appointment, the nurse asked whether he'd removed the wrist support.

“No.”

“There's some seepage from the wound. Keep protecting it after we clean it. Being home doesn't mean you should walk more.”

When the dressing lifted, sweat broke across his forehead. She paused. He told her to continue, gripping his trousers until the seam wrinkled.

When it eased, he asked whether he could go somewhere else.

“Today?”

“To get a phone first. There's another hospital. I'm looking for someone.”

She glanced at the wheelchair outside.

“A short trip by car with help is different from walking around a building yourself. You've already been out today. Don't try fitting several journeys into it. Your shoulder and wrist still need protecting.”

He nearly said it wasn't several places. It was the one place he had left. But she was checking that his bunched trouser leg wasn't pressing on the wound. He asked about arranging his next dressing instead.

“Still on your own at home?” she asked.

“For now.”

She gave him a clean slip with the number for dressing inquiries.

“Save this once you have the phone. If something serious happens at night, call emergency services. They can send help. We can't.”

She wrote the numbers separately. He listened. Some parts of his life could no longer wait for his sister to come back and deal with them. He repeated when he should ask for help.

The nurse wrote down his next appointment and the warning signs that meant returning sooner. He kept the sheet without arguing about those few hours.

Outside, he asked the escort to stop by the wall and showed him the old mobile-service slip.

“I need somewhere that can replace the SIM. How much to add a stop on the way home?”

The escort explained that their booking covered the return journey, then called to check the extra travel and waiting charges. Lin Mo worked through them on the back of a receipt. Today's dressing, transport, food: each needed its place.

“You need a handset too?”

“The old one's gone.”

“Don't rush into buying an expensive one. It only needs to work.”

Lin Mo nodded. He might have an entire rewritten city to face, but first he needed a telephone.

He gave up the plan to go straight to the other hospital today and paid the agreed extra fare. It felt like finding another door shut against his chest. He smoothed the edge of the photograph protruding from his notebook and said they could go.

In the waiting area, a little girl tugged at a boy's sleeve.

“Wait for me, Ge.”

The boy had been walking quickly. He stopped.

Lin Mo watched until they disappeared into the daylight beyond the doors.

“In a hurry?” the escort asked.

“Yes.”

After a moment, he added, “But I need to actually get there this time.”
