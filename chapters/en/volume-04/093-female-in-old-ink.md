# Chapter 93: Female, in Old Ink

In the morning, Lin Mo asked the nurse to open the curtain a little.

His ID had lain on the table all night. The light had changed direction. The word had stayed the same. When the doctor came, he offered the card and his notebook, open only to the page he had written last night.

“I want to check my old records.”

“We can't look up the history of your identity documents here.”

“What about your records? I've been to this hospital before.”

He had remembered sometime after midnight. After leaving the army, he had twisted his ankle on freshly mopped stairs. He'd resented having to get an X-ray. Then he'd taken off his shoe in the waiting area and found he couldn't get it back on. His sister had laughed over the phone. What was the hurry? The floor wasn't going anywhere.

He gave the approximate period and described the injury.

“You want us to check your previous treatment?”

Lin Mo shook his head, then nodded.

“That too. I want to know whether you recorded me as male or female.”

The doctor considered this.

“We'll establish that it's the right visit first. Some older material may take time to retrieve. And if we only find information that's been updated since then, that won't tell us what was written originally.”

That was the distinction Lin Mo had been struggling to express.

“I want the original.”

Rounds continued. While the nurse changed the dressing on his calf, he asked three times whether he could sit up. On the third, he caught himself. She raised his pillow.

“Checking records and getting out of bed are separate things. You can wait sitting down.”

At breakfast he couldn't peel the lid from his porridge. His damp left fingertips kept slipping. When the attendant at the next bed reached over, he pulled the bowl back.

The hand stopped.

“Say if you need help.”

The attendant returned to wiping the other table. Lin Mo stared at the sealed bowl, his ears hot. Eventually he pushed it toward the edge.

“Could you lift this corner? I can do the rest.”

The attendant did exactly that. His first spoonful hit his teeth; the second found its way. Asked about family, he said he hadn't managed to contact anyone. An unused spoon was left beside him. No more questions followed.

Halfway through breakfast, he remembered the rest of that old phone call. After laughing, his sister had offered to bring slippers. He'd said no, he'd get his shoe on. She had waited until he admitted how swollen his foot was. Even the laces hurt.

He hadn't always managed without help.

Back then, he had known who would answer when he said he couldn't.

Later that morning, he sat on the bed's edge with assistance. His right leg felt muffled underfoot. He understood the pain but couldn't distribute his weight in the old way. When he leaned forward through his shoulder, the nurse stopped him.

“Don't compensate with another injury.”

Once he was back on the bed and breathing evenly, he asked how long before he could go home.

“That depends on whether you can get up safely, use the bathroom, feed yourself. Without someone there, you need to be steadier.”

He glanced at the empty chair. He didn't tell her he'd survived worse.

That afternoon, a records clerk brought an application. He gave his name, showed his ID, and confirmed he wanted only his own records. She turned the signature line toward his left hand. The last character came out cramped.

“Is that your usual signature?”

“I normally use my right.”

She noted the injury rather than asking him to reproduce his old handwriting. The search would take time. Copies had their own procedure. She would look, but couldn't promise an hour.

“I also need to find someone.”

“A patient here?”

“My sister. Another hospital.”

“We can't retrieve their files. If you have her paperwork, take it there.”

He disliked how every route ended after a few steps. At least people were telling him where.

While he waited, he asked for the belongings bag.

The nurse held it open with gloved hands while he checked the gray coat himself. First came the payment slip from its inner pocket. Dried water marks, the small identifying mark that matched his ID, the words he'd added elsewhere on the paper.

Male when I came in.

He kept them to himself. The paper was worn thin where he'd pressed too hard.

“That isn't one of ours,” the nurse said.

“I know. I need to keep it separately.”

With the patient's name facedown, he slipped the receipt between two cleared pages of his notebook. This time he watched himself move it. His ID went there too. He clipped the pen back onto the cover.

His keys and wallet were together in another pocket. Metal struck the bed frame. The shortest key had a bright worn notch near its tip. At home, the lock needed a slight turn back at the end. His sister used to say he treated it like a screw.

He didn't announce that he could go home.

The keys were still here. That was all he knew.

The wallet held a few banknotes, a bank card, old receipts. Damp had stuck one compartment together. He counted the cash and wrote down the sum. Not enough to ignore his expenses, but enough for a journey. He requested the itemized bill and asked whether he could pay by card and PIN without a phone.

“You can try at the cashier. Don't go there by yourself.”

The nurse put his wallet in a clean pouch. He felt the thin blister pack still inside the coat's left pocket lining. He left it there and covered it with the sleeve before the clothes went back in the bag.

Toward evening, the clerk returned with two sheets.

One was the result of the record search. The other was a printed scan of the old paper chart. Gray edges and the shadow of a folded corner had survived the scanner. She explained that this was a visit held in this hospital's archive, not information retrieved from elsewhere.

Lin Mo checked the name, ID number, ankle injury. He remembered the doctor putting a pen on the X-ray envelope and telling him to stop insisting he could stand. The treatment was there.

So was his signature.

“Was this image changed today?”

“Today we retrieved and printed it. The image was archived before your current emergency visit. You can request a formal review if you dispute the contents. That's as much as I can tell you.”

“Was the sex added from the current system?”

She brought the scan closer.

“The information on the search sheet may reflect the current patient profile. This part is on the original page. It was scanned with the handwriting.”

Her finger left the word visible.

Female.

The clinical note began by describing a female patient too. The uneven ink continued into the account of swelling and tenderness. When that had been written, he had never entered the theater. No audience had ever demanded he change his clothes.

He searched for printer streaks, then checked the name again. He wanted one wrong detail that would let him give the whole thing back.

Even his botched pen stroke was there.

After his sister had finished laughing, he'd signed with the phone wedged between his ear and shoulder. The last stroke had slipped. It was still slipping now, completing consent for an examination of a woman.

“Someone with the same name?”

“That's why we checked the ID number.”

“Could the entire file have been misplaced?”

“We can record that concern. A formal review isn't something I can conduct at your bedside. My saying no wouldn't settle it.”

He withdrew his hand. He had almost caught her sleeve and demanded another answer.

She hadn't done this to him.

“Please write down how to request the review.”

She listed the office and required documents on a small sheet. It would be slow. Forms, waiting, perhaps still no explanation. He kept it anyway.

“One more thing. Don't overwrite the old record because I've questioned it.”

“Corrections require a process and supporting material. We don't change the original because someone asks us to.”

His shoulders sank a little into the pillow. He needed a procedure that refused to take his word for anything to protect the evidence contradicting him.

“Leave it as it is, then.”

She recorded his request and left his copy of the application.

After she went, he placed the scan beside his ID.

One had been in his pocket. One had remained in a hospital archive.

Across a distance he couldn't measure, both had already agreed on what was true.

When the doctor returned that evening, Lin Mo pointed to the papers.

“It isn't just a mistake in today's registration.”

The doctor read the scan.

“It establishes that the old record says this too. I can't explain from this alone why it differs from what you remember.”

“You think I've remembered twenty-five years wrong?”

“I haven't reached that conclusion.”

Lin Mo held his gaze, then swallowed what he'd been about to say. He remembered lights-out in the barracks. Clipped hair itching inside his collar. His sister calling him Ge. None of those things could be held up for a doctor to examine and stamp.

“Treat the injuries first. I'll keep the records.”

“All right.”

“This body doesn't mean I'm willing to erase everything before it.”

The doctor wrote that down. Lin Mo didn't ask to have it read back this time. He could see the pen moving.

That night, he borrowed the ward telephone.

The first half of his sister's number came easily. The rest tangled with another number he used to call. He wrote down what he remembered, studied it, and finally tried the sequence he trusted most. A recorded message said the call couldn't be connected. He checked the digits again without trying other combinations.

“Try later?” the nurse asked.

“Not yet. I might be calling someone else.”

He kept the number in his notebook, clearly marked as uncertain. He had usually called straight from their chat. Rarely had he said the digits together. Losing his phone hadn't obliged the world to return everything he'd entrusted to it.

“Tomorrow I'd like to learn to get out of bed.”

“We'll ask the doctor and the rehabilitation team. First you learn not to fall.”

He nodded without bargaining.

Back in bed, he put away the record and opened to Xiao Lu's page. Lost contact at the corner. The space after it remained blank. On the next line he wrote: Payment slip retained. Start with the original hospital.

Then he took out the old chart again.

His ID could have entered the theater with him. This paper hadn't. It had waited here until he returned in an unfamiliar body, and recognized him without hesitation.

For the first time, Lin Mo was truly afraid of the door at home he had yet to open.
