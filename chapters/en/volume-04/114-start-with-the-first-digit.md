# Chapter 114: Start with the First Digit

The first attempt went wrong.

By the fourth group, Lin Mo's eyes had strayed to Xiao Lu's name on the phone. The care worker read the next digit; he answered from the following line of his old notes.

“That number isn't here,” she said.

He stopped with a sound unfinished in his mouth.

The notebook lay open near its beginning. Back then, he'd copied the account in groups separated by short dashes, preserving the source of a payment made for him. Later injuries and other people's names crowded the book. Those early lines looked strangely spacious now.

“I mixed the lines.”

He asked her to move the phone aside, leaving only the old page before him.

“Finish this one first.”

She read slowly. He watched every stroke, stopping at digits he'd left open when writing, letting her continue only once they were clear. The new record lacked two groups. He wouldn't let hope race ahead of the pen into those blanks.

At the end, one group matched.

Only one.

He closed his eyes. The earliest index had shown only the final digits too. Only after the fold opened fully had he and Wang Hai checked from first digit to last. Wang Hai had tapped twice beside the notebook, confirming the entire sequence. He hadn't skipped the beginning because Lin Mo already wanted to rush to Xiao Lu's program.

Now he didn't know where that man was either.

“Circle the matching part?” the care worker asked.

“Not on the old page. A fresh sheet, please.”

She found an ordinary notepad and copied the groups Zou had sent, leaving generous spaces. The unfamiliar handwriting helped Lin Mo return to the present. Unable to write, he kept feeling important things must lose weight. Yet another hand was steadily carrying them forward.

She finished and laid the pen outside the paper.

“Still these two gaps. And the words at the beginning aren't clear.”

The heading said only Link, not the complete field name. He realized he hadn't even established what kind of number he was comparing to the old account.

He asked her to call security again.

Zou was patrolling the corridor. No more voice from the rear room; nothing new on the recording. Lin Mo didn't ask them to wake it. Could they read the start of that line in the photograph? He needed its whole position first, before making the number into an answer.

“When the maintenance man gets back,” Zou said. “He's dealing with a leak in another building. Someone's watching here. Everything's still secure.”

“All right.”

The rear room needed watching. The hospital also had real leaks, broken things, rooms awaiting repairs. Hearing his sister's nickname didn't entitle him to stop everyone's work at this crack.

He put down the phone. The urgency in his chest remained.

Nurse Zhou arrived while he waited for the photograph. She'd learned from a colleague treating him that he was still here and stopped by at shift change. She asked about his injuries first, not whether he was still looking for his sister.

He told her he'd found another lead.

“She contacted you?”

“No. There's a record connected to her. I haven't finished checking it.”

She looked at the table: open notebook, notepaper with two blanks, no photograph of his sister. He didn't know how to explain black paper and someone disappearing around a corner. Nor did he want to hold up half a number and demand she withdraw what she'd said about a year ago.

“I kept everything you told me.”

“If there are details you want to verify, use the contact information they gave you.”

“What if I really do find her?”

Zhou paused. Her small work bag remained in her hand, clasp undone, a corner of a lunch-box cover showing. She had things to do after work too. She hadn't come specially to choose a side.

“Then first see what help she needs.”

He looked at her and slowly nodded. He'd prepared for an argument, not a sentence that left him somewhere to go next.

Before leaving, she moved the cup inward from the table's corner, out of an elbow's path. She didn't touch the notes or demand to inspect his evidence. At the curtain, she reminded him to eat dinner, then smiled: he was looking at the unwashed lunch box in her bag. After the curtain closed, the urgency remained. But he no longer needed to fill both gaps immediately to make room for today's little hope.

Outside, someone hung a bag on a bicycle's handlebars, then doubled back before leaving to retrieve a fallen spring onion. Even an ordinary mistake allowed a return to put it right. As the rider vanished beyond the window frame, Lin Mo remembered Xiao Lu looking back, telling them to move the cart away from the sloping wall. She could still see his danger. Her own body could find nothing to bear its weight.

Only now was he reaching the edge of the paper that called it insufficient existence.

When the care worker came to reposition him, he asked her to move the phone farther away.

She hesitated.

“So I won't keep staring.”

“Where?”

“Somewhere I can hear it ring.”

She left it faceup across the little table. This distance was his choice. He hadn't abandoned the message or let one photograph occupy the whole bed.

He rested with his eyes closed. Awake, without assembling missing digits. His shoulder pain slowly became a dull weight. The cushion remained under his knee. When the support strap pressed uncomfortably, he asked someone to check and adjust it rather than removing it himself.

By the time the phone rang, he could hear Zou's first sentence through.

“The heading's clear. Look first. I won't read you the number.”

A short edge divided two lines. The upper one said Channel, with Xiao Lu fully visible after it. Below was Payment-source link. The number the remnant had read extended along that line behind the support.

Lin Mo kept the photograph open.

“My old record is a payment-source account too.”

“Shall we try to see the missing parts?”

“Read them first. You don't need my number.”

Zou understood. He and the maintenance man read separately, writing what they could see before combining their versions on one sheet. At one position they differed: one saw a closed shape; the other saw the top interrupted by paper fibers. The maintenance man shifted the camera sideways slightly. The pale patch separated from the digit.

“It's a split,” Lin Mo heard him say. “Don't count it as a stroke.”

Another group complete.

The care worker added it in a different pen. He waited until she'd finished, then compared groups with the old page. One matched. The next matched. At the remaining gap, both stopped.

“This part's still inside,” Zou said.

Lin Mo said nothing. He knew what he wanted to say. Only one group missing. Everything visible matches. Let that be enough. The thought was even considerate: it would let the two men leave sooner and give him good news for tonight.

The care worker laid the pen crosswise and waited.

“Leave it blank.”

She nodded without shortening the space.

The voice in the rear room woke then.

Old Chen asked for water, then his bag. Zou gave the agreed answers. The bag wasn't here. Nobody had taken it. Lin Mo had visited today and heard him say he was cold.

“He's gone?”

“Upstairs. On the phone.”

Lin Mo called to him. Old Chen's voice slowly gathered toward them, repeating half an old line before suddenly asking whether he'd brought paper.

“My notebook.”

“Then write… something for me.”

“Tell me.”

Silence. The maintenance man steadied the slightly wobbling lamp stand. The beam stayed off the main sheet; the gray shape grew no clearer. Old Chen kept trying a word. Air scraped through something throatlike and narrow. No word formed.

“Forgot.”

“I'll wait.”

“You wait. I'll still… forget.”

Zou didn't interrupt. Much later, unmistakable anger entered the voice.

“I don't only ask about bags.”

Lin Mo's nose stung.

“I know. You mend schoolbags, complain about thick needles. You want to know if anyone's looked for a name for you.”

“That was… acting.”

“Did sewing it hurt too?”

“Pricked my finger. Who told him… not to move the camera?”

Lin Mo hadn't heard that before. He had it noted as something Old Chen had said today, without using a new detail to vouch for the fragment. It only felt as though someone trapped behind the same question had finally pushed out another complaint.

“Tell me what you want written down when you remember.”

“Don't rush me.”

“All right.”

After a while, Old Chen began reading the lower line himself. He read the first part from the beginning, matching what Zou and the maintenance man had seen outside. Then he reached the group hidden behind the support.

Two digits. His voice caught.

Lin Mo didn't supply the answer from his own page.

The thick needle scraped inside the paper. As the old recorded man's voice prepared to speak, Old Chen seemed to move aside.

“Noisy,” he murmured.

The maintenance man asked what he could do. Lin Mo told him only to leave the paper alone. He couldn't invent a way to stop something he didn't understand just because he was anxious.

Zou moved his phone away, keeping its speaker clear of the other noise. Old Chen's voice didn't follow. It stayed where it was, finishing the last group in fragments.

The care worker entered the final digit.

Only when her pen left the paper did Lin Mo return to the old page. Beginning to end, once more. Where the dashes fell, which digits looked open at the top: the new record omitted nothing.

The whole sequence matched.

He didn't speak immediately. The care worker looked up and offered water. He nodded. The first sip only wetted his lips. He swallowed the second.

Zou waited on the phone.

“It matches,” Lin Mo said. “Someone used this source account to pay a price for me. Following it led me to Xiao Lu. This transfer record has the same link.”

“Your sister?”

“The one connected to her.”

His chest loosened, then hurt again. The connection was finally complete enough. He wanted even more to hear his sister speak, if only to ask whether he'd eaten. Paper wouldn't ask. The fragment on the phone couldn't ask for her.

Old Chen suddenly called out.

“Don't keep… reading farther.”

“Where?” Zou asked.

“Underneath is… me. You've all pressed over it.”

The maintenance man stepped back at once, saying nobody was pressing on the paper. Old Chen didn't answer. The gray shape withdrew into an old crease occupied by new writing. Hearing his voice thin, Lin Mo understood he might not mean the hands in front of him.

He asked Zou to stop recording. They had the complete number. Nobody needed to read it ten more times now.

“Old Chen. Yours is written down too.”

“What is?”

Lin Mo looked at the notes, suddenly unsure which thing to repeat. Bag, name, son, or what he'd just forgotten he wanted recorded?

“You said you'd tell me when you remembered.”

A little breath came from the gray, almost a strained laugh.

“Then… don't write it for me first.”

After the call, Lin Mo had the new sheet tucked beside the original account, corners inward, without covering the old writing. The care worker asked if there was more to follow. He looked at the transfer field extending farther into the photograph.

“Yes.”

“Rest for today?”

“Rest first.”

He had her close the notebook. His left arm hadn't healed; his right wrist remained immobilized. Eating and dressing changes after he went home still needed rearranging. But when he asked tomorrow where his sister had gone, he'd have one complete, matching source to ask with him.
