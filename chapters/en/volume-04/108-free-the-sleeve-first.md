# Chapter 108: Free the Sleeve First

The first clip fell and rolled beside the board.

Tang left it. His long-handled tool withdrew and settled against the second clip. Only his shoe tips showed on camera, behind a temporary mark farther back than the yellow line.

The younger maintenance worker, Feng, held the board's rear end in both hands. They'd practiced releasing clips on an ordinary old box, then replaced the tools that would touch the floor with long-handled ones. The board pinned the red-marked clear wall. The base slanted above it like an overturned chair.

“Second one's coming out,” Tang said.

Plastic cracked sharply.

Lin Mo straightened instinctively. His left shoulder reminded him where he was. He settled against his pillows and watched the narrow screen without speaking.

Xu stood behind and to one side of the workers, holding the phone. His breathing swayed the image. One corner of the box was missing. Lin Mo asked him to step back, rather than reach past anyone to include it.

“Now?”

“Enough. Watch your feet first.”

With the second clip released, the base dropped slightly. Feng pressed the board forward against the clear wall while Tang laid the freed section of ordinary frame flat.

The red mark stayed dark.

Behind the barrier, someone asked why they couldn't smash it.

Tang steadied his tool and looked up.

“You picking up the flying pieces?”

Silence. Xu asked the person to leave; footsteps hurried away. Lin Mo remembered yesterday's broken pliers. Broken things were still there. Losing their original shape didn't mean they no longer needed looking after.

Feng asked about another board.

“Get the spare ready,” Tang said. “This one's holding. Don't shove both in and lift the box.”

The second board lay outside the line, an old dark water stain in its grain. Scissors, gloves, and an empty long-handled clamp waited elsewhere. No tools blocked the retreat. They agreed on who would withdraw first before continuing.

Lin Mo called Tang's name.

He stopped.

“We don't have to finish today.”

“I know.”

“If the glue's too strong—”

“I've dismantled boxes before,” Tang interrupted, a little impatient. “Tell me if it changes. Leave the glue to me.”

Lin Mo shut his mouth. He could understand the impatience. After twenty years at a job, nobody wanted instructions over the phone on holding a knife. But downstairs they also had to tolerate someone who'd seen other things, who heard scraping metal and remembered a gate that wouldn't stay closed.

He didn't explain. When Tang looked up again, he said, “All right.”

Tang nodded toward the camera. The second clip was free; the frame lifted a little. On an ordinary box, that movement would be enough to trust that the next step would follow the same course.

The normal hospital announcements called two examination numbers. Lin Mo heard them over the phone. He didn't check the red stroke each time, keeping his eyes on the actual point of contact.

“The third one's glued. Can't pull it straight out.”

The narrow strip from the old base repair. Their long-handled knife could cut along the glue without disturbing the black scrap under the shutter. Feng held the board firm. Tang slid the blade into the clear seam.

The tip traveled less than an inch.

The whole box gave a small, sudden contraction.

The black scrap hadn't moved. The patched strip bowed like a string held taut too long, then sprang free at a corner beyond the board. The clear wall's upper edge rose with it, exposing the red stroke to the light.

“Push it down!” Tang shouted.

Feng bore down immediately. The old frame jammed beneath the board, preventing it from reaching the floor. The clear wall bent only halfway.

Twenty lit up.

Smaller than in last night's photograph, cramped on slanting plastic, with too little room to straighten. The counter shutter stayed still. The original display remained black. Only the box shone, two red digits drawing a thin line of light through the dust.

Lin Mo heard a faint “Please.”

Beyond the phone's edge, the care worker approached with warm water. Seeing his face, she set it down and came within reach.

“Trouble downstairs?”

“They're still where they were.” His voice tightened.

The camera lurched left.

The long-handled knife dragged halfway in, its end striking the clamp Tang had just withdrawn. The clamp's adjustment screw sprang sideways and caught his work-jacket cuff. He'd released the handle, but that little snagged scrap of fabric pulled his arm back.

“Your sleeve!” Xu shouted.

Tang's shoe slid forward an inch. The temporary mark remained ahead. Tape couldn't stop his foot. Through a jacket pulling tight, danger had reached someone outside the line.

Tang didn't wrench backward. He bent his knees and pulled open the jacket's front with his other hand, giving his shoulder room to slip out.

“Don't pull me!”

Xu's reaching hand stopped behind his back. The camera tilted toward the floor. A shoe, the board's shadow; nothing else.

“Put the phone down,” Lin Mo said. “Help him.”

The image shook, then settled on the top of the barrier. The box disappeared from view.

Feng shouted that something was jammed under the board. Don't lift it, Tang said. Use the second one. Wood scraped urgently, heavily across the floor, punctuated by several small cracks of plastic.

Lin Mo didn't shout left or right into a scene he couldn't see. He could hold none of those tools. The only thing the picture told him was that nobody was arranging the emergency for his viewing convenience anymore.

“The sign's still secure!” someone called from farther back.

Xu repeated it loudly.

The main window hadn't restarted. The box on the floor was the problem. Lin Mo held those locations apart in his mind, refusing to let the crashes merge them.

Fabric strained and tore over the phone.

Plastic slapped hard against the floor. The thin voice from the box broke off.

“Got it down!” Feng shouted.

“Scissors,” Tang said. “Stopped pulling. Cloth's still caught.”

“Here.”

“Don't reach in front. My left hand.”

Scissors opened and closed. The first cut didn't go through. Tang swore, so briefly Lin Mo barely heard. Another snip.

A moment's quiet.

The connection hadn't gone. He heard several people breathing hard, Xu saying it was free, back up, mind the tools. Lin Mo opened his mouth to ask about injuries, then left the space to footsteps.

After a while, Xu retrieved the phone.

Tang leaned against a wall, his work jacket slit a long way up from the cuff, exposing an old shirt. His arm moved. Xu asked him to turn it, checking hand and wrist. One patch of skin was red from the cloth's pressure. No visible bleeding.

Feng still held the second board against the clear wall. Beside the first lay the frame that had jammed it. Someone passed a long wooden batten in from the side to secure the board, then let him release it slowly.

Tang reached for the phone. Xu drew it back.

“You get checked first.”

“I'm not hurt.”

“Last night you said you didn't need dinner.”

Tang glared, but stopped arguing. By the time a nurse examined him, he was sitting on a hastily fetched stool, shoulders finally lowered. Lin Mo saw the little hole in his cuff. An ordinary adjustment screw could catch it. No hand reaching through glass required.

Feng explained that he'd pinned only the middle of the wall. The repair strip was at the edge. They hadn't discovered its tension until they cut it. Sliding the second board in diagonally had pressed that raised corner down separately. Only then had the number gone out.

“You had to cut your sleeve to get away?” Lin Mo asked.

Tang took the phone, still breathless.

“Board went down, tools stopped dragging. Screw was still caught. I cut the damn cloth, not my arm.”

He lifted and shook the torn sleeve, as if trying to shake off that snag itself.

Watching, Lin Mo realized he was biting his lower lip. He released it slowly, already tasting blood.

“Leave it for today.”

“No shit.”

They looked at each other through the screen. The rebuke let Lin Mo breathe a little easier. Someone handed Tang water. He drank, spilling some onto his shirt.

“Lying down doesn't make the box safe,” Feng said. “Every panel that can rise needs pinning first.”

“And that repaired edge.” Tang wiped his mouth. “My repair. Thought I knew how it'd spring. It's old. Much stiffer now.”

Nobody suggested they only needed to work faster.

Everyone withdrew. Both boards stayed in position. Nobody forced the tools free. Someone separately watched the original sign. A new handover instruction: the clear wall could display a number again; the detached frame mustn't be removed on its own. Tang identified the ordinary repair pieces he recognized without letting anyone lift that side for a better view.

Only after those arrangements did Lin Mo speak quietly.

“This morning I only watched the red mark.”

Xu asked what he meant.

“I told you not to move the box. Didn't think it could stand up as you took it apart.”

“So we stopped. We'll think again. You're floors away. Our hands aren't yours to account for too.”

Lin Mo didn't answer immediately. He remembered reaching past the inner baffle in the theater. He'd lifted it only a little. Old Chen had been forced to stay. Even now, trying to see one layer farther, he could send other people's tools into places he couldn't see.

Saying everyone had volunteered wouldn't make that any lighter.

“Next time, talk through where someone could get trapped too. If we can't make sure they can withdraw, we wait.”

Tang looked at him.

“Fine. Don't turn up tomorrow in a wheelchair saying you'll feel better watching in person.”

Lin Mo looked from the screen to his left arm.

“Not now.”

The care worker had already called a nurse. She neither hurried the people on the phone nor asked which words mattered, only checked the pillow beside his shoulder. He'd held himself tense so long that the place supporting him no longer did.

Xu ended the call first.

When the screen darkened, Lin Mo heard the observation room again. Someone beside him ate an orange, peel on a tissue. Outside, an empty trolley passed with uneven wheels knocking out a steady rhythm.

The nurse examined his bitten lip, then checked whether the tension had worsened his shoulder and knee pain. He said honestly that they hurt. Didn't call it the black paper summoning him downstairs.

“No more watching today,” she said.

He looked at the phone. “If they—”

“They have people. So do you.”

He swallowed the unfinished sentence. The care worker brought the water back. He drank a little, warmth stinging his lip.

Toward evening, Xu sent a short message. Tang had no new injuries. The box wall was secured. No more dismantling tonight.

A photograph followed. The cut jacket hung over a chair back, one sleeve dangling above a handwritten note. Tang had written it himself.

Don't throw away. This is where the sleeve snagged.

Lin Mo stared at the words and finally felt a little hungry.

Today, what remained was a torn sleeve. Its owner was still there to decide it didn't explain enough, to insist on adding a line.

He put the phone down and asked the care worker to serve his dinner. She adjusted his pillows and asked if the height was right. He tried swallowing a mouthful and nodded, without turning back to the newly darkened screen.
