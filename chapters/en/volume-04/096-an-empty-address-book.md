# Chapter 96: An Empty Address Book

An electric scooter blocked half the ramp outside the mobile shop.

The escort went inside to find its owner rather than tilt the wheelchair through the gap. Lin Mo waited under the awning. A man emerged holding a service form, moved the scooter, and apologized. His glance rested briefly on the wrist splint before he returned to the queue.

Lin Mo gathered the cloth bag onto his lap. Without Xiao Lu's cup, it was lighter. His arm still curved protectively around the place where the cup should have been.

At the counter, an employee moved a chair aside to make room for him.

“The phone and the SIM were lost together?”

“Yes. I want the same number.”

He laid down the old service slip and his identity card. She checked the number, asked about the account, then took him through the identity verification. He couldn't see her screen. He watched the card, its little ink mark still visible, being lifted and put down.

She didn't frown or summon anyone to compare the photograph.

The verification went through.

Last night he'd wanted the card to prove something was wrong. Today he needed it to reclaim a telephone number. Both uses occupied the same small rectangle. He had to choose which mattered first.

“We can replace this SIM. Once the new one is active, the old one won't work.”

“All right. Keep the same plan.”

She showed him the charges and the account's status. He read each item, then asked whether anything else was compulsory.

“No. You can buy the phone elsewhere too.”

His hand loosened over his wallet.

Several handsets stood beneath bright advertising cards. He pointed to one of the cheaper models.

“Calls, messages, looking up an address. That's enough. It needs everything for charging.”

She put a demonstration phone within reach of his left hand. Small screen, plain casing. He tried holding it, reaching for the dial pad with his thumb. His shoulder pulled before he touched the button.

He returned it to the counter.

“Can I answer with it lying down?”

She demonstrated the speaker and volume controls, then let him try. He opened Contacts by mistake. Back, then another attempt. This time he found the little speaker.

His old screen had cracked outside the hospital pharmacy, a line running through the time display. Afterward he'd touched around it, afraid of a glass edge. His sister had told him to have it repaired. When things settled down, he'd said.

The crack had accompanied them through queues and prescription collections. It had crossed the final conversation on that screen.

This glass was smooth. His thumb still moved aside.

The employee offered another color. He shook his head and asked her to raise the ringing volume. He needed to hear it from the bathroom. Nobody would be there to call from the other room that his phone was ringing.

“This one.”

He declined the installment offer and the extra accessories. Once he'd checked the charging equipment and warranty arrangements, he paid by card. The receipt curled out. He flattened it with his left hand and slipped it into the notebook.

When the new SIM was fitted and the signal appeared, he opened Contacts almost immediately.

Empty.

He dragged the page downward. Nothing arrived. He returned to the top, as though he'd simply been standing in the wrong place.

“Where are the old numbers?”

“Your number has been restored.”

“No. The ones I saved. My sister's.”

Understanding, the employee lowered her voice.

“We've replaced the line. Contacts stored on your old phone don't come back with a replacement SIM. If you made a backup, you may be able to restore it through that account. Do you have another device?”

“No.”

“Can you sign in to the backup account?”

He thought. He remembered the messaging icon, the place his sister's picture occupied. He couldn't remember deliberately setting up a contacts backup. The old phone had remembered so much for him. Habit had persuaded him those things belonged to his own memory.

“Not at the moment.”

“Then I can't promise we can recover them. Replacing the SIM won't restore conversations from the old phone either.”

Behind him, the escort waited. He didn't hurry Lin Mo or produce his own phone to announce that the internet knew a trick.

Lin Mo typed sister into the search field.

The page stayed empty.

Once she'd complained that he had saved her under her name, as though she were anybody. What if he forgot her name?

Who forgot his own sister's name?

She'd taken his phone and made her entry so long that an incoming call displayed only the first half. He'd told her that would make it harder to find her in an emergency.

Even that unfinished name was gone now.

“Leave the backup for now.”

He put the handset down and took out the nurse's slip. His hand shook; the paper wouldn't land where he wanted it. He waited, then straightened it.

“Would you show me how to save these? I'll press the buttons.”

She guided him. Dressing inquiries first, then the escort service. He also saved the emergency number, keeping it distinct from the nursing line that couldn't send anyone at night.

Words appeared in the address book.

He knew every word. None belonged to the voice he wanted first.

The escort called from the service phone. The new handset rang sharply enough to make Lin Mo flinch. On the second ring, he touched the green symbol.

“Can you hear?”

The same voice reached him from two places.

“Yes.”

He called back to check outgoing calls, then gathered the purchase and replacement receipts. His identity card returned to its place in the notebook. Last, he picked up the phone.

It hadn't grown heavier. He still needed his entire left palm beneath it.

The sum on the receipt was one he could pay, but not one he could forget afterward. He wrote down the transport money he still needed and crossed the proposed phone case off his list.

“Just keep it away from your keys,” the employee said.

He folded clean wrapping paper between phone and keys, then checked the papers again. Even the old service slip, its work now finished, went back into the bag.

Today, at least, he had brought an old document somewhere and come away with something he needed.

On the journey home, the word Hospital appeared on a distant bus. He leaned forward before realizing it wasn't his sister's hospital, then settled back. His shoulder hurt steadily now. He held the bag in place and didn't request another detour.

The escort asked about lunch.

Only crumbs remained in the wrapper he'd left on his desk.

“There's a food shop downstairs.” He described it. “Could you ask whether they deliver to the door? If they don't, I'll pay you to collect something today.”

After checking the assistance time remaining on the booking, the escort went to ask. Through the car window, Lin Mo watched steam rise beside the shop entrance. Its old illuminated sign, one tube always dark, had been replaced by plain plastic. The person carrying food still used a towel beneath the bowls.

The escort returned with the shop's number. They delivered nearby, slowly at busy times; orders needed notice. Delivery didn't include help inside the flat. Lin Mo checked the price and precisely which door they would reach, ordered lunch, and noted that he would have to arrange breakfast ahead.

Even a meal needed a route rebuilt.

At home, the escort helped him over the threshold and through the turn to sit down. Pain caught up in layers, as though his legs were only now completing the journey. He asked for his light water containers to be filled and the charger put within reach of the bedside socket.

“Keep the cable out of the way of my feet.”

The escort routed it beside the table leg. Lin Mo needed two attempts to connect the charging plug. The man waited until it was secure, then checked Lin Mo's number against the call he'd received and updated the service details.

Future trips still needed advance booking.

The door closed. The phone didn't ring again.

Its screen darkened beside the bed. Once, his sister's messages had seemed endless. What vegetables should she buy? Did he want yogurt? Don't move the things on the desk. Busy, he sometimes left them unanswered. He would speak to her when he got home.

Now he was home. Everything was here.

He woke the screen and found nothing to answer.

When lunch arrived, he called through the door for the delivery person to wait. He stood as he'd practiced and crossed the few steps slowly. Rather than carry soup and rice together, he asked for them to be placed on the small stool inside. He could close the door himself.

He carried the rice to the bedside first. The soup stayed on the stool. Cold rice was preferable to another fall.

Halfway through the meal, his hand grew steadier.

In her last ordinary message, his sister had told him to eat before he came. He'd been thinking about the ward. An ordinary instruction hadn't seemed like something that needed preserving.

Later, in the lift, he'd deleted Where are you and replaced it with Have you eaten?

He still didn't know whether he'd sent it.

He took another mouthful. The rice had hardened a little. He finished what he could, rested, then collected the soup, saving enough strength for the return.

Afterward, he opened the notebook and removed his sister's original hospital payment receipt.

The new phone lay beside it. Patient name, hospital name, payment items, patient number: all legible. The small service number near the bottom crossed an old fold in its last two digits.

He didn't guess.

He flattened the paper under the lamp, read each digit, and copied the hospital's full name onto a fresh page. His identifying mark remained, along with the words he'd written in the theater: Male when I came in.

He didn't fold that sentence out of sight to make the receipt look ordinary.

This was the paper he'd brought back. He would use it whole.

On the phone, he found the hospital's own public page and matched the name and address. Its switchboard number differed from the old printed number. He recorded both, adding where he'd found the new one. Age alone didn't make either true or false.

His sister had once circled an entrance on the hospital map because it was nearer her ward. He'd continued using the door he knew, carrying his bags the long way. What difference did a few extra steps make?

Now every step needed counting.

He drank some water and disconnected the charger. Raising the phone toward his ear immediately hurt his shoulder. He laid it flat, as the employee had shown him, with the dial pad open.

The white page waited.

He wrote his questions first. Where was the old department now? What should he bring? How should he get there?

Only then: Where is my sister?

His finger rested beside the screen. He didn't delete any of the three saved numbers to make room for the person still missing.

Nobody had to give up their place.
