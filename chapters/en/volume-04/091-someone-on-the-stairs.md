# Chapter 91: Someone on the Stairs

Water dripped beside his ear.

Lin Mo tightened his right arm until the cup pressed through its bag against his ribs. Then he remembered there should be a cart beneath him. He reached for its edge with his heel and found cold concrete. Water soaked through his torn stocking.

He opened his eyes.

A bare pipe crossed the ceiling. Water gathered around the white crust at a joint and fell into a hollow inches away. There was no lamp. The pale light along the wall came from a high window.

“Xiao Lu.”

His voice was high and hoarse. It struck the low ceiling and returned. The second time, his aching throat managed only the last syllable.

Nobody answered.

He raised his head. The bag strap still circled his right forearm, its opening trapped beneath him. The wood was gone. No cushion lay on his left. Beyond him were empty cartons, broken plastic pipe and an overturned rack, with no fresh wheel tracks that he could see.

He didn't try to stand.

He moved his toes, then drew a knee toward his chest. His right knee trembled with pain. Putting weight through his left shoulder dragged him back beneath the mirror. He eased off, supported himself on the outside of his elbow and worked his back toward the wall. His gray coat dragged through the water, chilling his waist.

The bloodstained white cloth clung to his left forearm. He moved its damp corner away from the wound and left it resting in his elbow.

The notebook remained inside his clothes.

He felt its cover and the clipped pen, then kept his hand there. He remembered his name. Wang Hai had crossed ahead of him; Sun Ning had looked back with Old Chen's bag in her arms. His sister's hand had left his. Her final moving lips had made no sound he could hear.

It all remained in order.

He opened the notebook against his abdomen. The first stroke ran too long. He was gripping too tightly, and his wrist shook. Adjusting his hold, he wrote only what he knew: he had crossed the outer doorway, awakened underground, and could see none of the others.

At Xiao Lu's name, he stopped.

Lost contact at the corner.

He left the next space empty. He wouldn't write dead or escaped. Clipping the pen back, he remembered his sister asking whether a wrong word could be crossed out and written again. He'd complained about her scattering eraser crumbs everywhere.

Now he would rather keep an unanswered space than fill hers for her.

He tucked the notebook away, checking that it wouldn't slip, and looked beyond the cartons.

“Wang Hai? Sun Ning?”

Three drops fell between his calls. Another sound rumbled farther away. Holding his breath, he distinguished tires passing over a joint. The sound came from outside and was receding.

He shifted the strap toward his elbow, away from the swollen wrist. Lifting the opening, he checked the cup. Its lid remained tight. Papers were crowded to one side, their corners clear of the water. He closed the bag and settled it on the dry patch between his legs.

“Xiao Lu. I'll find someone.”

Only afterward did he notice he'd paused for an answer.

A rusty iron door stood across the basement. Two rows of screw holes marked the wall beside it, surrounded by a paler patch where something had once hung. He stared, unable to turn that patch into the doorway he'd come through.

Shoes passed the high window, blocking the light. Someone said they couldn't park there and should reverse farther. A horn sounded. Its ordinary impatience hurt his chest.

“Someone's down here.”

Too quiet. He tried again. The engine rose, swallowing the words, and he coughed until the bag nearly rolled into the water.

He pulled it back. He couldn't compete with an engine. He needed the exit.

The door was only a few strides away. Once, in training, he could have covered that distance in a breath. Now he freed the skirt beneath his knee and dragged a leg across the first dry patch. The broad belt dug into his hip. He loosened it enough to breathe.

His weight settled in unfamiliar places.

The theater had taught him to place his feet closer together, to stop trusting his old shoulder width. Yet on waking he'd briefly expected his old body back. That moment was over. The concrete allowed no room for it.

The door wasn't locked.

Bracing with his left foot, he pushed at its lower edge. Rusty hinges shrieked. Behind it was a narrow flight of stairs, brightening toward the top, where a slanted board blocked half the opening.

Fresh air came down, carrying the smell of cooking oil.

He rested against the frame. He could count every step. Counting didn't get him up them. When he tried raising himself on his better leg, the injured knee buckled inward. He sat before he fell, protecting his right wrist.

The bag struck the threshold. The cup knocked inside.

An old handrail ran up the inner wall, its fixing bolts exposed. He tested it with his forearm. It held. Turning his back uphill, he moved his hips onto the first step, waited for the pain to recede, then eased his heels after him. Only once he was secure could he pull the bag alongside.

The second step was harder.

Cloth scraped his wounds. The fresh scab on the back of his hand split. He remembered the wall behind Xiao Lu: he'd been able to touch it, but not her. Everything here allowed his touch. Concrete, railing, door, dust. None knew where she'd gone.

“Easy,” he whispered.

He meant himself. Nobody else had to obey.

A muddy handprint marked the wall, its fingers short and broad. He placed his hand beside it without touching. His own was narrower than he remembered, the knuckles reshaped. So often, waking had meant finding what still worked before acknowledging that the hurting body belonged to him.

This time too.

He carefully moved a stone from beneath his hip. Only afterward did he realize he was crying. Tears reached the corner of his mouth. He had no strength to wipe them away.

He rested on the third step until sweat glued his hair to his forehead. Above him, a shutter rattled upward. He knocked his heel against the stair; the sound was dull. A discarded length of plastic pipe lay nearby. He picked it up and tested it. It bent without breaking.

He struck the iron rail instead.

Three knocks. A pause. Three more.

The shutter stopped. A man complained about somebody blocking the fire exit. The board shifted.

“Hello?” Lin Mo called.

A middle-aged man in dark work clothes peered down, carrying work gloves. Sweat darkened his collar.

“What are you doing down there?”

Lin Mo couldn't explain his arrival in a sentence.

“I'm hurt. I can't get up.”

The man saw the blood. Pocketing his gloves, he descended two steps.

“Where did you fall from? Up there?”

“I don't know how I got here.”

“Been drinking?”

“No. Don't pull my arm. My shoulder's hurt.”

The reaching hand withdrew. The man called upstairs for someone to stop reversing the truck and bring a phone. Lin Mo asked whether anyone else was below.

“I only just opened the back. Wait. I'll take a look.”

He took out his own phone, switched on its light and went down to the doorway. Without climbing over the rack, he swept the beam across the floor and behind the cartons.

“Nobody where I can see.”

“Any other doors?”

“Used to be separate storerooms. Don't know what's sealed. I work next door.”

He sounded impatient, but didn't pretend he'd searched everywhere. Returning, he reached for the bag. Lin Mo trapped it against his stomach.

“This stays with me.”

“Nobody's taking it. Stop trying to climb.”

Lin Mo loosened his grip. “It's my sister's.”

The man left it alone. Someone upstairs called him Zhou and said the customer was waiting. He told them to unload what they could; he'd follow. They complained about carrying it shorthanded. After a pause, he shouted back to leave the heavy stuff.

Lin Mo wanted to tell him to go. He'd occupied too many routes other people needed to leave by. Before he could speak, Zhou braced a foot against the swinging iron door to keep it from striking him.

“You can stay sitting?”

“Yes.”

“Then rest. You don't have to impress me. I've put my back out loading trucks.”

Lin Mo finally let himself lean against the stair behind him. This man's livelihood waited upstairs. He'd stayed anyway, with his foot against the door.

Someone passed down a thin blanket, saying it was all they had in the vehicle. Zhou asked before covering Lin Mo's torn stockings and dusty legs, keeping the fabric off the calf wound. His movements were clumsy, and more useful than hauling him upright.

Another person raised a phone.

“Is this a film? Those clothes—”

“We're calling an ambulance. Keep the entrance clear,” Zhou said.

The phone lowered. Lin Mo gathered his coat, covering the blue fabric and loosened belt beneath the blanket. People called about deliveries and clearing the way. Their voices overlapped without anyone directing them.

He suddenly wanted water.

Xiao Lu's cup was beside him. He felt its round lid through the bag but left it closed. He didn't know how long the water had been there, and couldn't bear to spill the one thing they had finished fastening for her.

Thinking he wanted a phone, Zhou asked about family.

“I lost mine.”

“Remember a number?”

He thought of his sister and the hospital payment slip. He wanted to ask for a phone to call her. First, though, someone had to get him off these stairs. Finding her would require a clear head and a body that could keep going.

“The ambulance first. Please.”

“Called already. She's giving them the address.”

A woman in an apron crouched above them, phone to her ear. She read the street sign twice, then stood to check the old number above the door. Lin Mo tried to retain the words.

“They'll find us,” Zhou assured him.

“If someone comes looking, tell them where I went.”

Zhou crouched a step away.

“Your name?”

“Lin Mo. Lin as in forest. Mo as in silence.”

“I'll note where they take you.”

Lin Mo asked him to repeat the name. Puzzled, Zhou did. Both characters right.

An empty paper cup fell from above, rolled two steps and stopped. The woman muttered at her own clumsiness and fetched another. Listening, Lin Mo could finally separate the traffic from an approaching siren. Someone was following actual streets toward an address that could be spoken aloud.

The woman finished confirming it, then looked down to answer the dispatcher's question.

“A woman, twenties. Conscious and talking. There's blood on her. She can't walk.”

Lin Mo raised his head.

The word had come so naturally. No lights appeared. Nobody waited for his agreement. He'd barely escaped, and people on the street were already using it to call for help.
