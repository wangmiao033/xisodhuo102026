# Chapter 113: The Words Added Later

When Lin Mo woke that afternoon, somebody was calling another bed number.

He kept his eyes closed until the voice stopped. A curtain opened and an empty bed rolled past. He couldn't reach his phone. His fingers moved against the sheet, and the care worker asked whether he wanted water.

“What time is it?”

She told him. Security had called, she added, but said not to wake him. The voice had returned at the counter. The guard had recorded it; Lin Mo could call when he was ready.

“Water first, please.”

He drank slowly. Swallowing still hurt. Just as she set down the cup, Zhao Yun appeared outside the curtain with a small bag of bananas. Her father wasn't with her.

“He held his own bowl today,” she said. “There's still a mark on his hand. We'll ask about discharge when the doctor comes this afternoon.”

Lin Mo asked about hers. She flexed her thumb carefully. It moved, though the dressing felt tight. The bite mark on her other hand had faded without disappearing.

“When I saw the ticket going into my skin, my mind went blank.”

“Take things slowly now you can hold them again.”

She looked down at the bananas and realized he couldn't peel one. Did he want some now? Half, he said. She peeled one and gave it to the care worker to break into pieces, wrapping the rest for his bedside cabinet.

Her father had always complained that she took too long, she said. Even getting two tickets had required questions. Then she stopped, her eyes reddening, before managing a small smile.

“He understands going slowly now.”

Lin Mo swallowed the sweet mouthful. He remembered the strength of her hand supporting his lower back at the counter.

“He'll wait for you this time.”

“He'd better.”

She had to return to him. Before leaving, she gathered up the plastic bag so it wouldn't catch on the bed. Lin Mo watched the curtain close. If that window reopened, other people would approach it as she had, simply hoping to spare someone in their family a little walking.

He called security.

Xu's colleague answered. His surname was Zou; they'd met in the staff corridor that morning. Xu had gone home.

“It says there's something pressed underneath,” Zou said. “The mechanic photographed words. I'll send them.”

The photograph came quickly. The camera had moved slightly left of the earlier rear view, showing an existing gap between the support and the main sheet. The paper hadn't been lifted. The corroded edge still held it in place. From this angle, more strokes could be distinguished.

After front-stage structural failure.

Only the end of the next line showed: storage transfer.

A screw hole from the removed cover was visible in the corner. Lin Mo asked Zou to keep the whole image as well as send a close-up. If somebody later cropped out just the words, they should still be able to find the sheet and support those words belonged to. He'd seen too many things survive with only their first half intact. He wanted the surrounding space preserved too.

His breathing accelerated as he studied failure. When the front stage collapsed, he'd been lying on a low trolley. His sister had remained at the turn in the corridor. Old Chen had disappeared long before that. These words couldn't simply be inserted into the man's memories.

“Did the writing just appear?”

“Nobody saw it being written. There are pale marks in this morning's photo too, but the angle only shows the outside.”

“What else did he say?”

“Kept asking whether something was pressing on him. We haven't touched the paper.”

Zou brought the phone inside the doorway, keeping it two paces from the frame. The mechanic was there. The lamp now rested on an ordinary work stand, and another person watched the sign's fastening from the side.

Lin Mo heard them identify themselves. Then silence.

“Old Chen.”

His voice reached the paper through the speaker. Eventually, a rasp answered.

“Who put you...farther away?”

“I'm upstairs. You're hearing a phone.”

“Can't see.”

“It's enough if you can hear me.”

The old needle scraped across the table. A faint breath caught beneath it. Lin Mo waited, looking at the meal record beside his bed. The care worker had marked how much he'd eaten. Those dark strokes and the faint marks downstairs had nothing in common except that someone had needed to see something before recording it.

“When you said sent afterward, did you mean that line below?”

“Pressing.”

“Had you seen it before?”

“Not...here.”

The voice repeated here. Lin Mo wanted him to start again. But the speaker seemed aware of his own difficulty, and anger suddenly broke through.

“I'm looking now!”

“All right. Tell us the words you can see.”

“Front...stage. That part broke. The part behind it...didn't.”

Lin Mo swallowed his next question. The photograph said structural failure. It didn't say everything had ended. He'd seen the front stage collapse and passed through its outer door. He hadn't seen the back section the writing referred to.

Zou retrieved the phone. “Half a frame beside that word. The mechanic can change angles without moving the support.”

“Stay where you can stand. Don't push the phone behind the paper.”

“We'll zoom.”

The care worker propped Lin Mo's phone and adjusted the support that had slipped beneath his leg. Absorbed in the call, he gasped as she moved it. Someone downstairs immediately asked what was wrong.

“My leg's being moved up here. If everything's all right there, carry on.”

The words brought him back to his bed. Every sound of pain he heard didn't belong to that paper.

The second photograph showed two layers of different tones beside the opening. The older one read Original Owner Deactivated. Pale new writing crossed it, ending at a tiny turn, as though another record had folded there and left an overlapping trace.

Residual source material: local reuse.

Lin Mo understood why the mechanic had said something lay underneath. The commands they'd encountered at the window, the calling and waiting and suspension, occupied material that had once held other people.

“It used the old sheet to make something new,” Zou said.

“At least those layers are together.”

Lin Mo's voice was low. Source material reduced everything he'd heard of Old Chen's fear to something that could be used. The original owner could be deactivated while a suitable piece of him went on serving a counter.

“Who gave it to them?” the slit asked.

Lin Mo looked at the phone.

“I asked...who gave it?”

Zou was silent. The mechanic swore softly and said there was no signature.

“Nobody's named,” Zou answered.

“Then...wasn't given.”

Lin Mo didn't correct what that could establish. The absence of a field in a photograph wouldn't return what Old Chen was asking for.

“I heard you refuse,” he said.

After a long time, half an acknowledgment came through.

They continued examining the new line. An old fold divided it. The left half was visible from behind; the right turned inward over the support. Photographs from the permitted positions finally revealed a few disconnected fields.

Performer binding released.

Insufficient existence.

Lin Mo pressed his fingers into the bedsheet.

After Xiao Lu left her station, her body had gradually lost the weight they needed to support her. But he hadn't seen any text appear before her. When the light closed, all he could touch was the wall.

These lines might describe many people.

“Is there a number after that?”

Zou asked him to wait. Enlarging the faint line only showed fibers. The mechanic tried a lower angle, then stopped. Metal blocked the view. Any lower and his phone would touch the original support.

He withdrew without removing another layer.

A sound came from the paper, almost a breathless laugh.

“You...can't see.”

“How much can you see?”

“Words...my side.”

“Read them to us.”

Lin Mo regretted it immediately. All morning, Old Chen had been trying to locate the part of himself being pressed down. Already Lin Mo had stationed him behind another counter, requiring him to read out the answer he wanted.

“Are you willing to read?” he asked instead.

This time no answer came at once.

A shoe shifted against tile. Zou told somebody not to crowd the doorway; the phone was close enough to hear.

“What about mine?” Old Chen asked.

Lin Mo looked at the notebook beside his bed.

“I haven't found the reply about your son. The part I recorded ends after you asked for the name.”

“Find...that first.”

He could say yes, give the promise now and figure out how later. But Old Chen had followed a promise that might never have existed all the way to the grille.

“I'll look at what's exposed here for anything about your question first. I can't reach farther inside, or ask people to take it apart now.”

A pause.

“I can't reach...either.”

Lin Mo relaxed his fingers.

“You can stop.”

“First...write these.”

Zou opened his own log on the table. Upstairs, Lin Mo listened. Old Chen read one character at a time, stumbled and restarted. Forced preservation came first, then a transfer link and a string of numbers.

Halfway through, the voice became the child saying Doesn't scratch.

“Don't write that,” Old Chen said.

Zou stopped his pen. Lin Mo asked him to retain the digits in separate groups, leaving blanks where they couldn't hear. The care worker confirmed that the recording on their end had saved.

A little of Old Chen's voice returned.

“Xiao...Lu.”

Lin Mo went rigid. His shoulder shifted off its cushion, and pain flashed white behind his eyes. The care worker supported him immediately, telling him to lean back.

He couldn't hear his own reply. Someone downstairs asked whether he'd heard the name.

“Yes.”

“Someone you know too?” Zou asked.

Lin Mo waited through the pain before answering.

“Where is she?” the slit asked.

“She couldn't come with us when we left.”

“Who told her...not to go?”

“I don't know.”

Now he could hear it plainly. Old Chen knew nothing of the collapse. He was reading new words pressed over this small surviving piece of himself. Having found a familiar person among them, he was asking Lin Mo where she'd gone.

“She's my sister,” Lin Mo said.

A faint scraping came through the phone.

“You...found each other?”

“She recognized me.”

His mouth trembled. The news had come too late, so late that Old Chen no longer had a body with which to hear it. But Lin Mo wanted him to know: the thing they'd asked about, waited for, struggled to explain in those corridors had happened, for a little while.

“That's good.”

The voice faded.

“Mine...not yet.”

Lin Mo lowered his head. Tears fell onto the coverlet. He didn't promise to find the answer for him. Both unfinished searches needed something real from him. The importance of one couldn't sweep the other aside.

After a long wait, Zou said the gray had dispersed.

“Don't call him again. Enough for today.”

“Two groups of numbers missing.”

“Leave them missing. Send what you've got.”

The handwritten groups arrived, Xiao Lu beneath them. He recognized the name, but it wasn't connected to a complete record yet. A nickname couldn't establish that he'd found his sister. A reference to preservation couldn't tell him she was warm, free from pain, waiting patiently.

He asked the care worker to open the early pages. His injured right wrist had still managed straighter writing then. The payment-source account was divided into groups. He remembered Wang Hai checking those digits against the index and nodding.

“Start here,” he said.

Outside, daylight remained. The care worker switched on the lamp and moved the water away from the notebook. Lin Mo looked at the complete old number. When his breathing steadied, he began with the first digit.
