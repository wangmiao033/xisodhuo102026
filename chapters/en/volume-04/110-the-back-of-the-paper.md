# Chapter 110: The Back of the Paper

On the third morning, Lin Mo got one small thing right.

Wait until someone had hold of him before moving.

His body kept trying to get ahead of the offered hands, as though doing it quickly enough would mean he'd stood up by himself. Today he sat on the edge of the bed and waited until the care worker had steadied his back and waist. Only then did he position the foot that could take his weight.

Someone else supported the injured leg. He didn't push against the armrest with his right wrist. Nobody used his left arm as a handle. Once in the wheelchair, he settled against the back. Sweat dampened his temples, but he didn't immediately need the bed again.

The doctor checked his condition and asked about the pain.

“A short trip. Seated, with assistance. The same assistance getting back into bed.”

“Yes.”

“You're not going to help dismantle anything.”

“They finished yesterday. Today I'm looking.”

The care worker was watching too. He made it simpler.

“Then I'll come back.”

She draped a jacket over his legs against the cooler corridor. The fresh dressing hadn't soaked through. His finger remained wrapped, his shoulder supported, his wrist immobilized. Permission for ten minutes or so outside the room hadn't healed any of those injuries.

Before the bag went onto his lap, he asked her to remove the water bottle and lighten it. Notebook inside, phone in the outer pocket. Xiao Lu's bag remained on the chair against his wall at home. Old Chen's old bag had never come back here with him at all. He could bring only his own record of what had happened.

Xu waited outside the lift.

He didn't begin by asking whether Lin Mo felt better. Seeing two people settle the injured leg, he held the door. Someone inside carried an insulated food container. Lin Mo glanced at the lid, then away.

On the ground floor, the wheelchair turned away from the front of the old window.

Xu led them past the service desk into the short staff corridor. A wall on one side, unused partitions stacked on the other. The boxes that had obstructed the route were gone. At a narrow doorway, the care worker paused to check the chair's width. Xu secured the door open.

“Small threshold ahead.”

They slowed and crossed it gently. Lin Mo didn't reach for the doorframe.

The back room smelled of rust and old adhesive.

Tang stood near the sign, in a different work shirt. He cleared a tool cable from the floor and moved the lamp stand aside.

“Actually stayed in the chair, then.”

“Would you rather they carried me?”

Tang grinned. “Your mouth's recovered.”

Lin Mo almost laughed, checked his shoulder support, and settled for moving one corner of his mouth. The care worker stopped the chair in a clear space by the wall, applied the brakes, and adjusted his feet. They were some distance from the sign, away from the glass, with the return route unobstructed.

At last he saw the sign he'd struggled to reach from the front.

SERVICE OPEN faced the back room.

The words remained clear. Two independent restraints held the spindle below them. Service Suspended still faced the hall. The original wedge remained, and the fully lowered shutter sealed the public opening.

The main black sheet clung to the rear edge of the support beneath the sign, split along the spindle. It was small. Nothing like the expanse of counter he'd imagined during the calls. Someone bending forward could hide it with half their body. This little thing had held four people inside the yellow line until he couldn't even release his own wheelchair brakes.

He looked down. The shutter's bottom rail concealed the detached scrap on the floor. From here he couldn't see that piece, or the red-marked face of the former box.

Xu showed him a photograph taken from the front that morning. Small scrap under the shutter. Separated plastic panels secured face down. Nobody had swept the scene clean to make it look as though nothing had happened.

When he had finished looking, Lin Mo asked Xu to take the phone back.

“Quiet out there today?”

“Quiet. The injured people are still under observation. Nobody's rushing them home just because this has gone silent.”

Lin Mo nodded and returned to what was in front of him.

The removed access cover lay in an ordinary tool tray. Tang pointed out that its screws had never connected to the spindle. Removing it hadn't turned the sign. The old supporting plate that held the main sheet remained in place. Braces supported its rust-thinned edges without gripping the paper or enclosing it in a little room.

The dark surface behind the rust hole faced Lin Mo.

The back of the main sheet.

Being seated lower gave him a better angle than the standing workers. Old adhesive had yellowed along the edge. Pale marks lay deeper inside, lost in glare when the lamp shone straight at them.

“Raise the light. Don't bring it closer to the paper.”

Tang adjusted the stand, sending light down at an angle.

The marks emerged.

First, the character for mending.

Lin Mo stopped halfway through a breath. It took a moment to finish. He didn't ask them to push him closer. He followed the opening downward: the next character, clothes, then the narrow shape of a four.

CLOTHES MENDING FOUR

He knew those words.

Once they'd belonged among rack references, old scenes, voices repeatedly extracted. A man had stood beside them, afraid of someone reading his notebook, afraid of forgetting his child, afraid the answer he was offered wouldn't be the one he'd wanted.

Now the words were here. The man wasn't.

Lin Mo remembered Old Chen's hand growing lighter. By the time Xiao Lu reached them, there had been no way to bring him out from beside the shelf. Clothes and shoes had vanished together. The feeling of his grip had remained in Lin Mo's palm. Later, telling Wang Hai and Sun Ning, he always needed to pause when he reached the hand.

His own hands rested beside the wheelchair armrests now, unable to reach forward.

“You've seen this?” Xu asked.

Lin Mo nodded.

“Somewhere else. That name was attached to an old segment of somebody's life.”

“Whose?”

“Someone who helped me.”

Tang locked the lamp stand. He didn't assume that meant Lin Mo's sister.

A second line, finer than the first, lay farther right. Its end disappeared into darkness. Lin Mo asked for a slight change in the light. The shadow of the support moved aside.

ORIGINAL OWNER DEACTIVATED

No name. No destination.

The words fastened themselves to an old page in his memory. Deactivation meant the account couldn't return to the stage. It hadn't caused every clip, label, and voice outside to disappear with the person. He had watched those things continue to be used.

Surviving words couldn't make a whole Old Chen stand up again.

“Is this the lead you needed?” Xu asked.

Lin Mo wanted to say yes. Instead, his sister's photograph flashed before him. The changed way she addressed him on the back. The same old crease. Hospital records recognized him, but remembered a different past. Now another old label sat before him, waiting to be believed.

“It's something I recognize,” he said. “I don't know why it's here.”

Already much more than he'd known last night.

Xu took out his notebook but didn't write yet. Should they photograph the two areas separately?

“Include both. The hospital wording and these words behind it. Not just the part I recognize.”

Tang frowned from behind the lamp.

“Is this old paper backing something new? Or did the new stuff grow on it?”

“I've seen people's voices used as material,” Lin Mo said. “Split up, put somewhere else. Made into something they never agreed to.”

Tang looked at Service Open.

“Like cutting a recording together?”

“In some ways. But the person was in it too. The person felt the pain.”

He stopped. A long tool had been severed here. Tang's sleeve had nearly taken his hand with it. Talking about some distant scene couldn't explain their yesterday, or justify asking them to stand closer.

Xu closed his notebook.

“You want to keep this to find that person?”

Lin Mo watched the words. He didn't immediately deny it.

“I want to know whether anything's left.”

“We can't reopen the window for that.”

“We won't.”

He answered quickly. The pain came afterward. He didn't know whether the pinned words might also be a door, needing only a hand. But if hearing something about Old Chen meant making a passerby take a ticket, he couldn't even ask them to try.

Tang picked up an ordinary screw beside the lamp and put it in his tool case.

“Then we'll keep the light on it. Lamp's ours. Doesn't need its permission.”

Lin Mo watched the white light. An ordinary wall socket powered it. It illuminated paper and rust without demanding any part of him. He settled against the chair, letting it stay at the angle they'd found.

Tang photographed both lines from the position Lin Mo indicated. Glare spoiled the first image; the second was clear. He didn't bend the paper. He fixed the lamp at that angle and showed Lin Mo the result.

The rust hole and a corner of the sign remained in shot, establishing where the words were visible. Lin Mo asked him to send the image to his phone. The original stayed here.

“You can't take the paper,” Tang said.

“I wasn't planning to.”

“You were staring like you'd take the whole counter.”

Lin Mo looked at his hands. “I had to have them take my water bottle out of the bag.”

Tang didn't laugh. He looped the cable against the wall, clear of the wheelchair's return path. Lin Mo watched him finish that ordinary movement. At least nobody was blocking the way back today.

The care worker asked about his shoulder.

“Aching a bit. I can still sit.”

She checked the time and adjusted his arm. She didn't hurry him into turning around, or treat his answer as permission to stay indefinitely. Their short visit was still being timed.

Lin Mo looked toward the other side of the shutter. Someone in the hall called a child's name. A loud answer, followed by a plastic bag rustling. No audience here. Those sounds loosened something in his chest. They were still in a hospital. People could finish their business and leave.

“Mr. He, who went in to help that day,” he asked. “Did he get his shoe back?”

Xu blinked. “Yes. His wife put it in a bag.”

“Good.”

He wasn't sure why he'd needed to ask just then. Perhaps because the shoe hadn't been reduced to a prop in a frightening story. The man whose foot had been injured still had a life to resume.

An edge of the black sheet moved.

Lin Mo looked back immediately.

The door hadn't opened. Tang hadn't touched the lamp. The care worker's hand remained beside the chair. Within the gap, the thin gray surface swelled almost imperceptibly, then settled, like an attempted breath from very far away.

He waited.

No number lit. No please. Nobody said his name.

Only a slight sound between the old paper and its support.

“Did you hear that?” Lin Mo asked.

Xu lowered his phone and looked at the opening.

“Yes.”

Tang withdrew the hand he'd started toward the lamp stand. Everyone stayed where they were. The door behind them remained open. The care worker still watched over the chair and its brakes.

Lin Mo didn't call Old Chen's name.

First he held on to the small sound he'd actually heard. It had said nothing yet. The words Clothes Mending Four faced him from the back of the paper.
