# Chapter 105: Don't Move It Yet

Most of his meal remained beside the bed. The care worker had just moved the tray and asked whether he wanted more.

Looking at the photograph, Lin Mo asked her to wait and prop the phone securely.

Twenty occupied one upright wall of the clear box.

They'd left nineteen in front of the window that morning. This round evidently intended to continue.

With the left fingers he could still move, he opened the photo message, checked the sender's number, rang it, and selected speaker. Xu asked whether he'd seen the photograph.

“Yes. Everyone outside the line?”

“Outside. Nobody's touched it.”

“The wedge?”

“Still there. Someone checked from the side door. Service Suspended hasn't moved.”

Lin Mo asked him to wait. He rang for the nurse and explained that he needed a call about that morning's incident. He wouldn't leave the bed. She checked his shoulder, adjusted his pillow, and told him to stop if he felt worse. A ringing phone wasn't a reason to force himself upright.

He agreed.

This time, he didn't have to send his body back across the line first.

Xu said they planned to remove the box. It had held stationery at the old counter. Someone had lowered it with a long rod to stop passing shoes from carrying away the scrap. No power supply. No display inside.

“Two more photographs before moving it? From where you are. Don't cross the tape or raise the shutter.”

“Which sides?”

“The side nearest the yellow line, and the gap. Include the whole box.”

The first arrived quickly.

Twenty glowed against the inner wall beside an old scratch. The box was upside down, its sides hanging toward the floor. The shutter's bottom rail obstructed one edge, leaving a narrow opening. The black scrap lay there, half of it still caught beneath the metal.

From this angle, the box did resemble a counter made impossibly small.

Lin Mo didn't say so yet.

In the second image, the scratch appeared at a different angle. Twenty remained in place. The number wasn't on the tiles, wasn't lying flat with the paper. It was using the clear plastic to shine outward.

“In person, is the number on the box or the paper?”

“The box. You can see it from different positions. The paper's black.”

“Was there a number before the box went down?”

“No. I'll send that picture too.”

Timestamps beneath the images, one before the other. Lin Mo asked the care worker to swipe back for him. Shutter, black edge, yellow line: already there. No box, no red light.

At least twenty had appeared after the box was placed.

More reliable than warmth in his leg.

“It may be using the box to continue as a window. I can't be certain. Keep everyone beyond the existing barriers. Nobody crouching closer for a look.”

“We're staying back. The long hook can reach the handle.”

“Don't carry it somewhere else.”

Xu paused.

“How do we remove it, then?”

Lin Mo examined the folding handle on the upturned base. One end protruded. Lift and carry it, and the numbered face would travel toward people. Clearing this patch of floor wasn't worth delivering a small working counter to somebody else's feet.

“Can you tip it over where it is? Put the numbered face downward. Leave it at the scene.”

Voices conferred. Hoarser than at noon, the maintenance man said two rods would do: one against the edge, one hooked through the handle. Nobody needed to enter and steady it.

“You're certain that'll stop it?” Xu asked.

“No. Watch the number when it begins to tilt. If it gets brighter, or anyone feels unwell, stop.”

His shoulder throbbed. The care worker looked at him. He nodded, indicating that she shouldn't raise the head of the bed any farther.

The phone stayed connected. Nobody was being asked to test his guess with their body.

Xu confirmed that Lin Mo could see only photographs, not the scene as it happened. Yes. If what they saw differed, they must stop, never keep going just because he said to. That morning, there had at least been glass, everyone visible on both sides. Now floors separated them. By the time a picture arrived, anything in it could have moved.

“We'll watch,” Xu said. “Anything wrong, we back off.”

He checked that another colleague could watch the sign from behind the counter, then sent waiting passersby farther away. Lin Mo heard barriers scrape. Someone asked what had happened. This way was temporarily closed, came the answer.

The phone was placed on a surface well away from the tape.

“Starting.”

Lin Mo had only the earlier photograph.

Plastic scraped softly over tile. The maintenance man said, “Wait.”

Lin Mo didn't hurry him.

“The bottom of the number's missing,” Xu reported. “One edge raised. Not over yet.”

The photograph arrived a little after his words. Twenty had compressed against the bottom of the wall, folded downward through its middle. The scrap remained under the shutter. It hadn't followed the box.

“Paper hasn't moved?”

“No.”

“Sign still secure?”

“Yes. Someone's watching.”

Lin Mo asked them to hold there. Had anyone suddenly heard their name called? Any numbness in hands or feet? Xu asked each person. Nobody had.

That couldn't exclude every danger. It at least kept them from forcing an unknown movement through to its end.

“Continue in that direction. Don't take the paper with it.”

The scraping resumed.

A thin syllable entered the call.

“Please.”

Lin Mo's stomach clenched.

The word didn't emerge from the phone as a thing. The observation-room lights didn't dim. Something far away had spoken. An ordinary microphone had caught it and sent the sound to his ear.

“Who's speaking?”

After a moment, Xu answered. “Over by the box. Just that one word.”

“Stay back. Don't answer it.”

A rod struck the floor. Lin Mo's heart sank.

“Still holding!” the maintenance man called. “Hook's attached. The other rod touched down.”

Lin Mo slowly released his breath.

Another photograph. The box tilted steeply. Its numbered wall showed only a narrow bright edge, an eye almost shut. The scrap hadn't curled upward. The bottom rail still held it.

“Finish turning it,” Xu told the people there.

Plastic struck hard.

Lin Mo waited for the next image.

A second. Two. Nothing arrived. On his finger, the gauze had stopped soaking through, though an earlier dark-red patch remained. That red could tell him nothing about downstairs.

“Mr. Xu?”

“Here. It's over. Number's gone.”

The photograph arrived.

The box lay on its side, the formerly glowing wall facing the tiles, handle askew. Half the scrap was exposed. No new number. No tiny figure he could recognize in the picture.

The maintenance man said he'd crouched and listened. The sound had stopped too. He'd stayed beyond the tape, away from the box.

Lin Mo asked them not to right it for another photograph.

“Leave it like that. Wait, then check.”

Xu agreed. The call stayed open.

In the quiet, Lin Mo discovered that his leg had been rigid the whole time. The care worker adjusted its support. Moving the knee brought sweat to his temples. Could he finish the call now? One last check, he said. Then he would put it down.

He couldn't live every minute as his next narrow escape.

After a while, Xu returned to the phone. No red number had reappeared. The main sign remained fixed at Service Suspended. The shutter hadn't moved. The scrap was still there. Nobody would take it.

Lin Mo kept those facts separate.

“The three tickets from this morning?”

“On the tray, inside the cordon. Same as before. Blank, except a little gray on nineteen.”

“Any new paper elsewhere?”

“None found so far. Nothing from the dispenser.”

So far wasn't all clear. They could establish that twenty no longer shone through the box, and this small site of danger hadn't been carried elsewhere.

“Leave these things where they are. Don't box them for storage. We don't know which piece might start working in a new place.”

Xu had added Do not move to the handover register. Staff would remain outside the cordon. The passage was diverted. An opaque movable screen also blocked access to the counter, clear of the paper and everything inside the yellow line. It would keep people from reaching in or taking things.

“Don't come downstairs for this tomorrow.”

“I won't make my own way there.”

Lin Mo paused.

“But I want to keep looking into the paper. When you find old photographs, may I see them? Only the sign and window. No patient information.”

Was he looking for something else?

He looked toward the bag. His sister's photograph was in the notebook. Xiao Lu's bag waited on the chair at home. Tonight he could be beside neither the sister the hospital remembered nor the Xiao Lu he'd held. A crack left by the theater had already reached ordinary people's feet.

“First, where this sheet came from.”

When the call ended, he asked the care worker to save the number as Hospital Security Duty Phone. She settled his phone and asked whether he still wanted dinner.

“Yes.”

The food had cooled. He asked her to take care of it and lay back for a while. No hurry to spread out the notebook. His left hand couldn't write. His mouth could still speak. A record didn't have to look the way it once had.

Later, he made another short recording.

“Old window three. All injured people out. Sign remains at Service Suspended. After the clear box tipped sideways, twenty and the voice disappeared. The black paper remains. It hasn't been destroyed. What happens if it's removed hasn't been tested.”

He asked for the recording to be paused and checked that it had saved.

Then a separate one, only for himself.

“The hospital says my sister died a year ago. I saw Xiao Lu. She knew me. I haven't found her.”

His throat hurt. Lights came on beyond the window. A trolley passed in the corridor. He waited for it to recede.

“Today, a man went to help someone and his foot was cut open. Next time I see something like this, I need to stop people first.”

A short sentence. It gave him no arm strong enough to hold a tool. It set no date when he'd stand again. He stopped the recording and left the phone by his pillow.

The bag stayed beside the bed. He asked the care worker to open it and follow his directions to locate the identity card, then the photograph and original receipt held inside the notebook. Then she put everything back and closed the zip. Only the new treatment papers and security number had been added. Hearing the list steadied him. New clues couldn't crowd out the old ones about his sister. Each inquiry had to continue from the evidence that belonged to it.

Another photograph arrived from the duty phone.

An older image of the counter, dated before today. The sign stood straight. Beneath Service Open, the surface was clean.

No black paper.

He remembered discovering the changed photograph at home, the way his sister addressed him on its back no longer matching his memory. One old image couldn't decide the past for him. It did make questions specific. Who took it? Where had they stood? Who later closed the window? Those people might still answer in their own voices.

Lin Mo enlarged the foot of the sign. An unbroken plastic cord hung there. The cord he'd caught with his fingers had been half gone.

He rang the newly saved number.

“After this photograph was taken, who last handled the sign?”
