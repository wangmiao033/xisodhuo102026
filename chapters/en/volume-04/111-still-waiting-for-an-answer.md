# Chapter 111: Still Waiting for an Answer

The breathy sound came again. Thinking someone was dragging a shoe outside, Lin Mo turned toward the door. But the care worker behind his wheelchair hadn't moved. Neither had Xu. The maintenance man crouched beside the sign's original rear support, shining his lamp over the ordinary cover they'd removed earlier.

The sound came through the corroded gap.

“...won't go through.”

Lin Mo's left elbow left its cushion. Before he knew what he meant to do, his shoulder hurt. The care worker supported his forearm and lowered it again.

“Keep your arm here. Tell me where you need to look.”

“Don't move me.”

Beside a pale scrape on the black paper lay the words Clothes Mending Four. Original Owner Deactivated was partly obscured. Farther in, tightly packed layers separated along a narrow slit, trembling like a cracked leaf.

The maintenance man shifted his light. The slit kept moving.

“Draft from the back door.”

Xu returned the door to its earlier, partly closed position. The paper trembled less. The faint sound at the counter continued.

“The needle was too thick that time.”

Lin Mo felt cold glass against his back. But he was sitting against padding. There was no glass here. He remembered Master Tao's counter, the thick needle, the new schoolbag. An empty chair waiting for a child's reply.

Xu bent down. “You know that voice?”

“I've heard it.”

“Where?”

“When he was still here.”

“You heard a man?” the mechanic asked.

Lin Mo nodded.

“Sounds like two people. One underneath, one drowning him out.”

Xu started recording from where he stood, stating their location. No figure appeared on his screen. A small waveform responded to their voices. Lin Mo didn't ask him to hold it against the slit. Old Chen's voice had been cut up before, given another voice to accompany it. Somebody else's child could be added after his question. This might only be something left over.

“If you hear anything about a bag, tell me,” he said.

“I haven't heard a bag mentioned.”

Neither had Lin Mo. He was already waiting for it, placing the last conversation he remembered ahead of the voice that hadn't spoken yet.

He looked at the mechanic's feet. The removed cover lay faceup, four screws in a bottle cap beside it. There was room to retreat. He took in those things before looking back at the paper.

“So far, only the needle.”

Xu added that to the recording.

The voice weakened. The mechanic indicated the layers without approaching them.

“Something under the cracked surface. We'd have to peel it to see everything.”

“Don't.”

“I'm showing you where. Don't lift your arm to point.”

Lin Mo realized he'd tensed his elbow again. The care worker adjusted the cushion closer to his body.

Two faint scrapes came from the paper. Something gray swelled beneath the black surface, then subsided, almost like a face trying to turn behind thick cloth. He could find neither eyes nor a mouth. Even the feeling that somebody was turning toward him depended on that one small movement.

“Old Chen.”

His voice struck the close walls.

No answer.

The mechanic retreated and set his tools on a vacant table. Pliers clinked against it. At the same moment, the voice sharpened.

“You can at least give me the name?”

Lin Mo closed his eyes. The same sentence. He remembered the break there. Master Tao had moved the slider, the end light had come on, and Old Chen had insisted he'd once heard a promise.

Lin Mo wanted the next words too. He could still see Old Chen pressing his fingers against his knee, waiting for someone to explain why the promise he remembered had been cut away.

“No more knocking,” Xu told the mechanic.

“I was putting things down.”

“I know. Leave the table for now.”

They fell silent. The shutter remained lowered. The sign remained at Service Suspended, held by both the wedge and the additional mechanical fastening. Lin Mo could see one support. The mechanic checked the other and returned with a nod.

The gray patch dimmed.

“Old Chen, I'm Lin Mo. The recording you wanted ended after you asked for the name.”

A short intake of breath came through the slit.

“Who told you to stop?”

“That was the end of the material they brought out. We watched it together.”

“I...watched?”

A curved line appeared in the gray. A forehead, perhaps; perhaps a crease. Lin Mo couldn't identify a whole face. But the question differed from the exchange he remembered. It seemed to answer what he'd just said.

“It's talking to you?” Xu asked.

“I can't tell yet.”

The gray shifted toward the door.

“Who?”

Xu went still. Lin Mo looked at him and waited.

“My surname's Xu. I'm on duty at this hospital.”

“Who told you...to put things away?”

“Nobody's doing that.”

The reply stretched into a rasp. The mechanic started to bring the lamp closer. Lin Mo asked him to leave it. More light wouldn't necessarily bring a person back.

“Where's my bag?”

Lin Mo's throat tightened.

Old Chen had asked at the end. Lin Mo had said Wang Hai hadn't read through the notebook, only to be asked how he knew. Later Wang Hai had placed the old tag and viewing slip against the outside of the notebook. Sun Ning had kept the bag closed and carried it toward the outer door.

He remembered her hand beneath its base.

“Sun Ning had it when I last saw it. She was coming out with us.”

“Sun...who?”

“The woman in the rest room with the injured arm. You knew her.”

A pause.

“Don't look inside.”

“We haven't gone through it here. The bag isn't here.”

“Give it to me.”

Xu glanced at the small cloth bag on Lin Mo's lap, then back at his face. He didn't reach for it.

“I can't get it now,” Lin Mo said.

“Again. You say you can't.”

An apology almost escaped him. Lin Mo wanted to explain further, to make certain Old Chen knew Sun Ning hadn't abandoned his things. He could tell the whole journey again, stretch it out until the man on the other side understood where his bag had gone. Then another sound came through the slit: a thick needle catching in a schoolbag seam.

“Doesn't scratch,” a child said.

Lin Mo's stomach tightened. No child appeared in the gray. The answer was clean and fluent, easier to hear than any of the broken questions.

“Does it scratch your back? Put it on.”

The old male voice joined it, low and steady. A father and child completing an exchange whose ending was already known.

“Not that!”

A hoarse cry forced its way between them.

The mechanic backed into his tool bag. The care worker gripped the wheelchair handles. The gray twisted beneath the paper. The old voice faltered, then tried again.

“Does it scratch—”

“Stop,” Lin Mo said. “Don't join the child's answer to him.”

Xu's hand hovered. There was no playback button. He looked at the mechanic. Both turned toward the exposed corner of paper.

Neither tore it.

“I asked...the name.”

“I heard.”

“You didn't ask.”

“You did. The answer wasn't entered with the original segment. That's all I found.”

He didn't tell Old Chen what he'd once heard, didn't turn fifteen years of insistence into a mistake for him. The needle sounds faded. The child's answer dissolved into a trailing syllable.

“Do you still remember me?” Lin Mo asked.

“Lin...”

A black line divided the gray. The next word failed twice. Lin Mo nearly supplied it, then shut his mouth.

The care worker touched the chair. “Time to go.”

“One more minute.”

“You're sweating. We've already stayed a little longer.”

He had no hand capable of pushing the chair forward. The deepening pain in his shoulder couldn't decide whether this was real. Yet he kept watching, waiting for the voice to finish his name.

Xu asked whether they could recline the chair slightly.

“He needs to lie down upstairs,” the care worker said.

“All right,” Lin Mo managed.

The gray seemed to hear.

“You're leaving?”

“I need treatment.”

“Just me...again...”

The voice broke. Lin Mo leaned away from the backrest, then forced himself against it. He remembered the raised grille. Nothing underneath had been pinning Old Chen down. They had still watched the weight vanish from their hands.

There was nothing here that could support a person either.

“What do you want me to stay and hear?”

Silence. At last, barely above the counter:

“What's my son's name?”

Heat gathered behind Lin Mo's eyes. He didn't have it. Old Chen had remembered only Chen. The space after it had remained empty. This thin sheet couldn't suddenly make the answer his to give.

“At the end, you remembered Chen. You couldn't say the rest.”

“Chen...”

The gray withdrew a fraction.

“I'm Chen.”

Slowly, with an edge of annoyance. Lin Mo didn't know how much remained. At least the voice refused to fill his son's whole name with his own surname.

“I left the space,” Lin Mo said.

The care worker eased the chair backward. Xu stepped aside. The mechanic moved the rubber doorstop clear of the wheels. Their movements were careful, but the gray began to disperse.

“Lin Mo.”

Suddenly, his whole name.

The chair stopped.

“Put your hand...down. Don't wear it out on me.”

Lin Mo looked down. His left arm lay on its support, his right wrist hadn't risen. The concern about exhausting his hand echoed something he'd heard before. But the voice had called him back first.

For a moment he could only listen. The request to spare his hand belonged near another counter, another lamp, a time when Old Chen could still turn away from a screen. This time there was no hand reaching out to stop him.

He waited. The gray didn't return. The slit was black; even the needle had gone quiet. The mechanic checked the fastening from behind. He didn't cover the paper or enclose it in another box. Xu recorded the time the voice stopped.

“When I come again, I'll call your name first,” Lin Mo said.

He didn't know whether the words had been heard, whether anything would retain them until the next waking. He let the chair retreat. At the doorway his sleeve brushed its padding, almost like a sigh behind him.

Ordinary footsteps grew loud in the corridor. Someone approached with a trolley of clean bedding, complaining that a wheel was pulling sideways again. Xu helped straighten it, and it rolled past.

Lin Mo didn't ask anyone to reopen the door.

“This person you knew died?” Xu asked quietly.

“I watched him die.”

“Then in there...”

Lin Mo looked at his palm. He'd brought nothing back in it.

“He's still asking the same thing.”

The lift arrived. The care worker positioned the chair while Xu held the door. The narrowing opening took away the corridor until only an ordinary gray wall remained.

Upstairs, Lin Mo wanted someone to open his notebook.

“Find the lines about Old Chen first. Don't read the hundred-thousand part yet.”
