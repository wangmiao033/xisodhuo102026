# Chapter 101: Wait Inside the Yellow Line

Beyond the glass doors, the escort stood with his back to the hall, speaking into his phone. He pointed toward the ramp, presumably telling the driver where to stop.

Lin Mo's notebook rested on his knees. The pen was clipped in place. His last question was still visible.

Then where is the Xiao Lu I held?

He added nothing. Nurse Zhou's contact slip lay against the page. He felt its folded corner before closing the notebook, ready for the car. Upstairs, he'd wondered whether to move the clean chair when he got home. What he really feared was opening the door to that empty place again.

Her bag could stay where it was.

Morning sunlight made a slanting frame on the floor. He watched its bright edge. Once the wheelchair crossed it, perhaps he could stop thinking about a year ago for a while.

Something sounded at the old counter against the wall.

A quiet sound, like a tissue tearing free.

The counter had once distributed examination documents. After the service moved, three linked seats, a ticket dispenser, and a yellow rectangle on the floor remained. An arrow pointed toward the new location. The shutter stopped halfway down, leaving the countertop exposed. Coming in, Lin Mo had seen a cleaner's mop catch on a loose edge of the yellow tape.

His wheelchair stood just inside the rectangle's outer edge. The escort had chosen the place because it was out of the way and sheltered from drafts.

The dispenser sounded again.

A small ticket emerged slowly from its slot.

An elderly man in a brown cap looked from his own ticket to the counter. The young woman accompanying him was searching for the new location on her phone.

“Dad, it's upstairs now. We got the wrong ticket. Come on.”

He reached for a seat back. Above him, a dark display lit up.

“Number seventeen, please proceed to window three.”

An ordinary voice. Softer, even, than the machine calling names elsewhere in the hall.

The old man raised his ticket.

“See? They're calling us.”

His daughter hesitated. The relocation arrow remained. Behind the glass, however, someone seemed to have sat down. A pale vertical line ran behind one shoulder. She put her phone away and helped her father approach.

Lin Mo looked at the line.

Not a chair back. The shutter's hanging strap. It passed through that shoulder, its lower end still swinging.

“Wait.”

His voice didn't reach them.

The old man offered his ticket beneath the glass. No hand came to take it. The pale shape rose slightly, revealing two hollows darker than the surrounding surface.

Suddenly, the man went onto his toes.

He'd withdrawn his hand, but the ticket clung to his palm, dragging his entire arm toward the countertop. His daughter caught his waist, thinking his legs were failing. Then she screamed.

“His hand! Let go!”

Five wet marks appeared on the counter.

They weren't water. His nails pressed against the edge while the skin of his fingertips crept forward, bunching into thin folds. He braced his other hand against the corner. His mouth opened without sound.

A man in work clothes rushed over and caught his coat. All three lurched half a step backward.

The ticket stayed attached.

The dispenser stopped feeding paper. A line appeared beneath the number.

Remain in the waiting area while numbers are being called.

Lin Mo pressed into the backrest. People still crossed the hall. A parent pushing a stroller glanced over as though hearing an argument. Someone stepped aside for his wheelchair, was about to cross the yellow tape with one toe, then smoothly changed direction, as if gently redirected.

The escort was still outside.

Lin Mo called him. The man turned, phone at his ear, and raised a hand: one moment.

He could hear.

Lin Mo touched the wheel rim, trying to retreat. The brake was on. The wheel barely moved. Reaching down for the lever hurt his shoulder and sent the bag sliding across his knees. He caught its opening before the originals and photograph spilled out.

He could ask for help. He didn't yet know how to let someone enter safely. The daughter was deep inside the rectangle. The workman stood close to its edge. Near Lin Mo, years of cleaning had faded the tape almost white. Neither he nor the escort had noticed it.

That made the glass doors seem farther away. Being visible, being audible, didn't mean everyone received the same version of what was happening.

The workman finally pulled the old man back to the seats. His ticket split. He gasped, fingers bending again, though the skin had turned the bloodless white of something soaked too long.

His daughter crouched to wrap his hand in tissues.

“I'll get someone,” the workman said.

Two strides took him to the line. He put his right foot over it.

His body folded in place.

The shoe landed outside. His knee didn't follow. His trouser leg stretched into a sharp point, caught on something invisible, then tore. He fell backward. His shoe remained beyond the tape. Blood spread rapidly through his sock.

The daughter screamed.

Lin Mo called the escort. Outside, the man checked his screen, then looked up in puzzlement.

“Don't come in,” Lin Mo said. “Look at the yellow line. People are hurt inside it. Get hospital staff. Don't cross.”

“Which yellow line?”

“Where you parked me. Can you see the woman crouching in front?”

A pause.

“Yes. Is her father ill?”

“The counter caught his hand. Another man's foot is bleeding. Please get someone now.”

He didn't mention the theater. Its name couldn't save anyone here.

The escort said something to the driver, ended that call, and came through the doors. Two steps short of the tape, he stopped. He'd listened.

The old speaker sounded again.

“Number seventeen. Do not leave.”

The torn ticket tightened against the old man's palm. Nothing encircled his wrist, yet all five fingers curled inward. Blood ran along the paper's edge. Seventeen darkened.

His daughter tried to tear it off.

“Don't,” Lin Mo said.

She looked at him with reddened eyes.

“It tore before. That's what let him come back.”

“Look at the tear first.”

The split crossed the number's final stroke and stopped midway through the paper. Thin thermal paper, something a thumb should shred, had settled into his palm lines. When she pulled, his skin lifted with it.

She let go.

The workman propped his injured foot on another seat and opened the torn sock. No puncture. A narrow cut crossed the ankle, too deep to see its bottom. He pressed his coat against it.

“Can we drag the whole chair out?”

“Your shoe crossed. Your leg didn't,” Lin Mo said. “Don't try with your body again. Let the people outside look first.”

The man stared at his shoe and stayed still, jaw clenched. He'd moved so quickly to help. Now he needed two breaths for a sentence. Behind the counter, the shape remained seated, neither pursuing nor hurrying, as though every delay would eventually be charged to someone else.

The new ticket still hung from the dispenser, its reverse facing Lin Mo. Gray writing showed through. From here he could read only the last two instructions.

When service is suspended, temporarily leave the marked waiting area.

At the end of this round, the waiting area will be cleared.

He hadn't felt this cold in a long time.

Warm air touched his neck. His injured leg hurt beneath its dressing. All of that was real. The horror hadn't taken him elsewhere or erased the ordinary world first.

It had found an empty place among ordinary things and sat down.

“How many tickets did you take?”

The woman produced another. “I wasn't sure one ticket would cover both of us. So I pressed twice. Eighteen.”

Beside the seats, the workman shook his head, breathing hard.

“None. I came to help.”

Lin Mo studied the counter. The window-three marker hung over the pale figure. Beneath the shutter stood a small reversible sign: Service Open. Part of the word Suspended showed on its reverse. An old instruction occupied a plastic holder beside the window: Staff leaving temporarily must turn the sign to Service Suspended.

Something people used.

He couldn't call it an answer yet. He remembered its position.

The escort returned with an information-desk employee. Leaning from outside the tape, she asked what was wrong with the old man, then noticed the workman's abandoned shoe.

Blood marked the inside of its heel.

The escort crouched beside her and pointed his phone inward. He stopped the recording, then turned the screen toward Lin Mo to show him the clip. On it, the old man sat with his daughter supporting him. Their clothes matched what Lin Mo saw. Only the counter was empty, the shutter strap hanging still.

“Can you see someone sitting behind the glass?” Lin Mo asked.

The escort looked up, then down at the screen again.

“Not on the phone.”

Lin Mo didn't ask him to try another angle. They couldn't spend all their rescue time persuading a screen to agree with him.

The employee picked up the shoe. Her expression changed.

“How did this happen?”

“Don't cross yet. Can you get behind the counter? Turn that sign around?”

She didn't immediately obey. “This window's been closed for three days. Nobody should be in there.”

She went around to its side and knocked twice on the glass.

The pale shape didn't turn.

The knocking came from inside the old man's chest.

Twice.

He doubled forward. His lips turned purple. His daughter caught his shoulders, calling for him.

The employee withdrew her hand as though something had stuck to her fingers. The escort was already calling hospital security, giving their location and describing the injured people. He no longer waited for someone to name what they were seeing.

Lin Mo drew the cloth bag tight against his thighs. His left shoulder caught; he inhaled sharply, keeping the splinted wrist still. He couldn't stand, couldn't drag anyone clear, couldn't even propel his wheelchair quickly. Recognizing danger hadn't restored the body he'd once used to confront it.

He could still watch. Still speak.

The daughter looked up.

“Do you know what's happening?”

The question overlapped with memory. Not his sister's voice. A question he'd asked so often, and heard from so many others.

“I know we can't move at random. Don't throw away the ticket. Support your father. Don't touch the glass again.”

Seventeen went dark on the display.

A short bell sounded behind the counter.

The ticket came loose from the old man's palm.

His daughter reached to catch it. Lin Mo saw that the number remained on her father's hand. Blackness seeped along his palm lines toward the wrist.

The paper had left. The number hadn't.

The display changed to eighteen.

“Number eighteen, please proceed to window three.”

The woman slowly raised her head. Her father still couldn't breathe properly. Suddenly she folded her ticket into a tiny square and lifted it toward her mouth, as though swallowing it would put it somewhere nobody could find.

“Don't.”

She stopped. Tears spilled down her face.

The unclaimed ticket crept another half inch from the slot.

Lin Mo could finally read its number.

Nineteen.

The words beneath the display changed.

One remaining.
