# Chapter 120: Let Go Here

Old Chen didn't understand where he meant to go.

As Lin Mo explained again, the child's old “Doesn't scratch” sounded from the crack. He waited for the recorded man's answer, then gave his own name once more.

It was the afternoon of day thirty-one. After having his wrist reassessed, he'd come to the rear room for one last, time-limited visit.

The brace stayed on. Planning to visit somewhere strange hadn't earned him broader permission. The examination found nothing in yesterday's collision requiring an immediate change of treatment. His shoulder, wrist, and knee still needed care. When he mentioned exploring the unknown passage, the doctor had plainly told him not to.

He didn't edit that objection out.

“I found a basement,” he told Old Chen. “Another part of the transfer record you saw was there.”

Thin gray gathered over a small patch of the old creases.

“Mine?”

“Xiao Lu's receipt. I haven't found the answer you wanted.”

“Then… what did you bring?”

Lin Mo asked the care worker to open the notebook and read back Old Chen's request. If they found the missing answer, preserve the original words. Perhaps no promise had been made. Perhaps the answer was a refusal to look. Don't turn it into good news first.

After a long silence, the paper asked, “Who wrote that?”

“You asked us to.”

“Don't invent it for me.”

“We haven't.”

Watching the gray, Lin Mo realized this might never become a proper goodbye. Old Chen might forget the basement, remember only someone reading half a page before leaving.

“I want to look farther inside. If I find your original answer, I'll try to keep it. If I don't, I can't tell you I have.”

“What… am I called?”

His throat tightened.

“I still don't know your original name.”

“The child, too…”

“We haven't recovered his either.”

The gray contracted. Old mending sounds circled around it. Old Chen offered no shortcut to the core, no promise to wait at the other end. He remained inside a scrap of old paper at the hospital, unable to find his own name or his son's.

Lin Mo stayed until his allotted time ended, then asked for the notebook to be closed.

At the door, Xu said the watch arrangements would continue. They'd contact the hospital about anomalies and preserve what recordings they could. He couldn't promise the same person would be present whenever a voice appeared.

“You've already helped so much.”

Lin Mo didn't ask Xu to come or have the paper peeled from its support. As his chair withdrew, half a breath came from the crack, almost calling him. He waited. No whole words followed.

“I'll go now.”

The next morning, his care worker slipped the photograph into the notebook, face toward the page, its corners clear of the fold. His original ID, old payment receipt, and current medical papers remained in the same cloth bag. Securing it beside the chair, she asked how much he needed today.

He looked at the things on the table and first asked her to put the large insulated flask away.

“Two small water bottles.”

“You can barely hold one steady.”

“I know. The flask would be worse.”

Looking for his sister didn't solve that difficulty. She packed the bottles separately in the chair's side pouch, two portions of portable food above them. Dressings, tissues, and his usual prescribed medicine went into a light care bag. His jacket covered his legs without pressing the right knee. The phone went where he could see it.

Last came the small clip-on lamp he'd asked someone to buy yesterday. She fixed it outside the armrest, pointing at the front wheels and the ground before his left foot. It needed no hand to hold it and didn't press on his supported arm. With fingers he could still move, he touched the low switch twice. On, then off.

She turned the button so he wouldn't need to rotate his braced wrist.

“Switching a light on doesn't mean you can push doors with that hand.”

He nodded. The third time, only his fingertip moved. The beam dispersed beyond the living-room doorway. Around a corner, the people behind him couldn't help with their flashlights. This little lamp could show the ground near his feet, not a safe route.

His supplies meant water and food for a while. He had nobody to rely on for eating, changing dressings, or getting past steps once inside.

The care worker picked up the last bag, then put it down.

“You can't manage alone.”

“I may need help from people in there.”

“Have you seen them?”

“No. One footprint.”

Her expression held none of the brightness of good news. He showed her the basement photograph without presenting the print as a friendly survivor or a promise of assistance. She returned the phone and said scheduled care wasn't a rescue service. If he disappeared, she'd use his address and contact the people who needed to know. She wouldn't shut the door and wait on an uncertain promise to return.

“All right.”

“I'm not asking whether you want rescuing.”

He lowered his head.

“I know.”

Xiao Lu's bag remained on the chair by the wall, its straps tucked away from the wheels. The cup and old things inside hadn't moved. After looking at it, he asked her to keep it as it was. Missing today's return time mustn't turn it into clutter to throw out.

She tucked tissues into the care bag's outer pocket without promising to seal his home forever.

The doorbell rang.

Yuan and his colleague transferred him from the bed's edge to the wheelchair, checked the supports, and asked whether the plan had changed. Lin Mo spoke before they left.

“I want to go through that opening.”

Yuan's hands stopped on the handles.

“We won't take you through.”

“I know. Take me to the original basement. Keep the return journey. The corridor is my decision.”

“How will you get back in that condition?”

He couldn't say he would. His left foot could propel the chair briefly on level ground. At the broken-corner tile, he might not even be able to turn.

“I don't know if I can.”

Yuan released the handles and faced him. If Lin Mo was asking them to push him into danger, they'd cancel. Their service ended at the verified real-world location. Nobody could guarantee anything beyond it. Even there, Yuan would urge him to return and call for help if necessary.

“Paying us doesn't mean we'll do whatever you ask.”

“No. And it doesn't pay you to abandon me.”

Lin Mo asked him to keep the address, both door locations, and the trip's timing. If something happened, seek help for the known site. Don't follow his shouts around an unknown corner. Yuan didn't laugh or produce a paper whose signature would supposedly end all responsibility.

Finally, he agreed to the original scope: transport to the basement, waiting, and return. His colleague would report the circumstances to their service, including the possibility that the return might fail. If Lin Mo continued toward danger, they'd seek help. They wouldn't keep his secret.

Lin Mo nodded.

From the vehicle, he saw somebody in a barber's cape outside the shop downstairs. The barber gently turned the customer's head. Lin Mo glanced at his supported left arm rather than dwelling on his shoulder width at his last haircut.

This was the body he had to use today.

Zhou waited by the stairs. Hearing the plan, he immediately asked whether Lin Mo had lost his mind.

Lin Mo didn't use his sister to silence him.

“You don't have to come down.”

“That's my decision. You couldn't even get through that iron door facing forward.”

“I remember.”

Zhou crouched and tucked a dangling strap back beside a pouch, more roughly than necessary.

“Then stop acting like you didn't notice.”

Lin Mo agreed.

The storage man permitted them only in the original real-world room. He'd reported the inexplicable opening to the relevant maintenance staff. The room was out of use, with no deliveries scheduled. Barriers remained; the old board rested against the wall. Nobody had permission to remove more wall or investigate another sealed storeroom.

They descended using the same professional equipment. On level ground, Lin Mo transferred to his low-seat wheelchair and waited for his right leg to be settled before looking at the opening.

It remained.

Receipt to the left. Narrow iron strip across the floor. The first tile shone white in the flashlight; dust covered the broken-corner tile farther on. Black fabric at the turn hung still. Two days hadn't produced a smooth route or his sister's voice.

Yuan stopped outside the barriers on the real-world side.

“This is as far as we look. We can still go back.”

Lin Mo knew it was true. He could wait, heal, seek someone stronger and more willing to risk it. He might never find them. Nobody had guaranteed how long Xiao Lu could wait. These weeks had only taught him to sit securely again, find a route, and say whose help he needed for each step.

“I want to cross.”

By the iron door, Zhou asked what was in there.

“Somewhere the theater left behind. The layer inside it. That's all I can call it yet.”

“Are there people?”

“Maybe. Maybe only things they left.”

He didn't describe a city with known entrances and exits. Inner World still meant only this corridor that refused to fit the real wall, somewhere beyond the receipt he hadn't traveled.

The storage man told him to stop. Yuan kept hold. Lin Mo stayed put and explained what he knew and didn't know again. He wouldn't promise to return in two minutes, ask them to follow and carry him, or use his sister's name to demand that several people disappear together.

“I have to find her.”

Nobody suddenly agreed.

Yuan moved to the side and called for assistance, giving the known address and Lin Mo's condition. He stayed in the real-world basement, neither crossing the iron edge for him nor leaving. Zhou guarded the original door and watched the chair. Whoever called from inside, he said, make sure you knew who was on this side first.

Lin Mo asked them to move the barrier enough to leave room for retreat. The storage man shifted only the end near the original iron door, keeping a way out for those still in the basement. The warning before the strange corridor stayed.

Lin Mo lowered his left foot, wiping moisture from its sole on dry concrete before planting it. His right leg remained on the footrest. The clip-on lamp lit the short distance between front wheels and iron edge.

Yuan released the brakes without letting go of the handles. Lin Mo propelled the chair little by little, Yuan following, until the front casters stopped on level concrete outside the strip. His body and every wheel remained in the real world.

Dust inside lay close to his toes.

He checked the casters, then the broken-corner tile. He didn't reach for the frame with an injured hand. His right leg stayed supported, his left shoulder against its cushion. An ordinary footstep sounded on the stairs behind him. Yuan's call was still connected.

“Let go here,” Lin Mo said.

Silence behind the handles. Then Yuan released them, remaining close enough to catch him.

Lin Mo held the chair still with his left foot. Concrete was hard beneath his sole. He looked down the corridor into its depths and finally nodded.

“I'm going in.”
