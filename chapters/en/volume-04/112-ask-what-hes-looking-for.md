# Chapter 112: Ask What He's Looking For

The first thing Lin Mo saw on Old Chen's page was a long stroke of ink.

By the time he'd written complete, his injured wrist could barely hold the pen. Xiao Lu had told him the final stroke didn't have to be neat. He'd dragged it across the paper. Now the notebook lay on the bedside table, a clean cup holding down an empty corner.

“Shall I read it?” the care worker asked.

“Let me look first.”

She raised the bed and settled him before taking away the wheelchair. His shoulder still pulsed with pain. The doctor hadn't approved any increase in activity; they would reassess him as his injuries developed. The short trip had taken more out of him than he'd admitted downstairs.

He couldn't go back today.

Body removal. Current original owner. Seatkeeper.

The handwriting was still as he'd left it. He read about his trapped left sleeve, about Old Chen staying in the source-owner position until he could withdraw. Something thickened in his throat.

When the care worker returned, he asked for the preceding page.

“Keep your hands there. Tell me which way.”

Clothes Mending Four. Rack C, Slot Seven. The break after the question about the name. There was no complete answer, no name for Old Chen's son.

Relief came first: he hadn't forgotten the unfinished things. Then the relief itself felt cruel. A name the man had wanted until his death had become a space Lin Mo was glad he hadn't filled incorrectly.

Xu sent a short recording. Lin Mo asked the care worker to play it.

The mechanic spoke. Someone called Old Chen. A rasp came from the small speaker. The final Lin Mo remained clear even when they lowered the volume.

He waited for the recording to finish.

“Old Chen.”

The screen was paused. Of course nobody answered.

“Again?” she asked.

“No. Help me keep that file.”

Something in the rear room had seemed capable of replying. Bringing its recording back upstairs didn't make the phone a new bag containing the person. He asked her to put it away from the water and looked back at the notebook.

Wang Hai had asked him a question in the rest room. When Lin Mo told Old Chen to leave first, had leaving actually been possible? Lin Mo's hand had still been trapped beneath the panel. If Old Chen turned, it would fall. Reading the account, he remembered the question and his own answer: I hadn't given him another way.

He didn't add a check mark beside the death record because he'd heard a voice today.

“Could you record something?”

He stated the date, then hesitated. Behind the old hospital counter, he said, they'd heard speech associated with Old Chen. Some repeated earlier material; some seemed to answer people nearby. He recognized the voice but couldn't see a whole body.

“One more thing. If we speak again, I need to ask whether he wants to hear me describe that last day.”

The care worker stopped the recording. What should she call the file? He gave the time and place. Not Old Chen Is Back. Those words were so easy, they might even stop the pain briefly. But before giving them to Sun Ning or Wang Hai, he needed hands he could return to them. At least he needed to know what could be preserved here.

When she closed the notebook, he asked her not to cover the long ink stroke.

Lunch was noodles. The care worker lifted a portion with chopsticks, but the long strands hung stubbornly over the bowl. Embarrassed, she offered to fetch a spoon.

“Cut them shorter,” Lin Mo said. “Nobody's being graded for elegance.”

She laughed and borrowed kitchen scissors.

He didn't open his mouth far enough for the first bite. Broth touched his lower lip. She waited for his nod before wiping it. For a while, swallowing each mouthful was all he did. By halfway through the bowl, the cold in his stomach had eased.

“Was that voice a relative?”

“Someone I went through things with.”

“Still in there?”

He looked at the notebook without answering yes. She didn't press.

Xu came that afternoon with the room's log. No more speech. Nobody had knocked on anything. After the gray disappeared, the mechanic had asked whether there might be other injured people inside.

“I didn't know what to tell him,” Xu said.

“Old Chen's body was gone. I saw that.”

Lin Mo read the account. At the raised grille he paused, then described holding the man's hand until his fingers met only his own. Xu watched him rather than the paper.

“How many times have you seen something like that?”

“Once has lasted me until now.”

Beyond the curtain someone wanted a test form. A phone rang twice before being answered.

“If it speaks tomorrow, should we call you?” Xu asked.

“You can let me listen. Don't ask the phone where he is. I've come back upstairs. Whatever's there stayed downstairs.”

“The answers would come from the room. Understood.”

Lin Mo asked them not to crowd around questioning it. Sometimes the voice resembled Old Chen; sometimes it repeated an old recording. Even Lin Mo had anticipated words before they'd been spoken.

“If he starts talking, hear what he wants to find first.”

Xu wrote that down. He was changing shifts, he added, and might not be able to accompany another visit.

“Don't rush down tonight because I won't be here tomorrow.”

“I can't even lift this bowl.”

“Then eat what's in it.”

Lin Mo looked at the empty bowl. “Already done.”

The next day, reassessment brought permission for another short, fully assisted visit within the hospital. Lin Mo agreed to return on time. He drank and used the bathroom before leaving instead of postponing everything in his hurry to get downstairs.

Xu's colleague was on duty. Xu came too, in his own jacket, stopping on his way home. He had things to do afterward. Lin Mo didn't ask him to stay all day.

There was no new chair in the room. The ordinary cover remained where it had been set down. The main black sheet stayed on the sign's original support. The separate scrap beneath the shutter, with its flattened box panels, was being watched from the front. Nothing had been brought up to join the two pieces.

A needle scraped first.

Lin Mo had the wheelchair positioned and gave his name.

“You again.”

His heart jumped.

“Do you remember me coming yesterday?”

“Your hand hurts.”

That went back too far to establish anything about yesterday morning. He didn't quiz the voice about his clothes or ask Xu to raise a certain number of fingers. He waited.

Gradually, the voice left the mending lines and asked whether the bag had come.

“Not yet. I haven't found Sun Ning either.”

“You haven't looked.”

“I don't know where she went after the outer door.”

“I know...I don't know either.”

The second part weakened, as though the speaker had stumbled over his own words. For the first time Lin Mo heard the effort of choosing what to say.

Xu returned his pen to his pocket without retracting the tip. Blue ink caught his jacket. As he tried to wipe it away, the slit spoke.

“You stabbed...with the pen.”

Xu froze.

“Your clothes.”

He retracted the point, revealing the new spot. The gray faced only the narrow area beside the frame. The care worker changed position behind the wheelchair without drawing a response.

“Can you see me?” Xu asked.

“Blue.”

His jacket was dark gray. Lin Mo nearly objected. Then Xu moved his fingers off the ink, and the voice asked what he'd written.

“It's a stain. No words.”

A pause.

“Then don't give it to me for a name.”

Lin Mo's eyes burned. The responses didn't all connect, weren't all reliable. But that complaint had come from something happening now. He stopped treating the voice as a witness required to answer every question correctly.

“You want help finding your son's name?”

“Mine.”

“Your name?”

“The one...before.”

The gray contracted with a fine scraping sound. Lin Mo waited for the rest.

“What he called me. Dad...there was a name before.”

Lin Mo didn't decide who he meant. Perhaps Old Chen wanted the name his son had called him, perhaps something from an earlier family memory. The direction of a single sentence was difficult to retain. The paper kept trying to send the same schoolbag exchange through him.

“No schoolbag today,” Old Chen said.

Lin Mo repeated the request to the mechanic.

“I'm not playing anything.” The man spread his hands.

A needle touched the table. The child answered, Doesn't scratch. The gray shrank.

Lin Mo didn't suggest another tool to silence it.

“I know that isn't what you want. You don't have to answer.”

“I shut up...it still...”

He stopped speaking. The old male voice continued along a smoother line in the paper. Afterward, a quiet curse forced its way out of the gray.

Xu turned his head.

Lin Mo almost laughed, his eyes painfully hot. Even refusing to speak took this much effort.

“I'll keep a record out here. That last answer wasn't yours.”

The gray eased open.

“Where's out here?”

“The hospital.”

“I got out?”

Lin Mo's lips turned cold.

“You didn't come out with us.”

He meant to explain everything, from the source-owner position to Sun Ning carrying the bag. The voice interrupted, asking about his hand. Lin Mo said he remembered holding it at the end.

“And then?”

“I couldn't hold on anymore.”

For a long time, nothing.

“Cold.”

There was the support below the slit, the hard counter beside it. No shoulders to cover. No hand he could warm between his own. Lin Mo wanted to lean closer. The chair stayed still, the care worker's hands on its handles.

“Can what's left here feel pain?”

“I said...cold.”

“All right. I heard.”

He stopped layering questions over it. Nobody suggested carrying the paper to his bed. Someone called the mechanic from the corridor. He answered, told them not to come closer, and kept the light in place.

After a while, the gray moved by itself.

“Underneath...a new line pressed over.”

Lin Mo looked below Clothes Mending Four. Pale horizontal marks extended behind the black surface. The original bracket concealed most of them; tiny angles showed through the corroded opening.

“What does it say?”

“Can't read it all.”

“Anything about the name you're looking for?”

“At the front...sent afterward.”

The voice abruptly slipped back: You can at least give me the name? The gray broke apart at its edges, as though the speaker could no longer find where he'd been standing.

Lin Mo didn't press him to keep reading. The mechanic took a photograph from his position, keeping the scale marker clear of the bracket instead of pushing it into the paper. The faint marks were even harder to see on-screen.

The care worker said it was time. This time Lin Mo didn't request another minute. If the voice returned, he asked Xu to explain that nobody had taken the bag and nobody had found the missing name yet.

“Anything else?”

“Tell him I heard him say he was cold.”

Back upstairs, the care worker tucked in his blanket. Lin Mo remembered Old Chen's leaking cup, his habitual complaint about the water cooling before he drank it.

“Warm water?” she asked.

“Please.”

He drank half a cup and lay back slowly. The blanket warmed his skin. The cold voice at the counter still had nowhere to go.
