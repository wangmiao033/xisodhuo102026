# Chapter 102: One Ticket Left

The point of ticket nineteen trembled in the slot.

Lin Mo watched the woman's hand. Eighteen was folded inside it. A thin black line marked the reverse, as though a fingernail had scored the paper from the other side.

“Don't give it to the window. Unfold it. Keep it flat.”

Her hands shook. On the second attempt, it opened. The black line followed the crease. It had already printed itself on her thumb.

The workman reached to help.

“Don't pass it to anyone yet.”

Lin Mo didn't say what would happen. He didn't know. Seventeen had stayed on the old man's palm after the paper fell. That was enough to keep him from pressing eighteen into another hand.

Two security guards arrived. The taller, thinner one moved the onlookers back and set up barriers. His colleague crouched, extending a baton toward the coat pressed against the workman's ankle.

“Dressings first,” said an arriving nurse. “Leave what's holding the pressure.”

She put gloves and dressings on a tray and slid it over the line. Nothing stopped it.

Lin Mo watched, then looked at the abandoned shoe.

Objects crossed. A person hadn't been able to.

The nurse rolled an empty paper cup inward. It struck a chair leg and toppled. She didn't step after it. Instead, she asked the daughter to pick it up and see whether her father could say his name. Twice he opened his mouth. A surname emerged, muffled as speech through a wet towel.

“Don't give him water yet. Turn him slightly. Don't lay him flat. Watch his chest. Is he breathing?”

The woman obeyed. She took off his cap; sweat soaked the lining. A pale band stayed on his forehead. He squeezed her sleeve. She bent close, telling him she was here.

With the nurse directing her, she helped press the workman's wound. Someone brought another wheelchair. Nobody pushed it over the tape. That worn strip had acquired a solidity no wall had needed to prove.

The tall guard went around the counter. A short staff passage led to a locked door. Keys were coming. He photographed its label, telling whoever was on the phone to bring the keys for the old document window, not look under the department's current name.

Old. Lin Mo nearly called him back.

His sister's admission had needed the right old record too. He gripped the bag and forced the hospital bed from his thoughts. A living father sat here. His daughter was wiping his mouth.

“Number eighteen. Do not leave.”

The ticket tilted toward the counter, twisting her wrist. She clamped her arm to her chest instead of offering it as her father had. Her fingertips rapidly whitened.

“Will it drag me over?”

The pale shape shifted forward half an inch.

“Get low. Don't let it pull you down.”

She crouched against the seats, keeping her other hand on her father's shoulder, as though letting go might let him vanish.

Outside, a guard traced the dispenser's cable to its plug and pulled it out.

The rollers stopped. Nineteen advanced no farther.

For an instant, Lin Mo stopped breathing. Then he saw that the red display remained bright.

It hadn't been connected to the dispenser.

The information-desk employee found a connector dangling beneath the display, plastic wrapped in tape, copper wires already removed. She held it up without speaking.

Behind the glass, the shape opened its mouth.

“Number eighteen, please proceed to window three.”

The sound came from the speaker. The darkness inside that mouth widened sideways, passing the edges where a face should have ended. The daughter lowered her head and bit the back of her free hand.

The escort asked her name.

“Zhao.” She gasped. “Zhao Yun.”

“Zhao Yun. Listen over here. The nurse says to stop biting your hand.”

She looked up. A name spoken by a real person seemed to pull her a little distance out of eighteen. She released her teeth, leaving deep marks.

Lin Mo nodded to the escort.

“The car's waiting,” the man told him. “Don't worry about that.”

He moved opposite the back of Lin Mo's chair and pointed out the cleared passage. If Lin Mo could leave, he would receive him here. He didn't promise to cross and carry anyone. He didn't pretend to understand the ticket. His shoes remained half a step from the tape.

Was it the same car? Would there be another charge? How long could it wait? Lin Mo asked none of these. He pushed the notebook deeper into his bag, beyond any dampness on the tray.

The keys arrived.

The tall guard opened the side door. A maintenance man in blue work clothes followed him. Through the glass, Lin Mo saw the rear ceiling light come on: boxes, an upturned chair, a cable duct.

The pale figure didn't brighten.

The maintenance man stood behind it. “Nobody here.” He reached for the shutter.

“Don't raise it!”

His hand stopped beside the strap. The guard repeated the warning and directed him to the sign. Both could hear the people outside. Neither seemed to see what occupied the center of the counter.

“It rotates,” the maintenance man said. “The pivot's jammed. I'll push from behind.”

The sign rocked. Service Open lifted. Part of Service Suspended appeared on the reverse.

The speaker's last syllable choked off.

Zhao Yun's hand dropped half an inch. The workman drew a sudden breath.

“Not so tight.”

The maintenance man heard and pushed harder.

Lin Mo looked down at his rear wheel. Beside the tire near the tape, a narrow ridge of dust had gathered. When the sign moved, a draft scattered it. He wouldn't test the change with his foot. He asked the escort to remember when it had happened.

Objects could cross. People couldn't. Moving the sign had briefly eased the pain. Separate observations. Fear mustn't crush them into one answer that claimed to explain everything.

The sign sprang back.

Blackness on the old man's hand surged past his wrist crease. He convulsed, teeth knocking together. The guard caught the maintenance man's hand.

“Wait.”

Lin Mo's palm was wet. Something had changed. Service Suspended was more than comforting print. When someone exposed it, something tightening around them had loosened.

“Don't force it. Find what's caught.”

The maintenance man felt along the base. When he withdrew his hand, a shallow cut marked his fingertip.

“Metal clip across the front. I can't pull it from behind. Someone clamped it before the move, to keep it from turning.”

His phone light found a small gleam at the frame's right foot, near the opening for documents. Lift that clip from the front, and the pivot should turn.

The guard fetched a long litter picker. Staying outside the tape, he extended its jaws from the side. They passed over the counter edge.

A straight groove appeared in the shaft.

He barely released it in time. The jaws snapped off inside the counter. The handle sprang back, knocking over a barrier post.

Louder than any scream. Heads turned across the hall. People who'd been edging nearer stopped where they were.

The tall guard stared at the break. He didn't retrieve it.

Lin Mo noticed an old scratch in the glass. Beneath it was the place the old man's ticket had passed before his hand was caught. The broken tool had crossed there too. The clip sat slightly nearer to them, close to the outer counter edge, mostly hidden by the frame.

Being able to reach something wasn't the same as being safe to reach for it.

He asked the guard to mark the scratch on the outside of the glass. The pen made no knock. Now those behind the counter could see the boundary too.

“Things crossing there get damaged. Lift the clip without crossing it.”

The guard tried a shorter rod. Beneath the frame's folded lip, the clip could only be nudged sideways. Each nudge slid the entire sign inward. The maintenance man held its base and told him to stop. A little farther, and the clip itself would pass the marked line.

“Pinch it from above. Lift it toward you. I'll hold the pivot down from behind. Don't push it in again.”

The rod withdrew. Someone outside suggested breaking the glass. The guard glanced at the old man's still-heaving chest and shook his head. They weren't unwilling to use force. Every apparent weakness might transmit it to someone they had no intention of hurting.

The maintenance man carefully nudged the broken jaws aside without touching them. Under the ceiling light, sweat ran down his temples.

Zhao Yun cried out.

One stroke of eighteen remained on her ticket. Blackness seeped into the base of her thumb. The paper faded to gray-white, its number being erased.

A bell sounded.

Lin Mo looked at the dispenser.

One remaining.

Nineteen waited more than an arm's length away. He couldn't reach it. Dragging the whole wheelchair with his injured shoulder would take too long.

“Bring me that ticket.”

The workman looked up.

“Don't touch the number. There are gloves on the tray. Use them. Hold a blank corner.”

The man glanced at his bleeding foot, then at Lin Mo.

“What do you want it for?”

“I want it to finish serving the last number. Keep it from clearing the area straightaway.”

He didn't know whether it would skip nineteen if nobody took it. Between seventeen and eighteen, at least, there had been an interval for service. All he could try was to preserve that final interval, giving the people on either side of the counter time to work together.

He couldn't promise what having no ticket meant. Cleared remained on the paper. He hadn't forgotten how the theater treated those left until the end.

“I can take it,” the workman said.

“You're still bleeding. Put it on my knees.”

It wasn't that Lin Mo could bear more. He was here. He'd seen the clip. He had one chance to bring the words about suspending service to the window.

The man pulled on a glove, pinched the blank edge, and brought the paper across on the tray. It settled on Lin Mo's knees, nineteen facing upward.

He pressed his left thumb against it.

The thing behind the counter finally turned toward him.

Its two hollows were at the wrong heights. One almost touched the counter; the other hid behind the shutter. Inside them, his wheelchair's reflection appeared, his torso stretched long, his head diminished to a lump.

He didn't look for his face.

He watched the ticket.

“When it calls me, push me to the counter. Don't take my hand past that mark.”

Zhao Yun let go of eighteen. Her blue fingers could still bend. She looked at her father, then at Lin Mo.

“But you can't move either.”

“That's why I need your help.”

She stood. Following the escort's instructions from beyond the tape, she released the wheelchair brakes. Lin Mo asked her to straighten his injured leg first. His foot mustn't slip off the footplate. Only when it was settled did he turn nineteen into his palm.

Eighteen went dark on the display.

The blank paper slipped from Zhao Yun's hand.

“Number nineteen, please proceed to window three.”

The escort called him again. Lin Mo answered on speaker and left the phone at the mouth of the bag, so he could still hear the man behind him as he approached the counter.

The wheelchair began moving. The voice came from very close.

“I'm watching. Slowly.”

Lin Mo lifted his chin toward the maintenance man.

“Don't turn it yet. Wait until I say.”
