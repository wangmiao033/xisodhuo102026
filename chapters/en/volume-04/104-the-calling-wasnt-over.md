# Chapter 104: The Calling Wasn't Over

Only when the nurse cleaned his finger did Lin Mo realize his teeth were still clenched.

She asked him to relax his jaw. Gauze lifted from the wound. The pain became distinct enough that he could breathe again. The force at the counter hadn't followed him into the treatment room. Every injury it had left was here.

His left shoulder was more swollen. The doctor asked how he'd strained it, where the pain began, then reviewed his earlier papers and arranged examinations. Fresh blood had seeped into the dressing on his right knee. His splinted wrist needed checking too. Not bearing directly on it didn't mean nothing had pulled against it.

Lin Mo didn't say he could endure it.

He tried lowering his left hand to his leg. Halfway, the arm stopped. The escort supported it, helping him lower it gradually. Watching that small movement, Lin Mo understood: alone at home today, he might not even manage the few steps he'd practiced.

“Stay for now,” the doctor said. “We'll decide after the results. You can't transfer yourself. Don't raise that arm again.”

He nodded.

Beyond the curtain, Zhao Yun asked about her father. He needed observation; she couldn't take him home yet. She repeated observation, as though hearing there was a next step might let her put down some of what had happened before it.

Lin Mo closed his eyes.

This morning, Nurse Zhou had told him they hadn't saved his sister. Someone else was hearing continue to observe. He wasn't envious. He suddenly wanted to know who had listened for the next sentence on his sister's behalf.

Who had stood outside a curtain during that year?

He asked the escort for his phone.

Not the records office. He couldn't retain a list of required documents now. Instead, he asked the man to open the recorder and leave it beside his pillow. He stated the time and place, then began at the old counter.

“Seventeen offered his ticket. He was caught. The man without a ticket was injured trying to cross the yellow line. Turning the sign partly loosened the force. When it faced fully toward Service Suspended, we got out.”

He paused.

“I don't know what would have happened if nobody took nineteen. I took it. It called nineteen. It didn't immediately clear the area.”

He wasn't awarding himself answers. Pain, exhaustion, the questions that would come afterward could bury everything until only I escaped again remained.

The escort saved the recording and showed him its duration.

“Anyone else to contact?”

The food shop downstairs. Lin Mo hadn't ordered lunch for today; an uncertain memory wasn't a reason to make someone bring a meal for nothing. On the call, he checked whether an order was awaiting delivery, then explained that he was still at the hospital. If he couldn't get home, he'd contact them separately.

Next, the transport service.

The return car had waited too long. Nobody knew when examinations and observation would finish. He couldn't leave a driver waiting indefinitely. He checked the charges already incurred and how ending the remaining wait would be settled, then confirmed the car could be released. When he could go home, he would make a new booking.

The escort's time had an end too.

He helped with transfers between the immediate examinations. Then hospital staff arrived with their own trolley and took over, helping Lin Mo onto an observation bed. The service reclaimed its wheelchair. Bag and papers went beside the bed. Before leaving, the escort explained directly to the receiving staff where they mustn't pull him.

“Didn't get you home as planned.”

“You got me out.”

The man didn't promise he could always do that. He examined the scrape on his left hand, said he'd need to clean it, and pointed out the number for Lin Mo's next booking. It was already saved. Lin Mo looked carefully anyway.

He wouldn't forget the distance that man had crossed, from beyond the glass doors to inside the yellow line.

Afternoon light had moved across the observation room when the results began arriving. No new fracture had been found from today's strain, the doctor said. But his shoulder pain and restricted movement had worsened; the soft-tissue injury still needed watching. His finger had been treated, his knee redressed. He would stay overnight, require help moving, and be reassessed.

No new fracture didn't mean he could raise his arm again.

He settled it as instructed and placed the water where someone could help him drink. When he needed it, he called a staff member instead of trying to grip the cup with his newly bandaged finger.

Zhao Yun visited once. Thin gauze covered her fingers. Her father recognized people and could speak complete sentences. The black marks on his hand had faded somewhat, but the doctor wanted them to stay. The workman was being treated too. His family had arrived.

“Your family…” She stopped and asked whether he needed anything fetched.

“My things are here.”

She looked at the cloth bag without touching it. He asked her to check the zip. Important papers inside. She closed it fully and settled the strap where it wouldn't slide. Such a small task. Only when she'd finished did he discover he'd been holding his breath.

“I nearly swallowed the ticket.”

He looked at her bitten hand and remembered how quickly she'd lifted the paper, fear moving faster than thought.

“Don't hurt yourself again.”

She nodded. She needed to return to her father. She would see Lin Mo tomorrow. He didn't promise he'd still be there, or use her visit to question her about the first ticket. She'd already told him: the wrong dispenser.

An ordinary mistake should have remained an ordinary mistake.

The tall guard came that afternoon.

He obtained the nurse's permission and stood only briefly beside the bed. His surname was Xu. Holding his register open, he asked Lin Mo to explain the warning about the sign again.

“They went in to clear the equipment. I told them to leave the sign alone.”

“Who went in?”

“Maintenance. They aren't treating what you've described as rubbish. They're worried more people will approach. Everything's disconnected. They want to dismantle it for inspection.”

“It kept calling after you unplugged it.”

“I saw. That's why I'm asking.”

Guard Xu showed him four entries: wedge undisturbed, Service Suspended facing outward, shutter closed, area cordoned off. Below them were the other injured people's destinations, then Lin Mo's. He didn't turn to their entries. He studied the first four.

“When I left, something black was under the shutter. Did you see it?”

Xu took out his phone.

A photograph taken from outside the tape showed a thin black edge beneath the shutter, a ruler alongside it. Clearly not a joint between floor tiles. A closer image showed paper fibers beneath the strip, its edge curled, as though adhesive had come loose.

“They haven't touched it. This?”

“Yes.”

Lin Mo asked him to keep the second image open.

A gray-white trace marked the black scrap. Not a whole line. Only the left half of the character for please. Staring, he thought its vertical stroke looked straighter than the ruler beside it.

He remembered the thing folding through its middle when the sign turned.

“What does the back of the sign look like?”

“They photographed it from behind. Didn't turn it back.”

The third image.

Service Open faced inward as it should. At the bottom of the sign, however, a blackened sheet had been pasted on. One edge had split along the pivot. The gap was about as wide as the scrap beneath the shutter. The beginning of a sentence remained legible.

While numbers are being called.

The words that had appeared on the display.

“Was this added later?”

“I don't know. The maintenance man helped move boxes before the doors opened this morning. Says he didn't touch the sign. They're looking for old photographs of the window.”

Xu didn't claim nobody remembered attaching it. Didn't declare it had appeared from nowhere. He showed what remained at the scene, photograph by photograph.

“Don't tear it off yet. Don't take that small piece away from the counter area either.”

“It's already outside the shutter.”

Cold passed down Lin Mo's back.

They'd secured the sign. Suspended service at the whole window. Something had still escaped beneath the closed shutter. They had stopped the working part. That didn't mean every fragment remained where it belonged.

“Someone used a long rod to lower an upturned clear box over the scrap,” Xu continued. “The rim rested on the tiles to either side, without pressing on the paper. When the edge nearest the yellow line touched down, a red spot appeared inside. I told everyone to back away. We've left it alone.”

The last photograph showed a dim red point beside the scrap, like part of an unlit number coming on.

Lin Mo's leg went numb.

A small gray mark remained where the ticket had pressed. The doctor had seen it and hadn't kept scrubbing as if it were dirt. Through the cloth, Lin Mo thought the place felt warm again.

That sensation proved nothing. His dressing had been changed. He'd sat too long. Many parts of him hurt. He wouldn't turn every discomfort into a new ability that could show him the way.

But the red point was in the photograph.

“Are all four edges of the box against the floor?”

“No. One side's against the shutter. There's a gap.”

“Don't put anything else over it. Keep people back. Don't enclose it on every side, making another waiting area.”

He stopped. That was a deduction, not a rule he already knew.

“I don't know whether the box made it light up. Leave everything as it is. Don't send someone in to test it.”

Xu went outside to make a call. Lin Mo couldn't hear all of it. Suspended. Don't touch. Wait for someone to inspect it. Words found one by one inside danger that morning, now passed to another person guarding the scene.

He touched his notebook's cover. He couldn't open it.

His question about his sister remained inside. He'd brought it down from six, expecting to take it home and begin again with another telephone call. Instead, another piece of paper in the same hospital had injured four people. The other three had come here on ordinary business. None had spoken of the place he'd survived.

If something like this could remain here, the theater's collapse hadn't taken all its danger away.

He wanted to turn over every black scrap. Perhaps Xiao Lu had left words inside one.

Looking at his supported arm, he pressed the impulse down.

Between his not having seen where she went and a black sheet revealing her whereabouts, too much was missing. He couldn't send an unsuspecting person to a counter for his sister's sake.

When Xu returned, he asked who was guarding it.

“Maintenance and our staff. That area's staying closed tonight.”

“Dismantling it tomorrow?”

“Waiting for instructions. Tell us what you saw. Don't go there yourself.”

“All right.”

He requested a number for the security staff on duty, solely to check changes in this incident. Xu read out the duty-phone number, then asked the nurse to put the slip in the bag's outer pocket. Lin Mo gave him his restored number too, asked him to check it, and said he could send updates from this incident.

Before leaving, he glanced at Lin Mo's leg.

“How did you know to turn the sign?”

“The ticket said so. Halfway through the turn, the grip actually loosened.”

“Seen anything like this before?”

Lin Mo neither nodded nor denied it.

“I've met things that make people obey written words. I only saw these words today.”

Xu closed his register. He didn't ask again.

Near dusk, food arrived. Lin Mo asked a care worker to open the lid, steady the tray, and help him eat. He didn't force his left arm upward. The rice was neither too hot nor too cold. He chewed the first mouthful a long time. Swallowing, he realized hunger had made him shake.

Beside his pillow, the phone lit with a photograph from the security duty number.

No explanation.

Inside the clear box, the partial red number had completed itself.

Twenty.
