# The Broken Bench

From a distance, the bench looked perfect.

It stood beneath a tree at the bend in the path, facing a small patch of grass instead of a road. There was space beside it for Biscuit, no steps on the approach, and just enough shade to turn the photograph into the kind of picture Eve could have used without thinking.

Up close, the middle slat had split.

Mabel stopped before Eve reached it.

“There,” she said.

Eve lowered her phone.

A narrow crack ran through the wood beside one of the bolts. Someone had wedged a drink carton beneath a leg, which explained the slight tilt. There was no sign, no tape, nothing to distinguish it from a perfectly usable place to stop.

“Have you reported it?” Eve asked.

“Last week. I have the reference number.”

Of course she did.

Eve took a wide reference photograph and then a closer one of the damage, keeping Mabel and Biscuit out of both. She did not test the bench by sitting on it.

“Do you want me to send another report?”

“Can we add a photograph to the first one? I'd rather not persuade them there are two broken benches.”

Eve wrote the reference number beside the location.

Her map had acquired its first bright cross.

---

They had agreed on one short section of the route, with a possible second only if Mabel and Biscuit were comfortable. Eve had arrived with a ruler, colored pens, and a homemade key that made her look more prepared than she was.

Green meant seating. Blue meant water. A little stair symbol meant a step.

At the next corner, the key began to fail.

The café had chairs outside, but only when the pavement was dry. The owner allowed people to rest briefly without buying anything if there was space, but not during a queue that stretched across the door. There was one shallow step near the entrance and a flat approach to the outside tables from the other direction.

Eve looked at her neat symbols.

“It's becoming less neat,” Mabel observed.

“It's becoming accurate.”

“Less neat, then.”

Biscuit inspected a leaf caught beneath a table. The café owner came outside with a cloth and agreed to answer three questions, provided they remained three.

Eve asked where people could sit, when the chairs were usually out, and whether dogs could stay beside their owners outside. The answer to the last question came with a request to keep the entrance clear and dogs on leads.

She wrote the details. Then she asked permission to include the information in a small public guide and promised to send the wording before it went up.

“That was four,” the owner said.

“The fourth prevented an argument later.”

“Worth it.”

He went back inside.

Mabel gave Biscuit a quiet moment before they moved on. Eve resisted the temptation to call the café a safe stop. It was a place whose conditions she had checked today. The difference was beginning to fill an entire page.

---

At the pharmacy corner, a delivery lorry blocked the lowered part of the curb.

Eve glanced toward the road, looking for another place to cross.

“We can wait,” Mabel said.

“It might take a while.”

“It might.”

Eve stopped searching for the clever route around the inconvenience. They stood in the shade, well back from the traffic, while the driver finished unloading. Biscuit sniffed a patch of pavement with the sustained interest of someone who had been given a newspaper.

“Does he always read that slowly?” Eve asked.

“Depends on the news.”

The driver moved the lorry. They crossed at the proper place and continued toward the flower shop.

There was a wooden seat outside it. Mabel had marked it on her original map, but today it held three crates of plants.

The florist looked genuinely surprised when Eve asked whether it was meant for sitting.

“It was,” she said. “Before spring.”

“Will it be again?”

“Possibly. I couldn't promise when.”

Eve changed the green dot to a note: display bench, not currently available for seating.

It felt disproportionately severe. The florist was pleasant. The flowers were beautiful. None of that made the space usable.

Mabel looked over her shoulder. “Good.”

“I'm beginning to understand why you brought your own map.”

“I brought the other one first.”

She tapped the old folded page. “That's how I got this cross.”

---

Before they turned back, Eve found a narrow path behind the flower shop. It appeared to connect with the next street, avoiding the busy corner entirely.

“Could this work?” she asked.

Mabel stayed at the entrance with Biscuit while Eve checked the sign on the gate. Private access. The route belonged to the flats above the shops.

She returned without opening it.

“Not a public shortcut.”

“Pity,” Mabel said. “It looked promising.”

Eve crossed the line off her rough sketch. She had wanted to discover something useful enough to justify all the pens she had brought. Instead, the work kept consisting of finding out why an attractive answer wasn't an answer.

“Are you disappointed?” Mabel asked.

“A little.”

“You've saved somebody else the walk to a locked gate.”

Eve looked back at the entrance. The sign had been visible all along, if a person stopped hunting for the pleasing route long enough to read it.

She added the gate to the notes and capped the pen.

They finished at the bakery, where the bench was out and the entrance clear.

Eve had checked Wednesday's hours. She showed Mabel the note. Mabel nodded as if the bakery had passed a modest but important examination.

Biscuit drank from the bowl Mabel had brought. Eve photographed only the bench after asking the baker again, then sat with her phone in her lap.

She wanted to title the guide Easy Walks for Senior Dogs.

It sounded useful. It also sounded as if she knew far more than she did.

“Would you mind if I asked Julian about the wording?” she said. “Not about Biscuit's care. About what the map can honestly say.”

“Ask him. Don't put my dog's appointments in it.”

“I wouldn't.”

Mabel looked at her until she added, “And I won't mention them as an explanation for the route.”

“That too.”

Eve made a note. A good intention was becoming less and less like a shortcut.

When they parted at the crossing, Mabel took her original map home. Eve kept photographs of the public sections and the broken-bench reference number. She had not copied the corner containing Mabel's private shopping notes.

It was a small omission, but a deliberate one.

---

Julian had ten minutes after his last appointment, not an afternoon. Eve sent the question first and arrived at the agreed time with one page, rather than her whole laptop.

He read the proposed title while standing at the end of the desk.

“Easy for which dog?” he asked.

“I thought you might say that.”

“You still wrote it.”

“It looked friendly.”

“It can stay friendly without deciding what a dog can manage.”

Eve took the page back and crossed out “easy.”

“I don't want it to become a warning label with a map attached.”

“It doesn't need to. Say what you observed. Seating, steps, opening hours, surfaces. You can't tell someone their animal is fit for the route because another animal walked it.”

“So, Rest Stops Around the Neighborhood?”

“That tells me what it is.”

“It also sounds like something posted by a transport committee.”

“Transport committees sometimes have chairs.”

She laughed. He sat down briefly and pointed to a blue symbol.

“What's that?”

“Water available from the café, with their agreement.”

“Make clear what the arrangement is. Don't let a symbol promise a permanent bowl on the pavement.”

“I'll write that people should bring their own supplies.”

“And make their own plans for their dog. If they have questions about what their dog can manage, they can ask the person responsible for its care.”

She revised the line in ordinary words instead of copying his phrasing into something grander.

Julian watched the pen move.

“This is useful,” he said.

She looked up too quickly. “Even without the sad music?”

“Especially if it has the actual opening hours.”

“Mabel would agree with you.”

“She often has a point.”

Eve was curious about how often they had spoken, but the curiosity was not a reason to ask about a client's history. She turned the page instead.

“Can I put your name on it as a medical reviewer?”

“No. I haven't assessed the routes. I can check this short piece of wording, if that's useful. Don't make my glance at a map into a clinical endorsement.”

“Fair.”

It was fair. She still felt the small embarrassment of having reached for a more impressive credit than the work justified.

He handed her the page. “Send that paragraph when you've changed it.”

“Just the paragraph.”

“Please.”

His phone rang. Eve gathered her things without making him apologize for the end of the ten minutes.

---

At home, Dumpling was lying across the seat of her chair as though he had been appointed to a committee of his own.

Eve moved to the other chair.

The revised map looked less like a set of promises and more like a record of an afternoon. She added a date to every checked location. At the bottom she put a contact address for corrections, then reconsidered and used her work address rather than the personal one attached to Dumpling's search notice.

She wrote a brief introduction. This was not a recommended exercise plan, a medical guide, or a guarantee that a café would always have a spare chair. It was a starting point: places to ask about, obstacles to know about, information checked on a particular day.

Then she removed half the introduction because it sounded as if she expected someone to sue the bakery.

The shorter version was clearer.

She sent Julian only the paragraph he had agreed to see. She sent Mabel the locations and notes concerning their walk. She sent the café owner the exact wording about his chairs.

Last, she added the photograph to the existing bench report through the listed reporting channel and saved the acknowledgment. No promise of a repair date appeared. She marked the bench unavailable, not “being fixed soon.”

Dumpling stretched, and his paw landed on the edge of the map.

“You've contributed a chair shortage,” Eve told him. “That's all the fieldwork you're doing today.”

Her phone lit up with Mabel's reply.

You have the café's name wrong. It's on the window in your photograph.

Eve opened the picture. Mabel was right.

She corrected it, grateful that the mistake had been found while the map still belonged to a handful of people instead of everyone who might share it.

Then she wrote herself one last note for the next walk.

Look at the place before you improve the story.
