# Chapter 246 — A Room for the Future

About seven months after she and Julian had made their decision over carrots and cold coffee, Eve went upstairs to fetch her good cutting mat and found that the spare room door would open only the width of a hand.

“Don't push it,” Julian called from inside.

She rested her forehead on the edge of the door. “I had narrowed the options down to pushing it and standing here.”

“Stand here for another second.”

Something scuffed the floor. Then the door opened and Julian came out sideways, carrying an empty shelving unit. Its bottom corner brushed his trouser leg. A square of paper clung to the sole of his slipper.

“Where's the mat?” she asked.

“Behind the chair on the left. Unless you mean the one with the burn.”

“That's the one.”

“Good. The burn faces me in my dreams now.”

Eve stepped aside to let him pass. Since they had learned they were expecting twins, they had both caught themselves turning one thing into two in their heads: a chair, a laundry basket, an extra person making breakfast. It was a habit with no sense of proportion. The room only had one door, and for the time being the door mattered more than any number of matching things that might eventually go through it.

She followed Julian partway down the landing. “Where are you putting that?”

“I thought I'd take it to the bottom of the stairs. Then we can decide where all that work stuff should go.”

“I thought we already decided it would go in there.”

He stopped. She could see him looking past the end of the shelving unit, which put one of its wooden uprights in front of his eye.

“I thought that meant for now,” he said.

“It means it's still mine. It has to go somewhere I can actually use it.”

He set the unit down carefully alongside the wall. The square of paper came off his slipper. It was a red dot Eve had put on the shelf to mean keep.

Julian picked it up and held it out to her.

“I was trying to make it possible for you to get to the chair without going around a stack of things.”

“You were doing that. I don't think you meant to move my work out of the house.”

“I hadn't got as far as where I'd put it,” he admitted.

She looked down the staircase at the patch of floor he had planned to take it to. A stack of clean tea towels already occupied the chair there. They had been working their way out of decisions by moving them downstairs.

Eve wanted the spare room ready too. The want could come over her very suddenly. Some mornings she passed the door and pictured a lamp turned down low in the dark, a basket where she could reach it without stepping across the room. At other times she noticed the height of the windowsill and remembered how pleased she had been when the good evening light landed on a drawing spread out there. Neither picture seemed less true for turning up on the same landing.

“If we take the spare chair out,” she said, “can the blue cabinet go under the window?”

He looked through the doorway. The cabinet was shorter than the shelving unit and deep enough for the things she used most. Getting it under the sill meant moving the chair back to their room. The chair had been hers, with a narrow shelf on the side for a book. It was also one of the few places upstairs where Julian could sit and put on his shoes without catching his elbows against anything. He asked if she minded having it near the bedroom door.

“Only if you empty it of jumpers first.”

He went to fetch the tape measure. She went to get the mat. Instead of bending behind the chair until she could reach it, she called him back. When he handed it over, she remembered that the burn had been there since her first flat, from a saucepan she had once put down without looking. It had not got in the way of a straight cut in years.

They measured the corner and marked the cabinet's proposed width with two scraps of paper. The drawer on the right would open. The left one would, too, if they turned the cabinet slightly to get the skirting board clear. Julian took out the heavy drawers before moving it. Eve sat on the landing chair after he made it available and sorted what was inside one drawer at a time. There were three kinds of tracing paper. All three of them had made her money at some point. Only one was still particularly good.

She kept two.

Dumpling appeared when all the furniture had stopped making a noise. He examined the square the cabinet had left on the floor, put one paw into the space and withdrew it. Then he went to find the grey blanket in their room. Eve followed far enough to see him settle onto it. The chair could go by the door without taking the corner he liked. Julian carried the empty shelving unit downstairs and set it flat in the unused cupboard, past the stack of tea towels. They left the middle of the spare room clear. There was space now for the two things they expected to need most, even if they hadn’t bought them yet. They had already agreed that when the children could follow, they would have an upstairs boundary: no one sent after the cat, and a closed bedroom door when he needed it. For today it took no more than leaving his blanket where it was and making sure boxes didn't block his way to it.

At half past eleven Eve went downstairs for a video call with Robin, who was opening the venue for a workshop. The entrance table was in the wrong place. Eve knew before anybody said so because the door handle appeared three inches from the corner of the picture. She was preparing to explain the effect the first queue would have when Robin turned the phone around. A second table was already being moved to the wall. One of the team had checked the route with the workshop lead and decided to use the end door.

“I've still got the sign on the outside,” Robin said. “I can make the arrow point the other way.”

Eve closed her mouth on the first half of an instruction she hadn't been asked for.

A client had queried a line on a brief she had written. Robin wanted to check the meaning before confirming a version, not to hand her the workshop. Eve found the right page, explained how far the wording change could go and kept the more substantive revision for a paid discussion the following week. Robin made a note. In the background a cup fell over without breaking. Nobody came to the screen to ask what to do about it.

“Are you in on Thursday?” Robin asked.

“For the client slot, yes. And I want to see what people did with the ink last time.”

“I'll put aside the comparison sheets. You don't have to carry them back with you.”

Eve said thank you. As soon as the call ended, she realized she'd been looking at the stack of blank paper beside the laptop as though she were about to find a reason to reprint something. The people at the venue had paper. She had half an hour until lunch was likely to be ready. She took that half hour and lay down on the sofa.

When she woke, Julian had made two sandwiches with different fillings. He had left them covered on the counter to let her choose. One contained cucumber to a depth that suggested he had got halfway through cutting it and decided stopping would be harder. She took that one.

After lunch she brought the household rota out from under the sugar bowl. The later weeks were still in pencil. Their agreement about his clinic hours had come through in an actual conversation with the clinic, rather than through Julian saying that he could usually leave on time. The team had arranged who would answer routine questions when he wasn't due there. Some of his sessions couldn't move; the agreed changes were to the ones that could. Eve had picked a last week for onsite work before leave, with room to shorten it after talking to her own clients. Neither of them had tried to write a date for everything becoming ordinary again.

“I spoke to the care place,” he said. “They want us to call nearer the time. I wrote that on the back; there's nothing to pay or agree today.”

“Thank you. Did you also mean to be up this early every morning?”

She showed him the pencilled squares. He'd marked his first shift and then used an arrow all the way down the page. Three mornings that needed to be his, and several they had never discussed.

“I thought I could get food done before the clinic,” he said.

Eve ran her finger down the squares. The end of the arrow went through a margin note she had made about the venue. “You've made a long arrow for a man who came back yesterday and tried to open the fridge with his keys.”

“I didn't think it was locked.”

“You looked offended that it was.”

He drew his chair in. She waited while he looked at the whole page. They were allowed to leave guesses in pencil, he said after a moment. It didn't make them a commitment to one way of dividing every morning. She wanted a daytime hour no one used to discuss what she ought to be doing, particularly on days she'd stopped work earlier than she had planned. He wanted one evening a week when he could come home without assuming he should fix something on the way. They chose two rest periods they could actually protect in the coming week. His early-start arrow got rubbed out, while the morning jobs they'd agreed on stayed. He would make the supper he'd already planned; she wouldn't keep awake in the sitting room to review his menu.

“Pasta,” he said.

“How controversial.”

“With the leftover cucumber?”

She shook her head, and he put the paper back under the sugar bowl where both of them could get it.

Near evening, Eve went back upstairs to see how the room took the light. The low cabinet sat under the window. She tried the drawers again. The one with the tracing paper came all the way out without touching the skirting. She put the cutting mat on top for the moment; if she took it out for a job, there would still be room to set something else there.

She had wanted the door to open all the way. It did now, without catching the chair or the cabinet. Upstairs still smelled faintly of the dust they had found behind the furniture. No arrangement they'd made that day had put another person in the room yet. Julian came up with a clean cloth, saw her standing there, and left it on the cabinet.

“Do you want to stay here a minute?” he asked.

She said she did. He stood beside her. They didn't speak until Dumpling came out of the other room, stepped around the piece of marker paper they had forgotten to pick up, and sniffed the clean cloth.

“You can have the other one,” Eve told him.

There was no other one. Julian glanced at her, smiling already. Eve folded the cloth and set it where they would be able to find it. She let Julian draw the door partway closed without shutting it. Dumpling could come out when he’d finished looking.
