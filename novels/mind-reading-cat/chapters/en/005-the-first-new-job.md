# Chapter 005 — The First New Job

By the follow-up appointment, Dumpling had established three household policies.

The drawer was his. The expensive-looking toy Sophie had brought was optional. The crumpled receipt beside it was of considerable interest, provided Eve stopped trying to photograph him enjoying it.

Eve had established one policy of her own: receipts were not toys, particularly receipts she might need to return something.

Their negotiations remained ongoing.

She arrived at the clinic with the loan carrier, the care record, and a folder containing the responses to the found notice. No one had yet supplied details that matched well enough to establish ownership. That wasn't proof of anything beyond the fact that they hadn't found the person, if there was one.

Tessa greeted her from behind a stack of clipboards.

“Morning. How has he been?”

Eve gave the useful answer first. Eating, resting, movement, anything she'd noticed, questions she'd written down. Then she added, “He disapproves of the sofa.”

“That one probably isn't medical.”

“Good. It's outside my current treatment budget.”

Tessa laughed and checked them in.

The waiting area contained a small dog sitting firmly on a man's shoe and a woman trying to keep a carrier level while removing her coat. Eve moved her tote off the empty chair so the woman could put her things down.

Dumpling watched through the front of his carrier. The dog squeaked once. He turned away. Eve shifted the carrier to give him a quieter view, remembering that arriving with an animal did not entitle everyone else to meet it.

On the wall, an adoption-event poster announced the weekend in four fonts.

Eve tried not to look at it.

The poster won.

At the top, enormous letters promised FIND YOUR PERFECT FRIEND. Beneath a collection of smiling animal photographs, a much smaller paragraph explained that attendance did not guarantee adoption and that follow-up assessments would be required. The address occupied the same line as the contact number. The date was orange against pale yellow.

Someone had worked hard on it. That was the problem. She could see the effort in every competing thing it wanted visitors to notice.

She looked down at her own folder instead.

Inside was the found notice she'd remade after Tessa's advice: a clear image, the approximate location, a contact route, no claim that the cat had been abandoned. She had also drawn a simple tracking sheet so she could distinguish a genuine response from a person merely saying they had seen an orange cat once.

Tessa called her name.

Julian checked Dumpling's progress and explained the continuing care in terms Eve could follow. The injury was improving. He didn't declare the cat ready for a sporting career, but nothing about the ordinary follow-up required the disaster Eve had worried herself into preparing for.

She asked her questions. He answered them. When she tried to add a question about the cat's grave dislike of a cushion, he said, “You may have bought an ugly cushion.”

“It was discounted.”

“That doesn't establish innocence.”

Eve looked at him.

He was checking the record.

She decided he knew exactly what he was doing.

Before they finished, she showed him the found notice and response sheet. “Is there anything here I shouldn't share?”

He read rather than merely glancing. “The identifying details you've kept separate should stay separate. Tessa can check the contact arrangements with you.”

He looked at the second page again.

“You made this?”

“Yes.”

“It's clear.”

Eve's first impulse was to say it only took ten minutes. Her second was to explain that she'd spent far longer than that fixing tiny things nobody else would notice.

She managed, “Thank you.”

Julian turned the sheet toward the light. “People know what you're asking them to do.”

That was a more precise compliment than You're so creative. It landed in a place she hadn't realized was still sore.

She tucked the page away before she could look too pleased.

In reception, Tessa was trying to explain the weekend event to someone on the phone.

“No, not first come, first served. You can meet them, but the foster team will discuss whether— Yes. I understand. The headline is rather enthusiastic.”

She ended the call and glanced at the poster.

Eve made the mistake of glancing at it, too.

“You have a face,” Tessa said.

“Most days.”

“A particular face.”

Eve hesitated. “The information's there. It just has to fight the headline to get out.”

Tessa folded her arms. Not hostile. Waiting.

“The big line promises an outcome,” Eve said. “The small print explains a process. People remember the promise.”

“That's the sixth call.”

“I don't think another exclamation mark will help.”

“We tried three.”

Julian, passing behind the desk, stopped.

Eve immediately wished she'd kept her professional opinions inside the folder. Critiquing somebody's volunteer poster in a clinic wasn't the same as being asked for feedback.

“Sorry,” she said. “I wasn't trying to—”

“Could you show us what you mean?” Tessa asked.

Eve took a sheet of scrap paper and sketched two boxes. The event's purpose first. Then the date, place, and steps a visitor could expect. The animal photographs could remain; they didn't have to carry the entire explanation.

She changed the headline to something less grand, then crossed it out because it sounded like a municipal parking announcement.

“That's not the final wording,” she said.

“Reassuring,” Julian said.

She looked up. “You're welcome to contribute something worse.”

“I usually leave written complaints to the front desk.”

“Not voluntarily,” Tessa said.

For a moment all three of them were smiling at the same piece of paper.

Then Eve realized she'd started doing unpaid work again.

Her pen stopped.

There was no villain here. Nobody had tricked her. She'd seen a problem and moved toward it before asking whether she had the time, whether they wanted a full solution, or whether the problem had a budget.

She put the pen down.

“That explains the issue,” she said. “A finished design would need the confirmed event details.”

Julian nodded. “Can you quote for it?”

Eve blinked.

“For what?”

“The design. We have a small budget for event materials. Dr. Clark needs to approve the amount, and the foster volunteers need to check the adoption information.”

Tessa took out a clipboard. “We need one main information sheet, the animal cards, and clear instructions for visitors. We don't need a new logo. Please don't let anyone request a new logo.”

Eve looked between them.

They knew about the cat. They knew she'd asked about costs. Her wet shirt and exhausted jokes had probably communicated the rest.

“You don't have to find me something to do,” she said.

Julian's expression changed, but not into the gentle pity she'd prepared to endure.

“We need these materials.”

“I mean because I—”

“If you don't want the job, you can say so.”

That stung before she understood why. She had expected him to reassure her about being useful. Instead, he was treating usefulness as something a person could sell and still refuse.

“I do want to discuss it,” she said carefully. “I just don't want you paying for something because you feel bad.”

“Then don't quote for feeling bad.”

Tessa lowered her eyes to the clipboard. “Please quote for the printing sizes, though. Those are a recurring tragedy.”

Eve laughed, partly because she needed to breathe.

They moved out of the way of the reception queue. Dumpling remained in his carrier beside Eve in the quieter spot, supervised and unimpressed. She wasn't going to turn the animal's follow-up into an entire day at work.

Ten minutes, she decided. Enough to gather the brief, not solve everything.

She asked who had final approval, how many animal profiles were ready, whether the volunteers could provide accurate descriptions, and what print sizes the clinic could actually use. Tessa answered some questions immediately and marked others to confirm.

Julian was called away before they finished.

“Send the quote,” he said. “Include revisions and payment timing.”

“You've commissioned creative work before.”

“I've watched people disagree about what 'a few changes' means.”

Then he went back to his patients. He didn't stay to make sure Eve felt encouraged. Strangely, that helped.

At home, she put Dumpling's carrier in its familiar corner, let him settle, and made lunch before opening the brief. This time the order was deliberate.

She calculated the time. Added the confirmed deliverables. Limited the included revision round. Wrote that additional work would need agreement. The number at the bottom was modest but not an apology. She had allowed for checking the proofs after printing, too. A design that looked wonderful on her laptop could still be unreadable in a visitor’s hand, and she intended to find out before the visitors did.

She opened the sample volunteer profiles attached to the brief. One file described a dog as perfect for everyone; another listed only age and coat color. Eve opened a fresh page for questions rather than polishing claims she couldn't substantiate. Who had observed the behavior? In what setting? What did a visitor need to ask the foster carer instead of assuming from a photograph?

She marked those items for confirmation. Clear design couldn't turn uncertain information into a fact, however attractive the typeface.

Her old supervisor would have called this slowing down the emotional hook. Eve could hear the phrase so clearly that she nearly deleted the questions. Then she remembered Tessa repeating the same explanation on the phone while an actual person waited at the desk.

The work wasn't to make the biggest promise fit in the smallest space. It was to help someone arrive knowing what kind of decision they were about to make.

She put the questions beneath the brief, saved both files, and returned to the number at the bottom of her quote. This included the time it would take to get the information right, not only the time her hands would spend arranging it.

She almost reduced it.

Instead she checked the calculation.

When she sent the quote, her hand trembled more than it had sending the handover email. Refusing free work was one thing. Asking someone to choose her at a price was another.

The reply came near the end of the afternoon. Tessa confirmed Dr. Clark's approval, the responsible contacts, and the payment arrangement. The volunteers would send the completed profiles that evening. One correction to the print size appeared in bold.

Eve read it twice.

Then she made a new folder.

Not NEW LIFE. Not PROVE THEM WRONG.

*Weekend adoption event.*

Dumpling climbed into his drawer and began washing his face with his uninjured paw, showing no interest in her professional milestone.

“We have a job,” Eve told him.

She stopped.

“No. I have a job. You have a drawer.”

The cat settled more comfortably into it.

For once, the division of labor seemed clear.
