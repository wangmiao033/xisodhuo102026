# Chapter 129 — Which Part Can Someone Else Help With?

Anita's offer occupied three words in Mia's message: She can help.

By the time Eve reached the flat, those words had expanded into a chair at the kitchen table, a plate of biscuits, and the first genuine smile she had seen on Mia all week.

Anita lived one floor below. She had met Toby with Mia several times in the building and outside, and had offered to discuss helping after Mia admitted how difficult the first weeks had been. She wore a coat with a missing middle button and carried her own diary.

The diary reassured Eve more than the smile.

“Tuesday and Thursday,” Anita said, opening it. “Around lunchtime. For the next month, then we see. Not next Thursday because of the dentist.”

Mia's pen hovered.

“Right.”

“I work at home those days. That doesn't mean I'm available all day.”

“Of course.”

Eve recognized the small movement in Mia's face as an imagined broad rescue narrowed to two specific windows. It was not ingratitude. Eve had made the same face at room-hire conditions.

Nora had suggested that any new helper meet the relevant support professional and understand the agreed care before taking over. Anita was willing. She was not volunteering to become an improvised trainer or to manage a situation beyond what she could safely do.

“I can be part of the plan,” she said. “I can't be the whole plan with a cardigan.”

Leah laughed from the sink.

Mia put the pen down. “I think I heard more than you offered.”

“It happens. My sister once heard house sitting when I said I could water a fern.”

Toby lay near the kitchen doorway, with room to retreat. He had met Anita in the flat once already, and today she did not try to win him with a flood of attention. Eve watched him lift his head when her bag rustled, then lower it again.

Mia had asked Eve to help make the household schedule readable after the support appointment. The specialist had assessed Toby and discussed an individual plan with Mia and the household. Eve was not translating that into advice of her own. The professional instructions stayed intact; her job was to make clear who had agreed to do what and where the relevant contacts could be found.

She spread three blank sheets across the table.

“Why three?” Mia asked.

“Because one very impressive sheet becomes illegible.”

“That's your professional answer?”

“It's my expensive one.”

One sheet held the week's agreed care. Another listed the confirmed backup arrangements. The third was for questions they still needed answered. Eve wrote nothing in a gap just because an empty square looked discouraging.

Mia's current working pattern included two longer days and three shorter ones. The original walker could cover some of the revised needs after reviewing them, but the additional time cost more than Mia had allowed. Anita's two visits would help within the agreed plan, yet they could not replace every hour of paid support or solve the difficulties around absence.

“Could I move your Thursday to Friday?” Mia asked Anita.

“No. Friday I'm out.”

Mia nodded and crossed out her arrow.

Eve liked that moment more than she expected. A refusal had arrived, been understood, and left both people in their chairs.

Rachel came in wearing her work lanyard. She looked at the schedule and pointed to Wednesday morning.

“I'll be asleep. Put that in, please.”

Eve handed her the pen.

Rachel wrote SLEEP in letters large enough to be seen from the door.

“Artistically strong,” Leah said.

“It's a recurring commission.”

They laughed. Mia laughed too, though she was studying the uncovered hours.

The financial sheet was not laid across the table for everyone. Mia had already worked through the figures privately and showed Eve only the options she wanted help comparing. A few subscriptions could go. So could a planned weekend away. Those savings were real but did not cover an ongoing expense forever.

“I could stop putting money aside,” Mia said.

“All of it?” Eve asked.

“Not the emergency amount. The other part.”

“What happens next month?”

Mia sighed. “You're doing the future voice.”

“I'm reading the month under this one.”

They left that question unresolved until Mia had spoken to her manager. She had requested a meeting about a more predictable pattern, knowing that fewer late shifts could mean less pay. Eve did not suggest a better attitude at work would make an employer provide unlimited flexibility.

Anita checked her watch. “I need to go in ten minutes.”

Mia looked startled. “We haven't finished.”

“We've finished my part.”

There it was again, the edge of a useful offer.

They confirmed the two windows, the introduction with the support professional, and how Anita would tell Mia if she became unavailable. Any access arrangement would be agreed by all the residents. No key changed hands that afternoon just because the conversation had gone well.

Before leaving, Anita asked where the notes would live. Mia pointed toward the kitchen cupboard, then stopped.

“That's where the cleaning things go.”

“And where I'll look for cleaning things,” Rachel said. “Not instructions.”

They tested a folder on the shelf by the hall. Leah could reach it without moving the bags; Anita could find it without opening anybody's private cupboard. Mia put the professional guidance inside, with its date visible, and removed an older sheet so two versions wouldn't disagree silently.

Eve watched Anita try the arrangement once, from the doorway. She found the right page but missed the contact tucked behind it.

“Front pocket,” Mia said, moving it. “That's better.”

The small rehearsal took four minutes. It revealed more than another promise that everyone would be careful. Eve added the version date to her draft and crossed one item off the questions sheet.

At the door, Mia thanked her without adding another request. Eve followed a few minutes later with two draft sheets and a photograph of the third taken only after Mia covered private details. She would make one clean version, then Mia would check it with the people involved.

On the way home, Eve stopped at a print shop. Her own community-session materials were ready, but she had forgotten the small sign explaining where participants should wait. The person behind the counter offered a heavy display board at a discount.

“It would look professional.”

“It would live in my bathroom,” Eve said.

She bought one plain print.

That evening, she worked at her desk while Dumpling occupied the cushion behind her. The household schedule looked neat on screen. She almost removed the question marks before remembering they represented unanswered questions rather than formatting defects.

Julian arrived at the time they had agreed, carrying bread for supper. He waited while she finished the last line.

“Is that work or a favor?” he asked.

“A favor. One hour, because she asked for something I can actually do.”

“How long have you spent?”

“Fifty-three minutes. You may put away your whistle.”

“I haven't bought one yet.”

She sent the draft and closed the laptop. Dumpling stepped onto the warm space it had occupied, forcing Julian to put the bread elsewhere.

“Your seating consultant is active,” Julian said.

“He's expanded into food logistics.”

They ate at the table. Eve wanted to talk about the afternoon, but she kept Mia's numbers and care details private. Instead she described how much she liked a blank space that had not been filled with somebody else's imaginary availability.

Julian listened, then said, “I'm unavailable next Saturday afternoon. I meant to tell you before we made plans.”

“Oh.”

The disappointment surprised her. They had not arranged anything, but she had pictured a walk.

He noticed. “Sunday?”

“Yes. I'd like Sunday.”

Dumpling rubbed against Julian's leg and then Eve's, making a small circuit beneath the table. Eve scratched his cheek when he stopped beside her. The arrangement did not require a cat to keep both people in the same place all weekend.

Mia's message came the next afternoon. Her manager had agreed to a revised schedule for six weeks, then a review. She would lose some additional pay attached to late hours. She had calculated the difference, kept a smaller paid-care arrangement, and postponed the trip.

“It isn't a bargain,” she said when Eve called.

“No.”

“But it's an actual number. I kept being frightened of a number I hadn't added up.”

There were still limits. The employer had not promised permanent accommodation. Anita would not cover evenings. The paid backup needed notice whenever possible. The group had confirmed its own support and the next review, but it could not supply a spare household.

Mia had chosen to continue with Toby under the revised arrangement. Nora remained part of the process, and the option of discussing a responsible return had not been withdrawn as punishment for trying.

“Do you feel sure?” Eve asked, then heard the unfairness in it.

“About every day? No.”

Eve let the answer stand.

A week later, the first version of the new arrangement encountered a very ordinary problem. Anita's meeting ran over. She called Mia as soon as she knew, following the contact plan, rather than assuming another resident would notice. Mia was already on her shorter shift and could change her return time with her manager's agreement. The paid backup was not needed that day, but the contact had been available.

Eve heard about it when Mia stopped by the neighborhood center after Eve's session. Four chairs had been used again. Eve was wiping the table before the next group arrived.

“I cried in the staff toilet,” Mia said, accepting a folded cloth. “Then I made the call.”

“What did Anita say?”

“She apologized. I said it was okay.”

“Was it?”

“It was inconvenient. I didn't want her to stop telling me.”

Eve looked at her. Mia was rubbing a stubborn pen mark from the tabletop, her expression concentrated and tired.

“The system worked,” Eve said.

“The system swore a bit.”

“Most do.”

They finished before the committee came in. Eve counted the pens and found the same missing one was still missing. Mia carried the small box to reception while Eve returned the visitor badge.

Outside, Mia showed her a new photograph. Toby was asleep beside an overturned slipper. There was no caption.

“He had a better night,” she said. “I'm trying not to turn one night into a forecast.”

“That sounds sensible.”

“It's annoying. I'd like a forecast.”

Eve smiled. “So would everyone who hires this room.”

The wind lifted the edge of her sign. Mia caught it before it folded around the box.

“I wanted someone to tell me I could do this alone,” Mia said. “I think that was part of why I didn't ask sooner.”

Eve considered the schedule, the enormous word SLEEP, Anita leaving when her part was finished. The support had not made Mia's responsibility smaller in the way she had feared. It had made its shape visible.

“Do you want company home?” Eve asked.

“Just to the crossing. I need to get back, and you've got that box.”

They walked together that far. Mia turned toward her flat, where the evening still contained feeding, work messages, the agreed routine, and whatever Toby made of the weather. Eve turned toward her own, carrying the entire community activity against her hip.

Neither of them offered to carry the other person's whole life. They had, between them, managed a table and a short stretch of pavement.
