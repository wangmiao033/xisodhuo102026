# Chapter 180 — Dinner Beyond the Station

At lunch, Julian put his suitcase beneath the table and accidentally extended its handle into Eve's knee.

It was not the farewell gesture either of them had planned.

“An ambitious opening,” she said, moving her chair back.

“I'm sorry.”

He pressed the handle down. This time it obeyed.

She rubbed her knee, more surprised than hurt, and passed him the menu. The case had worked all the way from his flat, one wheel providing the promised commentary. He had checked the fixed handles too. Everything was packed. He had his travel details, accommodation confirmation, and the little card with the carrot tucked into his notebook.

Eve knew about the card because he had told her. She hadn't inspected the luggage to make sure her affection occupied adequate space.

They ordered sandwiches and soup. She chose something easy to finish, then discovered that even an ordinary sandwich seemed complicated when she kept glancing at the time.

“We have enough time,” Julian said.

“I know.”

She put her phone face down.

“You can check,” he said. “I'm checking too.”

That was better than pretending they had become timeless people eating timeless lunch. His train left in the afternoon. There would be a moment when he needed to stand up, however much crust remained on her plate.

They talked about his room. It would be ready when he arrived. He knew where to collect the key and had saved the directions that avoided the station steps. Orientation was Monday morning. After that he would learn the actual shape of the first week, including whether Saturday afternoon was as free as the provisional plan suggested.

“You don't have to solve the visit tonight,” Eve said.

“I'll tell you what I know after orientation.”

“Good.”

She asked whether he had bought food for the journey.

“A sandwich.”

“We're currently eating sandwiches.”

“I've chosen a theme.”

“Strong artistic direction.”

He offered her half of his pickle. She accepted, and for a few minutes they managed to talk about nothing that would be different tomorrow.

Outside, the pavement was busy. Julian pulled the case close behind him so its wheels wouldn't catch someone's heel. Eve carried her own bag and his folded coat while he adjusted the handle. At a crossing he reached for the coat, then took her hand instead until the lights changed.

The station was less romantic than the word departure had sounded in her head. A child was furious about a dropped snack. Somebody stood in front of the ticket machines holding a bank card as though it had betrayed them. An announcement echoed against the roof and dissolved before Eve caught the last half.

They found his service on the board. On time.

She could have resented it for that.

They were early enough to stand away from the main flow of people. Julian checked the platform information and put his phone away. Eve gave him back his coat, which removed the last object she could fuss over.

“Did you bring your charger?”

“Yes.”

“Room address?”

“Yes.”

“An entire functioning adult personality?”

“Mostly.”

She laughed, then looked at his collar because looking directly at his face had become difficult.

He touched her sleeve. “What do you need?”

She considered saying nothing. It was an attractive answer, tidy and impossible to disappoint.

“A hug. And then you should go through while there isn't a queue.”

He put his arms around her. His coat was caught awkwardly between them. The station continued making station noises, indifferent to whether she had collected enough of this moment to last three weeks.

“I'll miss you,” she said against his shoulder.

“I'll miss you.”

“You can still be excited.”

“I am. You can still be upset.”

“I have prepared thoroughly.”

His laugh was small and close to her ear.

When they separated, he brushed his thumb once over the back of her hand. They kissed without trying to turn it into an event for the people passing behind them.

“Message when you get there,” she said.

“When I've found the room.”

“Or if the room turns out to be a cupboard.”

“Especially then.”

He took the suitcase. At the barrier he looked back, lifted a hand, and went through. The troublesome wheel made its familiar sound over a seam in the floor. Then the crowd shifted, and she couldn't see him.

Eve stayed beside the pillar for a little while. Not until the train departed. Just until her hands stopped feeling as if they ought to be holding something.

Her phone had no new message. Of course it didn't. He had barely left her sight.

She put it in her bag.

The route home was one direction; the place where she was meeting Sophie was another. Dinner wasn't immediately, and for a moment Eve considered going home first, making tea, checking that Dumpling was comfortable, then finding a reason not to leave again.

He had been comfortable when she'd fed him and left. His ordinary evening would start when she returned after dinner. The person who would spend the intervening hours staring at a door was Eve.

She sent Sophie a message instead.

I'm out of the station. Early. Going to walk for a bit before meeting you.

Sophie replied that she was still finishing something and would be there at the agreed time. No emergency rescue, no sudden rearrangement of another person's afternoon.

Eve walked.

She stopped in a bookshop and picked up a book about window gardens, then put it down when she realized she was trying to furnish the venue's empty flowerpot through sheer emotional displacement. In the next aisle she found a slim collection of essays and read the first page standing up. The writing was funny. She stayed for the second page because of that, not because Julian was on a train.

She bought it with her own spending money, got the receipt, and resisted tucking it away to forget for several months.

By the time Sophie arrived at the small restaurant, Eve was sitting by the window with a glass of water and the book open at the beginning.

“Did I interrupt personal growth?” Sophie asked, hanging her coat.

“An essay about a disastrous camping holiday.”

“Excellent. I have indoor disasters.”

Sophie looked tired but pleased with herself. Her event had involved a stage curtain delivered to the wrong venue and a replacement that was too short. The producer wanted the audience entrance delayed. The lighting operator had suggested a different setup. Somebody had spent twelve minutes looking for a person who had been standing beside them wearing a new coat.

Eve laughed at that last part.

“I shouldn't. I've done it.”

“I know. You once asked me if I'd seen Sophie.”

“You were wearing a hat.”

“A persuasive disguise.”

They ordered dinner. Sophie told the story without making herself the sole heroic adult. The lighting operator had solved the visible problem. Sophie had reorganized the arrivals and made sure the team doing the extra work actually got a break. She had also told the producer that the next schedule needed paid setup time that reflected reality.

“What did they say?” Eve asked.

“They said we'd discuss it.”

“That phrase should cost money.”

“I sent a written follow-up. With numbers.”

Eve wanted to see the numbers. Sophie said she could show her after they'd eaten. Work advice could wait fifteen minutes.

Their food arrived, steaming and untidy. Eve burned her tongue slightly because she'd been listening and forgotten to test the first bite. Sophie passed her the water without pausing in her account of the missing coat-owner.

Only after the plates had been mostly cleared did she ask, “How was the station?”

“Busy. He caught his train.”

“And you?”

“I didn't try to hide his ticket.”

“A proud moment.”

Eve rubbed a drop of condensation from her glass.

“I keep wanting to check whether he's sent anything. Even though I know he's traveling. I thought being sensible about him going would make me less ridiculous afterward.”

“Sensible doesn't sedate you.”

“No. Poor service.”

“Are you worried about something specific?”

Eve thought about it. “Mostly that I'll miss him, which isn't exactly something I can repair.”

Sophie nodded and didn't try.

She asked what he was looking forward to learning. Eve described it as best she could, careful not to convert half-understood clinical language into expertise. She could explain the part she understood: he wanted supervised practice and the chance to learn from a different team.

“He looked happy when he accepted,” she said.

“Good.”

“Yes.” Eve smiled, surprised to find it uncomplicated for a second. “He did.”

They ordered one warm pastry to share because neither wanted a whole dessert. This was a decision with a long history of failure between them.

Sophie cut it into two visibly unequal pieces and pushed the larger toward Eve.

“You'll accuse me otherwise.”

“I would never.”

“You'd wait three years and mention it at my wedding.”

They ate slowly. Eve told her about Dumpling's occupation of the suitcase, leaving out the temptation to treat it as a prophecy until Sophie asked why she was blushing.

“I may briefly have wanted the cat to have a strong opinion on professional development.”

“Does he?”

“Apparently he prefers the blanket.”

“A workable career path.”

Eve's phone vibrated beside the book.

She looked at the screen. Julian had arrived and found the room. The key worked. There was a desk, an excessively cheerful bedspread, and a shared kitchen downstairs. He had sent a photograph of the view through the window: a wall, part of a tree, and a small ordinary patch of sky.

No figure of him in the picture. No way to tell what he was feeling except the words he'd chosen.

Here safely. Going to find groceries before the shops close. How's dinner?

Eve smiled at the screen, then looked up to find Sophie taking the last bite of pastry.

“That was on my side.”

“You left it unattended.”

“For six seconds.”

“Travel is unpredictable.”

Eve laughed hard enough that the server glanced over.

She wrote back, Dinner good. Dessert stolen. Glad you're there safely. Tell me about the bedspread when you have time.

Then she turned the phone face down and asked Sophie what the producer's proposed setup time had actually been.

They split the bill. Outside, Sophie headed for her bus and Eve went home with the book beneath her arm. The evening had not cured the hollow feeling that returned when she thought about Julian's unfamiliar room. It had contained dinner anyway.

Dumpling met her in the hallway, stretched, and walked toward his bowl. She took off her shoes, washed her hands, and attended to the animal who was actually waiting for her.

Later, wearing the borrowed sweater, she opened her book again. She read three pages before checking her phone, which was an improvement she did not need to turn into a contest.
