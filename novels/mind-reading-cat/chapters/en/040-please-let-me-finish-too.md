# Chapter 040: Please Let Me Finish Too

Eve collected three unused sheets, an obsolete proof, and the nerve she had spent most of the tram journey misplacing.

Tessa put the paper into a clean envelope.

“Is that everything?”

“For the job, yes.”

“An excellent distinction.”

Eve looked at her, but Tessa had already turned to help someone find a misplaced phone. The remark appeared to concern nothing more mysterious than completed work. Eve was the one carrying an unfinished conversation around beneath her coat.

The new card remained readable from the waiting chairs. A small reflection crossed the lower edge in the afternoon light, but not the main text. She checked it once, made no unnecessary adjustment, and stepped away.

Julian came out wearing the dark jacket whose broken zip Mabel had noticed at dinner. He pulled it closed without catching the lining.

“She fixed it,” Eve said.

“Properly. Apparently my repair involved moving the problem two inches.”

“You repaired it first?”

“I used pliers.”

“That sounds persuasive.”

“The zip was not persuaded.”

He held the door while she moved through with the envelope. Outside, a delivery cyclist was securing a bag to a rack. A small cloud of warm air escaped from the bakery next door whenever someone entered.

Julian glanced toward the tram stop.

“Are you going straight home?”

“I wanted to ask you something first. Not about the statement.”

He looked back at her.

“Do you have twenty minutes?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Really twenty minutes, not twenty minutes made out of something you're supposed to do?”

His expression changed slightly, enough that she wondered whether she had sounded accusatory before even reaching the accusation.

“I'm finished for the day,” he said. “I need bread. The bread can come with us.”

They went into the bakery. Eve had expected a serious conversation to begin with solemn eye contact, not with Julian discovering that the loaf he wanted had sold out. He chose another, considered a pastry, and put it back when the assistant said it contained something he disliked.

“Raisins?” Eve asked after they had paid separately.

“An unnecessary surprise.”

“You must find fruit cake morally difficult.”

“I try not to think about it.”

They walked to the small paved square beyond the side street. It was too early for the evening rush, and the bench nearest the low wall had dried in the weak sun. Eve wiped a remaining damp patch with a tissue before sitting. Julian set the bread between his feet, where it could neither roll away nor become a proposal about their relationship.

She was grateful for the ordinary arrangement. She was also running out of harmless things to notice.

“I've been thinking about Monday,” she said.

He waited.

“When I started explaining the card heading, you told me we couldn't publish the conversation before I'd finished.”

Julian looked down at his hands, then back at her.

“Yes.”

“I know you thought I meant something else.”

“I did.”

“The correction wasn't the problem. Well, it wasn't the whole problem.”

A bus turned beyond the square with a deep mechanical sigh. Eve waited for it to pass. Her mouth felt dry, and she resented that a small, reasonable complaint required such elaborate physical preparation.

“When you interrupt with a definite no, particularly in front of other people, I feel embarrassed before I even find out whether you've understood me. Then I spend the rest of the conversation trying to sound less foolish.”

Julian did not immediately answer.

Eve nearly helped him. She could say he was busy. She could explain that she knew he didn't intend it. She could turn her own complaint into a minor administrative fault that inconvenienced nobody.

Instead she pressed the envelope flat against her knee and let the silence remain.

“I embarrassed you,” he said finally.

“Yes.”

“I'm sorry.”

The apology came without an explanation attached to its ankle.

Eve looked at him more directly.

“Thank you.”

“I should have let you finish and then asked if I wasn't sure.”

“Yes.”

She had imagined needing more words. Perhaps some of the speech she had prepared was designed for a defense he wasn't making.

Julian moved the paper bag away from a small puddle beneath the bench. The gesture was unremarkable, and she liked that he could do it without making the apology feel unfinished.

“I start answering the question I think I've recognized,” he said. “Sometimes before I've heard the question you're asking.”

“That's what it feels like.”

“It isn't your job to become clearer at twice the speed.”

A laugh escaped her, mostly relief.

“I was considering a formal preamble. 'Before you object, please allow me to identify the subject.'”

“I would deserve it.”

“I don't want to have to use it.”

“No.”

He said that one softly enough that it wasn't an interruption at all.

A woman crossed the square pulling a shopping trolley. One wheel caught in a gap, and she backed it out with a practiced tug before either of them could rise to help. Eve watched her continue. Not every pause was a request for somebody else to take over.

“I didn't say anything at the time,” she admitted.

“You can say it later.”

“I know. I'm practicing.”

She rubbed her thumb along the envelope's seam. There were reasons she had found it difficult, and none of them belonged on a clinic invoice.

“When I first brought Dumpling in, I didn't know what I was doing. Then you offered me work. You've answered questions when I was worried. Sometimes I feel as though pointing out something I dislike means I'm forgetting all of that.”

His brow tightened.

“You paid for clinical work. You did the work you were hired for.”

“I know.”

“And we've also helped each other.”

“Yes.”

He turned slightly on the bench so that he was no longer looking straight across the square.

“None of that buys the unfinished part of your sentence.”

The words made her look down. Not because they were romantic. Because they were so precisely the answer to something she had been disguising as manners.

“Good,” she said. “I have a lot of unfinished parts.”

“I've noticed.”

She glanced at him.

He looked almost alarmed, then saw her expression and laughed.

“Not that way.”

“Please continue. I'm demonstrating exemplary patience.”

“I meant you often make a joke before saying what you want.”

Eve considered arguing. A defensible joke presented itself immediately, which seemed rude of her mind under the circumstances.

“I do,” she said instead.

“Sometimes I don't know whether you want an answer or whether the joke was meant to end the subject.”

“So you guess.”

“Sometimes.”

“And sometimes you just tell me what to do.”

“Yes.”

There was enough acceptance in the word that she didn't have to make the accusation bigger to keep it alive.

“I can try asking more plainly,” she said. “But I'd still like you to wait.”

“I will try. And if I catch myself after I've already done it, I should stop and ask you to finish. Not wait for you to argue your way back in.”

That sounded like something a person could actually do imperfectly. Eve trusted it more than a promise that every future conversation would be patient and beautifully timed.

For a while they sat without talking. Somewhere behind the bakery, bottles were being tipped into a collection bin. The sound came in bright crashes, followed by a silence that seemed larger because nobody rushed to occupy it.

Eve breathed out and discovered she had been holding her shoulders unnecessarily high.

“That was what I wanted to say.”

“I'm glad you did.”

He didn't ask whether she felt better now, as though an apology should come with a receipt. She ate the small plain bun she had bought and found it tasted of actual butter instead of nervousness.

Julian watched a pigeon approach a fallen crumb near the far end of the bench. He kept his own bread bag closed.

“What were you going to do this evening?” Eve asked.

“Listen to a radio play. Possibly repair a lamp.”

“With pliers?”

“I'm retiring the pliers from work beyond their competence.”

She smiled.

“Do you ever finish the radio plays?”

“Usually.”

“I fall asleep, wake up for a scream, and then feel unfairly uninformed.”

“That's most of the plot in some of them.”

She told him about a mystery film she had watched after the Pocket Path delivery. The culprit had been revealed by a second umbrella, not a second costume, which she considered a promising development for the genre. He asked a question, let her describe the absurd ending, and didn't attempt to turn her enjoyment into a recommendation she ought to act on.

When the church clock beyond the shops struck, Eve checked her phone. Twenty-seven minutes had passed.

“I borrowed extra time.”

“I stayed.”

She put the phone away.

They walked back toward the junction, discussing whether a lamp was worth repairing if its owner disliked the shape. Julian argued for useful light; Eve argued that a person could want useful light from an object that didn't resemble a worried mushroom.

“Mine resembles a perfectly calm mushroom,” he said.

“Then keep it. Emotional stability matters.”

At the crossing they went in different directions. No invented errand prolonged the walk. He had bread and an evening; she had a cat expecting dinner and a collection of unfinished notebooks that had survived another day without improvement.

“Thank you for listening,” she said.

“Thank you for telling me.”

It was enough. She didn't need to explain to him that it was enough.

At home, Dumpling stood near his bowl with the severe attention of someone who had not been informed that honest conversation could delay a meal by seven minutes. Eve fed him before taking off her coat.

After eating, he followed her to the table. The envelope slid from her bag, and he put one paw on it.

“Those are obsolete,” she told him.

He settled more firmly.

“Fine. A career in document retention.”

Her phone lit up beside the occupied papers. Sophie had written, *Could I come over tomorrow afternoon? No need to make anything elaborate. I'd like somewhere that isn't a venue.*

Eve looked at the message, then at the clear corner of her table. She could offer an hour, a chair, perhaps a late lunch. She didn't need to ask Sophie to look at a draft while she was there. She didn't need to infer a whole crisis from the fact that her friend had asked for company.

*Yes. Come for a late lunch if you want. Nothing to help with. Just you.*

Sophie's answer arrived a moment later.

*That sounds good.*

Eve put the phone down and moved a notebook from the spare chair.

Dumpling watched the newly available seat, then looked at her.

She couldn't tell what he had noticed.

Tomorrow, she could ask her friend how she was and leave enough room for the answer.
