# Chapter 102 — A Beautiful Stall Can Be Wrong

The little stage in the photograph was beautiful.

It had a pale arch, a velvet cushion, and a string of imitation pearls arranged so precisely that Eve could almost hear the designer holding her breath. On the cushion sat a white dog in a waistcoat. The dog's face occupied very little of the image.

“The display rotates,” the applicant explained. “So people can photograph from both sides.”

They were standing in the courtyard, where the actual paving had an uneven patch and the wind kept lifting the corner of the printed proposal. Eve held it down with her notebook.

“How long would each animal be on it?”

“The session is three hours, isn't it?”

“The market is. I mean one animal.”

The woman looked surprised by the distinction.

“We can offer water. Obviously.”

Eve looked again at the tiny rail surrounding the platform. It looked like decoration, not anything meant to keep an anxious animal secure. The pearls were props. The white dog was, apparently, also a prop.

“I can't approve a continuous animal display,” she said. “Do you have a version that demonstrates the products without requiring a pet to remain there?”

“They're pet accessories.”

“You could display them on forms. Owners could examine the fastenings and sizes. You wouldn't need the rotating stage.”

“But the stage is the attraction.”

The applicant was not shouting. That made the disagreement harder to escape into righteous anger. She had paid for a stand before, she explained. Photographs brought customers. Plain rows of products looked like a shop people could visit any day.

Eve understood the commercial logic. She had spent years making ordinary products look like events.

“I know why you want it,” she said. “It isn't suitable for this market.”

The woman folded the proposal along its existing crease.

“Then I don't think the fee is worthwhile for us.”

“All right. I haven't confirmed the booking or taken payment.”

They parted politely. Eve remained beside the patch of paving, holding a notebook that now contained one fewer source of income.

The organizer came out with a key ring and two paper cups.

“They're not taking it?”

“No.”

“I thought they were our strongest applicant.”

“They have a strong display. It doesn't fit what we've promised.”

Eve took the tea. The paper warmed the cold crease across her fingers.

There were reasons to refuse a project and there were consequences. She could not make the second disappear by being confident about the first. They had discussed basic suitability in the agreement, but a principle written while every stall remained hypothetical had been cheaper than this conversation.

Inside, they opened the budget together. The committee's small support allocation and the confirmed stall fees covered the essentials if they kept the original plan modest. The decorative entrance board was no longer sensible. Neither was renting extra furniture to disguise empty spaces.

“We could leave the place here for another seller,” the organizer said.

“Yes, until the deadline. But I don't want to spend the missing fee before someone pays it.”

The organizer drew a line through the arch rental. Eve crossed out the matching image on her draft poster. She liked the image. Removing it still made the plan better.

A second applicant arrived carrying a folded fabric tunnel beneath one arm.

This proposal was a timed obstacle contest. Owners would encourage their pets through the tunnel, and the fastest would receive a prize. The applicant showed Eve a video on his own phone: several excited people leaning forward while a small dog stood still at the entrance.

“We could use music,” he said. “Build up the atmosphere.”

Eve asked him to turn down the volume before watching the rest. The dog eventually turned away. The clip cut before anyone acknowledged the refusal.

“Could you do an owner demonstration instead?” she asked. “Show how the tunnel folds, explain the dimensions, let people decide whether it suits their own space? No race, no required participation.”

“That won't draw a crowd.”

“We're not promising a crowd around an animal that doesn't want to take part.”

He rubbed a thumb along the folded frame.

“What about a model?”

“A model?”

“My nephew has an extremely ugly stuffed dog.”

Eve almost laughed, then saw that he was serious.

“That could demonstrate the opening. If the display stays inside your allotted space.”

They measured it. Fully extended, the tunnel blocked the walkway. Half folded, beside a photograph showing its full size, it fit. The applicant tried drawing the stuffed dog's imaginary route with one finger and admitted he would need a clearer explanation than a competition.

“People could ask questions about storage,” Eve said. “That seems to be the part everyone discovers too late.”

He glanced at the bulky thing under his arm.

“Fair.”

He accepted the smaller format. His confirmation included the revised description, not merely Eve's memory of a promising conversation.

By late afternoon she had declined one applicant, revised another, and found a mistake in her own plan. The attractive corner she had reserved for a photo backdrop was the best place for a clear route to the indoor toilets.

She moved it on paper, tried the alternative outside, and discovered the alternative blocked the bin access.

“No backdrop,” she said eventually.

The organizer looked at her.

“I was about to suggest that.”

“Please suggest it sooner next time.”

They laughed with the tired relief of two people allowed to dislike something pretty.

On the way home, Eve stopped at a shop selling pet supplies. She wasn't searching for a last-minute sponsor or an endorsement to replace the vet corner. The owner had requested information about a small stall, and she needed to see the products before agreeing to anything.

The shop's sample table was aggressively ordinary. Washable mats. Bowls. Spare clips. A carrier displayed with its door open and nobody inside it.

A customer was trying to fold a travel bowl and failing.

“It goes the other way,” the owner said, then stopped himself reaching over. “May I show you?”

The customer handed it over. Two quick movements produced a flat circle.

Eve thought of the rotating stage.

The shop owner listened to the event description without asking how many times Dumpling would appear. He wanted to know whether the stall would be sheltered and if he could demonstrate cleaning one of the mats with plain water.

“We'll need to keep the water contained and the walking surface dry,” Eve said. “Can you use a tray?”

“Yes. I have one.”

He fetched it. It was scratched, unattractive, and exactly the right size.

She gave him the application information and told him when she needed an answer. The fact that she liked the demonstration did not permit her to bypass the ordinary booking process for him.

At home, Dumpling lay across a sunless part of the rug, considering an empty patch of floor with the gravity of a creature whose appointments were private.

Eve sat nearby with the revised plan. She felt an irrational desire to tell him that she had refused an entire stage for the dignity of animals. It was a very impressive speech to make to someone who had no obligation to care about her paperwork.

Instead she checked his water and settled at the table.

The empty stall space remained annoying. An open rectangle was not automatically generous design simply because she had failed to fill it. It needed a use, or at least an honest acceptance that it might remain empty.

She called the organizer.

“Could we use part of the free area for a short owners' exchange? Not a health session. Ordinary things people have learned about living with pets. Moving house, finding storage, planning who does the cleaning.”

“Would you lead it?”

“I could include a brief structured conversation in the existing programme. We should invite people who actually want to speak, not assume every visitor will provide a story.”

“What if nobody does?”

“Then it's chairs where people can sit. We don't announce a panel we haven't arranged.”

The organizer agreed to ask two residents who had already expressed interest. Eve prepared a small invitation that made clear nobody needed to bring an animal, supply a touching story, or discuss anything they preferred to keep private.

The replies were not cinematic.

One person wanted to explain how she'd labeled supplies so her sister could find them during a weekend visit. Another wanted advice on where neighbors bought durable cleaning cloths. A third could not attend but asked whether ordinary secondhand storage baskets would be acceptable at a stall.

Eve checked the event's selling rules before answering the third. Useful did not mean exempt from arrangements.

By the end of the week, the supply shop had confirmed. The tunnel seller sent a photograph of the stuffed dog. It had one ear pointing permanently backward and a facial expression of exhausted judgment.

Eve laughed aloud.

She obtained permission to use a close-up of the tunnel itself, not the nephew's toy, for the stall preview. The owner could bring the toy on the day if he wanted; the promotional image didn't need a character with an accidental career.

The revised poster looked less spectacular than the first version. It showed what visitors could actually do: browse local supplies, meet neighbors, exchange practical experience, and sit down without buying a pet accessory first. The clinic's image was absent. So was any promise that Dumpling would attend.

Julian looked at the proof when she showed him over tea that evening.

“Would you understand what it is?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Would you understand you can come without an animal?”

He pointed to the line she had made too small.

“Not quickly.”

She enlarged it.

“Thank you. That was the actual question.”

“Glad to have passed.”

“It wasn't a test.”

“I meant reading the small print.”

She laughed and put the proof aside. There was no medical approval hidden in his comment, and she would not use his name beneath it.

After he left, she sent the final version to the organizer. The reduced income forecast remained attached to the internal plan, alongside the smaller expenses. Nobody had earned permission to pretend the loss wasn't real.

But there would be a clear route through the courtyard. There would be things people could examine without requiring an animal to stay still for a stranger's photograph. There might even be a useful conversation in the space where the rotating stage would have stood.

Before closing the folder, she added the shop owner's tray dimensions to the equipment list. She also wrote down who would take the wet cloths away afterward. It was less pleasant than arranging beautiful colors, but it meant somebody would not discover the job at closing time and do it for free.

Eve looked once more at the empty rectangle on the old plan, then filed that version away.
