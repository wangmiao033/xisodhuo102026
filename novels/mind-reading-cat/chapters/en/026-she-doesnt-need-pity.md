# She Doesn't Need Pity

Mabel's first objection was to the word “still.”

Eve had written it near the top of her notebook: still takes her elderly dog out every day.

“Why wouldn't I?” Mabel asked.

They were sitting on a bench outside the clinic, where Eve had been collecting a signed approval for a finished clip. Mabel had come out carrying a dog lead, a cloth bag, and a folded sheet of paper that looked as though it had survived several arguments.

The dog at her feet was biscuit-colored and broad in the chest, with gray around his muzzle. He had inspected Eve's shoe and decided it did not require further investigation.

“I meant that the routine matters to you,” Eve said.

“Then write that.”

Eve crossed out “still.”

“What's his name?”

“Biscuit.”

“Because of the color?”

“Because of a biscuit. The circumstances were not flattering to either of us.”

Biscuit rested his chin on Mabel's shoe.

Eve looked at the folded paper. “Tessa said you were looking for somewhere easier to walk.”

“Somewhere to sit. Walking I can find.”

Mabel unfolded the page across her knees. It was a printed map of the nearby streets, covered in small marks. One corner had been repaired with translucent tape. Another contained a shopping list that had become part of the geography.

“This says there's seating here.” She tapped a small green square. “There isn't. Not unless you count the top of a locked electricity box. This one has three steps. This one has a bench, but somebody's decided it needs a planter directly in front of it.”

Eve leaned closer.

“And this?”

“An excellent place to buy thread.”

“That isn't a rest stop?”

“It could be, if you like thread.”

Eve laughed. Mabel did not seem to mind that.

---

The idea had arrived from Tessa as a practical question, but Eve had begun improving it before she even met the person involved.

An older woman, an older dog. A daily walk. Companionship. The sort of short film that could be made with quiet music and close shots of a hand on worn fur.

She knew how the opening would look. She had half a caption in her head before Mabel objected to “still.”

Now Mabel was tapping the map with a blunt finger.

“Do you know someone who could make this readable? I don't need a television program. I need to know whether I can get around the corner without discovering the seat's gone.”

“I could help check the information.”

“Check it how?”

“Walk the routes. Ask the businesses. Mark where there are steps, where there isn't public seating.”

“With the dog?”

“If that's comfortable for you both.”

“Not all of it in one go.”

“No. Of course.”

Mabel looked at her notebook again. Eve had left the word “companionship” below the crossed-out line.

“I'm not lonely,” Mabel said.

“I didn't—”

“You were getting there.”

Eve closed her mouth.

Across the street, a delivery driver tried to work out which of three doors belonged to the address on his phone. Mabel pointed him toward the middle one without rising from the bench. He waved his thanks.

“My daughter calls often enough to have opinions about my curtains,” she continued. “I have people to see. I have a cupboard full of mending, most of which belongs to people who claim they can sew. What I don't have is a reliable place to sit halfway to the park.”

Eve turned to a clean page.

“All right,” she said. “Tell me about the halfway point.”

---

They began with a walk to the bakery, not the park.

Eve asked before taking out her phone. Mabel agreed to a few reference pictures of the street, but not to an interview on camera yet.

“Learn what you're looking at first,” she said.

Biscuit moved at a steady, unhurried pace. He stopped at a tree, considered it, and then continued as if he had approved a small proposal.

Eve kept herself beside Mabel rather than backing up in front of them with the lens. Without the camera to give her a job, she became aware of how often she wanted to fill silence.

“How long have you lived—”

“Here long enough to remember when that place sold shoes.”

Mabel pointed at a shop selling phone cases.

“Were they good shoes?”

“No. That's why it's a phone shop.”

Eve laughed again.

At the bakery, the pavement narrowed where a sign stood outside. Mabel waited for two people to pass before moving through with Biscuit. A man coming out held the door and said hello to the dog.

“Your usual?” he asked Mabel.

“Not today. She's inspecting the kingdom.”

Eve explained the map. The baker came out far enough to look at the bench beneath the window.

“People can sit there,” he said. “Customers or not. As long as the entrance stays clear.”

“Is that all the time you're open?” Eve asked.

He thought. “When we're open, yes. We bring it in after closing. And if the delivery van's here, it gets moved.”

She wrote that down rather than turning it into a permanent symbol for “bench.”

Mabel watched her pen.

“That's the sort of thing,” she said.

It was the first praise of the afternoon, and Eve felt absurdly pleased by it.

---

They sat while Biscuit rested near Mabel's feet. Eve asked whether she could take a photograph showing the bench and the clear space beside it. Mabel stepped out of the frame without being asked.

“No face?” Eve said.

“Does the bench need one?”

“No.”

“Then let's spare it.”

The photograph was not moving. It did not tell a story of loss or courage. It showed a bench, part of a bakery window, and the corner that needed to remain clear.

It might actually help someone.

Mabel took a small tin from her bag and checked its contents, then put it away again. Eve had expected dog treats. Inside were safety pins, thread, and a measuring tape.

“I thought we might need repairs,” Eve said.

“Usually somebody does.”

“Do you sew professionally?”

“I did alterations for years. Now I do fewer and charge people who say, 'It's only a little job,' more accurately.”

Eve looked up.

Mabel smiled for the first time, a quick crease at one corner of her mouth. “You know those people.”

“I recently met several.”

“Good. You're learning.”

A woman leaving the bakery stopped beside them and held up the hem of her coat.

“Would this be difficult?” she asked Mabel.

Mabel glanced at it. “For you or for me?”

The woman laughed. They arranged a time for a proper look, not an instant repair on a public bench. Eve noticed how easily Mabel asked the woman to bring the coat clean, and how easily the woman accepted the instruction. No apology accompanied it.

When she had gone, Mabel caught Eve watching.

“A coat isn't improved by pretending you can hem it without seeing the lining.”

“I was admiring the scheduling.”

“Admire the stitching when it's finished. Scheduling is just telling people when you are available.”

Eve had managed to make a much more complicated discipline of it. She wrote the bakery hours down again, this time leaving room for the answer she did not yet have.

Biscuit shifted. Mabel checked the lead and offered him a drink from the water she had brought, using a small bowl from the bag. Eve let the camera stay in her lap.

“Could I make a short piece about the map?” she asked after a while. “Not about you being lonely. About the things people forget to check.”

“Who would see it?”

“Mostly local viewers, I think. I can't promise how far it goes. I could show you your parts before posting, and leave out anything that identifies where you live.”

“And if I don't like it?”

“We don't use your part.”

Mabel studied her for a moment.

“Don't make Biscuit look sad because he's taking his time.”

“I won't.”

“And no music that sounds like someone has died.”

Eve felt heat reach her ears. The exact music she had imagined was not quite that bad. It was not entirely innocent, either.

“Understood.”

Mabel nodded. “You can come walking with us. We'll see about the film.”

---

They turned back before the park.

Eve had expected to do more. She had brought a charged phone, a notebook, and the sort of enthusiasm that assumed preparation entitled it to a full afternoon.

Mabel did not apologize for ending the route.

“This is enough today,” she said.

“All right.”

Eve walked with them as far as the public crossing, then said goodbye instead of following them to a door she had no need to know.

Mabel gave her a time for another short route and a number where she could be reached. Eve read it back. Biscuit sniffed the base of the crossing post and ignored the exchange.

On the way home, she passed the park entrance they had not reached. The path looked lovely from the street. Late light picked out the leaves. She could almost see the opening shot she had planned.

She kept walking.

---

Dumpling was in the window when she returned, watching a plastic bag caught in a shrub outside. He turned toward her, then back to the bag. Its career was apparently more interesting than hers.

Eve put the notebook on the table and opened the editing program.

She created a folder labeled WALKING MAP. Under it she made three smaller ones: CHECKED, TO CHECK, and DON'T USE.

The empty interview plan went into the last one.

She did not delete it. It was useful to see how quickly she had written a person into a role before hearing what that person wanted.

Her phone buzzed. Mabel had sent a message.

The bakery shuts early on Wednesdays. Ask him, not me. He keeps changing it.

Eve smiled and added a question mark beside the opening hours.

Then another message arrived.

And Biscuit is not “still going strong.” He is going at his own speed.

Eve looked at the phrase she had already typed into a note for the possible caption.

Caught twice in one afternoon.

She deleted it and wrote back, His own speed. Got it.

Dumpling abandoned the window and jumped onto the empty chair. He sniffed her notebook where Mabel had pressed the map flat. Eve moved the pen out of his way.

“You didn't meet her,” she said. “So you don't get an opinion.”

He sat on the edge of the DON'T USE printout she had just laid beside the computer.

Eve took a reference picture of her own crowded table, for no audience except herself. Three folders, an imperfect map, a cat obscuring the least useful sentence.

Then she called the bakery to ask about Wednesday.
