# Chapter 144 — Two Projects, Not the Whole Account

The sticker on Lydia's folder still said PARTNER PROGRAM.

She peeled it off while Eve sat down, then discovered it had left most of its glue behind.

“That looks worse,” Lydia said.

“Usually does halfway through.”

They were meeting in the branch office again. This time Eve had brought no forecast. She had brought a short proposal and three questions about the work Good Company needed done.

Lydia set the damaged folder aside.

“The campaign team can't separate the account requirements from that package,” she said. “The quarterly minimum goes with the partner program. It isn't available for independent commissions.”

“I understand.”

Eve had practiced the answer on the bus without making it sound like either gratitude or surrender. It still caught slightly on the way out.

“We do have a smaller content budget,” Lydia continued. “Less predictable. Two possible jobs at the moment.”

She opened a document. One project was a customer guide to understanding dimensions on product listings. The other was a practical visit guide for this branch, whose upstairs appointment room and side collection point confused enough customers that staff kept repeating directions.

Neither involved the first-pet campaign. Neither required an animal, a personal household story, or access to Eve's independent account.

The visit guide had a modest fee and a deadline tied to new signs being installed. The dimensions project needed more development, but the budget existed.

Eve read both briefs.

“Could we look at the route downstairs before discussing the format?” she asked.

Lydia glanced at the time. “We have twenty minutes before the delivery gets busy.”

They went down through the stockroom and emerged beside the service desk.

A customer stood near a sign pointing toward COLLECTIONS. Beyond it, an arrow marked APPOINTMENTS. The two arrows pointed in different directions; neither explained that the appointment room also had a separate accessible entrance.

“Which collection?” the customer asked a staff member.

“What are you collecting?”

“My order.”

“Yes, which kind?”

Eve waited until the exchange had been resolved before taking out her notebook. No camera appeared.

“Do people know there are different kinds?” she asked Lydia.

“Apparently not always.”

A staff member named Priya joined them after finishing with the customer. She pointed to the word collections.

“That sign means booked returns from the service partner. Online orders are at the side counter.”

“What does the order confirmation call it?” Eve asked.

Priya checked a blank sample on the desk computer. “Collection point.”

Eve looked at the sign again.

“There may be a vocabulary problem before there's a route problem.”

Priya laughed. “I've been trying to explain that without sounding as if I hate arrows.”

They walked the routes, including the alternative entrance, and checked where each instruction needed to begin. Eve suggested that the guide use the same names as the booking and order messages. Lydia would need the relevant teams to agree on those names before anyone designed a cheerful map around the confusion.

At the foot of the stairs, Eve asked which services actually required the upstairs room. The answer was narrower than the signs suggested.

“That needs checking with the provider,” Lydia said.

“Put the confirmed details in the brief. I can make the route clear once we know what we're directing people toward.”

Back upstairs, the job had become more interesting.

It was still small. It would also mean somebody carrying a large parcel could reach the right counter without asking three strangers for help. Eve knew how to make that improvement visible.

They discussed a single illustrated guide, a mobile version, and a short sequence of photographs taken while the shop was closed to customers. Priya could help verify the directions during paid work time, arranged by her manager. Nobody needed to play a delighted visitor for the camera.

Then Lydia opened the dimensions brief.

The sample product page showed a storage box, a cheerful room, and a number that described the outer width. The usable space inside was smaller. Another listing supplied height without making clear whether it included the lid.

“Customer service has examples of the confusion,” Lydia said. “They can remove identifying details before sending them.”

Eve sketched two rectangles.

“Can the product team verify these?”

“Yes.”

“Then I can show what each measurement includes. It should also say which measurement the customer needs to take at home.”

“Can it be attractive?”

“I'd prefer that.”

Lydia smiled. “People sometimes hear accurate and decide everything must be gray.”

Eve added a small shoe beside one rectangle to show scale, considered it, and crossed it out.

“Not that attractive.”

They agreed to keep actual measurements legible rather than replace them with a decorative comparison. The completed guide would explain packaging conventions for the selected products. It would not tell people which enclosure or equipment was suitable for a particular animal.

Eve could already see the design. Plain drawings, one question per panel, a place to check whether the stated size included the parts likely to catch against a shelf. Useful work, with a client able to confirm its facts.

The contract discussion became difficult in a less dramatic way than the earlier call.

Good Company's standard commission document still referred to partner content. It gave the brand approval over any connected social posting during the project period. Lydia thought this was leftover wording; Eve needed the document to say so.

They marked it for replacement with the actual deliverables.

The brand would approve accuracy and its own presentation before publication. If a requested change introduced a claim Eve could not support, they would resolve it before use. The finished materials would run on named brand channels for the agreed period. Her archive, unrelated work, and account identity would remain outside the contract.

Lydia asked whether Eve might share the finished guides voluntarily.

“Possibly. I'd decide once they're finished. It can't be part of what this price buys.”

“Then I'll leave it out.”

There was no offended silence. Lydia moved to the next clause.

Eve felt herself relax, then paid attention again. Relaxation was pleasant; it was not a substitute for reading.

They did not sign that afternoon. Lydia needed the revised document approved, and Eve wanted to read the final version without a shop delivery arriving beneath her feet.

At the door, Lydia said, “I hope the earlier conversation didn't make you think we wanted something irresponsible.”

Eve held her folder against her side.

“I think the campaign wanted a route that didn't include stopping. Some of my work has to leave that option visible.”

Lydia considered that.

“We won't agree on every part of it.”

“No.”

“But these two projects?”

“I think I can do them well.”

“Good. I'd rather buy those than spend a quarter arguing about the rest.”

Eve agreed. It was an oddly satisfying way to leave a meeting: neither person converted, both with a possible job.

On the way home she met Claire for the coffee they had arranged the week before. Claire was carrying a small box of lighting clamps and looked as though one had offended her personally.

“Replacement?” Eve asked.

“Replacement for the replacement. I may be opening a museum.”

They found a table with room for the box. Claire described a shoot that had moved indoors because the outdoor location looked beautiful and sounded like a freight depot. Eve asked whether the client had accepted the change.

“After hearing the recording. Sometimes evidence is very loud.”

When Claire asked about Good Company, Eve described the two smaller briefs.

“Will you enjoy them?”

“The route guide, yes. The measurements one might make me unbearable in shops.”

“You were previously a delight?”

“Unverified.”

Claire smiled over her cup.

Eve had expected to feel she was reporting a retreat. Instead she found herself describing the confusing sign and the space inside the storage box. She sounded like someone discussing work she understood.

“The bigger package would have made the quarter easier,” she admitted.

“I'm sure.”

Claire didn't promise that the right client would now arrive to reward her. She asked what Eve would do with the remaining days.

“Find out which ones I can actually afford to keep for the room trials.”

“That sounds less entertaining than shopping for a new microphone.”

“It is. The microphone was going to be extremely good for my character.”

They spent the rest of the coffee discussing Claire's clamps and a film both had disliked for different reasons. Eve went home without asking her to read the contract for free.

The revised agreement arrived two days later.

Eve checked it against their notes. The reference to account-wide approval had gone. The two deliverables had separate fees, dates, costs, and approval steps. One delayed project would not quietly require her to hold all the other's dates open. There was no minimum work after these commissions and no guarantee that they would lead to the larger program.

She asked for one correction to the branch guide's update responsibility. The brand needed to own later changes to its entrance arrangements; Eve could not promise to discover a moved counter forever.

Lydia agreed. The corrected version arrived that afternoon.

Only then did Eve sign.

She saved the countersigned copy when it came back and moved the two project fees into confirmed work, according to their payment dates. The figures improved. They did not reach the discarded column.

She sat with that for a moment before entering the production time.

Then she sent the first invoice required by the schedule and began the route draft.

Her initial map was too handsome. It made the two entrances appear nearly identical because she had drawn them at equal size for balance. The actual customer entrance was broad and glass-fronted; the alternative entrance needed a particular turn beside the neighboring wall.

She changed the drawing.

Next, she replaced the word here with the exact doorway name. A person arriving through a message link would not necessarily be standing where the designer imagined them.

Dumpling came to inspect her moving pencil. Eve offered him a toy on the clear part of the floor and put the loose eraser away before he could consider it. He chased the toy twice, then sat on it.

“A short engagement,” she said.

He began washing one paw.

The next morning, Lydia returned the draft with Priya's practical correction. Eve had shown a door opening into the route when it opened away from it. The error changed where someone should wait with a parcel.

Eve corrected it and thanked them for checking.

There was no argument about tone. No request to make waiting sound like a failure. A real piece of work had met the people who knew the place and become better.

She sent the revised drawing, then opened her own account to answer a question about the borrowed-room session.

The old adoption-readiness link remained where she had left it. The new commissions had their own folder, their own deadlines, and enough space around them for work that led somewhere else.
