# Chapter 201 — Cancellation Notices Are Harder Than Promotion

The supplier called while Eve was watching rain collect along the bottom edge of the venue's front window.

“We can't promise tomorrow morning,” the woman said. “The van hasn't left. Several routes are delayed.”

Eve looked at the empty space beneath the preparation table. The washable mats and printed packs were supposed to be there by now, ready for Friday's owners' exchange and Saturday's larger session.

“What about this afternoon?” she asked.

“I can't promise that either.”

The answer was careful rather than evasive. Eve could hear other phones ringing behind it.

“Don't ask the driver to make an extra trip for us,” she said. “Send me the revised window when you have one. We'll decide about the order separately.”

After ending the call, she opened the weather and transport notices. Rain had continued through the night. Several local services were running with delays, and the forecast offered more of the same. Nothing in the notices suggested that attending an optional workshop was a good reason to take chances.

She knew what she needed to do.

She opened the promotional post she had scheduled two days earlier.

A cheerful photograph of hands arranging cards filled the screen. Beneath it, her own words promised a useful afternoon and the chance to leave with something finished.

Eve removed the scheduled post before she could begin bargaining with it.

Robin called a moment later.

“I'm still at home,” they said. “The bus route's changed. Do you need me to try another one?”

“No. Stay there.”

“What are we doing about tomorrow?”

“I'm looking at it.”

“You've gone quiet in the way that usually means you know.”

Eve pressed her fingers against the table edge.

“Cancelling Friday and Saturday.”

Robin let out a breath. “All right. What do you need remotely?”

“First, let me confirm what I'm paying for. Your cancellation time under our agreement still stands. If you'd like a short admin shift today, I'll pay that separately. You don't owe us a whole day because the weather ruined two sessions.”

“I can do ninety minutes this afternoon. Then I have another commitment.”

“That would help.”

They chose a time and divided the customer contacts. Robin would work through the Friday list and mark who had received the notice. Eve would handle Saturday and the payments. Neither would tell people to come to the venue for a refund.

After the call, Eve sat down to write.

Promotion came easily when she had something she believed in. She knew how to lead with the useful part, show the room clearly, and make the booking button feel like an invitation rather than a demand.

Cancellation required the useful part to be disappointment.

Her first sentence apologized for circumstances beyond their control. She deleted it. Customers did not need a defense brief before learning whether to leave home.

She wrote the dates first. Both sessions were cancelled because of continuing heavy rain and disrupted travel. Everyone booked would receive a full refund. If they preferred to move their booking instead, they could reply, but nobody needed to choose a future date to get their money back.

She added the next time messages would be checked, the expected refund processing period, and a request not to travel to the venue. Then she read the notice aloud.

It sounded plain. It also answered the actual questions.

Eve sent the direct messages before publishing the notice on the account. The customers who had already paid deserved to hear before strangers did.

The first reply came within a minute.

Thanks. I was worrying about getting the carrier onto the bus.

The second said only, Please refund.

The third customer called.

“I took time off for this,” the woman said.

Eve shut her laptop halfway so she would stop reading new messages while listening.

“I understand. I'm sorry we're cancelling after you've arranged that.”

“Could you just run a smaller group? I can still get there.”

“We won't be running either session. We can't ask the host or other customers to travel, and some of the materials haven't arrived.”

“I don't need the materials. I wanted the session.”

Eve looked at the chairs waiting in their careful semicircle. She could hear exactly how much the woman wanted what she had paid for. Wanting it didn't create the people or conditions needed to provide it properly.

“I know. I can't offer that this weekend. I can refund you today, and if you want to hear about the next dates later, I can send them when they're confirmed.”

There was a long pause.

“Just refund it.”

“All right.”

The call ended without anyone being reassured.

Eve entered the refund. The confirmation appeared in a bland green box that looked much more pleased than either participant had felt.

She leaned back and listened to the rain.

At noon, the upstairs neighbor knocked on the open inner door, holding a folded umbrella away from the floor.

“Are you still having the weekend event?”

“No. I've cancelled.”

“Good. The entrance is getting slippery. I've told the building manager.”

Eve went to look from the dry side of the doorway. Water blew beneath the outer canopy when the wind changed direction. She placed a clear temporary closure notice where people could read it without entering and moved the loose welcome mat back so it couldn't slide underfoot. The building manager would handle the shared entrance. She didn't need to improvise a construction job with towels.

“Are you on nights?” she asked.

“Tonight. Assuming the transport behaves.”

“I'll keep the phone calls away from the stairwell.”

He nodded and left. It was an ordinary exchange, without anybody congratulating her for not making his life harder.

She returned to the desk and found a customer asking whether pets could wait inside while owners sorted out transport.

There was no event to wait for. Eve replied directly, making that clear, and told them not to set out. She checked that the original booking description hadn't left an old arrival instruction visible. It had. She updated that too.

The administrative work kept producing small loose ends. One booking had two participants but only one contact. A customer had paid through a route that needed a manual refund request. Another asked to transfer, then realized the proposed date clashed with work and changed back to a refund.

Eve completed each one without turning their choices into evidence of loyalty.

During Robin's remote shift, they caught a duplicate entry that would have made Eve refund the same booking twice.

“Please admire my contribution to the balance,” they said.

“I admire it enormously.”

“Good. Friday's list is done. Two people haven't confirmed they've read the message. I'll try the contact method they supplied, then leave the status clear.”

“Thank you.”

At the agreed end time, Robin reported what was complete and logged off. Eve stared at the remaining payment request and felt the familiar urge to ask for just a little longer. She processed it herself instead.

By midafternoon, the amount leaving the business account had become painfully real.

Some supplier costs would be recovered. Some preparation time and printing costs would not. The materials still in transit would be useful later, but useful later was not the same as cash available now.

The supplier emailed two options: cancel the undelivered goods or accept a later delivery once the routes were running reliably. Eve checked the stock cupboard before answering. They would use the mats for sessions already on the calendar, and the printed packs contained no dates that would make them useless next week.

She kept the order, asked for the unnecessary express charge to be removed, and received a revised invoice. The saving was modest. She filed it beside the original rather than treating it as compensation for everything else.

Then she opened the month's figures and entered the actual losses. She left Robin's pay where it belonged.

Julian messaged during his own break to ask whether she was still at the venue.

For another half hour, she replied. Then home. Sessions cancelled.

He sent a short acknowledgment and said when he expected to finish at the clinic. She did not ask him to look over the refund figures. She wanted him to tell her the money would somehow return, and that was precisely the prediction neither of them could make.

Instead, she ate the sandwich she had brought and drank the tea that had gone cool beside it.

A notification offered a weather video from a nearby street. Eve watched only long enough to realize it showed people struggling through pooled water, then closed it. Her own account's notice looked dull beside the dramatic images filling the feed.

She opened the camera by habit and aimed it toward the front window.

Through the glass she could make a compelling picture: blurred umbrellas, the white letters of You, Me & Paws, chairs waiting in the dim room. She knew the caption before she had taken it. Something about the community keeping warm together.

Nobody was keeping warm together here. The room was closed. People were at home, or trying to finish ordinary work, or disappointed about a cancelled afternoon.

She lowered the phone without taking the photograph.

The closure notice already said what she needed people to know.

Before leaving, Eve checked that the supplies were stored safely, shut down the equipment she wasn't using, and made a slow circuit of the room. The empty corner still held its single chair and the flowerpot Julian had brought long before opening. The pot remained empty. She turned it slightly so it sat clear of the edge, then caught herself trying to improve a room nobody would see.

She left it alone.

The building manager confirmed receipt of her entrance report. Eve locked up and took the route that was still operating normally, allowing extra time rather than hurrying between connections. Her shoes were wet by the time she reached the apartment, but the journey was uneventful.

Dumpling met her in the hallway, sniffed the unfamiliar wet smell, and retreated a few steps while she removed her coat.

“Wise,” she told him.

She hung the coat where it could drip without reaching his things and changed into dry socks. There were clean towels in the cupboard. She used one without needing to remember whether she had washed it.

When Julian came home, she was sitting at the table with the refund confirmations open.

“Finished?” he asked.

“Everything that can be processed today. One is waiting on the payment service.”

“Dinner first?”

She looked at the total once more, then closed the laptop.

“I had to disappoint someone who wasn't being unreasonable,” she said.

Julian put down his bag. “That's hard.”

“She was right about taking time off. I still couldn't give her the session.”

He pulled out the chair beside her. He didn't offer an argument that would make the woman wrong.

Eve leaned against him for a minute, listening to water run down the window.

Then she stood to help with dinner. The weekend would be smaller than planned, the account would contain less money, and nobody needed to board a bus tomorrow to prove the venue mattered.
