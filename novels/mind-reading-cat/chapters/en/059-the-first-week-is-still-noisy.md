# Chapter 059 — The First Week Is Still Noisy

On Monday morning, Pip barked through Eve's attempt to record a voice-over.

She stopped after the third interruption, leaving the microphone on its stand. On the screen, her sentence ended halfway through the word *comfortable*.

Dumpling lifted his head from the sofa. After a moment he put it down again.

Eve could have moved the recording to the bedroom. Instead she stood beside the desk, listening, and felt a small unwelcome disappointment. She had known the Friday conversation couldn't repair everything. Knowing it and hearing the result were apparently different activities.

Her phone rang.

The coordinator did not begin with a greeting.

“Have you heard?”

“The barking? Yes.”

“We've had a message.”

“From Ben?”

“No. Another resident. They want to know what the meeting achieved.”

Eve looked at the unfinished waveform. “What does Nora say?”

“I haven't contacted her yet. I thought perhaps you could—”

“Friday is my follow-up. If you need something before then, we should agree what it is. But the committee should receive the report first.”

She heard herself sounding stiff. The stiffness was partly directed at her own desire to rush downstairs and earn proof that the plan had worked.

“All right,” the coordinator said. “I'll ask for the times.”

“Please don't promise them I'll stop it today.”

“I won't.”

When the call ended, Eve moved the microphone. She recorded in the bedroom, sitting at the edge of a chair so the curtain would soften the sound behind her. It wasn't the setup she preferred, but the client needed a clear voice-over rather than a demonstration of the building's social progress.

She finished the work before checking her messages again.

The caretaker had adjusted the door on Friday afternoon. It still latched. It no longer struck its frame with the same startling clap, though Eve wasn't qualified to assess more than the ordinary sound she heard passing through it.

Nora had reported that a parcel delivery and two unexpected visitors had come through before nine. The early clothing collections were not the whole morning.

Ben sent no message that day.

His silence was not a result she could use.

On Tuesday, Eve took her laundry downstairs and met Nora carrying a bundle of finished clothes. The bundle was wrapped in a clean sheet, pinned neatly at the corners.

“Customer?” Eve asked.

“Shop delivery. I'm doing it myself this week.”

“Because of the changes?”

“Partly. The person who usually collects can't come later.”

Eve held the door while Nora maneuvered through. The adjusted closer moved slowly enough that neither had to catch it with a shoulder.

“That part is better,” Nora said.

“Have you told the coordinator?”

“I told her Monday was bad. It felt strange to send another message saying one thing was good.”

“You can say both.”

Nora shifted the bundle. “Apparently I can do a lot of things that take more time.”

There was no accusation in her voice, but Eve felt it anyway. A communication plan could sound gentle from the side of the table where she hadn't lost a customer or rearranged a workday.

“I've put the extra delivery time in the follow-up,” she said. “We'll need to see whether this version is manageable.”

Nora nodded, then looked back through the glass door.

Pip was not with her. Eve didn't ask where he was as though Nora owed her a care report every time she left the flat. Nora had told the committee she was arranging the dog's assessment through her own practice. That was the limit of Eve's role.

“Your cat doesn't make much noise,” Nora said.

“He has other methods.”

“What methods?”

“Standing on the exact thing I need.”

Nora gave a short laugh. “Pip takes a sock and waits for me to notice.”

“Effective management.”

For a few seconds they stood outside as two people describing ordinary pets. Then Nora went to deliver the clothes, and Eve carried her washing to the machine that liked swallowing small coins.

By Thursday, the private record contained three quieter mornings, one bad morning, and two people who disagreed about what the word *quieter* meant. It also contained a cancelled appointment, an extra trip to the shop, and a note from the caretaker confirming the door had been checked.

The coordinator wanted to post an update.

“Something positive,” she said over the phone. “Otherwise people assume nothing is happening.”

Eve suggested explaining what had been done and when it would be reviewed, without declaring improvement on everyone's behalf.

“Could we say the problem is being resolved?”

“We can say the committee is following it up. Resolved sounds as if we've already checked the result.”

The coordinator sighed. “You do make short notices take longer.”

“Yes,” Eve said. “That's part of what you hired.”

After hanging up, she read the sentence again and wondered whether it had sounded too sharp. She sent the draft with the agreed scope instead of adding a free reassurance paragraph to soften her guilt.

That evening a neighbor caught Eve beside the mailboxes and asked whether the committee had finally taken Nora's side. He held a leaflet about window cleaning, twisting it into a thin tube.

“I don't decide complaints,” Eve said. “The committee has explained how to report them.”

“But you were in the meeting.”

“Yes.”

“What did she say?”

“We agreed not to publish the conversation. If you've been affected, send your own account. You don't need a story about her to describe what you heard.”

He looked dissatisfied. Eve felt the pull of being more interesting, of giving him one harmless detail that would prove she hadn't been paid to sit uselessly in a room. She took her own envelope from the box and wished him a good evening.

Upstairs, the envelope turned out to contain an offer for a service she didn't need. She laughed at how much dignity she had invested in collecting it. Then she recycled it and went back to work.

Friday's follow-up began with Ben bringing a mug of tea.

He had asked to meet in the building's shared entrance alcove rather than the hall. Nora had agreed because it saved her leaving work for the walk. The coordinator stood beside the noticeboard, and Eve put her notebook on the low shelf, keeping the entrance clear.

The meeting would be fifteen minutes. There was no new public audience to impress.

“Wednesday was better,” Ben said before Eve asked. “Thursday too.”

Nora folded her arms, waiting for the rest.

“Monday wasn't.”

“I know.”

“I'm not saying it to start again.”

“I know,” she repeated, less sharply.

They compared only the times they had actually noted. Nora hadn't been home during one reported burst and didn't claim to know what happened. Ben had heard noise after waking for another reason on Thursday; he distinguished that from being woken by it.

Eve found herself writing less than she had expected. Precision did not always need more words.

Then the coordinator asked whether they could keep the later collection window.

“For another week,” Nora said. “Not exactly as it is now. I can't do all the shop deliveries myself.”

“What would you change?”

“One collection on Tuesday, after the time Ben said he usually gets up. The shop can manage that. The rest by appointment, with the reminder not to ring early.”

Ben looked into his tea.

“I don't get up at exactly the same time every day.”

“No. But I need a time I can give them.”

They settled on a narrow trial window that Ben said would usually be less disruptive. He didn't promise never to report noise during it. Nora didn't promise that Pip would suddenly become indifferent to visitors.

The coordinator wrote the next review date in her own diary. Eve's paid follow-up ended here; further facilitation would need a new arrangement. The residents could continue the committee's normal process without her standing between every message.

“Can we take that down?” Nora asked, pointing to the board.

Someone had added a small handwritten slip beneath the neutral contact notice: *How hard is it to teach a dog?*

Eve had seen it when she arrived. She had also seen Ben looking at it.

“That one's mine,” he said.

Nora turned toward him.

He set his mug on the shelf. “I put it up Monday. I was angry.”

“You could have sent the time.”

“I did. Then I put that up too.”

He pulled out the pin. The tiny paper curled in his hand.

“I shouldn't have written it. It was insulting.”

The apology didn't arrive wrapped in an explanation about being tired, although everybody in the alcove knew he was. Eve kept her pen still.

Nora stared at him for a moment.

“It was,” she said.

“Yes.”

She let the answer stand. She didn't tell him it was fine, and he didn't ask her to.

A resident came in carrying two shopping bags. They moved to let her through. The door closed behind her with its new, softer sound. From upstairs came one bark, then another, then silence.

All four of them paused.

Nora looked toward the stairs. Ben picked up his mug. Neither claimed the moment proved anything.

After the brief meeting, Eve and the coordinator walked to the hall to finish the invoice paperwork. The coordinator had hoped for a photograph to accompany the update, but there wasn't one to use. She accepted Eve's plain text with a small diagram showing where residents could find the committee contact.

“Will this seem like we paid for a conversation we could have had ourselves?” she asked.

“You could have,” Eve said. “You hadn't.”

The coordinator considered that, then laughed with less reluctance than last week.

“Send the invoice.”

Eve did, with the completed stages listed plainly. No discount for the fact that the dog still barked. No extra charge invented because the work had been emotionally uncomfortable.

On the way home, she stopped at the bakery. The last two rolls had been pushed to the back of the shelf, where she had to ask for them rather than point vaguely through the glass.

“For one person?” the server asked.

“For two lunches. One person.”

A simple answer. Eve was learning to appreciate questions with those.

At home, Dumpling had put his fabric mouse inside her slipper. She took it out before putting her foot in, offered it across the floor, and watched him chase it behind the chair. Nothing about the game could tell her whether Ben would sleep better tomorrow.

Her phone held a final message from Nora.

*The shop agreed to Tuesday. Thank you for writing down that the first version cost me work.*

Eve answered that she was glad they had found a version to try. She didn't ask whether the dog had been diagnosed, whether Nora liked Ben now, or whether everyone felt understood.

She saved the completed notice and closed the project folder. The next review belonged to the people who would still live with the problem after her invoice was paid.

Then she opened the sound file she had recorded on Monday. The client had approved it. The word *comfortable* made it all the way to the end.
