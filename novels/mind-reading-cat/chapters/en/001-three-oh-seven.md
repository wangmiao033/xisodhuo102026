# Chapter 001 — Three Oh Seven

At 3:07 p.m., Eve walked out of the office carrying a cardboard box marked TEAM OF THE YEAR.

The automatic doors closed behind her. Through the glass, the company slogan hovered above her head: MAKING EVERY KIND OF VALUE VISIBLE.

She moved half a step sideways. The box made a dry scraping sound against her coat, louder than she would have liked. No need to give a passing photographer the impression that she was a kind of value the company had noticed and subsequently disposed of.

The rain hadn't started yet. The wind got there first, slipping beneath her collar. Inside the box were half a tube of hand cream, a succulent that had stopped participating in its own survival, and a mug that said I LOVE MY JOB.

A consolation prize from last year's office raffle. She had meant to leave it behind, then gone back for it.

It had taken her five months to find a mug whose handle didn't burn her fingers. Her dismissal wasn't the ceramics department's fault.

“Eve.”

The receptionist followed her outside with a blue lanyard.

“This is yours. They took the card, but the strap—”

“Hasn't signed a noncompete.” Eve accepted it. “Thanks.”

The receptionist hesitated, then told her to get home safely. Eve appreciated that she didn't say, With your abilities. She had heard that enough today. People handed it over like a large counterfeit banknote, generous with something none of them had to honor.

Her phone lit up. A message in the work chat: Tomorrow's client meeting has moved forward half an hour. Thanks, everyone!

Her thumb went automatically to the reply box. Only then did she notice the gray notification beneath it.

She had been removed from the group.

The few letters she hadn't typed somehow made the box heavier.

The meeting with HR had lasted twenty-three minutes. Restructuring. Position optimization. The company regretted the situation, too. When she asked about the handover, they said a colleague would take care of the files. When she asked about her final payment, she received a written breakdown with dates.

At least that had dates. It wasn't another We'll be in touch.

She folded the sheet into her notebook. She signed no blank pages. Then she spent six minutes beside her desk, trying to remember what she'd forgotten.

Now she knew.

Next week.

The first drops struck the box. She pushed the notebook farther inside. Twenty-seven wasn't particularly young, but it was nowhere near old enough to declare her whole life a failure. She knew that. She had once designed an infographic called Losing Your Job Isn't the End, all warm colors and encouraging headings.

The client had requested a final line: Taking the first step is the best answer.

She had revised it seven times.

Right now, the best answer was that her bus arrived in four minutes and she had no spare hand for an umbrella.

A message from Sophie appeared.

*Out yet? I finish at six. Don't buy an expensive dinner. Wait for me.*

Eve sent a clinking-glasses emoji, then added: *Don't worry. Currently in possession of one mug. No expensive dinner.*

She didn't mention skipping lunch. Sophie was setting up an event; that morning she'd sent a picture of a floor covered in cables, captioned *My life is getting complicated.* Someone else's entire day didn't have to become a rescue mission just because Eve's had gone wrong.

The stop was around the corner. She ducked her head and hurried into the rain. Almost beneath the shelter, the box's bottom sagged. She caught it with her knee, but an old proposal slid out and landed on the wet pavement.

She had drawn the sun on its cover herself.

Today it seemed a little insensitive.

She crouched to retrieve it. The edges were already going soft. An older man held the box's corner while she gathered the paper. She thanked him twice; he waved and hurried onto the bus pulling in beside them.

Eve reached the door just as the driver was about to close it. He glanced at her dripping cardboard and waited.

“Getting on?”

She lifted one foot.

Then something cried behind her.

Not the bus doors. A small sound from the back of the shelter, as though whatever had made it would rather not be heard but hadn't quite been able to help itself.

Eve turned. Beneath the advertising panel, she could see the end of an orange tail.

“Miss?”

“Sorry. Go ahead.”

The doors closed.

For a second she wanted to run after the bus and explain that she hadn't meant it. She'd only intended to look. She wasn't applying for a new responsibility. Someone had taken her existing responsibilities away barely an hour ago.

A narrow recess lay behind the advertisement. An orange-and-white cat was pressed into it, its white fur flattened into little gray patches by rain and mud. It didn't run. It held its right front leg clear of the ground and tried to push farther back, as if the wall might agree to swallow it.

Eve crouched. The box had to go down beside her shoes.

“I don't have food,” she told it.

The cat stared.

“And I just lost my job. If you're interviewing people, you should probably see a few more candidates.”

One ear turned backward. She couldn't tell whether that meant it had heard her or whether the bus brakes farther down the street had frightened it. She withdrew her hand without reaching for the injured leg.

A young man passing under the shelter stopped.

“Think it's been here a few days. Someone left food.”

“Does it belong to anybody?”

“No idea.” He checked his phone. “Sorry. I'm late.”

“Sure. Thank you.”

She didn't dislike him for leaving. She had been in a hurry herself. Five minutes ago, she'd been chasing a bus that would take her home, where she could pretend this was an ordinary afternoon until she closed the door.

She searched for nearby veterinary clinics. The first had moved. The second line was busy. Rain thickened against the roof, and a dark stain spread through the base of her box.

The cat shifted. Its sore paw stayed off the ground. It made another sharp, small sound.

“Don't move,” Eve said, then felt ridiculous. “Not an order. A suggestion. Entirely advisory.”

The third call connected.

The woman who answered asked where she was and what she could see. Eve described it as clearly as she could, resisting the urge to use the injury terminology she'd just read online. The woman told her not to pull the cat out by force. The clinic could examine it, but she couldn't promise a price or an outcome over the phone.

“Can you bring it here safely?”

Eve looked at her box. Team of the Year had collapsed at one corner.

“I need a box that hasn't been rained through.”

“The convenience store on the corner might have one. Ask there first. Call us again when you have it, and we can help you avoid frightening the cat more than necessary.”

Not just Bring it in.

The next thing she could actually do.

Her legs had gone numb. She steadied herself against the advertising panel before crossing to the shop. The owner saw her dripping box and pointed at the floor. She hastily set it on a plastic crate by the entrance.

“Buying something?”

“A cheap towel. And do you have a sturdy empty box? There's an injured cat outside. I'm taking it to a clinic.”

He looked past her, then down at the cardboard.

“Not in that. Bottom's gone.”

“It was hired for a different position.”

He didn't laugh. But he fetched a clean, smaller box and a roll of tape from behind the counter.

“Pay for the towel first. Box is free.”

She almost promised to recommend the shop to people. Stopped herself. A free cardboard box didn't require a proposal presentation.

“Thank you.”

She bought the towel and moved her mug, notebook, and final-payment sheet into her cloth tote. The wet proposal went around the outside. The owner put the succulent on the windowsill.

“Collect it tomorrow.”

“It might not make it to tomorrow.”

“Whether you come back is your business. Whether it stays alive is its business.”

Eve blinked, then laughed.

Not the laugh she'd been using all day to make other people less uncomfortable.

The cat was still behind the shelter when she returned. Following the instructions over the phone, she approached slowly. She was clumsy. More than once she had to stop and wait. A claw caught in the towel, and she held still rather than tugging, keeping that small patch sheltered from the rain.

She thought the standoff would last forever.

Then the cat lowered its chin onto the towel's edge.

Getting out from behind the panel took time. By the end, sweat prickled beneath her damp hair and her shirt was soaked. Raindrops blurred the map on her phone. When the box rustled, she slowed immediately and checked that the towel hadn't covered its air openings.

“You can rate the transport when we arrive,” she murmured.

No answer. Only a faint scrape against the cardboard.

She added a note to the ride request: injured cat, secured for transport. The first driver canceled. The second called, confirmed what she was carrying, and told her to wait outside the shop.

When he arrived, she asked whether she could put her tote down by her feet to protect the floor. He glanced at the box.

“Take your time. Get settled first.”

Only once she was in the car did she notice the blue lanyard wound around her wrist. Empty. No identification card. No particular purpose.

A message from her mother appeared.

*Busy today?*

She looked at it for a while.

*Out right now. Talk later.*

This time it wasn't a lie.

Buildings slid backward beyond the wet window. The cat was quiet enough that she kept wanting to check it was still breathing. At a red light, a pink nose emerged from behind the towel and sniffed her hand where it rested beside the opening.

Eve didn't touch it.

“I'm taking you to a doctor first,” she said. “I don't know what happens after that yet.”

It was harder to say than Everything will be fine.

And somehow lighter.

The car stopped outside the clinic. Through the glass, someone passed carrying an animal carrier. The reception lights were on. Eve paid, caught sight of her balance, and quickly darkened the screen before lifting the box again.

She had meant to hide the worst of today. At least until she got home and closed her own front door.

Instead she pushed open somebody else's, wet through, carrying one more thing she had no elegant way to handle.

“I'm the person who called,” she said.

A paw emerged from the towel and hooked itself into her sleeve.
