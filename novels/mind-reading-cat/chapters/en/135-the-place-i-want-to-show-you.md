# Chapter 135 — The Place I Want to Show You

The library's reading room had become the large-print section.

Julian stood in the doorway, considering three shelves where a row of tables had once been. Eve waited beside him with her coat folded over one arm.

“Was this the whole place?” she whispered.

“Yes.”

“You described it as if there might be a west wing.”

“I was smaller.”

There were still two chairs beneath the window. Beyond the glass, the rear of the grocery shop showed a stack of plastic crates and the edge of a delivery van. Someone had painted the wall pale yellow, but rain had already left gray streaks beneath the drainpipe.

Eve looked from the view to Julian.

“Excellent prospect.”

“Wait until they unload the cabbages.”

She laughed too loudly and covered her mouth. A reader at the nearest shelf looked over. Eve mouthed an apology, and the woman returned to choosing a book.

They sat in the remaining chairs. Julian's knees were nearly level with the low windowsill. He looked less imposing there, with his coat awkwardly folded over his lap and a laminated notice about keeping the aisle clear beside his shoulder.

“I came after school,” he said quietly. “Usually when it was raining.”

“To read forbidden books?”

“To avoid going straight home and being asked what I'd done that day.”

“That dreadful question.”

“I never had an answer ready. I'd spent the entire day doing things. Then someone asked and I couldn't name one.”

Eve understood that more readily than she expected. Her own mother could make what did you do today sound like a request for a satisfactory use of the daylight. Sometimes the difficulty belonged to the question. Sometimes Eve had brought it with her.

“What did you tell them?” she asked.

“Nothing.”

“Breakfast has a predecessor.”

He smiled. “Apparently.”

Outside, a man lifted a stack of flattened cartons into the van. Julian watched with the absorbed expression he sometimes wore at his own window, as though enjoying having no responsibility for what happened next.

“I thought you might show me somewhere with animals,” Eve said.

“There was a pigeon on that roof fairly often.”

“Your first mentor.”

“Poor attendance.”

She turned toward the books rather than laugh loudly again.

Julian pointed to the far corner. “There used to be books about making things there. Models. Small experiments. I borrowed one about paper aircraft so many times the librarian suggested I buy it.”

“Did you?”

“No. Then I'd have had no reason to keep coming.”

The answer was so plain that Eve did not know what to do with the tenderness it caused. She wanted to know the boy who had needed a reason to sit near a window. She also liked the man beside her, who could now ask her to spend a morning doing very little there.

They wandered through the shelves. Julian found the craft section in another room and showed her a newer book containing elaborate paper models. He opened it to a bridge requiring cuts so small that Eve distrusted the illustration.

“You could make that?”

“Probably badly.”

“That's a category I can support.”

He put the book back. They did not need to buy paper or improve themselves before lunch.

At the front desk, Eve noticed a display of local postcards. One showed the library exterior before its entrance had been changed. She bought it for a small amount, then asked Julian whether he minded if she wrote on the back.

“What?”

“The date. Cabbage viewing with Julian.”

“You'll confuse future historians.”

“They can interview us.”

She wrote it with the pen at the desk. There was no photograph of him to post, no account update about the unexpected origins of a caring veterinarian. The postcard went into her bag beside the ordinary shopping list she had brought for the afternoon.

They ate sandwiches at a nearby café. Julian chose the table without the draught because he knew the door used to stick open, then looked faintly disappointed when somebody came through and it closed perfectly.

“Your local knowledge is deteriorating,” Eve said.

“I'll update the file.”

“Any other essential stops?”

“A shop that sold very cheap pencils. It's a travel agent now.”

She looked across the street at the window full of blue water.

“The pencils did well for themselves.”

Afterward they took different buses. Julian had errands near his flat; Eve needed groceries and an afternoon with her unfinished client draft. She kissed him outside the café and promised to show him her own place the following weekend, provided the forecast wasn't miserable.

It wasn't. The next Saturday was cool and bright, and their train arrived with enough time for Eve to discover she still knew exactly which exit to take.

Her mother had known about the visit. Eve had told her they were coming to the neighborhood for a walk and asked whether a short cup of tea later would suit. Her mother had a morning appointment and had said after three would be fine. Nobody had added lunch.

“This way,” Eve told Julian outside the station.

“You sound certain.”

“I spent a great deal of time being sent the other way.”

They passed the bakery where she had bought bread before the family meal and turned behind the shops. A narrow public path led to a footbridge over the railway tracks. Its green paint had faded almost to gray in places. The fencing was higher than Eve remembered, or she had edited it out of her memory to improve the view.

At the bottom of the steps, she stopped.

“It's a bridge,” Julian said.

“You've identified the major feature.”

“Were you worried I wouldn't?”

“No. I was worried I'd brought you an hour from home to look at a bridge.”

“I agreed before I knew the attraction. My mistake.”

She smiled and led him up.

From the top they could see the station platforms, the backs of shops, and a long section of track before it disappeared between buildings. A maintenance vehicle stood beside a shed. Beyond the far line, laundry moved on a balcony.

Eve put her hands on the railing, away from a peeling patch.

“I used to come here when my mother went to the big food shop. Once I was old enough to wait outside on my own.”

“To watch trains?”

“Partly. I made up where everyone was going.”

“Did you ask them?”

“They were inside trains.”

“A limitation.”

“I worked around it.”

She pointed toward the far curve. “Someone in a red coat was going to a new job. Somebody carrying flowers was going to apologize. People with suitcases were obviously having a more interesting life than mine.”

“Possibly carrying laundry.”

“I hadn't developed that plot yet.”

A train approached, slowing for the station. Its windows showed brief, incomplete lives: a man looking down at his phone, a girl leaning against an adult's shoulder, an empty seat with sunlight across it.

Eve watched them pass. She remembered wanting to be anywhere else while also worrying that her mother would finish shopping before she got back. Her imagined journeys had always been constrained by the need to carry one bag home.

“I thought the bridge would feel bigger,” she said.

Julian rested his forearms on the rail beside her.

“The trains are still reasonably large.”

“Thank you. I was afraid the whole operation had shrunk.”

He turned toward her. “Did you write the stories down?”

“Sometimes. There was a notebook. I gave everyone dramatic jobs. Explorer. Famous musician. Person who discovered things nobody else had noticed.”

“No content planners?”

“Tragically underrepresented.”

She told him about making a newspaper for two neighboring children, charging for copies she had drawn individually because she had no way to print them. The business had ended when one customer pointed out that his version contained fewer jokes than the other one's.

“Was there a refund?” Julian asked.

“A deeply disputed one.”

“So you've been training for years.”

“I wish I'd kept the accounts.”

They stayed until another train passed. Eve felt none of the overwhelming revelation she might have tried to put into a film. The bridge smelled faintly of metal and warm dust. Her hands were getting cold. Julian's shoulder touched hers, and she liked showing him a place where she had once been bored.

On the way down, he asked which stories she remembered best.

“The woman had actually bought the flowers for herself,” Eve said. “I changed that one after I saw her again. She got off at this station and bought them from the stall.”

“So the apology was canceled.”

“Yes. She just wanted flowers.”

He smiled. “A good revision.”

They bought hot rolls and ate them on a bench near the shops. Eve had not remembered the bench being there. Julian needed a napkin before she did, which restored a satisfying measure of fairness to the outing.

At ten past three they rang her mother's bell. She opened the door with a packet of biscuits in one hand and asked whether they'd eaten.

“Yes,” Eve said.

“Properly?”

“Bread with things inside it.”

Her mother looked at Julian.

“It was lunch,” he confirmed.

She let them in.

The curtains were new and a definite green. Eve said they looked good. Her mother said she had decided the others were purple, with the gravity of someone announcing the result of a long investigation.

They drank tea at the small table. The property supplement did not appear. Her mother asked about the library, then told Julian that Eve used to invent entire lives for passengers who might only be going to the next stop.

“She still does something like that,” he said. “Then she checks.”

Eve looked at him. It was a small description of her work, but she liked it more than an impressive introduction.

Her mother asked whether they wanted to stay for dinner. Eve said they had planned to take the next train back.

“You've only just got here.”

“We've been in the neighborhood all afternoon.”

“I meant here.”

“I know. Thank you for tea.”

Her mother was disappointed. She put the biscuits back in their packet, then offered two for the journey. Eve accepted them. There was no need to refuse something she wanted merely because she had refused something else.

On the train, Julian sat beside the window and Eve took the aisle. She gave him one biscuit, keeping the crumbs in her palm as carefully as she could.

“Worth the trip?” she asked.

“The bridge or the tea?”

“All of it.”

“Yes.”

She leaned her head against his shoulder. Outside, the backs of buildings slid past, briefly visible and then gone. Somebody on another bridge might be deciding that they were returning from an adventure.

They were returning to separate flats. Eve needed to feed Dumpling. Julian had laundry waiting. On Sunday they would each do things the other did not have to attend.

For the rest of the journey, she left the story exactly that ordinary, and kept her hand in his.
