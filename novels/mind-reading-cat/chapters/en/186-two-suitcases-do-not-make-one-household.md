# Chapter 186 — Two Suitcases Do Not Make One Household

Eve had given her mother directions to the wrong station before breakfast.

The mistake announced itself as a photograph of a ticket and a message containing only a question mark. Her mother had discovered that punctuation could carry an entire disappointed conversation, and used it accordingly.

Eve enlarged the photograph.

Then she opened the note she had made for Julian's aunt.

“Oh,” she said.

Dumpling lifted his head from the chair beside her, decided that this was not the announcement of food, and lowered it again.

It was Thursday of the second week of Julian's course. The following afternoon her mother would arrive for a short work visit, with a meeting that evening and another commitment on Monday morning. She had asked whether she could stay at Eve's flat between them. Eve had checked her own plans and said yes.

Julian's aunt was also arriving on Friday. She lived out of town and was coming to spend the weekend with her sister, Julian's mother, whose house was still on the other side of the city. The two visits had nothing to do with one another until Eve made them into a project.

Her mother had said she did not need collecting.

His aunt had said exactly the same thing in the family message Julian had shown Eve.

Somehow, Eve had ended up holding two rail itineraries, a list of possible lunches, and a promise to find the least inconvenient route for everybody. She had even added Julian's possible Saturday return to the page, although he had not yet confirmed it.

Now the words main station and north station appeared in the wrong places. The arrival times were correct. The people attached to them were not.

She called her mother.

“Don't follow that route,” she said.

“I wasn't going to. It starts somewhere I'm not arriving.”

“Yes. I mixed up two notes.”

“What two notes?”

Eve explained. Her mother listened, then asked why she was arranging transport for a woman she had never met.

“Julian's away. I thought I could help.”

“Does she need help?”

“I haven't asked exactly.”

“Then perhaps ask exactly before sending her to me.”

Eve laughed despite herself. “You're at the main station at two forty. Is that still right?”

“Yes. Then I can get the bus. You've told me which one.”

“The new message is the right one.”

“Comforting.”

There was a rustle at the other end.

“What are you packing?” Eve asked.

“Very little.”

“That sounded structural.”

“A bag.”

“What sort of bag?”

“A normal bag. You don't need to come.”

Eve could hear the familiar mixture of pride and evasion. She remembered the containers her mother had given them after lunch, including the cake box that had required its own seat. Very little might mean two nights' clothes or an entire evening's cooking.

“Is there anything that needs to go straight in the fridge?”

“Some soup. It's chilled. I have the insulated bag and ice packs.”

“Some?”

“Enough to be useful.”

Eve looked at her own refrigerator, which already contained normal groceries and a container Julian had left during their last dinner before the course. The food from that evening was long gone. The container remained, washed and waiting to be returned, occupying space because she had put something else in it.

“Please tell me how many containers before you leave,” she said.

Her mother sighed as if this was an unreasonable interest in arithmetic.

“Two. And a bag of rolls. And your sheets.”

“My sheets?”

“The spare set you left in the cupboard years ago. You said you could use them.”

Eve remembered saying that. At the time she had pictured the sheets traveling without the soup.

“Right,” she said. “Thank you. I'll make room.”

After they hung up, she corrected the route and sent a clear message retracting the first one. Then she checked the other conversation before she could compound the error with confidence.

Julian replied during his morning break.

His aunt was arriving at the north station at three ten, and his father had already arranged to meet her after an appointment nearby. She was staying with his parents, not at Julian's flat, and had no need for Eve to organize bedding or keys.

Eve read this twice.

On her page, beneath a question about lunch, she had written spare key?

She crossed it out so thoroughly that the paper nearly tore.

“I've invented an entire accommodation service,” she told Julian when they spoke briefly.

“Do I get a discount?”

“You haven't confirmed your own journey.”

“I can now. The Saturday session ends in time. I've booked the later train home, then back here Sunday afternoon. I'll be back at the accommodation Sunday evening.”

“One night.”

“Yes. At my flat. I need to check a few things there anyway.”

Eve felt a small drop of disappointment, then relief. Her mother would be in Eve's flat, the cat would still require his routine, and turning one night into a shared household rehearsal had never actually been on offer.

“I'd like to see you,” she said.

“So would I. Could we eat together after I arrive? If your mother wants to join us, ask her. If she's busy, that's all right too.”

Eve looked at the crossed-out notes.

“I've been trying to make everybody's weekend fit before finding out what they want.”

“What do you want?”

“To finish my client delivery tomorrow. To see my mother without conducting a station relay. To have dinner with you.”

“That sounds like enough.”

“It sounds suspiciously manageable.”

He laughed. Then he had to go back to the course, leaving Eve with three clear things instead of thirteen speculative ones.

At the venue that afternoon, Robin found her copying the actual arrangements onto a clean sheet.

“New session?” they asked.

“No. Two visitors.”

Robin looked at the discarded page, where arrows crossed meals and trains.

“Are they arriving by sea?”

“Apparently I was prepared for anything.”

“Except what they're doing.”

Eve pointed the pen at them. “You are becoming difficult to employ.”

“Fortunately, my booking ends at five.”

They worked through the ordinary preparation for Friday's exchange. Eve left the materials where Robin expected them and confirmed which part of the afternoon she would be there. She didn't add her mother's arrival to Robin's responsibilities. The room did not owe her free time because a relative had packed ambitiously.

That evening, her mother sent a photograph of the two soup containers inside the cooler. Beside it stood a suitcase and a broad cloth bag containing the rolls and folded sheets.

The phrase very little had lost its last defender.

Eve called again, this time with a precise offer. She could meet the train, help with the bags, and travel back to her flat, provided they went directly there. She would then need an uninterrupted hour to finish the client delivery before her mother left for the evening meeting.

“No sightseeing?” her mother asked.

“Not with chilled soup.”

“I wasn't planning to take it to a museum.”

“Then we agree.”

Her mother accepted the help without pretending the bags would carry themselves. Eve put the appointment into her calendar and deleted the second station entirely.

On Friday, the actual collection was less dramatic than her notes had promised.

She arrived with time to check the platform information and stand out of the main flow of passengers. Her mother's train was a little late. Eve sent one message saying where she was, then waited without asking for repeated updates from inside a moving carriage.

Her mother emerged pulling the suitcase and carrying the broad bag. The cooler was balanced on top of the case with a strap holding it in place. The arrangement looked sensible until one wheel caught a ridge and everything leaned toward the floor.

Eve stepped forward and took the cloth bag.

“Very little,” she said.

“Compared with what I could have brought.”

“That is not a usable measurement.”

They hugged awkwardly around the luggage. Her mother smelled of hand cream and the train's warm air. Eve had been preparing to manage her all morning; now she was simply glad to see her.

On the bus, they found space for the bags without blocking the aisle. Her mother asked about the client work. Eve began explaining the room photographs, then realized she was describing the staircase rather than the job.

“Did they like the pictures?”

“They've chosen the ones they want. I'm finishing the final adjustments.”

“Good. Don't put it off because I'm here.”

“I won't.”

Her mother looked out of the window. At the next stop she recognized a building from an earlier visit and pointed out that the shop beneath it had changed.

“You've been here before?” Eve asked.

“For work. You were at school.”

Eve looked at the building too. In her private itinerary, her mother had arrived as a complete beginner in the city. Apparently there were streets here that belonged to years of her life Eve had never asked about.

After a moment her mother said, “I can make tea without you supervising.”

Eve smiled. “We'll test that.”

At the flat, Dumpling inspected the new bags from a safe distance. Eve showed her mother where to put them so his usual route and quiet resting place stayed clear. The cat came closer when nothing moved toward him, sniffed the edge of the suitcase, and walked away with no interest in its owner's work itinerary.

They refrigerated the chilled food promptly. The two containers fitted only after Eve removed a nearly empty packet and rearranged a shelf. She had not yet opened the second bag.

Her mother produced the rolls, then the sheets.

“There,” she said. “Not much.”

Eve placed the folded linen on the bed. She would deal with sleeping arrangements properly after the delivery, rather than making her mother sit among unfinished piles while she pretended to be available.

Her phone showed a message from Julian: his aunt had arrived safely with his father. Attached was a photograph of a second cooler and a large shopping bag, apparently taken by his father with the caption reinforcements.

Eve showed her mother.

“Someone sensible,” her mother said.

“Someone with a refrigerator I hope.”

“They'll manage.”

Eve sat at her desk, opened the client's selected photographs, and found her mother quietly examining the kettle. There was a brief search for cups, a question about the tea, and then no further interruption. The apartment contained another person without immediately becoming an event Eve had to host.

When the final files went out, she checked the delivery message, closed the laptop, and joined her mother at the small table.

The tea was a little stronger than she usually made it.

“It works,” her mother said.

“What does?”

“The kettle. Your test.”

Eve laughed and took a sip.

Across the city, another suitcase had reached another house. Julian was still away learning things he would tell her about when he had time. Tomorrow's dinner was arranged, not the whole future.

For once, she knew where everyone actually was.
