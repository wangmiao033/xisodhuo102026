# Chapter 087 — Not a Reason to Stay

Julian was carrying a shelf bracket when Eve found him outside the hardware shop.

Not a shelf. Just one small metal bracket in a paper bag, which he held with the resigned care usually reserved for objects that had already caused trouble.

“Did it win?” she asked.

“The old one did. This is the replacement.”

They had arranged to walk together after his shift. He had warned her about the errand, and she had agreed that a shop selling screws was a perfectly acceptable starting point for a conversation. Now she was pleased not to be sitting opposite him with the job offer laid out like a test.

“What happened?”

“I put more books on the shelf than it liked.”

“Did it express this preference clearly?”

“Eventually.”

She laughed. He put the bag securely into his own and asked whether she had eaten.

“Yes. You?”

“Also yes. We can discuss employment without making it responsible for dinner.”

They turned toward the quieter street leading to the riverside. It was Wednesday evening, still light enough to see the ordinary untidiness along the bank. Someone had tied a child's lost glove to the rail. Eve noticed it, almost reached for her phone, and decided it could exist without becoming a useful image.

“The written offer arrived,” she said.

“Is it what they described?”

“Mostly. I asked about the outside-work clause this morning. They've answered in writing. I could keep my own noncommercial posts, and they would consider specific paid work without conflicts. But I can't assume I can keep all my clients.”

“Would you want to?”

“All is a dangerous word.”

He nodded.

That would have been a convenient place for him to say she had built something too valuable to leave. Or that regular income would be better for her. Eve had imagined both answers while walking to meet him and had prepared defenses against each.

He offered neither.

“What part would you most want to keep?” he asked.

She opened her mouth, then stopped.

“The account,” she said first.

“All of it?”

“Not the part where I refresh a number until it becomes a personal judgment.”

“A possible improvement in either job.”

She nudged his sleeve with the back of her hand. He smiled and waited.

Eve looked toward the river. The current was carrying a small leaf in a circle beside the steps. It kept nearly escaping.

“I like finding the actual problem,” she said. “Not just making something look better. Mabel needed places to stop. The information card needed two times to agree. Those aren't magnificent stories, but I like getting them right.”

“Could you do that at the organization?”

“Yes.”

The honest answer irritated her slightly. She had been hoping the things she valued would line themselves up behind one choice.

“I liked the interview,” she admitted. “They asked good questions. I didn't spend the whole hour wondering when they'd ask me to bring the cat.”

“Good.”

“And I like choosing what to try on my own. The little posts. The neighborhood work when it's properly scoped. I don't want every idea to need space in someone else's calendar first.”

They reached a bench. Julian asked whether she wanted to sit, and she said she would rather keep walking for a while. He adjusted his pace without turning the choice into a demonstration of excellent listening.

The next path was narrower. She went ahead around a puddle and waited for him on the other side.

“I think I'd miss the clinic work,” she said.

“Which part?”

“You're being very specific today.”

“I'm trying.”

She could hear that the answer wasn't entirely a joke.

“The work itself. Talking to Tessa about what people actually need before they arrive. Making something less confusing. Finishing a film that isn't only a flattering photograph of someone in scrubs.”

“That does eliminate one of my ambitions.”

“You'd have to apply elsewhere.”

He laughed, but she noticed that he looked down before answering her next question.

“Would you mind if I stopped?”

“The films?”

“The work. Being around as much.”

“Yes,” he said.

Eve's steps slowed.

Julian stopped beside the rail rather than letting the sentence follow her from behind.

“I would miss seeing you as often,” he said. “I like knowing we might get a short walk when the times happen to work. I would miss some of that.”

She waited for the advice attached to the admission.

There wasn't any.

“But that's not a reason you have to stay available to the clinic,” he added. “And I don't know enough about your accounts or the job to tell you what to choose.”

“You're allowed to want something.”

“I know. I'm telling you what I want. I'm trying not to call it a career assessment.”

The wind lifted a loose strand of her hair across her mouth. Eve pushed it away, grateful for a second in which she didn't have to answer beautifully.

“I thought you might say it wouldn't change anything.”

“It would change some things.”

“That sounds worse.”

“It might mean planning more. It doesn't mean I would stop wanting to see you.”

She looked at him then. He wasn't smiling to make the statement easier to dismiss.

“I don't want to choose it just to prove I'm independent,” she said.

“Then don't use me to prove either answer.”

The words were plain enough to catch. Eve felt a brief sting before she recognized its source. She had been waiting for him to make the decision emotionally clear, so that whatever she chose could be both brave and harmless.

He couldn't do that for her.

A cyclist rang a bell behind them. They moved farther from the path, and the interruption gave her somewhere to put the sting besides his face.

“That was an unfair job I nearly gave you,” she said.

“Which one?”

“Tell me what I want without influencing me, and also make sure I won't regret it.”

“I haven't qualified in that.”

“I should have checked your certificates.”

They stood there smiling for a moment. Eve could see his paper bag pressing against the side of his larger one. The bracket still needed installing. Tomorrow would contain shelves and schedules regardless of what happened to her career.

She asked whether he had ever turned down work he might have liked.

“Yes. Not always for excellent reasons.”

“May I ask about one?”

He described a period when he had kept accepting extra cover because declining seemed to require proof that he had something more important to do. Eventually the time he wanted for ordinary study and rest had disappeared. There had been no single villain allocating it all to him; he had repeatedly said yes.

“Did you fix it?”

“Some of it. I still find it easier to make an arrangement than admit I want a quiet evening.”

Eve remembered their argument about overloaded schedules. She didn't congratulate him as if the lesson were now safely behind both of them.

“Is this a quiet evening?” she asked.

“It was supposed to include a shelf.”

“We've damaged the programme.”

“Not beyond repair.”

They started back by a different path. When they reached the hardware shop again, it had closed; a worker was pulling a display stand indoors. Julian held the door for the awkward end of it, then stepped aside. Eve watched the ordinary exchange, two people solving the problem directly in front of them without knowing anything important about each other's lives.

She wished her choice could be reduced to holding one end of something.

At the corner, Julian asked what she planned to do next.

“Look at three months of money. Not just whether this month's rent can be paid.”

“Do you want company?”

She considered it. There was nothing wrong with company. But she knew how easily she could let his calm turn into a verdict she believed more than her own figures.

“Not for the first pass. Sophie said she can come over tomorrow for a little while. I want someone who'll notice if I've called unpaid hours freedom.”

“She sounds qualified.”

“She'll insist she's there for the tea.”

He asked whether she wanted another walk before Friday. She said not necessarily; she had enough to do without making them both attend her decision in shifts.

“But I'd like dinner Friday,” she said. “After I've answered. If you're free.”

“I am. At yours?”

“Yes. We can cook something undemanding.”

“You've seen me discuss books. Define undemanding.”

She promised not to select a recipe requiring them to fold anything into a shape. He accepted.

Before they parted, she reached for his hand. He took it and stood with her for another moment, neither heading in the correct direction.

“I would miss the accidental walks too,” she said.

“Then if you take the job, we'll arrange some deliberate ones.”

She liked that he said if, not when or despite.

At home, Eve found Dumpling on the cushion with one forepaw hanging over the edge. She sat nearby without moving him. He opened his eyes, inspected her, and apparently decided she required no immediate action.

The offer waited on her laptop. She read it again, this time imagining the actual work rather than the expression she hoped Julian would make when she answered.

She could picture the course pages, the amended timetable, the coordinator relieved to have someone check the conflicting instructions. She could also picture missing an afternoon conversation because she was on the other side of town. Both pictures were plausible. Neither was a warning issued by fate.

She wrote down the work she wanted to preserve and put a mark beside the parts that could realistically fit around full-time employment. Fewer than she had first assumed.

Then she made a second list: the things she could stop doing even if she kept working independently. Unpaid speculative samples occupied the first line. Refreshing the same response count occupied another.

She checked the journey she had actually made, including the walk between the stop and the office. Her first estimate had counted only the time in the vehicle. Correcting it cost her another piece of imagined leisure, though it still left a manageable working day. She wrote the longer time down.

Then she opened her current week and counted a different journey: three short trips made on separate days because she had said yes before asking whether tasks could be combined. Working for herself did not automatically make travel efficient. She could keep disliking the commute without quietly granting her own arrangements an exemption from arithmetic.

The two versions of her week looked less flattering and more useful. She saved both.

Dumpling stretched and stepped onto her lap. Eve moved the notebook before his paw could drag through the open page.

Julian's message arrived later: the shelf was repaired, with fewer books on it.

She sent back a photograph of Dumpling occupying the space where her notebook had been. No question accompanied it about what the cat thought she should choose.

Then she returned to the accounts folder. Tomorrow she wanted numbers that could disagree with her hopes without being accused of not caring.
