# Chapter 060 — Standing Together Through Ugly Words

The comment that bothered Eve most was not the longest one.

Beneath the committee's plain update, someone had written: *A lot of money to say dogs bark.*

She read it on Saturday morning while waiting for the kettle. The post contained the revised contact route, the fact that the door had been checked, and the date of the next committee review. It did not identify the residents or reproduce their private conversation. The committee had credited her communication work, as agreed.

Apparently that was enough to make the cost of her existence a public topic.

Dumpling sat beside the empty breakfast plate. Eve moved it to the sink before he could inspect the crumbs, then read the sentence again.

A longer comment accused the committee of caring more about animal owners than workers. Another said people who disliked dogs should live somewhere without other people. Both had gathered little signs of agreement.

Eve could answer the short one. She could describe preparation, listening, revisions, the difference between a complaint route and a public quarrel. She could write several excellent paragraphs that would sound increasingly defensive as they grew.

The kettle clicked off.

She made tea first.

Then she sent the coordinator a factual correction: the update's final line implied that all residents had approved the plan. Only the participants had agreed to the stated trial; the notice should say that.

The coordinator corrected it and thanked her. Eve watched the change appear. The ugly comment remained underneath.

Her contribution to the morning was not going to be winning the internet on behalf of one staircase.

She put the phone down and cleaned the small ring of tea she had left on the counter.

At eleven, she went to the hall to collect the sample cards and a cloth she had left after the earlier exchange session. The coordinator was standing on a chair, trying to reach a roll of paper on top of a cupboard. Eve asked whether she wanted someone to hold the chair. The coordinator climbed down instead.

“I've decided I don't need it badly enough,” she said.

“An excellent risk assessment.”

The cloth was waiting in a bag by the door. So was a printed copy of the update, with the corrected line marked in pencil.

“I'm sorry about the comments,” the coordinator said.

“They're on your page. You get more of them than I do.”

“Usually about heating.”

“Does the heating reply?”

“Not constructively.”

Eve smiled and folded the cloth. The coordinator explained that she would remove personal allegations under the page's normal moderation rules, not criticism simply because it was unpleasant. One resident had already sent a detailed report through the proper route. The report wasn't cheerful, but it was usable.

“We can't put that in the comments,” she said. “It would identify them.”

“No.”

“So it looks as though nothing happened.”

Eve glanced at the bag in her hands. Her old instinct would have been to find a moving little moment nobody could object to sharing. Ben removing the slip. Nora's hands loosening around her bag. Any of them could become proof, trimmed into a shape suitable for strangers.

“We don't have to prove it with their faces,” she said.

The coordinator nodded, then admitted she had expected Eve to be better at making people like the result.

“That is sometimes my job,” Eve said. “This time it was making the information clear enough to use.”

“And was it?”

“One person used the contact route. The next week will tell you more than the comments.”

She left without promising to monitor the page all weekend.

Outside, the cold had sharpened. Eve pulled her coat closed and walked toward the clinic, where Tessa had said the final paragraph about seeking professional help was ready for collection. It had been approved as the clinic's general communication, not as an assessment of Pip. Eve had already sent the agreed wording to the coordinator; the paper copy was for her project record.

Tessa passed it over with a spare clip attached.

“Keep the clip,” she said. “We have a drawer full. Nobody knows who orders them.”

“A self-sustaining supply.”

“I wish it happened with printer paper.”

Julian came out as Eve was tucking the page away. His jacket was over one arm. He was leaving, not pausing between appointments.

“You look as if you've argued with your phone,” he said.

“I chose not to. It was a very accomplished argument.”

“Would you like to tell me the version you didn't send?”

She looked toward Tessa, who was answering the ringing desk phone, then stepped outside with him.

“I'd rather not make your break into a consultation about my personality.”

“I'm finished for the day.”

“Actually finished?”

“Yes.” He held up the jacket. “Documentary evidence.”

They walked to the little public garden. The gate was open, and several benches stood empty beneath trees that had begun shedding leaves onto everything useful. Eve brushed one clear with the folded cloth before sitting, then realized she had turned a returned event supply into practical equipment again.

Julian sat beside her, leaving space for her bag.

She showed him the public update, not the private notes. He read the short comment and the longer ones without moving his thumb toward the reply button.

“That one gets to me,” she said, pointing.

“The money?”

“Yes. I know they don't know what the job included. I still want to justify every hour.”

“Would knowing the hours change their mind?”

“I don't know.”

He handed the phone back.

“What did you actually do?”

It was not an invitation to disclose the participants' lives. Eve described the work itself: asking for accounts separately, keeping accusations out of the shared summary, giving people room to say an arrangement cost them something, and refusing to call a first week's trial a cure.

She found she could explain it without quoting anybody's most vulnerable sentence.

Julian listened. A child passed with a scooter, followed by an adult carrying the helmet the child apparently considered optional until motion began. They stopped at the gate to negotiate it. Eve watched until the helmet was on, then looked back.

“The dog still barks,” she said.

“You weren't hired to treat the dog.”

“I know. That's what makes the comment so annoying. It takes the one thing I wasn't offering and announces I failed at it.”

Julian nodded.

“I'm glad you didn't make the owner into a sad story to get people on her side.”

Eve looked at him.

“That isn't as easy as it sounds,” he continued. “You could have made something people praised. You left space for a complaint to remain a complaint.”

The warmth in her face had nothing to do with the weather.

“Oh, well, Dumpling—”

She stopped.

Julian waited.

Dumpling had hidden beneath the bed. He had supplied no copy, chaired no conversation, and paid none of the time she had spent preparing. She loved him. None of those facts required giving him this particular piece of credit.

“Thank you,” she said.

“You're welcome.”

“I nearly moved the compliment to the cat.”

“I noticed.”

“He did very little.”

“Then perhaps you can keep it.”

She laughed, a small embarrassed sound, and put the phone in her bag.

For a while they sat without improving anything. A leaf landed on Julian's knee. He lifted it by the stalk, inspected the irregular edge, and set it beside the bench rather than grinding it beneath his shoe.

Eve watched his hand and remembered that they had lunch tomorrow.

“Is the noodle place still happening?” she asked.

“Yes. Unless you've changed your mind.”

“I haven't.”

“Then twelve thirty.”

“I'm looking forward to it.”

“So am I.”

He said it as simply as he had admitted walking the wrong way. Eve was beginning to understand that directness could make her more nervous than ambiguity, because she couldn't spend the whole afternoon disguising what she had heard.

A shout came from the path behind them.

“That's the woman who did the dog notice.”

Eve turned. A man she recognized from the building stood with another resident. He was not close enough for a private conversation, and he appeared to prefer it that way.

“Did you get paid for that?” he asked.

“Yes,” Eve said.

“Nice work if you can get it.”

Julian shifted beside her. Eve put her own hand on her bag, steadying it rather than reaching for him.

“If you have a question about the committee's spending, ask the committee,” she said. “The notice explains where to send concerns about noise.”

“I've already read your notice.”

“Then you have the contact.”

The other resident tugged lightly at his sleeve. They walked on, still talking. Eve could hear the word *consultant* said as though it were an unusually poor kind of furniture.

Her pulse had sped up. She hated that such a small exchange could take the warmth out of a pleasant bench.

Julian didn't follow them. He didn't announce his profession or turn the conversation into a defense conducted over her head.

“Do you want to stay?” he asked.

She considered it. Leaving would not mean the man had won. Staying would not make her brave by itself.

“For five minutes,” she said. “I was enjoying this before he arrived.”

“All right.”

They stayed. The garden did not become beautiful enough to compensate for the insult. A bus hissed beyond the railings, and someone dropped a bottle into a recycling bin with an unnecessarily dramatic crash.

Julian asked whether she had finished the seaside puzzle.

“No. The man is still missing his head.”

“Have you checked the horse pieces?”

“That would be a disturbing resolution.”

She laughed, and this time the sound came more easily.

When they stood, he handed her the cloth she had folded beside herself. She thanked him for listening. He said he had liked hearing about her work, which was a different thing from assuring her everyone would eventually appreciate it.

Before they left the garden, Eve stopped at the water tap to rinse a smear of soil from her fingers. The handle resisted. Julian waited while she tried again, then asked if she wanted him to have a go.

“Please.”

He turned it. Nothing happened.

“Excellent,” she said. “Now it's a shared defeat.”

A small sign beneath the basin announced that the supply was turned off for maintenance. They had both been standing in front of it.

Eve took a tissue from her bag. Julian read the sign once more, with the offended attention of a man whose useful hands had been beaten by a perfectly ordinary sentence.

At the corner they parted. Tomorrow was already arranged.

Eve went home, washed the cloth, and found Dumpling occupying the exact square of sunlight where she intended to put the drying rack.

She moved the rack.

Then she opened the project folder, filed the approved paragraph, and marked the agreed work complete. She didn't reread the comments before closing it.
