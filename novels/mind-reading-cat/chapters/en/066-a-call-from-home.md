# Chapter 066 — A Call from Home

Her mother called while Eve was trying to remove a price sticker from the bottom of a bowl.

The sticker had survived two washes and several changes of approach. Dumpling sat on a chair, watching the damp paper come away in pieces, apparently fascinated by human incompetence.

“Have I caught you at work?” her mother asked.

“Yes,” Eve said, then looked at the bowl. “Not this exact second.”

“I never know now.”

Eve put the bowl down.

Friday morning light fell across the table. On the laptop, Claire's revised cut waited beside a short list of outstanding checks. Eve had planned to review it after breakfast. Instead she had remembered the new bowl, which had led to the sticker, which had led to her mother catching her apparently unemployed over a sink.

“How are you?” Eve asked.

“Fine. The kitchen tap is doing that thing again.”

“What thing?”

“The thing I described last time.”

This was not useful information, but it was familiar. Eve leaned against the counter and asked whether the repair appointment had happened. It hadn't. The man had called to change the day, and her mother had been out when he called again.

“Would you like me to find the number?”

“I have the number. I need a person who arrives when he says he will.”

“I don't keep those in a drawer.”

“You used to be better at arranging things.”

Eve closed her eyes briefly. Her mother was talking about the tap. Probably.

Then she said, “Have you heard from any of the companies?”

“No.”

“Are you still sending applications?”

“I'm doing paid projects at the moment.”

“The cat things.”

“One of them is about people getting ready for an appointment.”

“With their cats.”

“Some of them have dogs.”

There was a pause that made the distinction feel childish.

“I thought this was something you were doing while you looked,” her mother said.

“It was. Now I'm seeing whether I can keep doing it.”

“For how long?”

Eve looked at the laptop. Her trial had a review point; she had accounts, deadlines, and costs she couldn't wish away. She could explain all of it. She also knew that a list of invoices would sound suspiciously like a person defending a collection of painted stones.

“I haven't decided forever,” she said. “I'm not doing nothing.”

“I didn't say you were.”

“No.”

Her mother sighed. Eve could hear a cupboard shutting at the other end.

“I just want you to have something dependable.”

“So do I.”

“Then come for a meal tomorrow. We haven't sat down properly in ages.”

Eve glanced at the calendar on the wall. Saturday afternoon was empty. She had protected it after a crowded week, though an empty square still sometimes felt like evidence that she ought to be doing more.

“What time?”

“Two. I'll make the baked rice you like.”

“With the burnt corners?”

“They're not burnt.”

“They're my favorite part.”

“Then don't complain.”

Eve smiled. There was her mother again, recognizable behind the concern. She agreed to come and offered to bring fruit.

“Something good,” her mother said. “Not the cheapest pears because you feel sorry for them.”

“I don't form attachments to pears.”

“You bought a whole bag with bruises.”

“They were perfectly usable.”

“So were the pears without bruises.”

After the call, Eve returned to the bowl. Dumpling had settled into a loaf, no longer interested in the sticker. She dried the base, put it aside to finish later, and opened the film.

Mabel's pause had survived. The new opening did not promise a simple experience to people whose journeys were anything but. Eve watched the full cut, checked the example page, and sent Claire the two small observations remaining in her portion. Then she updated the time spent and closed the file.

The work had taken forty minutes. No one had applauded. It was still work.

She considered sending her mother a screenshot of the completed review. Then she imagined the picture arriving without context, a tiny video window surrounded by notes nobody had asked to see. Her mother would either congratulate her too brightly or ask how much it paid.

Eve put the phone down.

At lunch she took an actual lunch break. This seemed an unnecessarily elaborate achievement until she remembered her old office, where she had eaten noodles beside a keyboard and called the whole arrangement stability.

Julian sent a photograph of the repaired cupboard handle. There was no patient or private paperwork in the frame, only a small piece of ordinary hardware looking pleased with itself.

*It passed inspection,* he wrote.

Eve smiled at the screen.

*By whom?*

*People who would prefer not to discuss it again.*

She typed that she would miss his principal conversational subject, then deleted nothing and sent it.

The warmth of Thursday evening returned without the sharp panic she had expected. She had asked him whether he was seeing anyone. She had not asked him to fill the newly opened place in her life before she could visit her mother safely.

Tomorrow could be a meal.

That afternoon, she walked to the fruit shop. A woman ahead of her pressed every peach as if testing furniture. Eve chose a small box that looked good without requiring a rescue operation, paid, and resisted adding something expensive merely because her mother might be worried about her finances.

At home, she set out a clean blouse and her gray scarf. Dumpling climbed onto the bed before she could fold the scarf. She moved it to the wardrobe shelf rather than negotiating its purpose with him.

“You are not invited,” she said. “There will be onions and opinions.”

He yawned.

On Saturday morning, Eve finished a short client correction that had arrived within the agreed review window. It concerned the wrong opening hour in a caption, which was not a subject on which artistic confidence could help. She corrected it, checked the source again, and sent the revised file before packing her bag.

The train journey to her mother's neighborhood took just under an hour. Eve had made it often enough to know which carriage usually put her nearest the exit, yet she still chose the wrong end and spent the final minute pretending she had intended the walk.

A young child across the aisle was eating a pastry with profound concentration. His father held the wrapper beneath it, failing to catch most of the crumbs. Eve watched for a moment, then turned back to the window.

She could remember being brought home on this route when she was small, falling asleep against her mother's arm. Her mother had carried the shopping and her coat and somehow still possessed a hand with which to straighten Eve's collar.

Worry had been useful then. Eve had needed someone to remember the train ticket, the cold weather, and whether she had eaten anything other than bread.

She did not know how to tell her mother that she could still want the arm without wanting every journey chosen for her.

At the station, she bought a loaf her mother liked and shifted the fruit box to her other hand. The short walk from the bus stop looked nearly unchanged. A shop had become a pharmacy. Someone had painted a formerly blue gate a color that seemed to have given up halfway toward green.

Her mother opened the door before Eve rang twice.

“You're early.”

“Seven minutes.”

“I haven't finished the table.”

“I can help.”

“No, no. Come in. Your hair's caught in your scarf.”

She reached up automatically. Eve let her free the strand.

The house smelled of baked rice and roasted vegetables. On the dining table stood three glasses, three folded napkins, and the good serving dish, the one her mother ordinarily described as too inconvenient to wash.

Eve stopped taking off her coat.

“Who else is coming?”

“Just someone for lunch.”

“For the family meal?”

“Yes. Owen. You remember my friend mentioning her son?”

Eve remembered several friends mentioning several sons over the years. She could not remember agreeing to become interested in one over baked rice.

Her mother took the fruit box.

“These are lovely.”

“Mom.”

“What?”

“Who is Owen?”

Before her mother answered, a man emerged from the kitchen carrying a jug of water. He was about Eve's age, perhaps a little older, with a tidy dark sweater and the uncomfortable expression of someone discovering that he had walked into the wrong half of a conversation.

“Hello,” he said. “I'm Owen.”

Eve looked at the jug. It contained slices of lemon. Somebody had made an effort.

“Eve.”

“I gathered.” He lifted the jug slightly. “Where would you like this?”

“The table,” her mother said. “And sit down, both of you. I'll check the oven.”

Eve remained beside the coat hooks.

“Could I speak to you first?” she asked.

Her mother's smile tightened, but she followed Eve into the kitchen. The oven fan made a steady, domestic noise that felt entirely indifferent to the occasion.

“Is this an introduction?” Eve asked quietly.

“It's lunch.”

“Did you invite him because you thought we might date?”

“I thought you might get along. Is that so terrible?”

“You could have told me.”

“If I'd said anything, you would have invented work.”

The sentence hurt because it contained a small truth and used it to excuse a larger choice. Eve had avoided some visits. She had also come today.

“I didn't invent work. I arranged to be here.”

Her mother took an oven glove from the hook. “He's a nice person. You don't have to marry him before dessert.”

“Please don't make it sound as if I'm being unreasonable for wanting to know.”

The glove stayed halfway onto her mother's hand.

For a moment Eve wanted to leave. The return train was familiar; the cat was at home; nobody there would ask whether a nice man with a jug was a problem she had created.

Then she looked through the doorway. Owen had sat at the end of the table, leaving both other places free. He was reading the label on the bottle of water with more attention than the information required.

She did not want to punish him for a conversation he hadn't heard.

“I'll stay for the meal,” Eve said. “But don't tell him I've agreed to anything else.”

“I haven't.”

“And next time, ask me.”

Her mother looked away toward the oven. “The rice will be dry.”

It was not an answer. Eve knew the difference.

She went back to the dining room and hung her gray scarf with her coat. Owen stood halfway, then sat again when she smiled at his uncertainty.

“I wasn't told the whole plan,” she said.

His face changed. “I wondered.”

“Have you been told I'm unusually enthusiastic?”

“Only that you like the corner pieces.”

“That part is accurate.”

He laughed, relieved or simply amused; she could not know which. Eve sat down at the place nearest the kitchen, where she could help pass the hot dish without surrendering her coat or her afternoon to anyone else's expectations.

Her mother brought in the rice.

For now, Eve reached for the serving spoon.
