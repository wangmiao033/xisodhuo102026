# Chapter 232 — The Moves He Lived Through

Nora's first note was written on the back of a shopping list.

Milk. Washing powder. A quantity of apples apparently disputed and crossed out twice.

Beneath them, in smaller writing, were a date and the words orange and white, short stay.

Eve looked at the line until the rest of the list seemed almost offensive. A life-changing piece of information had been left below laundry supplies.

Then she remembered the first frightened calculations she had made on whatever paper happened to be beside her. Important things did not always arrive with their own stationery.

“That isn't enough on its own,” Nora said. “I know. There's a message that goes with it.”

She brought up the exchange on her phone, covering unrelated names before setting it where Eve could read. Someone had asked whether the agreed short stay could begin on the stated day. Nora's reply confirmed it. A later message reported that the cat had arrived and was eating.

The photograph on the shelf had been sent during that exchange.

Eve compared the date with her existing notes. It fell after one of Daniel's feeding photographs and before the original rescue. The dates did not account for every day between them, but they placed one actual stay inside the gap.

“So you brought him home from the room?” she asked.

“Yes. I was already helping with some of the care there. The room wasn't available for the whole stretch people needed. I could offer those days at home.”

Nora pointed to her reply rather than asking Eve to trust the confidence of the recollection.

“I didn't offer indefinitely. I had commitments after that.”

There was a trace of defensiveness in the last sentence. Eve recognized it because she had used the same tone about money, work, and being unavailable to save every inconvenient afternoon.

“You don't have to apologize for having an end date,” she said.

“I still think about it.”

“That isn't quite the same thing.”

Nora looked at her, then nodded.

Beneath the table Dumpling shifted, brushing Eve's shoe. She moved her foot slightly to give him room and returned to the dates.

The next supported point was his departure from Nora's flat. A photograph showed him beside the same basket, with a packed carrier nearby. The picture itself established no journey. A message later that day, sent by the person collecting him, said they had reached the next temporary room with him.

Nora had kept her own reply: thank you, please keep the cloth with him.

“The same blue-door room?” Eve asked.

“No. Another space they were borrowing. I didn't take him there myself.”

“Did you see it later?”

“Once, while delivering food. I saw him there. That visit wasn't the same day he arrived.”

The distinction mattered. Eve wrote it down, then asked whether Nora minded her keeping the relevant dates and descriptions with the earlier search notes. Nora agreed to those parts, not the unrelated conversations surrounding them.

There was no photograph of that delivery. Nora could place it between two work appointments in her diary, but she could not honestly choose the exact day now.

They left it as a recollection with a limited interval.

Julian moved the cups farther from the papers. He did not reach for the pen or turn Nora's words into a neater account. Eve appreciated having one less person trying to improve the shape of the story.

She laid three blank scraps beside each other: the blue-door room, Nora's flat, the next borrowed space.

It was a small, unlovely map.

“Did he settle?” she asked.

“At mine, he ate and used the tray. At first he slept where he could see the door. Later he got on the chair.”

Nora smiled faintly at the second photograph.

“I thought that meant we'd managed well enough. It didn't tell me how he felt about every move.”

Eve looked toward the cat beneath her own table. She had spent evenings trying to decide whether a bed, a chair, or a box could prove that he felt safe. The years between them had not made the question easier.

“And when that room ended?”

Nora's fingers tightened around the edge of her folder.

“That's the part I can't tell you from being there.”

She showed a later message. The borrowed arrangement had ended. Several people were coordinating supplies and temporary household stays. Nora had been asked whether she could take the orange-and-white cat again. She had replied that she could not that week, and had offered to bring food later instead.

Eve felt her own disappointment at the refusal before she could stop it.

Nora must have seen something in her face, because she looked down.

“I know.”

“No,” Eve said. “You don't know what I was about to do in my head.”

Nora waited.

“I was about to make the whole thing turn on one person saying yes.”

The admission was unpleasant. It was also necessary if Eve wanted to keep listening to the woman rather than interrogate the person she might have been.

“There were supposed to be other arrangements,” Nora said.

“What do the messages actually say?”

She turned the phone back toward the table.

A few messages later, someone was asking whether the cat had arrived at the intended temporary home. The answer said no. Another person had understood that somebody else was collecting him. The exchange became confused about which person had which carrier and whether the last room had already been checked.

The first clear thing after that confusion was a search request.

Orange and white. The same description, now attached to one of the photographs Eve had just seen.

Nora did not claim to know which door had opened or who had held it. She had not been at the transfer. The messages showed that people could not locate him while sorting out the handover, and that they began looking. They did not show how he had got out, or whether he had been outside before the absence was noticed.

Eve read the short search request again.

The words were practical. Check nearby hiding places. Contact the group if seen. A picture where the face could be recognized.

Somebody had been looking.

She had not expected that detail to hurt so much.

“I went over after work,” Nora said. “I looked along the side streets with another volunteer. We didn't find him. I can't tell you that nobody kept looking after I stopped. I can only tell you which evenings I went.”

“Did you know he was injured?”

“No.”

“Or where he went?”

“No.”

There was nothing she could soften in either answer. The transfer messages preceded Eve's rescue, but they did not draw a line through the intervening streets. They did not explain the rain, the injured foreleg, or how long he had been managing alone.

Eve's pen rested on the page without moving.

She had wanted the missing section filled. Now she could see that part of it contained people who had also been asking where the cat was.

“That doesn't make it all right,” she said.

“No.” Nora's voice was quiet. “It doesn't.”

Julian's hand rested beside Eve's chair. She reached down and held it for a moment, without moving the papers out of view.

There had been a failure. That much could be said without inventing an abuser to receive every feeling the photographs stirred up. People had made arrangements that did not safely carry through. The fact that some had cared did not undo the consequence, and the consequence did not tell Eve exactly what each person had done.

She separated the supported events from the questions on a fresh sheet.

Nora watched her make the distinction.

“I thought you'd be angry with me.”

“I feel angry about parts of it.”

“Yes.”

“And sad. And relieved you kept these. None of those feelings is very good at putting dates in order.”

Nora gave a small, strained laugh.

Eve looked at the shopping list again. Apples. Washing powder. A cat arriving for a few days because somebody had found room to help.

“Was there a bed he took with him?”

“A cloth I sent. I don't know whether it stayed through the next move. I can't say every belonging went missing just because I didn't see it again.”

“All right.”

“He wasn't unhappy every minute I knew him,” Nora said, then stopped. “That sounds as if I'm trying to argue.”

“It doesn't have to be an argument.”

Eve took the photograph of him near the basket. A badly framed picture. A cat sleeping. One small piece of comfort, which did not absolve anyone or erase another small piece of fear.

“We can keep that too,” she said.

Nora's shoulders lowered.

They checked the main dates once more against the records Eve already had. Ada's room-use papers still established only the premises and their changing availability. Daniel's feeding photographs still showed his visits, not continuous residence. Nora's evidence added specific care and movement, and the confused transfer followed by searching. None of it identified his first home or explained his unusual sensitivity.

Eve refused the attractive sentence forming in her mind: now she understood why he gathered socks, sat in boxes, watched every departure.

She understood a little more about what might have made changes difficult. She did not understand every cat habit he possessed.

The clock showed they had used a little more than half the visit. Nora gathered the unrelated papers back into the folder while Eve saved the copies they had agreed she could keep. She checked each image was legible and returned the originals promptly, without treating their importance to her as a reason to hold them longer.

“Thank you for bringing the difficult part,” she said.

“I wasn't sure how to leave it out honestly.”

“I'm glad you didn't.”

Under the table, Dumpling got up. He stretched, walked past Nora's chair, and headed toward the kitchen with the plain assurance of someone who knew where the next useful event should happen.

Eve watched him go.

For months she had pictured a series of doors shutting. The notes now showed openings too: temporary offers, shared food, attempts that had not been enough. Neither picture could replace the cat walking through the home in front of her.

She put the pen down.

“It's nearly time for his small meal,” she said. “Would you like to stay for that before you go?”

Nora closed the folder and slipped it back into her bag. One photograph remained beside Eve's hand, the cat sleeping near the basket. Eve returned it too. She had the permitted copy; Nora could keep the print she had carried through all the years in which neither of them knew the other existed.

Nora looked at the clock, then at Eve.

“If it fits what you normally do.”

“It does.”

Eve stood, leaving the scraps of the unfinished map on the table. They could remain unfinished for a little while. In the kitchen, Dumpling waited beside the place where his own bowl belonged.
