# Chapter 053 — Not on My Way

At the far side of the square, Julian turned left.

Eve followed for half a dozen steps before she noticed the sign above the crossing. The station was to the right. So was the road he had once described while explaining why a particular bakery was not remotely convenient for him, however good its bread might be.

Her building lay in the opposite direction.

She adjusted the strap holding the three mats and waited for him to mention an errand. Perhaps there was another station. Perhaps he needed something from a shop that sold only batteries and birthday cards after dark. The city had accommodated stranger arrangements.

They passed the closed flower stall, then a narrow furniture shop where a lamp illuminated an armchair nobody could sit in until Monday.

“That's an ambitious chair,” she said.

It had a curved back and very short legs, as if the maker had run out of enthusiasm near the floor.

“You'd need help getting out.”

“Maybe that's the business. They sell you the chair, then charge for retrieval.”

Julian stopped to examine the price card. “Retrieval should be included at that price.”

Eve smiled at his reflection in the glass. Beside it stood her own, carrying a bag large enough for a weekend away. Their reflected shoulders were almost level because the pavement sloped beneath his feet.

Then they began walking again, still away from his route home.

“You know the station is back there?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Just checking. You had strong feelings about the man on the wrong train.”

“He had a much larger suitcase.”

He moved aside for a person carrying a folded bicycle. Eve waited beside the curb, and when he rejoined her she found herself with a choice she could not turn into a practical question forever.

“Is this actually on your way?”

Julian looked toward the next crossing. A delivery van was waiting at the lights, its indicator clicking loudly enough to reach them through an open window.

“No,” he said.

The answer was so unadorned that she almost supplied an excuse for him.

He could have said he needed the walk. He could have said he wanted to make sure she got home. She could have thanked him for being thoughtful and stored the evening among things done by considerate people for reasons unrelated to her.

Instead she asked, “Then why this way?”

He looked at her.

“I wanted to keep talking to you.”

The van turned. A bus rattled over the crossing behind it. The street continued to do everything streets ordinarily did, which seemed unreasonable to Eve.

She had a joke ready. Something about charging by the hour, or having enough embarrassing stories to justify a longer route. It rose easily, a familiar little bridge away from the place where she was standing.

She didn't use it.

“I wanted that too,” she said.

Julian's expression changed. Not dramatically. The tightness beside his mouth eased, and he looked down for a moment before meeting her eyes again.

“Good.”

The signal changed. They crossed together.

On the other side, Eve became absurdly aware of her hands. One held the bag strap. The other had been making occasional explanatory gestures all evening and now seemed to require instructions. She put it in her pocket, discovered a crumpled receipt, and left it there.

She did not have to reach for him. He had said he wanted to talk. She could believe that sentence at its actual size.

“What were you going to say before we stopped?” she asked.

“I was going to defend the chair.”

“After insulting its legs?”

“I thought it might be comfortable.”

“You're allowed to like an ugly chair.”

“Thank you.”

“I'm not allowed to help carry it up your stairs.”

“That does affect the purchase.”

The laughter returned, but it no longer had the job of hiding what had just happened.

They took the street beside the little public garden. The gates were locked, though a path through the neighboring square remained open. Eve could see the pale rectangles of plant labels beyond the railings. In daylight they announced Latin names she usually forgot before reaching the next bed.

Julian slowed when her bag slipped again.

“Would you like me to carry that for a while?”

She considered refusing automatically. Her shoulder did ache. The bag was not a test anyone else knew she was taking.

“Yes, please.”

He took it by the handles rather than pulling it over his head, leaving his own small bag on his shoulder. One mat immediately tried to escape. Eve pushed it back beneath the strap while he held still.

“Flexible,” he said.

“Exactly.”

Without the bag, her arms felt strangely light.

They talked about the places where they liked to stop without buying anything: the public garden, a secondhand shop with a continually changing window, the sheltered step outside the library where Eve sometimes ate lunch. Julian described a route along a canal that she had never taken. There was a bend where the towpath widened and people fished, though he had never seen anyone catch anything.

“Perhaps they don't tell you,” she said.

“Possible.”

“You do look as if you might ask follow-up questions.”

“About fish?”

“About whether they've completed the appropriate forms.”

He gave her a look she had become very fond of provoking: patient, offended, and unable to remain entirely serious.

At the next corner, a small grocery was closing. The shopkeeper was bringing in crates of vegetables, stopping every few steps to speak to a customer who appeared determined to extend a conversation through the entire task.

Eve stepped around the last crate and remembered that she needed bread.

“Can we stop for a minute? Unless they've finished.”

The shopkeeper heard and waved her inside. Julian waited beside the door while she found a loaf, then followed her as far as a display of apples.

“Those are good,” she said.

He picked up two.

At the counter, she discovered the loaf was sliced so thickly that one piece would occupy half her toaster. She bought it anyway. The shopkeeper added a small paper bag around Julian's apples, wished them a good evening, and returned to the conversation waiting outside.

No one asked whether they were together. No stranger supplied a convenient answer to the question Eve had not yet decided to ask.

She was unexpectedly grateful.

Her building appeared at the end of the next road. The upper windows glowed in uneven rows. Someone had left a small plant on the inside sill of the entrance, where it leaned steadily toward the streetlight.

Eve slowed.

Julian stopped beside the door and offered her the mat bag.

“Thank you,” she said, taking it.

“For the transport or the criticism of chairs?”

“Both. The service has been comprehensive.”

He shifted the flask inside his own bag. One of the cups tapped against its metal side.

“Would you like some water before you go?” she asked. “I can rinse those too. The tea's been sitting in them.”

He glanced at the entrance, then back at her.

“A glass of water would be good.”

She opened the door and led him upstairs. There was nothing unprecedented about his coming in. He had sat in her kitchen before. Yet the route from the pavement to her door felt different after hearing him say that he wanted their conversation to last longer.

Dumpling appeared as soon as she opened the flat, stretching so elaborately that she wondered whether he had hurried from sleep and was trying to disguise it.

Eve set the bags down clear of the doorway. Julian waited while she closed the door before lowering his hand for the cat to investigate.

“Your evening?” he asked Dumpling.

The cat sniffed his fingers, then rubbed his face against the back of his hand.

“Longer film,” Eve said. “Better seating.”

She hung up her jacket. Julian took off his scarf in the warmer room, folding it loosely over a chair back, then unzipped his jacket without removing it.

In the kitchen, Eve filled two glasses and rinsed the cups. A blade of grass had somehow found its way into the smaller one. She held it up between finger and thumb.

“An authentic outdoor experience.”

“I thought the flavor was unusually complex.”

He dried the cups with the towel she offered and packed them with the flask. The simple shared task kept her from searching for something impressive to offer. She had water. He had wanted water. That was enough for these few minutes.

Dumpling jumped onto the empty chair and pressed his nose into the scarf.

“No tasting,” Eve told him.

He looked at her with mild affront and sat instead.

Julian leaned against the counter, leaving the walkway clear. Eve showed him the loaf, holding one thick slice up through the bag.

“This may require architectural approval.”

“Toast one side at a time.”

“That's how they get you. First the chair retrieval fee, then the extended breakfast.”

He laughed into his glass.

For a little while they talked about breakfast, a subject she would once have considered too small to keep anyone interested. Julian liked an apple with something substantial rather than as a substitute for food. Eve admitted that she sometimes ate toast standing beside the toaster because taking it to a plate felt like adding unnecessary bureaucracy.

“There's a chair right there,” he said.

“Currently supervised.”

Dumpling had tucked his front paws beneath himself. A blue edge of scarf remained visible under his chest.

Julian checked the time and straightened.

“I should go.”

“Yes.” Eve put her glass down. “Thank you for walking the wrong way.”

“Deliberately.”

“I noticed.”

At the door, he took the bag containing his flask and apples. Eve held the cat back with her foot placed across the opening, not touching him, while Julian stepped into the hall.

“Good night, Eve.”

“Good night.”

She waited until he had started down the stairs, then shut the door.

Only when she went to move the mats out of the kitchen did she see the scarf. Dumpling had shifted, revealing the dark-blue fold over the chair back.

She could call down. Julian might still be in the entrance. She went to the window, but the angle gave her only the empty pavement beside the building.

Her phone was simpler.

*You left your scarf. It's safe here.*

She sent it before she could add anything about returning it immediately or arranging a convenient handover through the clinic.

Then she took the scarf off the chair and laid it on the side table, beyond the edge of her water glass.

Dumpling followed the movement with his eyes.

Eve looked at him, then at the dark window.

For once, she knew why Julian had come this far. He had told her himself.
