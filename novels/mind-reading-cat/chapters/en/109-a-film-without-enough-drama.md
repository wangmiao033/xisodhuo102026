# Chapter 109 — A Film Without Enough Drama

The most expensive shot in Eve's new film was a chair.

It had required moving the camera three times, waiting for a delivery to finish, and discovering that the light reflected off a laminated notice directly into the lens. In the final version it remained on screen for just long enough to show where a visitor could sit without blocking the passage.

Nobody would share it because they had fallen in love with the chair.

Eve watched the complete cut at her kitchen table. The film explained arriving, communicating changes, asking about costs, and understanding that reception passed information to the clinical team rather than making clinical decisions. It had clear captions, a transcript, and no secretly borrowed distress.

It also lacked the moment when music rose and somebody's face made the viewer feel better about the world.

She tried a different opening.

Then she tried the original opening again.

Dumpling, asleep beside a cushion on the sofa, supplied neither a miraculous expression nor a usable excuse for the delay. The file was due for final approval by four. It was now three twenty.

Eve sent the version that answered the questions.

Dr. Clark approved it after the last wording check. Tessa confirmed her own section, including the name and role in the credits. Julian's final correction had already been incorporated. The clinic would publish the film through its own account and use the approved link in appropriate visitor information. Eve's fee covered the agreed deliveries, not a guarantee of views.

She knew all of this. She had put most of it in writing herself.

At five, she still checked the number beneath the post.

At five twelve, she checked again.

The number had risen by an amount so small that she briefly suspected she was counting her own anxiety.

She put the phone in a drawer and made rice.

The rice took longer than expected because she had forgotten to turn on the cooker. This was not a failure that needed either a business mentor or a mind-reading cat. She pressed the button and ate a piece of toast while she waited.

Julian called after his shift, at the time they had arranged.

“Is the film out?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“Are you pleased with it?”

“That question is apparently unrelated to how many people have watched it.”

“It can be.”

She sat on the kitchen chair with the warm toast in her hand. “I think it does what we agreed. I don't think anybody will send it to a friend with seven exclamation marks.”

“I sent it to a colleague with one full stop.”

“A glowing endorsement.”

“He asked for the link.”

That made her smile. Then she admitted what bothered her. The film she had made earlier, the one that centered Julian and moved quickly past reception, would probably have held more casual attention. His face had already proved capable of doing that. The new version asked people to care about something they might not need until a difficult day.

“Perhaps they won't watch until they need it,” he said.

“Yes. Which is an excellent practical point and deeply disappointing to the part of me refreshing a screen.”

“I don't have expertise in that part.”

“Lucky you.”

They talked about his dinner and the book she had found. He did not offer to post a personal appeal on her behalf or turn their relationship into a way to rescue the statistics. When they ended the call, the rice was ready.

The next morning, Eve checked the comments once during the time she had set aside for the project. One person said the opening was too slow. Another wanted to know whether the clinic could provide an exact estimate of waiting time before arrival. A third thanked the account for including captions because she often watched with sound off.

Eve passed the service question to the clinic through the agreed channel. She did not answer it as Julian's girlfriend or invent a promise because the comment was public.

The criticism about pace went into her notes. It wasn't an attack on the worth of reception, and defending it that way would only make the commenter responsible for an argument they hadn't made.

She watched the first twenty seconds again. The pause before the main question was longer than necessary.

The content could be useful and still be edited better.

Within the remaining revision scope, she offered a tighter opening to Dr. Clark without changing the approved information. He agreed to review it. Tessa asked to make sure her first sentence wasn't cut in half this time.

“It won't be,” Eve promised, then checked rather than trusting the promise.

A week later, she returned for the scheduled review of how the film was being used. She brought her report and left the camera behind.

At reception, a visitor approached with a folder open to a page she didn't show Eve. The woman asked Tessa where she should raise a question about an estimate, then accepted the private arrangement the staff could provide that morning.

Eve looked away before the details began. The useful part wasn't what was in the folder. It was that the visitor knew a place existed for the question without having to announce it to the whole waiting room.

Tessa noticed Eve waiting and indicated the staff-room door.

“I'll join you when my cover arrives.”

Not in a minute. Not a hurried promise she might break to one person or another. An actual condition.

Eve went through and set out the report.

The totals were modest. The film had been viewed, though not widely. Some viewers stopped early. The clinic had sent the link to people for whom the information was relevant, but they had not tracked individuals' behavior or assigned every smoother interaction to a video.

Dr. Clark arrived carrying a cup with a spoon still in it.

“I've heard fewer people say reception should have explained the medical answer,” he said.

“Do you have a count?”

“No. An impression.”

“Then let's call it an impression.”

He accepted the correction, which made it easier for Eve to accept the smallness of her own evidence.

Tessa arrived after the handover and put a sheet of general feedback on the table. It contained no visitor names or case details. Three staff had noted questions people now asked more clearly. Two had noted that the film still didn't solve confusion about what to bring to a first visit.

“We have that information elsewhere,” Dr. Clark said.

“People may not find it from this page,” Eve replied.

They opened the public page together. The link existed beneath several paragraphs, where it could be missed without anyone being careless. Moving it nearer the video was a small change the clinic could make. It didn't require another emotional interview.

Tessa read one of the anonymous comments aloud, with permission for its use in the project review.

“I thought I had to know the right medical words before I called. Now I know I can say what I'm worried about and they'll ask.”

Eve looked at the line for a moment.

It was not an outcome she could convert into a claim that care had improved for everyone. It was one person's account of being less afraid to ask a question. The specificity made it matter more, not less.

Another comment was less comfortable. A visitor had watched the film, understood the possibility of delays, and still found the wait difficult when it happened.

“Knowing isn't the same as enjoying it,” Tessa said.

“No,” Eve agreed. “We shouldn't pretend the film fixes capacity.”

They added that distinction to the internal review. The public explanation could reduce an uncertainty without producing more rooms, more staff, or more hours in a day.

Before closing the meeting, Eve asked them to try the transcript link on a phone. Dr. Clark enlarged the text and immediately lost the heading off the edge of the screen. Tessa tried it on hers and found the same problem. Eve recorded the display issue separately from the wording, then sent the clinic the corrected file format included in her delivery scope. Nobody needed to turn this into a story of exceptional compassion. A person trying to read the information simply needed the words to remain on the screen. They agreed who would check the replacement after upload, so the correction would not end at sending a file.

Afterward, Tessa walked with Eve as far as the corridor.

“The cover arrangement failed on Tuesday,” she said.

Eve felt her small sense of accomplishment tighten.

“What happened?”

“Two people thought the other had taken over. We corrected the handover. Nobody got to say the whole idea didn't work and go back to me guessing.”

Tessa's tone was matter-of-fact. A failure had produced a correction instead of becoming evidence that she should never have asked.

“Do you want that in the report?” Eve asked.

“As a process issue without personal details, yes. It's relevant.”

“Not in the public video.”

“No.”

They parted there. Eve left with less of a success story and a better account of the work.

On the bus, she opened a blank note to draft a case study for her portfolio. The first sentence came too easily: giving a voice to the people behind the scenes.

She deleted it before the bus reached the next stop.

Tessa had possessed a voice before Eve set up a camera. What she had lacked was time, cover, and an agreement about whose job various pieces of work were. A film could be part of explaining the change. It could not award Eve authorship of another person's agency.

She wrote a more ordinary summary of the deliverables, the accessibility corrections, and the small body of feedback. The clinic would have to approve any public case study. For now, it stayed in her private project folder.

That afternoon, a prospective client asked why this film hadn't performed as well as one featuring Dumpling. Eve could have offered a long apology dressed as an analysis.

Instead she asked what the client needed their own viewers to do.

“Book the right service,” the client said. “People keep asking for something we don't offer.”

“Then we should judge a sample partly on whether it makes that clear. Not only on how many people watched for amusement.”

The client asked to see the brief for a smaller project. No contract arrived that day. Eve sent the agreed information and marked it as an inquiry.

At six she closed the laptop. Dumpling had moved into the patch of floor warmed by the last sun. She sat near him with her own tea, without placing a product beside his paws or turning his nap into an explanation of professional fulfillment.

Her phone buzzed once. Tessa had forwarded the confirmed approval of the final deliverables, with a short note: The bigger captions are easier from the far chairs.

Eve saved the approval, then stopped working.

The chair shot was still expensive. She was no longer sure it had been wasted.
