# Chapter 152 — The Welcome Outside

Biscuit inspected the doorway and declined it.

He did not bark. He did not pull toward anything more interesting. He placed his front paws on the last dry patch before the threshold, lowered his nose, and then stepped backward until Mabel stopped beside him.

Eve stood inside holding the chair cushion she had thought would make her look prepared.

“Hello,” she said.

Biscuit looked past her into the room. His lead remained loose in Mabel's hand.

“No,” Mabel told him mildly. “I'm not impressed either.”

Eve glanced behind her.

The long sofa had been collected. The remaining chairs stood comfortably apart, and the temporary welcome table no longer blocked the route. After the access adviser's visit, Eve had a short list of measurements and items to resolve before claiming anything about the venue's accessibility. She had adjusted what she could with removable furniture and kept the unresolved threshold question visible.

It was already a better room than it had been three days earlier.

Biscuit appeared unconvinced by improvements he had not requested.

“Would a mat help?” Eve asked.

“Perhaps. Perhaps he doesn't like the light on the floor. Perhaps he'd rather go somewhere else.”

Mabel looked down at him rather than toward Eve's supply box.

“He's had his usual walk. We can just go home.”

The words struck Eve harder than they should have. She had asked Mabel to come. She had confirmed the slot, brought water, moved the table, and imagined writing her first proper note about a visitor using the space.

If they went home now, the visit would be four minutes long and the note would say that the visitor had never entered.

She began to suggest waiting another minute. Then she noticed Mabel had shifted her weight twice while standing.

“Would you like to sit outside?” Eve asked.

“Have you got outside?”

That was a better question than it sounded.

A shallow covered strip ran along the unit's frontage. Its far end widened into a small recessed porch, away from the entrance and the route to the neighboring building. The landlord had agreed that Eve could test a chair there during this particular visit, provided the passage stayed clear. Eve had treated that permission as a backup for an unlikely problem.

“I have a chair and permission for this morning,” she said.

“Then bring the chair.”

Eve put down the cushion, carried out the sturdy chair with its back and arms, and placed it in the recess. Mabel checked that it stood steadily before sitting. Biscuit followed her without approaching the door again.

“There,” Mabel said. “We've arrived.”

Eve laughed, partly from relief.

“Would he like water?”

“I brought his bowl.”

Mabel reached into her bag and produced the familiar equipment of a person who had left home with her dog many times before Eve had begun drawing welcome points. Eve supplied fresh water. Mabel placed the bowl where it would not be stepped on, then let Biscuit investigate the space at his own pace.

He sniffed the chair leg, turned carefully, and stood beside Mabel's knee.

Eve brought a second chair for herself. It fitted only if she put it at an angle.

“That's your first problem,” Mabel said.

“The angle?”

“The fact you're about to sit in the way of my feet.”

Eve shifted it.

“Second problem. Now you can't get up without asking me to move.”

She shifted it again, leaving more space between them and less resemblance to the welcoming pair she had imagined.

Mabel nodded.

“Now we can be friendly voluntarily.”

They sat.

Across the street, a delivery driver tried to carry three small parcels beneath one arm. Eve watched the top parcel slide, saw him catch it against his chest, and felt grateful not to be the only person whose proposed arrangement had encountered actual objects.

Biscuit lowered himself onto the dry ground beside Mabel. She adjusted her bag so it did not lean against his back.

“I don't want to waste your morning,” Mabel said.

“You aren't.”

“You wanted to see whether we'd fit inside.”

“I did. Now I want to know whether sitting here would be useful.”

Mabel studied her face as if deciding whether this was a polite change of subject.

“For what?”

Eve looked toward the open door. Through it, the table waited with blank feedback sheets and two pencils. She had written questions about arriving, putting things down, and finding a comfortable seat. The answers were currently taking place outdoors without her form.

“For a short conversation. Waiting before a session. Taking a break without having to go all the way home.”

“Then yes. Not in rain coming sideways.”

“No.”

“Not if you call it seating and put one of those tiny iron stools here.”

“I wasn't going to.”

“You might have seen an attractive photograph.”

Eve looked at her.

Mabel smiled.

“News of the sofa reached me through you.”

They talked about the porch. Mabel wanted a seat from which she could see someone coming without sitting directly in the busiest route. She wanted to know whether she could stay ten minutes rather than book an entire activity. She wanted shade when it was hot and an honest warning when the space would be unpleasant, not a photograph taken during the only agreeable hour of the day.

Eve fetched her notebook, closing the door partway behind her so it would not swing into anyone. She did not try to lure Biscuit after her.

When she came back, Mabel was looking at the bare wall beside the entrance.

“You'll put a board there,” she said.

“Probably. Something small.”

“Put it where somebody sitting down can read it.”

Eve drew a little rectangle at a lower position.

“And don't make it pale yellow writing on cream.”

“I feel you're attacking a whole profession now.”

“Only its difficult members.”

Eve drew two chairs. Mabel took the pencil and moved one outward.

“Biscuit needs to lie somewhere that isn't under everyone's boots. Other dogs might not want him close. You can't arrange us all like cups on a shelf.”

“We wouldn't put visiting animals together automatically.”

“Good. Draw fewer cups.”

Eve erased a third chair she had added almost without thinking.

The porch was small. Keeping the entrance and passage open meant it could not become an overflow room whenever she sold too many places inside. It might accommodate one household waiting, or two people having a quiet conversation if the animals and the circumstances suited it. That was less impressive than an outdoor community terrace.

It was also the space she actually had.

She wrote that beside the sketch.

A woman from the center passed carrying a shopping bag and nodded to them. Biscuit lifted his head, watched her continue, and put it down again.

“Would you want to come to a session here?” Eve asked Mabel.

“Which session?”

“I'm making a habit of asking the wrong question.”

“Yes.”

Eve described a possible small exchange about keeping household information easy to find. Mabel thought that might be useful, especially if it did not become an hour of people recommending storage boxes.

“Would you bring him?” Eve asked.

“Probably not. He's content at home for that sort of thing. Today you invited both of us to see the place.”

The distinction was simple. Eve wrote it down anyway.

A venue could welcome a pet without asking it to attend every activity. A person who cared for an animal could still want an afternoon in a chair without the animal beneath it.

“Can I take a picture of the empty porch after you leave?” Eve asked.

“It's not my porch.”

“I mean for the layout notes. No picture of you or him.”

“Then ask whoever owns it.”

Eve smiled. “Already covered for the premises. I was checking you didn't want to help demonstrate.”

“No.”

“All right.”

Mabel turned the notebook around and added a short line beneath the chairs.

“What's that?”

“A place to put a cup.”

“I thought you disliked storage recommendations.”

“I dislike holding my own cup for an hour because someone likes bare surfaces.”

They tried Eve's small folding side table. It fitted beside Mabel's chair, clear of Biscuit, but its legs were easy to catch with a bag. Eve made another note rather than calling it solved.

After twenty minutes, Mabel said they should go.

Eve stood and moved her own chair out of the way. Mabel rose without having to ask for a path. Biscuit stood after her, stretched, and looked briefly toward the doorway.

For a foolish instant Eve hoped he might enter now, granting the morning a satisfying conclusion. He turned toward the pavement instead.

Mabel put away his bowl.

“Will you come back if we try this again?” Eve asked.

“Tell me what you've changed first.”

“I will.”

“And don't improve away the chair.”

Eve promised to keep an actual chair in the plan.

She watched them leave at their own pace. Biscuit stopped to investigate something at the edge of the pavement, and Mabel waited with the patience of someone who had more than once been made to wait herself.

Inside, the blank feedback sheets sat on the table. Eve gathered them for another day.

On the first page of her notebook she wrote that Mabel and Biscuit had used the porch for twenty minutes. The dog had chosen not to enter. The conversation had produced seating positions, visibility requirements, and a question about how outdoor access might work without blocking anyone else's route.

Nothing in the account required an explanation of what Biscuit had felt. She had not needed to know.

The landlord arrived while she was bringing the chairs back in.

“How did it go?”

“Mostly outside.”

She looked at the room, then at the porch.

“Was something wrong?”

“The dog didn't want to come in. The person still had useful things to say.”

Eve showed the landlord the sketch and asked whether she could include a small outdoor seating option in the future proposal. Not furniture left there now, not a promise to customers, just a measured option for discussion.

The landlord said she would need to consider the shared passage and what other occupiers used. Eve wrote that beside the drawing. There would be further questions if the venue arrangement continued.

For now, she put every item back inside and left the porch clear.

Later, over their arranged lunch, Julian asked about the visit. Eve described Biscuit stopping at the door and Mabel's refusal to be arranged like a cup.

“Did he go in eventually?” Julian asked.

“No.”

“Did Mabel get a comfortable chair?”

“Yes.”

“Then she may forgive the sofa.”

Eve passed him the notebook. Between the crossed-out furniture and the measured doorway, Mabel's pencil line for the cup remained dark and definite.

“That,” Eve said, touching the page, “is the first thing a visitor has designed.”
