# I Might Get a Job

The interview invitation arrived while Eve was trying to remove a strip of packing tape from the bottom of her sock.

For three seconds she assumed it was another reminder about an application she had already forgotten. Then she saw the salary range.

She stopped hopping.

Dumpling, who had been watching her from under the table, leaned forward as if the interruption had improved the entertainment.

“Oh,” Eve said.

The word was small enough to fit the room. The number on the screen was not. It would cover rent without requiring her to rearrange the other figures until they resembled a different month. It came with regular hours, paid leave, and the extraordinary possibility of buying groceries without first estimating how long a bag of rice could reasonably be expected to last.

She read the message again, this time from the beginning.

A small content agency. An in-house production role. Her portfolio had been recommended through a former professional contact. They would like to meet her tomorrow afternoon.

Tomorrow afternoon she had planned to edit a short piece about washing donated blankets.

The conflict was so uneven that she almost laughed.

Instead, she opened her calendar and stared at the small blue rectangle labeled LAUNDRY EDIT. She had typed it herself. No one had employed her to do it. Mia had lent her an hour and the use of a drying rack; she had promised to send Mia the finished clip before posting. That was all.

Still, moving the rectangle felt like breaking something.

Dumpling emerged from beneath the table and put one paw on the fallen tape. Eve collected it before it could become a project for his mouth.

“Not a toy. Neither is my sock, although I see why you were confused.”

He looked past her toward the remaining cardboard beside the door.

The furniture return had left two flattened boxes and an unreasonable quantity of doubt. She moved them behind the cupboard, where he could not knock them over, then sat on the floor beside him.

“I might get a job.”

His attention shifted to her knee.

“A normal one. With a chair that belongs to someone else.”

He placed his chin on her trousers.

It was tempting to interpret that as an objection. It was equally tempting to interpret it as permission. Eve scratched the side of his neck, avoiding the place she had spent far too long examining yesterday.

Whatever the old mark meant, it had not supplied an employment policy.

She replied to the invitation before she could ask a cat to choose for her.

---

Sophie answered her call with, “If this concerns a folding table, I have already moved six.”

“It concerns a salary.”

“That sounds lighter. Tell me.”

Eve read the description while Sophie dealt with something that clattered. A distant voice asked where the cable covers had gone. Sophie told the voice exactly where they had gone, then came back.

“It sounds worth an interview,” she said.

“You don't think the account would look ridiculous?”

“The account already contains a cat sitting in a laundry basket. Be specific.”

“If I start it, make a whole thing about working for myself, and then immediately go back to an office.”

“Did you promise anyone you would never use a staff kitchen again?”

“No.”

“Then you remain free to microwave a depressing lunch.”

Eve picked a loose thread from the seam of her trousers. On the other side of the room, Dumpling investigated the space the boxes had occupied.

“I don't want to abandon it just when people have started coming back.”

“An account can have a posting schedule. An employer can have a contract. Neither should be deciding your entire life before you have read the details.”

“That sounds suspiciously sensible for someone standing on a stage.”

“I'm under the stage. Different perspective.”

Eve smiled.

Sophie had to go. Before ending the call, she added, “Wear the blue shirt. Not because it makes you look employable. Because you don't keep touching the collar of that one.”

Eve looked at the shirt hanging behind the door. She had bought it while she still had a reason to distinguish Tuesday clothes from Saturday clothes.

After the call, she moved the editing appointment to the following morning and wrote to Mia. She would send the clip a day later than planned; nothing would go up without Mia seeing it.

Mia replied with a thumbs-up and a photograph of a mountain of clean towels.

Eve had prepared an explanation about opportunity, commitment, and the complicated economics of beginning again. The towels did not require it.

---

She went to the clinic that afternoon to collect a copy of Dumpling's care record for her own folder. The search for a previous owner was still open. Tessa had been clear about that: a neat folder would not turn uncertainty into a conclusion.

The waiting room contained a woman with an empty carrier and a child carefully reading every word on a poster about keeping treats out of reach. Eve waited until Tessa finished a phone call.

“The copy,” Tessa said, reaching for an envelope. “And the dates are on the first page. If you spot anything missing, tell me rather than improving it with a pen.”

“I only improve forms recreationally.”

“That is what concerns me.”

Eve took the envelope. Her interview invitation was open on her phone beneath it. She turned the screen down too late.

“Interview?” Tessa asked.

“Tomorrow.”

“Good luck.”

“It's only a conversation.”

“Most interviews are. Unless content planning has changed.”

Eve laughed, and Julian came through the door behind the desk carrying a stack of clean towels.

“What has changed?” he asked.

“Possibly her employment,” Tessa said. “Not our towel cupboard, despite repeated requests.”

Julian looked at Eve. “You have an interview?”

The question was neutral. It ought to have been easy to answer.

“Yes. A production role. Full-time.”

“Do you want it?”

“I haven't met them yet.”

“That wasn't quite what I asked.”

Eve shifted the envelope between her hands. The waiting-room child had reached the bottom of the poster and begun again, perhaps looking for a loophole involving grandparents.

“I want to know what it would feel like not to do sums before buying washing liquid,” she said.

Julian nodded once. He did not produce an encouraging speech about following her dreams. She was grateful, and then, inconveniently, disappointed by the space where the speech might have gone.

“The account would be slower,” she added.

“It can be slower.”

“And I couldn't come to everything.”

“You don't come to everything now.”

“I know. I mean—some of the filming. The things we've discussed.”

“We can arrange the things that are actually agreed.”

She looked down at the envelope. His response was reasonable enough to irritate her.

“What about Dumpling?”

“What about him?”

“If I work all day.”

“We can talk about a practical care routine once you know the hours. A job isn't an offense you need him to forgive.”

Tessa had turned back to her computer, but one corner of her mouth moved.

Eve rubbed her thumb along the paper seam. “I wasn't asking for forgiveness.”

“No,” Julian said. “You sounded as if you were preparing a statement for the defense.”

That landed close enough to make her quiet.

A door opened down the corridor. Someone called Julian's name. He set the towels in the cupboard, failed to make the last one fit, and removed two again.

“Ask them what a normal week looks like,” he said. “Not their best week. And whether the hours in the description are the hours they expect.”

“That's almost career advice.”

“It's how I would ask about a job.”

“Do you ask questions at interviews, or just stare until they confess?”

“Only if the towel storage is misleading.”

She smiled despite herself.

He went back down the corridor. Eve remained at the desk for an unnecessary second, holding information she had already collected.

She had wanted to tell him she would not simply vanish.

That was the awkward part. Not the cat. Not the people who might or might not notice a missing video. Somewhere between poster revisions and badly recorded explanations, she had begun to care whether this particular person expected to see her again.

She put the envelope in her bag before the thought could demand a name.

---

At home, the blue shirt had a crease down the front. Eve ironed it over a folded towel on the table, after removing Dumpling from the towel twice and then shutting him safely out of the room for the few minutes the iron was hot.

His opinion of the closed door was unfavorable.

When she reopened it, he entered with the solemn haste of an inspector who had been obstructed in his duties. He sniffed the cold table leg and went to drink water.

“No permanent damage,” she told him.

She made three lists on a clean sheet of paper.

Questions for them.

Conditions she needed.

Things she was afraid to ask.

The third list filled first. She transferred two items from it to the first and crossed out the heading.

The job might be a good one. It might not. She could not know from a number, any more than she could know Dumpling's whole past from a line in his fur. Both uncertainties annoyed her. Neither could be corrected by wanting an answer badly enough.

She printed two examples of her work, including a piece that had nothing to do with animals. She had almost left that one out. Then she put it on top.

Her phone lit up with a message from the recruiter confirming the time. Beneath the address was an additional sentence.

We loved the clever cat clips, too. Would be great to discuss that angle in person.

Eve read it twice.

It was not, by itself, a problem. People were allowed to like her cat. She liked him quite a lot herself, even when he deposited a stolen sock against her freshly ironed shirt.

But she added one more question to the page.

What exactly am I being hired to produce?

Dumpling settled against her ankle while she checked the route. He did not sit on the printout or prevent her from setting an alarm. For the first time all afternoon, she let that be merely something a cat had done.

She packed her bag before bed: portfolio, notebook, a pen that was actually hers. The care folder stayed on the shelf. It did not belong at an interview. Neither did a collection of apologies for needing wages. She checked that the pen worked, drawing a small, unnecessary square in the corner of the page.

She would go to the interview tomorrow.

She would come home afterward.

For tonight, those were two decisions she could make without asking anyone to understand the whole future.
