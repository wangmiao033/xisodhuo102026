# Chapter 088 — Three Months of Accounts

Sophie arrived with a bag of bread and a condition.

“I can stay until four. After that, I become a person with somewhere else to be.”

“You're already a person,” Eve said, taking the bag.

“Excellent. Keep that in mind if the spreadsheet starts crying.”

Thursday's table held the laptop, Eve's notebook, and three months of records. She had moved the tea to a separate surface after remembering what Dumpling had done to the receipt. The cat occupied his own cushion, unimpressed by the precautions.

Sophie hung her jacket over the back of a chair. It was the one she had bought during their afternoon out. A small white thread clung to a sleeve.

“Has it survived work?” Eve asked.

“Better than I have. One pocket contains a cable tie I keep forgetting to remove.”

“Useful.”

“Until I put my hand in quickly.”

They ate first. Eve had learned that an offer of tea could turn into an hour of borrowing somebody's attention before remembering lunch. Sophie talked about a booking with a more credible finish time, and Eve asked whether the equipment return had finally been assigned separately.

“It has. To someone who knows it's a task and will be paid for it. Radical developments.”

Only after they cleared the plates did Eve turn the laptop toward the table.

“I don't need you to tell me whether the job is respectable,” she said. “It is.”

“Good.”

“I need to know whether I'm lying to myself about being able to continue this.”

Sophie pulled her chair closer. “Show me what this is.”

Eve began with the totals from paid work. The three months were uneven. One looked almost comfortable until she separated the payment for work finished earlier. Another contained a bill she had issued but not yet been paid.

Sophie pointed to it.

“That's money you're owed, not money in your account.”

“I know.”

“Then why is it wearing the same colour?”

Eve changed the cell.

The total immediately became less cheerful.

They checked each job against its payment record. The quiet task took longer than the dramatic version Eve had imagined, in which a single number would reveal her correct future. She opened an invoice, found the matching receipt, closed both, and moved on. Sophie did not need to see client information unrelated to the calculation; Eve kept the names out of the summary.

One payment had covered an agreed preparation cost as well as her work. Treating the whole transfer as earnings made the month look better than it was. She separated it and watched another small piece of optimism disappear.

“Your expression is making me feel expensive,” Sophie said.

“You're seeing my face discover subtraction.”

“It was always going to come for you.”

Eve laughed, then found the record of Sophie's paid event work. That belonged in costs too, without a private asterisk saying friendship ought to have made it free.

Next came travel, supplies, and the ordinary services she used to deliver files. Nothing was ruinous alone. Together they explained why the gross figure had felt strangely distant from her actual balance.

The equipment purchase she had already postponed remained outside the plan. Her current kit still worked. Wanting a better light did not turn it into an emergency.

Sophie asked how many hours the busiest month had required.

Eve opened her calendar.

“Paid hours or all of them?”

“Do you only get tired during the paid ones?”

She had prepared for that question and still disliked hearing it.

They added preparation, revisions, travel, and the evenings spent making speculative material that no one had commissioned. Eve had counted some of those hours as practice. Some really had been. Others were attempts to make herself impossible to refuse.

The resulting hourly comparison was not flattering.

“This is why the salary looks good,” she said.

“It is good. You don't need to damage it to choose something else.”

“I keep trying.”

“I noticed.”

The kettle clicked off. Eve made fresh tea while Sophie stretched her shoulders. Dumpling lifted his head at the movement and went back to sleep when nobody opened the food cupboard.

At the counter, Eve caught herself rehearsing a speech about freedom. She had spent yesterday unable to move a small appointment because she had fitted three client calls around it. Freedom, apparently, could be booked in fifteen-minute slots and then accidentally double-booked.

She returned to the table without the speech.

“Can we look at the next three months rather than defend the last three?” she asked.

“Yes. What is already agreed?”

Not as much as the optimistic list suggested. Eve removed possible inquiries and the retailer's declined proposal. She kept confirmed work with its actual dates, then separated the later months where she would need new bookings.

The first month was workable at her current living costs. Beyond that, she would be using part of the savings she had deliberately kept. There was enough for a limited trial, but not enough to let every disappointing week become an indefinite extension.

She opened the household page. Rent, food, bills, transport, and Dumpling's ordinary care remained. The reserve she kept for unforeseen needs was not available to make a business graph prettier.

There was also money she had set aside for obligations not yet due. Eve left it separate instead of treating the whole balance as spending money. Where a cost was uncertain, she kept the cautious figure she had already allowed, with a note to check it through the proper channel. Sophie was not being asked to become an accountant because she had brought bread.

One small recurring charge appeared twice under different labels. Eve checked the actual record and removed the duplicate. The correction improved the result, though not enough to rescue any grand scheme. She felt absurdly pleased to find that careful checking could give money back as well as take it away.

Sophie looked at the line for going out.

“You've removed all of it.”

“I can be economical.”

“You can. You also eat meals with people. Does this plan expect you to become a cupboard?”

Eve restored a modest amount.

“And don't put Julian in as the solution,” Sophie said. “Not even as a joke where he buys every dinner.”

“I haven't.”

“Good. I'm checking the invisible cells.”

Eve felt herself smile, then grow serious. She had not entered his money anywhere. She could still imagine leaning toward a comfortable possibility whenever her own numbers looked thin. He had a steady job, a calm manner, and absolutely no obligation to become a column in her forecast.

She put the future months beside the offer.

The job won on predictability. Her independent work won on control over the kinds of small projects she could try, provided she actually stopped giving away the time meant for them. Neither won on every line.

Sophie didn't hurry her.

Eve looked at the row for speculative samples and crossed out the allowance she had made for them next month.

“What if someone asks?” Sophie said.

“I can show finished work. If they need a new sample made for their brief, we discuss a paid test.”

“And if they say no?”

“Then I don't secretly make it anyway.”

“That sounds like the expensive part.”

“It probably will be.”

They went through the week she had sketched. One bounded slot for the practical account posts. Existing paid commitments first. Time for seeking work, counted as work rather than performed late at night because it did not feel legitimate. No new long-term rent, no equipment purchase, no heroic promise to run everything.

At half past three, Sophie checked the time.

“We have thirty minutes. What decision can you actually make today?”

Eve closed the old months and left the forecast open.

“I can afford another three-month trial at this size. Not the version where I keep hoping the account suddenly pays for everything. Paid content work supports it. The small experiments have a limit.”

“What happens at the end?”

“I review what was paid, the hours it took, and what's booked. If I'm using the reserve I said I wouldn't use, I don't wait for a beautiful success story. I look for employed work again.”

“You can look before then too.”

“Yes.”

Saying it did not make the current choice less real. It made the choice less like a cliff she needed to leap from in public.

Eve wrote the review date three months ahead in her calendar, then placed a shorter check halfway through. She did not call either a launch.

“I want to try this,” she said.

Sophie studied her face. “Even with a decent job on the other side?”

“That's why I needed to work it out. I don't want to take the job only because refusing a good thing feels rude.”

“And you don't want to refuse because somebody told you independence should look a certain way.”

“No.”

She was relieved, but not extravagantly happy. The salary still looked attractive. The people had been reasonable. Choosing the trial meant giving up a genuine possibility, not defeating a disguised threat.

Sophie stood at four exactly.

“Send me no spreadsheet after dinner,” she said, putting on her jacket. “Unless it has developed legs.”

“Thank you.”

“You made the records. I annoyed the colours.”

Eve walked her to the door and asked about the weekend booking. They agreed to talk afterward, about Sophie's work rather than the consequences of Eve's decision.

When she returned to the table, Dumpling was sniffing the closed bread bag. Eve moved it into the cupboard and checked that nothing had been left within his reach.

Then she sat alone with the figures.

Without Sophie beside her, the choice felt less protected. That was probably appropriate. Her friend had helped check it, not signed a guarantee.

Eve wrote a reply to the organization thanking them and declining the offer. She kept it brief. No account of Julian, no declaration that ordinary employment would destroy her creativity, no request that they preserve the vacancy while she tested something else.

She saved the reply to read once in the morning, before the agreed Friday deadline.

There was still an afternoon task to finish. She opened the client's file and corrected the two lines she had promised, then sent it at the arranged time. The modest fee remained modest. It also remained real.

Later, she put the accounts summary in the same folder as her work plan. She could find it again when an attractive inquiry made her want to count money that had not arrived.

Julian asked how the day had gone.

*The numbers were less charming than you,* she wrote. *Useful, though. I've decided to keep trying the business for three months, at a smaller size than my imagination prefers. I'll answer the offer tomorrow.*

His reply came after a while.

*Would you like to tell me about it at dinner?*

*Yes. After we've eaten something.*

She shut the laptop and went to wash the cups. The review date would remain on the calendar even on days she did not feel brave. That, more than the relief in her chest, was what made the decision usable.
