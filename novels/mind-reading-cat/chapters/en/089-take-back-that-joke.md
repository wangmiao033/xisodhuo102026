# Chapter 089 — Take Back That Joke

On Friday morning, Eve declined the job before she had time to become a different person about it.

She read the saved message once, removed the word unfortunately, put it back, and finally admitted that a courteous adverb was not the important part. The answer was clear. She thanked the organization for the offer and the time they had given her, said she had decided to continue her current work, and sent it.

Then she stood up to make breakfast.

Nothing happened to the room. Dumpling was still waiting beside his bowl. The light still fell across the corner of the table where she needed to clean up yesterday's crumbs. A decision that had filled three days did not provide its own applause.

She fed him and herself, then opened the ordinary work for the morning.

The manager answered shortly before lunch, thanking her for letting them know and wishing her well. No wounded demand for an explanation. No last-minute increase designed to turn her choice into a game.

Eve saved the correspondence. The opportunity was now something she had actually declined, not a spare future she could keep half open in a browser window.

For a moment she wanted to tell somebody that she felt both pleased and foolish.

She sent Sophie a short message instead of the whole calculation again. Her friend replied with congratulations on answering before the deadline, followed by a photograph of an enormous coil of cable she was currently trying to store.

Eve sent sympathy for the cable. Then she put her phone aside.

She also answered the client who had asked about next month. Now she could give real dates, along with the scope she could manage. The client chose one and asked her to send the usual confirmation. It was a small consequence of having decided: a blank space in the calendar became actual work instead of a hopeful note.

Eve entered it, checked that it did not collide with an existing delivery, and stopped herself from filling the rest of the week just because an empty square looked accusing. She wanted to eat dinner with Julian without secretly finishing a proposal beneath the table.

Dinner was supposed to be simple.

By six, she had managed to make simple involve three bowls, a pan she should have washed earlier, and a packet of rice that did not appear to agree with the space in the cupboard. She was pressing it into place when Julian knocked.

“I'm not beginning with an impressive entrance,” she warned as she opened the door.

“I brought mushrooms. They don't expect one.”

He held up the bag. They had agreed what each would bring, and he had not arrived with enough food to quietly cover her groceries for a week. Eve appreciated the ordinary scale of it.

Dumpling approached him, tail upright, and rubbed against his trouser leg. Julian crouched to greet him before taking off his shoes. Eve left them to it while she moved the unstable rice packet away from the edge.

“I answered,” she said when he came into the kitchen.

“The organization?”

“Yes. They were very decent about it. Annoyingly consistent.”

“Would you rather they'd insulted you?”

“Only enough to improve the story.”

He smiled and washed his hands. She gave him the mushrooms and a board while she dealt with the rice.

For a few minutes, the conversation remained pleasantly small. Julian asked how large she wanted the pieces. Eve said not tiny. He held up one slice for judgment, and she told him she would accept the mushroom in its present form.

The rice packet split when she tried to close it.

Not dramatically. Just enough to scatter a narrow stream across the counter and onto the floor.

Both of them stopped moving.

“That part wasn't in the recipe,” Julian said.

Eve put the packet into a bowl before trying to repair it. She moved Dumpling out of the kitchen area and closed the door while she cleared the spill, checking beneath the cabinet where the grains had rolled. Julian stayed at the board rather than turning two people and a cat into a more ambitious cleanup problem.

By the time she finished, her mood had lost its careful shine.

“I may have chosen a career I'm incapable of funding and a dinner I'm incapable of containing,” she said.

“The dinner seems recoverable.”

“One reassuring assessment out of two.”

He looked up. “Do you want me to answer the other one?”

Eve leaned against the counter with the cloth in her hand.

“Not really. I checked it. It's a limited trial, not a declaration that rent no longer applies to me.”

“Then tell me what you checked.”

She described the distinction between received payments and hoped-for ones, the work she had stopped counting as an unlimited free extra, and the review date. Julian listened while finishing the mushrooms. He asked one question about what would happen if a confirmed payment arrived late.

“I've left room for that. Not infinite room.”

“All right.”

She waited for him to call the plan brave. He put the mushrooms into the bowl she offered and asked where the pan lived.

The absence of ceremony should have pleased her. Instead, a small uncomfortable part of her wanted him to make her choice feel important to him too.

“Well,” she said lightly, “if it all fails, you'll have to take me in.”

The sentence landed in the quiet between one task and the next.

Julian set the board down.

Eve smiled, expecting him to return something equally light. He didn't look angry, but his expression had changed enough for her to feel the smile working too hard.

“I'd help you think about what to do,” he said. “But I don't want that to be how you decide to be with me.”

She looked at the cloth in her hand.

“It was a joke.”

“I know.”

“You don't have to make a housing commitment because I spilled rice.”

“I don't think you were asking me to.”

That left less to argue with than she would have preferred.

He stepped aside so she could reach the sink, not away from her. The distinction was small and she noticed it because she wanted an excuse to feel rejected.

“I can care whether things are difficult for you,” he said. “I can offer help when we know what you need and what I can do. I don't want you to feel that having nowhere else to go would make us more certain.”

Eve rinsed the cloth longer than it needed.

“I don't feel that.”

“Good.”

She turned off the tap.

The answer should have ended it. Instead she heard her own sentence again, stripped of the convenient laugh. You would have to take me in. It assigned him a promise while protecting her from saying what she actually wanted.

She had spent the morning giving an employer a clear answer. Why did affection make plain language feel like walking outside without shoes?

“I'm sorry,” she said. “That came out badly.”

“You can take it back.”

“I do.”

He nodded, then looked toward the stove.

“Should we put the heat on now?”

“Yes.”

They cooked. Eve was grateful that he didn't insist on turning the apology into an immediate examination of her whole character. The vegetables needed attention. The rice stayed in its pan. A practical task allowed the room to become a kitchen again.

When the door opened, Dumpling inspected the clean floor and appeared disappointed by the lack of available disaster. Eve set his ordinary things out of their way and returned to the table.

They ate slowly at first.

She asked about the repaired shelf. Julian said it was still on the wall, which he considered a promising initial result. She asked whether the boat book had survived the redistribution of weight. It had been on the table, safely contributing nothing to the collapse.

The conversation loosened. Eve could laugh without pretending the awkward moment had never happened.

After dinner, Julian washed while she dried. He passed her a plate, and she put it away in the wrong place before realizing what she had done.

“Do you want me to go?” he asked.

She turned so quickly the towel slid from her shoulder.

“No.”

“All right. You seemed distracted.”

“I am. Not because I want you to go.”

She put the plate where it belonged and picked up the towel.

He waited, hands still in the water.

“I was making the joke do something else,” she said. “I wanted to know you wanted me here, in your life. Then I made it sound as if I wanted emergency accommodation.”

The words were embarrassing, but they were accurate enough that she could stop searching for better ones.

Julian dried his hands before answering.

“You can ask about the first thing without pretending you need the second.”

“I know that when somebody else does it.”

“It's often easier then.”

Eve laughed once, not quite comfortably.

She had no reason to believe he would be cruel. That wasn't the fear. The fear was that a serious question could receive an answer she couldn't improve with a change of tone.

On the other side of the room, Dumpling was investigating the space beneath Julian's chair. Eve noticed herself looking that way and turned back before turning the cat into an audience for something he could not decide.

“Can we sit down?” she asked.

They moved to the sofa. Julian left a comfortable space between them. Not a refusal, not a puzzle arranged for her to solve; simply somewhere she could choose to move or not.

She put the folded towel on the arm, realized she had carried it with her, and set it on the table instead.

“Strong opening,” she murmured.

“I liked the prop.”

She smiled despite herself.

For a moment, she almost began with the accounts again. She could explain that she had made the decision independently, that she wasn't expecting him to underwrite anything, that she knew they had separate lives. All true. None the sentence she had called him over to hear.

The accounts were saved. The job had been answered. She did not have to provide a financial certificate before speaking about affection.

“I like you,” she said.

He stayed still, looking at her.

“Not because you're useful when things go wrong. You are sometimes, but that's not what I'm trying to say.” She took a breath. “I like spending time with you when nothing needs fixing. I like the way you tell me a book is taking too long to read. I like that you can make a joke sound like a complaint from a very patient committee.”

His mouth moved at that, almost a smile.

“And I want to keep seeing you,” she said. “Not only because we're both free by accident. I want us to choose it.”

The room did not become silent in a special way. A vehicle passed outside. Dumpling scratched briefly at his own bed before settling. There were two cups still waiting to be washed.

Eve let those ordinary sounds remain.

“I've taken back the joke,” she said. “That part isn't one.”
