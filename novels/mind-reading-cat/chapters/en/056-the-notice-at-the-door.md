# Chapter 056 — The Notice at the Door

The notice had been pinned over a photograph of a missing glove.

Eve stopped beneath the apartment noticeboard with a bag of oranges against her hip. Someone had typed three lines in capitals, printed them on expensive-looking paper, and then undermined the effect by adding a furious sentence in green pen.

The dog in the second-floor corner flat barked every morning. Residents deserved peace. The owner should do something.

Below that, in green: *Some of us have JOBS.*

Eve was still reading when a woman came down the stairs carrying an empty shopping trolley. She saw Eve's gaze and stopped so abruptly that the trolley struck her heel.

“Wonderful,” the woman said.

Eve moved aside. “Sorry. Am I in your way?”

“Not you.”

The woman pulled the notice from its pins. One corner tore, leaving a small white triangle behind. She held the sheet with both hands, looking at the green sentence.

“I have a job.”

Eve didn't know whether the remark invited an answer. Before she could decide, the front door opened and a man in a delivery jacket came in, followed by a square of cold morning air.

“I didn't write that part,” he said.

The woman turned toward him. “But you wrote the rest.”

“I asked you twice.”

“You knocked once when I was asleep.”

“Yes. I know how inconvenient being woken up is.”

Eve wished the oranges had required more attention. One had escaped its shallow paper bag and was pressing against the handles, threatening to make her a participant for entirely different reasons.

The woman folded the notice into a hard little rectangle.

“If you wanted an answer, you could have left your name.”

“My name was on the first note.”

“There wasn't a first note.”

“Inside the door.”

“The fire door? Where things fall behind the radiator?”

A voice from above asked whether somebody could move away from the stairs. Eve stepped back. The woman lifted her trolley, the man passed toward the lift, and the conversation broke apart without getting any quieter.

At home, Dumpling investigated the oranges from a cautious distance. Eve put them in a bowl and stood at the sink, still hearing the man's sentence.

She had sometimes heard barking from the corner flat. A few sharp bursts while she made breakfast, occasionally more. She worked at home, but not in the room nearest that side of the building. She could put on headphones. That did not tell her what it was like for someone who needed to sleep.

Nor did a woman with an empty trolley tell her why a dog barked.

She fed Dumpling and opened the work she had actually promised to finish.

By eleven she had delivered a corrected caption and answered a client question. Her next message came from the hall coordinator, whose name still appeared in her contacts beside a small chair emoji she had added while preparing the exchange session.

*Would you have half an hour to talk about another piece of work? It isn't an event this time.*

They met that afternoon in the hall's side room. The coordinator was sorting returned keys into a biscuit tin. Several keys wore labels much larger than themselves.

“The residents' committee asked me to find someone who can make information people will actually read,” she said. “Your table went well.”

Eve sat down. “What information?”

The coordinator slid a folded notice across the table.

Same expensive paper. No green addition.

Eve looked up.

“You know about it?” the coordinator asked.

“I saw the version downstairs this morning.”

“I thought you might. The building's committee uses our office for correspondence. They have a small communication budget, and everybody is now writing to everybody else in capitals.”

She took out a second sheet. This one announced that dogs were members of families, not appliances that could be switched off.

“Who wrote that?” Eve asked.

“The owner of the dog. She wants us to put it beside the first one.”

“Have you?”

“No. I'm beginning to think the noticeboard doesn't have the qualifications.”

Eve smiled, then read the second sheet properly. It said the owner had lived there for years without trouble, that she walked her dog daily, and that people who had never spoken to her were circulating accusations. It also said the dog hardly barked at all.

“That's quite a gap,” Eve said.

“Between their accounts?”

“Between hardly at all and every morning.”

“We wondered if you could make a short, warm video. Introduce the dog. Remind everyone there's a person behind the door.”

Eve could see it immediately. An ordinary room, a dog's patient face, a woman who felt disliked. It would be easy to make something touching. She knew where she would put the first pause.

“What would the people being woken up do with that?” she asked.

The coordinator lowered a key into the tin.

“I hoped they'd be more understanding.”

“Would the owner hear their account too?”

“Yes, of course.”

“Before or after I make her sympathetic?”

The coordinator looked at the notice again.

Outside the side room, two people were trying to move a tall folding screen through a door that appeared to disagree with its dimensions. A scraping sound interrupted them, followed by an apology to the door.

Eve waited. She had learned that continuing to explain sometimes spared the other person the work of answering.

“All right,” the coordinator said. “What would you suggest?”

“First I need to know what people are reporting, and what they're willing to discuss. Separately. I live in the building, so they need to know that too. I can't determine who's broken a rule or diagnose the dog.”

“I wasn't asking you to.”

“The notices might make people think that's the job.”

She drew the sheets together, not to hide them but to clear a place for a new piece of paper. She described a small paid assignment: two initial conversations, a short facilitated discussion if both sides wanted it, and a practical notice approved for accuracy. No filming of private homes. No promise that a communication exercise would stop barking. The committee would retain responsibility for formal complaints.

The coordinator frowned at the reduced role of the camera.

“Would people listen without a video?”

“They might. They might not. A video won't let me promise otherwise.”

They discussed the fee. It was smaller than a filming job and more than the coordinator had imagined for a few words on a board. Eve included preparation and a follow-up call rather than offering them invisibly later. They settled on a limited first stage; the discussion would be confirmed only if participants agreed.

The coordinator would ask the owner and the resident who had submitted the complaint whether Eve could contact them. She would also remove anonymous additions under the committee's existing noticeboard practice, without pretending this settled the complaint itself.

Before Eve left, she asked, “What would count as useful to you?”

The coordinator tipped the biscuit tin toward herself. A key without a label lay at the bottom.

“If they could speak without us finding another sheet of paper afterward.”

“That sounds like a better starting point.”

By early evening, the introductions had been made. The owner was Nora. The man in the delivery jacket was Ben; he wasn't a delivery driver, as Eve had assumed, but worked a night shift at a distribution depot. He used the jacket because it was warm and had pockets large enough for his keys.

Both had agreed to separate conversations. Neither had agreed to sit together.

Nora called first.

“Are you the woman with the cat?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Then you understand.”

Eve looked across the room. Dumpling was lying inside an open cupboard she had temporarily emptied to clean. His expression suggested a long-standing tenancy.

“I understand living with an animal,” she said. “I haven't heard enough to understand what's happening in your flat.”

“He's not vicious.”

“I haven't been told he is.”

“People say things.”

“They do. I'd rather ask you what you've noticed.”

Nora was quiet. In the background, water ran, stopped, then ran again.

“Tomorrow at eleven?” she said at last. “Not earlier. I have work to finish.”

They agreed. Eve explained she wouldn't record the conversation. Nora asked whether she should bathe the dog first.

“No. I'm not coming to make a film.”

“Oh.” The relief in that one syllable was unmistakable even without anything supernatural sitting nearby.

Ben replied by message. He could speak outside the building at half past seven in the morning, before he tried to sleep. He didn't want someone knocking on his door at noon because they thought mornings ended when theirs did.

Eve entered both appointments. Then she put a note beside the morning one: *No camera. Ask before taking notes.*

She also checked the route from the side entrance to the low wall where they would meet. Her window overlooked a different courtyard; she couldn't hear that entrance from her kitchen. On the sketch she had begun, she marked her own flat as well as the corner one. It was an unflattering little correction. Living in a building did not mean living in every room of it.

She had a temptation to add *bring coffee*, which felt kind and might also look like choosing a side. Instead she asked whether the time allowed him to get home without rushing. He said it did.

Her own supper was halfway prepared when Julian messaged.

*I've found a place that appears to sell the correct amount of vinegar. Would you like lunch next Sunday?*

She smiled before opening her calendar. The Sunday he meant was eleven days away. She liked that he had chosen a real day rather than leaving the next time to happen by accident.

*Yes. I'd like that. Is there an incorrect amount of vinegar on the menu?*

*Optional. I thought you'd appreciate being asked.*

She sent a laughing reply, then almost described the entire argument downstairs. It would have been pleasant to have somebody tell her she had handled the proposal well before she had done any of the difficult work.

Instead she wrote that she had a new neighborhood communication assignment and might later need to ask what general veterinary information could responsibly be included. No names, no private details, and no request for an answer over his dinner.

*Send the actual question when you have it,* he replied. *A barking problem needs more than a caption.*

*Unfortunately for my business model.*

She finished cooking. Dumpling emerged from the cupboard when the bowl touched the floor, abandoning the premises without notice.

After eating, Eve went downstairs to put out the recycling. The two hostile sheets were gone. Beside the missing glove photograph was a small committee notice inviting residents with relevant observations to use the usual contact address.

Someone had straightened the photograph while removing the pins.

The glove was still missing. The dog would probably still bark tomorrow.

Eve checked the time for her first conversation, then went upstairs without photographing the board.
