# Chapter 076 — Eat Before the Date

At eleven on Sunday, Eve put on the shirt she had washed especially, inspected herself in the mirror, and immediately wanted to change it.

Nothing was wrong with the shirt. This was its most aggravating quality. It fitted, it was clean, and it offered no useful explanation for the sensation that she had forgotten an essential qualification for lunch.

Dumpling lay on the folded blanket beside her chair. He opened one eye when she emerged from the bedroom in the same shirt for the third time.

“I know,” she said. “You would have chosen fur.”

She had breakfast dishes to wash, so she washed them. Halfway through, her stomach made a noise that suggested breakfast had been a long time ago. Lunch was booked for two. She considered waiting, because arriving hungry seemed appropriate for a restaurant, then heard herself explaining this decision to nobody and opened the bread bin.

One small sandwich would not invalidate the date.

She ate it standing up before deciding that was ridiculous too and sitting down. The earrings she had found yesterday lay beside her cup. She put them on after eating, checked that both fastenings were secure, and left the mirror alone.

Julian's message arrived at twelve thirty.

*Still good for one forty-five? I have remembered the reservation and forgotten to buy washing powder. Only one of those affects you.*

She laughed with her mouth full and took a moment before replying.

*Still good. I've eaten something so I don't arrive ready to argue with the menu.*

*Wise. I had toast.*

She had somehow imagined him spending the morning in a state of quiet competence, with neatly folded laundry and no questions about his clothes. Toast and missing washing powder were easier to approach.

Before leaving, she checked the room, water, and usual care arrangements. Dumpling followed her as far as the rug, then stopped when she took her bag from the cupboard. She crouched for a brief greeting without turning the departure into a ceremony. Her keys were where she had put them.

Downstairs, the sun made the pavement look warmer than the air felt. She walked toward the bookshop, resisting the urge to send a photograph of her shoes to Sophie, who had explicitly reserved her day for herself.

Julian was there already, studying a window display of books about houses he did not appear likely to buy.

He wore a dark sweater beneath his coat. Eve noticed the sweater, his ordinary shoes, and the fact that he had turned toward her before she said anything.

“Hello,” he said.

“Hello. Have you chosen a mansion?”

“The smallest one has eleven bedrooms. I would lose the washing powder permanently.”

She laughed, and the first awkward moment passed without requiring a rescue.

“You look nice,” he said.

She almost explained the laundry.

“Thank you. So do you.”

They stood there for a second, looking more pleased than the conversation justified. Then Julian pointed down the street, and they started toward the restaurant.

It was smaller than the photographs suggested. Four tables occupied the front room, with more beyond an archway. Someone had placed a great deal of faith in a plant that was leaning toward the window. Their table was inside, away from the speaker, just as requested.

Eve took off her coat and caught one sleeve on the chair back. Julian waited instead of helping before she needed it. When she freed it, he sat opposite her.

“Ordinary glass,” he observed, nodding toward the distant window.

“A good sacrifice.”

The waiter brought menus and water. A corner of the tablecloth had been tucked under a short table leg to stop it rocking. Eve noticed, tried the table gently, and decided she liked the practical repair better than the flawless photograph online. Eve knew what she wanted because she had researched it with the intensity usually reserved for quotations, but she read the whole page anyway. Julian studied a handwritten addition clipped to his menu.

“Is that why you wanted to come?” she asked.

“The stew. A colleague described it three times, which seemed either an endorsement or a request for an intervention.”

“Three separate conversations?”

“Two. The first one was very thorough.”

She looked at the dish. “You've waited a while?”

“A few months. Every time I remembered it, it was Monday and they were closed.”

“You could have put it in your calendar.”

“I use the calendar for things I'm likely to forget.”

She raised her eyebrows.

He looked at the menu, then back at her. “Yes. I heard it.”

When the waiter returned, Eve ordered the mushroom dish without apologizing for wanting the thing she had already chosen. Julian ordered the stew. They agreed to share bread because both actually wanted bread, not because either was trying to appear easygoing.

The waiter asked whether they wanted photographs taken when the food arrived.

“No, thank you,” Eve said, then glanced at Julian. “Unless you did?”

“No.”

It was a small exchange, but she liked hearing him answer rather than deciding that her camera-free plan covered every possible preference he might have.

After the waiter left, Julian asked about the book she'd borrowed yesterday.

“The walking one? I haven't become outdoorsy. I liked a bridge.”

“Which bridge?”

She described the narrow footbridge and brown stream. He asked whether it was somewhere she wanted to visit.

“Possibly. I don't think I want every picture I like to turn into a destination I have to afford.”

He nodded. “I have a book about restoring old boats.”

“Do you own a boat?”

“No.”

“Do you want one?”

“Less with every chapter.”

She laughed so hard that the leaning plant briefly became the safest place to look.

The bread arrived, warm enough to release steam when Julian broke a piece. He offered the basket, then reached for the butter with a knife that was at an inconvenient angle. The knife slipped against the dish and made an unexpectedly loud clink.

“Very smooth,” he said.

“I was about to mention it in the review.”

“Please include the boat expertise.”

Eve took a piece and found she was hungry again, but not so hungry that the waiting had become an injury. She was grateful to the sandwich for its modest service.

Their food came while Julian was explaining that he could repair a loose shelf but had once assembled a flat-pack stool with two legs in the wrong positions. She wanted details. He tried to insist the distinction was subtle until she asked whether it had stood up.

“Only with support.”

“So it was a dependent stool.”

“For one evening.”

“Did you keep it?”

“I took it apart and read the instructions.”

“That is a rather disappointing ending.”

“The stool improved.”

She tasted the mushrooms. For a moment she forgot to produce an appropriate conversational response.

“Good?” he asked.

“Yes. I'm trying not to look too pleased with my research.”

“I think you're allowed.”

The sauce had a sharpness she hadn't expected. She took another bite, then asked about his stew. Julian considered it with such concentration that she waited for a professional assessment.

“Worth remembering on a Sunday,” he said.

They exchanged small tastes using clean utensils the waiter brought, neither solemn nor teasing about it. Eve discovered she preferred her own choice. Julian preferred his. The afternoon survived this lack of destiny.

“What do you cook when nobody's coming?” she asked.

“Eggs. Rice. Whatever requires the fewest things to wash.”

“That's not a menu. That's a labor policy.”

“It's effective.”

“Until every pan is soaking.”

His expression changed just enough.

“You do that too.”

“One pan,” he said.

“For how long?”

“Longer than necessary.”

She grinned. “I have a bowl that becomes invisible once I put water in it.”

They talked about household tasks they put off and foods they disliked despite trying to be reasonable. Eve admitted she couldn't bear a certain soft texture in cooked fruit. Julian disliked very sweet drinks but would finish one rather than admit he'd ordered badly.

“Why?” she asked.

“I don't know. I paid for it.”

“You also paid for the right not to finish it.”

He considered this. “That sounds expensive.”

“It's advice I rarely follow myself.”

The table beside them emptied. A family entered the front room, bringing a gust of cold air and an argument about where a scarf had gone. Eve noticed how easy it was to keep listening to Julian while the restaurant went on being a restaurant, not a stage prepared for their progress.

Then he asked whether she had been nervous.

Her fork paused halfway to the plate.

“Yes,” she said. “I almost changed my shirt several times.”

“Did you?”

“No. I just made it feel unwelcome.”

He smiled, then put his spoon down.

“I left early.”

“For the bookshop?”

“I thought if I stayed home I'd keep checking the time.”

She felt something soften beneath her ribs. It wasn't the satisfaction of catching him out. She had wanted the day to matter to him without having to prove it from the angle of his chair.

“I'm glad you told me.”

“I thought you might be.”

They finished at an unhurried pace. When the waiter asked about dessert, Eve nearly declined to demonstrate restraint, then remembered that she was not interviewing for a personality.

“I'd like to look,” she said.

Julian asked for tea. She chose a small cake, checked the price, and did not order a second fork on his behalf. He said he might have one bite if she didn't mind. She didn't.

The cake arrived on a plate much larger than the cake needed. Julian regarded it.

“Room for future development.”

“Don't. I'll start writing a proposal.”

They laughed. She ate the part with the crisp edge first and told him why when he asked. He preferred the softer middle. She gave him a piece from there, pleased by a difference that had such a simple use.

At the end, they settled the meal according to the turn they had agreed on, checking the amount without making payment a contest. Eve paid for her extra dessert. Julian held her coat while she found the sleeve, accepting her thanks without turning it into a flourish.

Outside, they stood beneath the restaurant's small awning. It was still early enough for the afternoon to contain something besides going home.

“There's a little exhibition a few streets away,” Julian said. “Local drawings. Would you like to see it? We don't have to.”

Eve looked at the street, then at him.

She had eaten the food she wanted. She knew about his unusable stool, his boat book, and the pan that soaked too long. None of these things belonged in the clinic introduction she had once filmed.

“Yes,” she said. “I'd like to keep going.”

He smiled and took out the folded leaflet that had been in his coat pocket all along.

“Good,” he said. “Now we just have to find the entrance.”
