# Chapter 210 — Returning the Weekend

On Saturday morning, Eve put the content clinic's receipts beside the record of preparation time and discovered that she had been calling three hours forty-five minutes a ninety-minute session.

She checked the dates.

Then she checked them again, as though the calendar might be persuaded to return some of the missing time.

Robin arrived carrying a reusable cup and the stock notebook.

“Bad?”

“Embarrassing.”

“Those can overlap.”

The Sunday afternoon content clinic was a small recurring add-on, separate from the larger weekend program. Customers brought ordinary photographs and worked on simple captions, cropping, and practical ways to show what they meant. No pet had to perform for the camera. Eve liked the sessions. She liked being able to give someone a useful answer in ten minutes.

The trouble was everything surrounding those ten minutes.

She opened the preparation record. Downloading sample files, checking permission to use examples, setting up the screen, answering advance questions, printing alternatives for people who preferred paper, clearing away, and sending the promised follow-up notes all belonged somewhere.

Until this morning, several of them had belonged in a place called I'll just do it.

Robin pulled out a chair.

“I've recorded my actual time,” they said.

“I know. This is mine.”

She showed them the notes she had reconstructed from her work calendar and file timestamps. Not every minute could be recovered precisely, so she marked the uncertain estimates rather than polishing them into false accuracy.

Even the conservative total was ugly.

“This Sunday's already postponed because of the repairs,” Robin said. “Are you thinking about the restart?”

“Yes. I keep saying we'll bring it back as soon as the room is ready.”

“Will we?”

Eve looked at the familiar little title on the calendar. She had designed the first announcement on a hopeful evening when a short, friendly session had seemed like an easy way to make the venue more useful.

“I don't think we should keep the weekly version.”

Robin waited.

“I could raise the price.”

“You could.”

“Or cut the follow-up.”

“You could.”

“You're being very unhelpfully reasonable.”

They took a sip from their cup.

“You asked what I thought last month. I said the Sunday close was getting late. Then we made the notes shorter and kept everything else.”

Eve remembered. She had treated the complaint as an editing problem because editing was something she knew how to fix.

“What would you want now?” she asked.

“A Sunday that ends when the rota says it ends. And enough time to prepare the next paid shift without remembering something on the bus home.”

The answer was quiet. It didn't give Eve a villain to defeat or a dramatic number to solve. It gave her a person's week.

She turned back to the receipts.

Keeping the session at its current price did not pay for the work it actually required. Raising the price enough would make it a different service, and she had no evidence that the current group wanted that service. Cutting it down further might work occasionally, but it would still occupy the weekend afternoon they kept pretending was a small commitment.

The larger program needed its own review. She couldn't settle that by glancing at this sheet. Its bookings, preparation, and costs were more substantial, and the quarter's accounts still needed a proper comparison.

She could settle this.

“We end the weekly content clinic,” she said. “No restart promise after the repairs. If I offer something later, it gets a new scope and price. It doesn't sneak back in as the same favor.”

Robin nodded.

“What happens to the existing bookings?”

Eve opened the booking record.

Three people held places on later dates. One had used a credit from the weather cancellation. Another had booked two sessions in advance. None needed to discover the decision through a public post.

“I contact them first. Refund the unused bookings. No automatic transfer to something they didn't buy.”

“And the notes from the last session?”

“Already owed. I finish those.”

That distinction mattered. Ending a format did not allow her to quietly abandon work people had already paid for.

She started with the customer holding the weather credit. The woman sounded disappointed but relieved to have a definite answer.

“I kept leaving Sundays open,” she said. “If you're not sure when the room will be ready, I'd rather have the money back.”

Eve confirmed the amount and the ordinary processing time. She didn't call it immediate when the payment service and bank still had their own steps.

The second call was harder.

“But I liked coming every week,” the man said. “I was just getting comfortable.”

“I know. I'm sorry to take away something that worked for you.”

“Couldn't you keep it once a month?”

Eve looked at the blank margin of the calendar. A monthly square would fit there beautifully.

“I haven't worked out a version I can reliably offer,” she said. “I don't want to promise one today to soften the answer.”

He was silent long enough that she could hear traffic on his end of the call.

“That's a shame.”

“Yes.”

She let the disappointment remain his without trying to turn it into reassurance for herself.

They confirmed the refund for his unused bookings. He asked whether the simple instructions sheets from the last session would still arrive, and she said yes, by the date already agreed. When they hung up, her hand was stiff around the phone.

Robin was checking the materials cupboard in the cleared area.

“Do you need a minute?”

“Yes.”

Eve went outside with her cup. The pavement was dry. Across the road, someone was taking down a sign with a price written in large cheerful numbers. Behind the sign, the ordinary window remained.

She had expected cancelling something to feel like recovering energy. At that moment it felt like letting somebody down.

Both things could be true, apparently, without either waiting politely for the other to finish.

When she went back inside, the third customer had replied by message. They preferred the refund and asked whether Eve still accepted individual paid editing work. She answered the actual inquiry with her current scope and rates, without assuming it would become a booking or subtracting imaginary income from the loss.

Before changing the public booking page, Eve checked the older announcement in the venue window. It still said every Sunday in letters she had chosen precisely because people could read them from the pavement. Leaving it there would mean answering the same confused question all week.

She took it down and replaced it with the plain closure notice. The corners of the old paper curled around her fingers. On the back, Robin had once written a materials count in pencil. Eve kept that useful scrap for the stock check, then put the rest with the paper recycling.

No customer applauded the removal. A passerby glanced at the new notice and continued walking. Eve was glad she hadn't tried to make the moment into a public declaration about choosing herself.

Then she completed the promised notes from the last clinic.

One photograph showed a dog's bowl in a confusing corner. With the owner's permission already recorded for private feedback, Eve cropped the example, marked the area that mattered, and explained why the wider view made the practical question harder to understand. She did not turn the customer's picture into public content.

The work took thirty-two minutes.

This time she wrote them down.

After sending the notes, she removed the recurring booking option and replaced the old announcement with a clear notice that the weekly format was ending. Existing customers had been contacted directly. Other services continued under their own arrangements. There was no promise of an exciting replacement immediately around the corner.

Robin checked the changed rota.

“The booked paid hours for this week stay?”

“Yes. We use the time for the stock check and outstanding follow-up, as agreed. Future shifts get discussed before they're offered.”

“Thank you.”

Eve didn't call the freed hours a gift she was giving the team. They had asked for work that fit its actual boundaries. She was finally changing the service that prevented it.

The building manager came by near noon with the next repair appointment. The rear area still needed its final work and clearance before normal use. Eve wrote the appointment down. She no longer tried to attach the content clinic's return to it.

That alone made the call shorter.

By the end of the paid session, the follow-up was sent, the stock counted, and the unused clinic supplies placed on an accessible shelf. Nothing needed to be thrown away in a ceremony. Paper could be used again. A bad recurring schedule did not have to be.

Robin zipped their bag.

“I might go to the market tomorrow,” they said.

“Good.”

“Not to buy things for here.”

“I was about to ask you to enjoy yourself, not source baskets.”

“Excellent distinction.”

They left at the agreed time.

Eve remained for the short close, then went home. She still had work planned for part of the weekend, including the fuller accounts review. Cancelling one add-on did not create an empty life. It created a visible gap that she could choose not to refill immediately.

Julian was at home when she arrived, making lunch.

“I can confirm Tuesday,” she said before taking off her coat.

He turned.

“For food?”

“For food outside our apartment. With chairs neither of us has to stack.”

“I support this proposal.”

She hung up the coat and told him what she had ended. He listened while she described the disappointed customer, the refunds, and the stubborn thirty-two minutes of follow-up.

“Do you think I should have offered monthly?” she asked.

“I don't know what it would cost you.”

“Neither do I.”

“Then it sounds as though you gave the answer you had.”

It wasn't permission, and she didn't need it to be. She had made the decision before coming home.

Over lunch, they chose a small restaurant they could reach without rushing. Julian checked its ordinary opening hours. Eve resisted researching every photograph of every dish until the date became another production task.

“What do you want to eat?” he asked.

“Something I didn't plan for other people.”

“That leaves a reasonable menu.”

Dumpling climbed onto the chair beside them and settled with his back toward the table. He showed no interest in either calendar. Eve stroked his shoulder once when he leaned close, then left him to sleep.

Later, she opened the quarter's accounts and set aside the larger weekend program for a proper review. The little content clinic had been an obvious leak once she counted the time. The flagship events looked healthier on the surface. She would need to test that impression rather than protect it.

For now, Tuesday evening had two names and no task list.

Eve closed the calendar before she could add one.
