# Chapter 157 — An Unromantic List

The landlord brought a bucket to the meeting.

Eve had expected a folder. She had a folder herself, with the revised room plan at the front and the proposed hours behind it. The bucket was gray, ordinary, and accompanied by a photograph of a stain.

“This was from the previous occupant,” the landlord said.

Eve studied the dark mark beside a skirting board.

“Water?”

“Something stored in the wrong container. The repair took three visits.”

She set the bucket beside the temporary table and laid the photograph flat.

“I don't mind the idea of animals. I mind finding out afterward that an idea has gone through the floor.”

Eve lowered her folder onto the table.

“Fair.”

The landlord took off her coat. Outside, a light rain had begun. Water dotted the glass, turning the unit across the street into a blurred rectangle. Eve had left Dumpling at home. Julian was due only for the short part of the conversation concerning how the proposed activities differed from veterinary work.

She had warned the landlord that he was not a partner in the business. The woman had said she understood. Eve would learn that understanding could still contain a hopeful exception.

They began at the back of the room.

The landlord showed her where cleaning materials might be kept, provided the cupboard could be secured and its contents stored appropriately. Eve opened it. A shallow shelf divided the space so awkwardly that the bucket would fit only on its side.

They both looked at it.

“I assumed this was larger,” Eve said.

“It is larger in the letting photograph.”

“Do you take those?”

“No. I complain about them.”

They laughed, briefly united by a professional skill used against ordinary dimensions.

Eve took the bucket out again. She could buy a smaller one, but the closed storage still had to accommodate what she actually needed. She wrote down the measurement. Neither of them tried to settle the problem by agreeing that she would be tidy.

At the rear door, the landlord described the waste arrangements. Eve repeated them back and asked about collections after animal-appropriate sessions. There would be no unattended animals, no boarding, no resident display cats, and no clinical waste generated by treatments because treatments would not happen here.

“What if someone asks your boyfriend to look at something?”

“Then he decides how to direct them to proper care. We don't turn the room into a clinic because he's visiting.”

“But he is a vet.”

“Yes.”

“That must help.”

“It helps me know the difference.”

Eve said it without smiling this time. She had used his profession as reassurance herself often enough to recognize the invitation.

The landlord nodded slowly and made a note.

Next came cleaning after sessions. Eve had priced appropriate supplies and paid clearing time. She had not priced a specialist response to every conceivable problem because she could not afford to promise one. When the landlord asked for a guarantee that no smell would ever reach the common passage, Eve looked down at her page.

“I can't guarantee that.”

The silence that followed felt expensive.

She continued before she could soften the answer into nonsense.

“I can state which activities we'll run, how many households attend, what happens if a session becomes unsuitable, and who cleans before the next booking. We can agree how complaints are handled. But I can't promise something no room in the building can promise.”

The landlord tapped her pen against the table.

“I don't want to spend my evenings mediating.”

“Neither do I. That's why I'd rather have a procedure I can use than tell you not to worry.”

The woman looked toward the bucket, then back at Eve.

“All right. Show me what you've costed.”

Eve opened the folder.

Her first budget version had hidden clearing under general preparation. The new one gave it actual time, and therefore money. A trial afternoon with more frequent changeovers would cost more than a single longer discussion. Animal-appropriate visits would require a different setup and recovery interval from an owners' talk without pets. She had reduced the proposed timetable accordingly.

The landlord read the figures carefully. She did not ask how many followers Dumpling had.

It was uncomfortable and unexpectedly restful.

When Julian arrived, he wiped his shoes on the mat and held his umbrella until Eve showed him where it could drip without becoming somebody else's obstacle.

“We have reached liquids,” she said.

“I can see that.”

He glanced at the bucket.

The landlord invited him to sit and asked whether he could confirm that the arrangements would prevent every animal health risk.

Julian did not reach for the folder.

“No,” he said.

Eve watched the woman's face change.

“I can explain the limits of a nonclinical venue,” he continued. “I can't certify a building or guarantee what will happen in somebody else's business.”

“I didn't mean a formal certificate.”

“A less formal promise would still be a promise.”

He spoke gently, which helped more than Eve had expected. The landlord leaned back rather than arguing.

“What would you tell people who come here?” she asked.

“That an activity description isn't advice for their individual animal. Owners remain responsible for deciding whether the session suits them and for seeking appropriate care. If there's a health concern, a social activity is the wrong place to diagnose it.”

Eve explained her existing referral wording and the separation of pet-free and animal-appropriate activities. Julian corrected one phrase that sounded as though she would assess an animal's fitness herself. She changed it immediately.

Then the landlord asked about insurance, and Julian looked at Eve.

“That needs the relevant provider,” he said. “It isn't my field.”

Eve took the current quote from her folder. She had already asked for the proposed activities to be described accurately. There were conditions she still needed clarified before signing anything. She marked those open rather than turning his presence into a substitute answer.

His allotted time ended while they were discussing an equipment cupboard. Eve thanked him. The landlord did too, with slightly less enthusiasm than if he had guaranteed the future, but without resentment.

At the door he squeezed Eve's hand.

“Call when you're finished?”

“Yes.”

He left for his own afternoon. The bucket remained.

They returned to repair responsibilities.

A clause in the draft made Eve responsible for damage arising during her use, written broadly enough that she could not tell where an old defect ended and a new accident began. She asked to photograph the existing condition with the landlord and record unresolved items before the keys changed hands.

The woman hesitated at the time involved, then walked to the window and pointed out a small mark Eve had not noticed.

“That was already there.”

“Let's include it.”

“And the rear catch sticks.”

“That too.”

They spent the next twenty minutes moving around the room together. Eve took clear, plain pictures, close enough to show the marks and wide enough to locate them. The landlord checked each note. Nothing needed flattering light.

When they came to the threshold, Eve brought out the access adviser's observations. The necessary work and permissions remained matters for appropriate professionals, not a weekend project for an enthusiastic tenant. They assigned who would seek each answer and what had to be resolved before regular activities could start.

The landlord looked tired by the end.

“This is a lot for such a small room.”

“It was a lot when we weren't writing it down.”

She smiled reluctantly. “You have an answer for everything.”

“No. There are four question marks on this page.”

They counted them. There were five.

Eve crossed out the optimistic total.

The last subject was opening hours. She presented the revised preparation schedule after speaking with Alex. No amplified sound, deliveries in agreed windows, furniture moved carefully, and a trial timetable narrow enough to keep the first months manageable. These were proposed arrangements, not permission to ignore the building's ordinary requirements.

The landlord asked whether Eve could commit to a larger minimum number of sessions. It would make the tenancy seem more established.

“It would make my costs larger before I know the demand,” Eve said.

“You'll have rent either way.”

“Yes. But I don't have to add wages for empty sessions to prove I'm paying it confidently.”

The woman considered that for a while.

They left the smaller schedule in place for the next draft.

By late afternoon the rain had stopped. Eve's folder contained more handwriting than printing. Some matters were settled; others required revised wording and external confirmation. The landlord took her bucket home, leaving the photograph of the stain for the condition record.

Eve stayed long enough to price the smaller secured cupboard and recalculate the clearing costs. The improved plan had no drinks income, fewer activities, and a real allowance for wear and cleaning. It looked less like a dream every time she made it more workable.

She put the pen down.

For several minutes she wanted Julian to arrive and say that she had done enough, that it would all be fine, that somebody else's certainty could relieve her of the remaining arithmetic.

A loose sheet slipped from the table when she reached for her phone. Eve bent to retrieve it and found the cupboard measurements underneath her shoe. The numbers she had copied were legible, but she had drawn the door opening in the wrong direction.

She went back to the cupboard and opened it. The narrow leaf struck the proposed position of the folding chair.

For a moment she considered leaving the correction until tomorrow. Then she moved the chair, tried the door again, and crossed out the little rectangle on her plan. The chair could live elsewhere. The cupboard needed to be usable by somebody carrying cleaning supplies, not merely look spacious in her sketch.

She took a second photograph with the door open and sent it with a short correction to the landlord. There was no reply yet. She hadn't expected one in thirty seconds.

By the time she returned to her phone, she had solved one small thing without pretending it solved the others.

Then she called him as arranged.

“Have you finished?” he asked.

“The meeting. Not the questions.”

“How many?”

“Five. We tried calling them four. It didn't help.”

He laughed softly.

Eve leaned against the temporary table and told him about the cupboard, the photographs, and the reduced timetable. He listened without offering to become the missing budget line.

“Was it awful?” he asked.

“No. Unromantic.”

“Those can be different.”

“I know. The landlord made a joke.”

“That sounds promising.”

“She also wants me to pay for damage I actually cause.”

“A severe reversal.”

Eve laughed, and the room no longer felt quite so heavy around her.

They arranged to meet for dinner after her remaining client edits were sent. Before leaving, she checked the latch, cleared the table, and photographed the final page for her own records.

In the margin, beside the words repairs and cleaning, the landlord had drawn a small square to indicate where the replacement cupboard might go.

It was crooked. It was a real possibility in a real room.

Eve left it crooked.
