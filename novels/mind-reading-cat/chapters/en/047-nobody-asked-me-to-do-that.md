# Chapter 047 — Nobody Asked Me to Do That

Sophie arrived at the café carrying a small plant in a paper sleeve.

Eve had covered half their table with the weekend grid, her estimates, and two printed messages. She gathered them into a stack so Sophie could put the plant down.

“Basil?” she asked.

“Mint. I'm starting with an opponent I understand.”

“It looks healthy.”

“Give us time.”

Sophie hung her bag on the chair and ordered coffee. Eve waited until the server left before spreading the papers again. She had rehearsed an efficient explanation on the walk over. Two clients, one clash, a short journey. The preliminary work could be done separately. The difficulty was just the middle of Saturday.

Sophie listened, turning the grid toward herself.

“Next Saturday?”

“Yes. Not today. I haven't completely lost perspective.”

“Who is running the table while you're gone?”

Eve pointed to the box.

Sophie looked at her own name, then at the ninety-minute gap.

“I thought you could cover that part,” Eve said. “You know how these things work.”

“What part?”

“The arrivals, keeping the conversation going. Making sure nobody signs the filming permission without understanding it. I can give you the questions.”

“And closing the table?”

“I'd leave everything arranged.”

“But you'd still be at the showroom?”

“Yes. You'd close it, pack the materials, and return the chairs.”

Sophie set the page down.

“No.”

The server returned with their cups. Eve moved a paper out of the wet ring beneath the saucer, grateful for three seconds in which no response was required.

“No to closing it?” she asked when they were alone again.

“No to being the spare person in the middle of this.”

“I'd help you the same way.”

Sophie was silent long enough for Eve to hear the sentence she had just offered as payment.

Then her friend said, “You haven't told me what the job pays.”

“I haven't settled the quote yet.”

“Does the quote include me?”

Eve looked at the cost sheet.

She had included transport, printing, a spare battery rental she probably wouldn't need, and the café coffee she was now regretting. Sophie appeared on the schedule but nowhere among the costs.

“I was thinking I could buy you lunch afterward.”

“I'm already buying lunch when I need lunch.”

It was said without a smile. Eve felt heat creep up her neck.

“I didn't mean your work wasn't worth paying for.”

“But you put it down as free.”

A spoon clinked at the next table. Eve wanted to lower her voice even though neither of them had raised theirs.

“It's a friend helping for an hour.”

“It's taking responsibility for a client event while the person they hired is somewhere else.” Sophie tapped the paper. “Nobody asked me to do that.”

“I am asking now.”

“You're explaining where I fit.”

Eve leaned back. The chair caught the edge of her coat and pulled it beneath her. She freed it with more force than necessary.

“You could just say you're busy.”

“I could. I'm saying I don't want this arrangement.”

The distinction irritated Eve precisely because she couldn't negotiate it by shifting the time. She looked toward the window, where a man was struggling to fold an umbrella that had opened inside out. For a shameful moment, she wished Sophie had offered an ordinary obstacle she could solve.

Her friend took a sip of coffee.

“I have my own conversation to prepare for on Monday. I'm not taking another job where I carry the decisions without being allowed to make them.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Eve stared at the box on the grid. She had listened on the river bench. She had understood the problem well enough to say something sympathetic. Then she had gone home and drawn a smaller version of it with a question mark after Sophie's name.

The anger didn't disappear. It became difficult to direct outward.

“I did know,” she said. “And I still assumed you would do this.”

Sophie rested both hands around her cup.

Eve drew the page back and crossed out the name. Not the tasks. The tasks remained, awkward and ownerless.

“I'm sorry. I wanted the jobs to fit, and I used you to make the picture look possible.”

“Thank you.”

“I don't have another solution yet.”

“You don't need one before you apologize.”

Eve nodded. She folded the grid once and put it beneath her bag.

They had twelve minutes left. She asked where the mint would live. Sophie took out her phone and showed her the narrow kitchen windowsill, measured with the ruler Eve had apparently forgotten her friend owned.

The pot would fit if the washing-up brush moved.

“Is the brush open to negotiation?” Eve asked.

“It hasn't replied to my proposal.”

Sophie smiled, but their coffee didn't become instantly easy. Eve let the remaining awkwardness sit with them. At half past ten, Sophie stood and took her plant. Eve didn't ask for a different kind of help at the door.

At home, she opened both client messages again.

Dumpling was sleeping on the sofa with one white foot suspended beyond the cushion. She left him there and called the hall coordinator before she could redesign the grid into another lie.

“I can't offer continuous filming and run the table by myself,” she said. “My first idea assumed help I haven't arranged.”

“All right,” the coordinator said slowly. “What can you offer?”

The question was less dramatic than Eve had feared. She took a breath and described two small rounds of conversation, beginning at ten thirty and eleven fifteen. Six places per round. People would handle ordinary outing items, explain what helped them, and leave practical suggestions on cards. No live animals, no medical advice, no camera moving among visitors.

“Would we still get something to share afterward?”

“A one-page summary of the suggestions, without people's names or private details. I can photograph the completed table after everyone has left. Anyone who doesn't want their card included can take it home.”

“We had hoped for a film.”

“I understand. I can't include that in this version.”

Eve waited through the silence. She could practically feel a free video trying to climb into her mouth.

The coordinator asked her to send the revised offer. The price would come down because filming had been removed, but it still had to include a second person for arrivals and room flow. Eve explained that staffing needed confirmation by Tuesday; if she couldn't secure it within the agreed budget, they would not pretend the larger version existed.

“Send the figures,” the coordinator said. “And put the limit on the notice. I don't want people arriving for an open session and finding all six chairs occupied.”

Eve wrote that down.

The call hadn't produced delight. It had produced a possible job.

Felix answered while she was making lunch. She turned off the hob before beginning.

“I can't be at the showroom on Saturday,” she said. “I have another request that overlaps. I haven't confirmed either onsite booking, but I need to be clear before you print anything.”

“So I need someone else for the live part.”

“Yes.”

He exhaled. “I hoped you could explain the new pocket better than I do.”

“I can help you prepare the explanation. I can film a short demonstration with you in the closed shop on Thursday morning and give you a simple cue card for Saturday. But I can't promise live coverage.”

“Would that film be ready for the event?”

Eve looked at the editing hours she had moved into Friday night.

“No. Tuesday afterward. I could deliver the cue card Thursday.”

“That's less useful for getting people there.”

“It is. If you need a finished promotion beforehand, you'll need a different arrangement.”

A drawer opened at his end. She heard the loose rattle of something being searched through, then his voice returned closer to the phone.

“What if I don't need a film of the event itself? We can demonstrate the bags on Saturday. The short film can show the same features the following week.”

“That would work.”

“No customers in it. Just me?”

“Just you, if you're willing.”

“I've become less mysterious since the last campaign.”

She smiled despite herself. They agreed she would send a fresh scope and smaller fee, with the same limited use of the finished material as before. He would decide after checking the numbers. His shop assistant could handle sales; he would do the demonstration himself.

Eve finished cooking. The lunch was plain and late, but it wasn't eaten over an editing window.

In the afternoon, she built the hall proposal around the chairs she had actually seen. Two rounds needed two sets of cards, not a decorative wall. She removed the camera rental and entered a realistic production-assistant fee. The remaining income looked disappointingly modest.

She checked the arithmetic twice. It stayed modest.

Then she sent the proposal.

Pocket Path's acceptance arrived first. Felix wanted one caption explaining the changed fastening, which was already within the stated revision round. He confirmed the Thursday appointment and Tuesday delivery. Eve accepted that booking in writing, with no Saturday attendance hiding in the cheerful closing line.

The hall coordinator called just before five. They could afford the smaller proposal, including the second person. She wanted the confirmation by Tuesday so they could print their final notice.

“I'll confirm the staffing by then,” Eve said. “You don't have a booking until I do.”

“Good. I'd rather know.”

Afterward, Eve laid the new grid beside the old one. Her Saturday contained one event. Her Thursday held a short shoot. There were still materials to prepare and an edit due the following week, but neither page required someone to work for a lunch she had never offered them.

She photographed only the revised schedule and put the phone down without sending it to Julian. She didn't want to announce that he had been right or prove that he had been wrong. The work was hers to organize, and the conflict between them remained its own conversation.

To Sophie she sent something shorter.

*I changed the proposals. I'm no longer leaving someone to cover an event I can't stay at. Thank you for telling me directly. I won't use Sunday's check-in to ask about this job.*

The answer came while Eve was washing the pan.

*Good. The mint has taken possession of the windowsill.*

Eve dried her hands and looked at the photograph attached: one small plant, a clean sill, the displaced washing-up brush standing in a mug.

She put a heart beneath the picture. No new question followed it.

On the sofa, Dumpling turned over and tucked the unsupported foot beneath himself. Eve sat at the opposite end with her book. For the first time that day, she wasn't waiting for anybody to volunteer the missing part of her weekend.
