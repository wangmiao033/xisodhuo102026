# Chapter 003 — Temporary Resident

By nine the next morning, Eve had searched for jobs, inspected her bank balance, and rearranged the same three lines of her résumé four times.

None of those activities made the clinic call sooner.

Her apartment was very quiet. Usually she spent this hour accusing the kettle of boiling too slowly, then leaving with her toast held between her teeth. Today the kettle finished. The toast cooled. There was nowhere she had to be.

She put her phone faceup beside the laptop.

When it rang, she answered before the second sound.

“Hello. Yes. I'm here.”

Tessa paused. “Good morning to you, too.”

Dumpling had settled. The clinic had reassessed him and would go through the care arrangements when Eve collected him. There was no owner information they could immediately use, and the found notice could go ahead with the details they had discussed. Tessa asked whether Eve had somewhere suitable for him to stay while they continued looking.

Eve looked at the apartment.

It had a sofa too narrow to nap on with dignity, a table with one uneven leg, and a kitchen so small that opening the refrigerator blocked the cupboard. Suitable wasn't the first word that came to mind.

“I need to speak to my landlord,” she said.

“Do that first. Call us when you know.”

She appreciated the absence of You already brought him in, didn't you?

After hanging up, she read the relevant part of her lease rather than trusting her memory. Then she called the landlord.

“No, I'm not opening a pet business,” she said three minutes later. “One cat. Temporary care while we look for an owner.”

“How temporary?”

“I don't know yet. I can update you.”

That answer was becoming an unwelcome theme.

The landlord wanted the apartment kept clean, no disturbance to neighbors, and notice if the arrangement changed. Eve repeated what she could agree to and asked for the permission in a message so neither of them had to remember a phone conversation differently later.

When the reply arrived, she saved it.

Then she made a budget.

She did not call the file NEW LIFE. Yesterday she'd made enough involuntary changes to last a while. She called it Expenses, which seemed safer.

Rent. Utilities. Food. Transport. The cat's agreed care and basic supplies. Her expected final payment appeared in a separate column, not as money already in the bank.

The total didn't make her feel inspired.

It did tell her she couldn't buy the tower-shaped cat furniture she'd absentmindedly opened in another tab.

“Congratulations,” she told the screen. “You have been eliminated from consideration.”

Before calling back, she walked through the apartment as if she had never been in it before. A trailing cable she usually stepped over now looked like something to put away. The narrow gap beside the washing machine needed attention. She moved the cleaning products and checked the window, then discovered that making one corner quieter involved moving nearly every noisy object somewhere else.

None of it resembled the sunlit apartments in the advertisements she'd been browsing. The old table still rocked. She folded a scrap of card beneath its short leg, tested it with her palm, and felt absurdly satisfied when it stayed still. One small thing in the room now worked better than it had an hour ago.

She closed the shopping tab and called Tessa back.

At the clinic, Dumpling watched her from a secure carrier with the offended concentration of someone who hadn't approved the interior design. A clean piece of bedding lay beneath him. His right paw was still treated carefully, but he no longer pressed himself into the farthest possible corner.

“Hello,” Eve said.

One ear turned toward her.

“That's more acknowledgment than I got at my exit meeting.”

Tessa handed her a written sheet and explained the practical arrangements. Eve asked questions, repeated the important points, and wrote the follow-up time in her phone. She wasn't going to improvise care from something a cheerful stranger posted online at two in the morning.

Julian came in halfway through the explanation. He checked that she understood when to contact the clinic and how to keep the home setup calm.

“Don't make him meet everyone today.”

“I have one friend coming over later.”

“One friend doesn't have to meet him today, either.”

Eve pictured Sophie standing outside the apartment holding two dinners and filing a formal complaint.

“Understood.”

He glanced at the inexpensive supplies she'd selected from the available options. “You don't need to buy everything at once.”

“I've already had that conversation with a six-level scratching palace.”

“Did it listen?”

“It was surprisingly reasonable when I closed the tab.”

Julian's mouth softened, briefly enough that she wondered whether she'd imagined it. He checked the carrier fastening rather than answering.

The carrier was a clinic loan, recorded on a slip that Eve folded with the rest of the papers. The arrangement made her feel better than an unspoken favor would have. She knew what needed returning and to whom.

“Do I bring the towel next time?” she asked.

“Only if you want the towel to attend.”

She looked up.

He looked perfectly serious.

That was annoying. It would have been easier if he had been reliably humorless.

Outside, she checked the carrier before lifting it. The trip home was unremarkable, which she suspected was the best review an injured cat could give a journey. Dumpling didn't shout. He didn't demonstrate gratitude. He sat with his paws gathered beneath him and watched the little slice of the world available through the front.

At home, Eve set him down in the quiet corner she'd prepared. She opened the carrier as instructed and moved away.

Nothing happened.

She waited.

Nothing continued happening.

“All right,” she said. “Excellent entrance. Very understated.”

She sat at the table with the written care sheet and pretended not to watch.

The apartment looked different with the carrier in it. Not prettier. The supplies crowded the corner where she usually dropped parcels. Her drying rack had migrated into the kitchen. A bowl sat on a mat she'd bought because the cheapest one was also easy to clean.

It was an inconvenient amount of space to give something that hadn't yet entered the room.

Her phone buzzed. A recruiter asking when she could speak.

Eve straightened, then remembered there was no video call. She replied with her availability and returned to her résumé. Under Strengths, she had written Adaptable.

Across the room, a nose appeared.

She lowered her gaze to the laptop.

Two quiet steps. A pause. Dumpling sniffed the floor, then retreated into the carrier.

“Also me,” Eve whispered.

The recruiter called. Eve gave the polished version of yesterday: her position had been affected by restructuring; she was interested in roles that used her content planning experience; she could provide a portfolio. She did not tell him about the rain or the box or the way she'd almost answered a group chat she no longer belonged to.

When he asked about salary expectations, she nearly named a lower number before he'd reacted to the first one.

The cat emerged again.

Eve watched him stop beside the unfamiliar bowl. She forced herself to stop talking long enough for the recruiter to answer.

There was no disaster in the silence. He simply explained the range they were working with.

She wrote it down.

After the call, Dumpling inspected a chair leg, rejected the view beneath the sofa, and turned toward a low cabinet. Eve had left the bottom drawer out while reorganizing the supplies. It was the only drawer in the apartment that wasn't packed with objects she would definitely need someday.

He put his nose inside.

“No,” she said automatically. “That one's for—”

For what?

She had bought the cabinet six months ago because she wanted to become a person who owned some empty storage. A person whose life hadn't filled every corner before she'd decided what belonged there.

Dumpling looked at her, then at the drawer.

“It's not a rental application. You can't just stare at me.”

He stared.

She removed the loose drawer fully from the cabinet and made sure it couldn't slide or trap him. After checking that the low, open space fitted the calm setup she'd been advised to provide, she lined it with suitable bedding and left it beside the carrier.

Dumpling ignored it for eleven minutes.

Then he lay down.

Eve stared back.

“That was my only empty one.”

He closed his eyes.

By the time Sophie arrived, Eve had learned that an hour could disappear while a cat did almost nothing.

Sophie set the food down softly. “Where's the new executive?”

“Resting. We're not holding a reception.”

“Good. I'm exhausted.” She peeled off her jacket and sank onto the sofa. “I spent forty minutes convincing someone a banner wouldn't fit through a door just because the design file did.”

Eve fetched plates. She wanted to tell the story of yesterday, but Sophie had a red mark on her hand from carrying something and looked as though she had been assembled hastily from spare parts.

“What happened?” Eve asked.

Sophie told her. The banner, the doorway, a client who'd measured the opening without accounting for the frame. Eve laughed, then offered a solution three sentences too soon.

“Already fixed,” Sophie said. “Let me complain first.”

“Right. Sorry.”

She listened.

Later, over cooling noodles, Sophie asked how Eve was doing.

This time she told the untidy version. The mug. The gray group-chat notification. The bus she'd let leave. She didn't make any of it a lesson.

Sophie listened, too.

“So what's the plan for him?” she asked eventually, nodding toward the corner.

“Find out whether someone's looking. Follow the clinic's advice. Keep him here for now.”

“And you?”

Eve glanced at the laptop, where her résumé still claimed excellent strategic foresight.

“Similar. Except the clinic won't write me a plan.”

Sophie smiled, tired but real.

After dinner, they washed the plates. Sophie left without trying to pick up the cat. At the doorway she hugged Eve with one arm because the other was carrying the food containers.

“Call me tomorrow. Not only if something's wrong.”

Eve promised.

When the apartment was quiet again, she checked the found notice and recorded the replies. None could yet identify Dumpling. She set a reminder to follow up rather than sitting there refreshing indefinitely.

The cat opened his eyes when she passed. She paused at a respectful distance.

“Temporary resident,” she told him.

A small white paw rested over the edge of the former drawer.

Eve turned off the overhead light, leaving the room soft around them. On her expenses sheet, the new line had stopped being a provisional note.

At the bottom, beneath food and rent, she typed his name. Then she shut the laptop without looking at the job listings one more time. There would be time for them in the morning.
