# Chapter 184 — His New Colleagues

Eve found Julian by his laugh before she saw his face.

It came through the open doorway beyond the reception desk, followed by a woman's voice saying, “You have to leave room for an answer.”

“I was leaving room,” he said.

“You supplied three possible answers inside it.”

Someone else laughed. Eve stopped beside the visitor chairs with her hand still resting on the strap of her equipment bag.

The receptionist looked up.

“Can I help?”

“I'm meeting Julian. He said to wait here.”

The receptionist checked, pointed to the chairs, and told her where she could get water. Eve thanked her and sat down. From this angle she could see through the doorway into a teaching room, but not beyond it. A whiteboard stood beside a table covered in folders. There were no patients or owners in sight.

Julian was sitting with four other people, holding a sheet of paper. A woman opposite him tapped a line with her pen. He looked down, listened, then crossed something out.

Eve knew that concentrated look. She had seen it when he listened to an anxious owner, when he worked out an awkward journey, when he helped her understand a question without making the answer hers by force.

Here it was directed toward something she could not read, among people who knew why it mattered.

For a small, disagreeable second, she wanted him to look up immediately.

He did not.

She took her camera bag off her shoulder and set it between her feet. Her neck ached from the work she had already done. She had eaten a bent sandwich on a platform, paid for a detour, and walked here with the foolish private conviction that arriving would make her the obvious center of the afternoon.

The conversation continued.

“Try it again without looking at the sheet,” the woman said.

Julian put the paper down.

“I'd want to understand what feels hardest to manage at home.”

Another person answered, apparently playing a part. Eve could not hear every word, only something about work and being alone in the evenings. Julian started to reply, stopped himself, and nodded instead.

The woman with the pen smiled.

“There.”

He rubbed a hand over his face and laughed once, ruefully.

At home people often seemed to turn toward him automatically. Even when he wanted an evening off, somebody had a question about an animal's appetite or an unexplained noise. Eve had become fiercely protective of his right not to be useful every minute.

She had not imagined that being less immediately useful might also be something he wanted.

The group began gathering their papers. Julian glanced toward reception and saw her.

His expression changed so quickly that she felt ashamed of the tiny contest she had invented in the chair.

He stood, lifted a hand, then said something to the others before walking out. He did not leave a sentence dangling behind him as proof of devotion. He finished it and came to her.

“You made it.”

“I have the authentic shoulder dent of a traveling photographer.”

He kissed her, brief and warm, with his hand against her coat sleeve.

“Can I carry the bag?”

“For a bit. It contains most of my earning capacity.”

“I'll avoid juggling.”

He took the strap, then looked toward the teaching room.

“We're done. They're just finding the handout for tomorrow. Do you mind a minute?”

Eve had expected this question to annoy her. It didn't, now that he had actually seen her.

“Go on.”

He hesitated. “Would you like to say hello?”

“Yes.”

He brought her only as far as the open doorway. The woman with the pen smiled and cleared a stack of folders off a spare chair, though Eve remained standing. Julian introduced her by name and explained that she had been working nearby. He introduced the others as fellow participants; the woman who had been correcting his exercise was a colleague practicing the feedback role, not his tutor.

Eve was glad she had not confidently named the scene to herself.

“How was the shoot?” a man by the whiteboard asked.

“Two people, a new room, and a vase with ambitions beyond its station.”

“That sounds familiar,” the woman said, looking at a tall arrangement in the corner.

Julian looked too. “Has that been there all week?”

“Yes,” three people said together.

Eve laughed with them. It was an easy joke, but their shared answer made her newly aware of the week she had not inhabited. They knew which cupboard concealed bad coffee. They knew what happened when the printer warmed up. Somewhere there would be an account of a wrong turning or a late lunch that had already become shorthand.

She could not enter those stories by standing closer to Julian.

The man at the whiteboard handed him a stapled packet.

“Your notes are on the second page.”

“Thank you.”

“And no preparing the whole exercise tonight. We divide it tomorrow.”

Julian raised a hand in surrender.

Eve knew that gesture too. Seeing another person anticipate his tendency to overprepare produced an unexpectedly complicated feeling. She was pleased he had been noticed accurately. She also disliked, for half a breath, not being the only person doing the noticing.

It was not an admirable feeling. It did not become more true because she was embarrassed by it.

She shifted her scarf and asked whether the morning had been very long.

“Useful,” Julian said. “Long as well.”

The woman laughed. “He has rewritten the same question about six times.”

“Seven,” he said.

They said goodbye. Nobody tried to keep him or competed for the rest of his afternoon. Outside, the air felt colder after the warm building. Julian adjusted the camera strap and offered Eve his free hand.

She took it.

For the first street she talked about the shoot. The stairs, the vase, the organizer's nervous laugh. Julian listened and asked whether she had got what the client needed. She had, she thought. She could not know what every delivered image would feel like to them until they saw it.

At the crossing he squeezed her hand gently.

“Are you all right?”

“Yes. A little tired.”

“That was an early train.”

“It was.”

The light changed. She walked with him across the road and decided not to let tiredness take responsibility for everything.

“I felt oddly outside things in there.”

He turned his head, then looked back toward the pavement ahead so they would not walk into a sign.

“I didn't mean to leave you waiting.”

“I know. That isn't exactly it.”

She searched for a way to describe it without making him apologize for having a week.

“You have people here who know what you're doing. I have messages about jars.”

He stopped beside a low wall, away from the flow of people.

“I haven't explained much.”

“You've been tired. And I haven't asked much beyond whether you ate.”

“I have eaten.”

“An outstanding start.”

He smiled, but he did not try to laugh the entire feeling away.

Eve looked at the packet under his arm.

“What were you learning?”

He began with the title of the exercise. It involved communication around realistic home-care plans, with people alternating between professional, owner, and observer roles. Eve listened to three sentences and held up a hand.

“Less heading. More what happened.”

Julian considered.

“I asked someone what they could manage. Then I filled the silence with what I thought they ought to be able to manage.”

“Oh.”

“In real work I have appointment times, a patient to think about, other people waiting. Here they can stop me and say, you did it again. It's uncomfortable.”

“Even when you know the answer?”

“Especially when I think I know the question.”

She could see him in the room again, looking down at the page while somebody younger or older or simply different pointed to a line. The scene had seemed to show a part of him belonging elsewhere. Now it seemed to show work he had chosen because he wanted to come back better at something difficult.

“Was the person pretending to be an owner?” she asked.

“Yes. A fictional scenario. We aren't discussing anyone's private case.”

He explained how the exercise had changed when he waited long enough for an answer. The imagined difficulty was not understanding the instruction. It was getting home in time to follow it. His first version had solved the wrong problem with increasing eloquence.

Eve thought of *the obvious cupboard* and her own attempts to explain Robin's job back to them.

“That sounds irritatingly transferable.”

“I was hoping to keep it professional.”

“No chance.”

They resumed walking. Julian told her about the other parts of the course without pretending that every one of them had transformed his life. Some were familiar. One was taught in a style that made him lose concentration. Another had exposed a gap he hadn't expected. He had a list of questions to take back to the clinic, not a new rank above everyone there.

Eve asked which part he wanted to try again. His answer took longer, and she liked hearing him find it.

“Could you tell me one of those questions when you get back?” she asked. “Not now if you need to keep thinking.”

“Yes. Would you tell me which photographs the organizers choose?”

She glanced at him. “Even the ones without the vase?”

“Especially those. I want to know whether removing it was worth the diplomatic cost.”

That was more useful than asking for every detail of each other's days. A question could wait for an answer without turning into a demand for proof. Eve felt herself grow curious about the room she had photographed again, rather than merely relieved that it had paid for most of the journey.

At a bakery window they stopped to look at an enormous iced bun. Neither of them wanted it enough to carry the sticky thing down the street.

“I wanted you to be glad I came,” she admitted.

Julian turned fully toward her.

“I am glad.”

“I know that now. I was inventing strange tests while you were busy becoming less eloquent.”

He laughed, then touched her cold fingers to his cheek for a moment.

“I can be very ineloquent about you if that helps.”

“Please don't do a workshop.”

“No handouts.”

She leaned into him. They stood there long enough for a woman behind the bakery glass to glance at them and then return to arranging buns. Eve was relieved that they were not interesting enough to interrupt her work.

When they moved on, she asked what he needed to do before his evening.

“Laundry,” he said reluctantly. “And buy things that can become lunches.”

“That sounds like one supermarket.”

“There are nicer places.”

“I already visited a very impressive staircase today.”

He looked at her, checking. She nodded toward the street.

“Show me where you buy your terrible lunches. We can improve one of them.”

Julian shifted her bag to his other shoulder, and they went to find a basket.
