# Chapter 045 — I Don't Know What He's Thinking

The cupboard door had been waiting to fall off for three weeks.

Eve knew this because the first message she had sent about the loose hinge contained a photograph of a tea towel she had since washed, lost, and found again behind the radiator. The repair appointment, after two changes of time, was finally for Thursday morning.

She cleared the counter before the maintenance worker arrived. Loose screws and a curious cat seemed like a combination that didn't need experimental investigation.

Dumpling watched from the kitchen threshold while she moved the cereal, the cups, and a jar of rice. When the worker knocked, he rose and followed her halfway down the hall.

The man outside wore a dark waterproof jacket and carried a toolbox whose handle squeaked. He gave the name and appointment details she was expecting. Eve checked them, let him in, and showed him the hinge.

Dumpling stopped at the sight of the toolbox. The man shifted it to his other hand; metal rattled inside. The cat moved backward, turned, and disappeared behind the sofa.

“Not a fan,” the worker said.

“He doesn't—” Eve began.

She stopped before finishing with *like you*.

The man hadn't approached him. He had arrived in an unfamiliar coat, smelling of rain and whatever had happened in the last kitchen, and brought a box that made a noise. Dumpling's retreat could be accommodated without becoming a character reference.

“I'll keep him out of the work area,” she said.

She put his water and familiar blanket in the bedroom. He came to inspect the arrangement, followed by the soft sound of the cupboard being opened. Eve waited until he settled on the bed, then closed the door so he couldn't wander among the tools.

The worker unscrewed the loose hinge. One of the fixing holes had widened, allowing the screw to turn without gripping. He showed her the problem, explained the repair, and asked her to keep the cupboard clear until he had finished.

Eve stayed at the other end of the kitchen with her laptop. He wasn't chatty. Neither was she. The useful thing about his visit was that the cupboard door eventually stayed where it was supposed to stay.

When he had packed up, she checked the counter and floor for loose pieces before opening the bedroom. Dumpling came out, inspected the doorway, and sat beside the empty space where the toolbox had been.

“Your assessment?” she asked.

He sniffed the floor.

“Yes. Mine too.”

She made tea in the newly uneventful kitchen and opened the notebook she had used for the cups. Between two untidy pages of observations was a note she had begun and abandoned: *The first time I knew he was warning me—*

Eve read it twice.

Then she crossed out *knew*.

At one of the early events, Dumpling had avoided a visitor. Eve had been ready to let that avoidance tell her what sort of person had come through the door. She remembered the folded event card and the chair she'd nearly made him cross the room to reach. Only conversation had revealed the ordinary concern she hadn't bothered to imagine.

She remembered the heat of embarrassment more clearly than the exact sequence. Memory had already improved her role, making it seem as though she had corrected herself almost immediately. She wasn't certain she had been that quick.

On a fresh line she wrote: *He moved away. I began to distrust someone before I listened.*

No name. No private circumstances copied from another person's difficulty. The mistake she needed to preserve was her own.

Below it she added the photo room: the closed carrier, the unfamiliar sounds, the temptation to make Dumpling's reluctance an answer about Felix. Felix had accepted the stopped trial. Later he had negotiated a smaller job, requested an ordinary revision, and paid the agreed invoice. Those actions had told her considerably more about working with him than the cat's choice of where to sit.

She shut the notebook before she could turn the morning into a comprehensive theory.

There was work to finish. A caption needed shortening; a file needed renaming; a client had supplied a photograph without telling her which version of the product it showed. She sent the question, completed the parts she could complete, and ate lunch before the food cooled.

Julian was coming for tea after work.

She had asked the previous evening because she wanted company and because she thought he might enjoy hearing about the murder mystery with the inconvenient bus stop. He had said he could stay for half an hour. Neither of them had attached a work subject to the invitation.

At four, Eve put her notebook in the desk drawer and took down two cups. She considered the blue plastic ones, then chose the mismatched ceramic pair she normally used for guests. Some objects deserved time away from inquiry.

Julian arrived carrying no clinic folder and looking as though he had remembered, on the way out, that his jacket had pockets. He spent several seconds locating his phone before switching it to silent.

“I've confirmed there's nothing I need to answer for the next half hour,” he said.

“That's a much better opening than telling me you're probably about to be called away.”

“I'm refining my technique.”

Dumpling came to the hall, recognized him, and rubbed against his trouser leg. Julian let him sniff a lowered hand, then followed Eve into the kitchen.

The cupboard door stood closed.

“You fixed it?” he asked.

“Someone qualified to have opinions about hinges fixed it. This morning.”

“Good.”

“He and Dumpling didn't become friends.”

Julian glanced at the cat, who had followed them and was now investigating the empty chair.

“Was it noisy?”

“Some of it. I put him in the bedroom while the tools were out. He settled on the blanket.”

She poured the tea. Julian moved the nearest mug away from the table edge as Dumpling jumped onto the chair, an ordinary precaution performed without interrupting their conversation.

“Did he come back out afterward?”

“Yes. Immediately conducted a floor inspection. I don't know what he thought of the man.”

Julian looked up with a small change in expression.

“What?” Eve asked.

“Nothing.”

“No, that was a specific nothing.”

He accepted the cup she handed him.

“You used to sound more certain about his opinions.”

Eve sat down. There was a biscuit tin between them, one corner dented from its previous career as a container for camera attachments. She opened it to discover that the biscuits had settled into the shape of the dent.

“I used to say cats know everything.”

“Frequently.”

“It saves time when you don't want to explain yourself.”

“And now?”

She turned one broken biscuit so it would fit on a plate.

“I don't know what he's thinking,” she said. “And I know even less about what everyone around him is thinking. I kept treating those as the same question.”

Julian waited.

The waiting was new enough for her to notice and familiar enough now that she could use it. She didn't have to rush toward a joke before he took the conversational space away.

“I've made some wrong guesses,” she continued. “Then I remembered the moments when I seemed right much more clearly. It's an excellent system if the goal is to keep agreeing with yourself.”

“Most of us are quite accomplished at that.”

“Have you included yourself?”

“Yes. Reluctantly.”

She smiled, then looked at Dumpling. He was pressing one paw against Julian's jacket where it hung over the chair back. There were things Eve still couldn't explain, and the closed drawer wasn't an answer to them. But she didn't need to turn this conversation into a revelation she hadn't prepared to make. What she had said was true without pretending it was the whole mystery.

“I wanted him to make people easier,” she said. “Especially when I was afraid of getting something wrong.”

Julian took a biscuit from the plate.

“People are inconveniently variable.”

“Even vets?”

“Especially vets before lunch.”

They ate for a moment. The flat was quiet enough that Eve could hear Dumpling's claw catch lightly in the jacket lining. Julian freed it without taking the jacket away, then folded the loose edge out of reach.

“What changed?” he asked.

“A few things. A friend told me she wasn't ready to explain something. It turned out waiting worked better than guessing.”

Eve left Sophie's work circumstances where they belonged. Julian didn't ask who or what she meant.

“And I noticed how willing I was to count looking at something as an answer,” she added. “Depending on what answer I wanted.”

“That sounds familiar too.”

She looked at him.

He rolled a crumb between his fingers, considering.

“Sometimes I think I've understood a question before the person has finished asking it,” he said. “Then I answer the one I expected.”

Eve remembered their walk after the clinic work, the difficulty of telling him what his interruptions did. He didn't present this as a new discovery that erased the apology. He simply seemed to be finding the habit in another place.

“I'm glad you're letting me finish this one,” she said.

“So am I.”

The words were plain. They warmed her more than she expected.

To give the feeling somewhere ordinary to go, Eve reached for the mystery novel on the sideboard and showed him the map printed inside its cover. The house occupied the center of a dramatically empty landscape. The bus stop had been omitted.

“Misleading cartography,” Julian said.

“Exactly. The murderer could have taken public transport.”

“Was there a timetable?”

“No.”

“Then the bus may have been more mysterious than the crime.”

Eve laughed and told him about the detective who had measured the distance to the station while ignoring the bus, and the suspect whose supposedly impossible arrival could have been achieved with a return ticket. Julian described a radio play in which he had spent most of an episode confusing two characters because they both appeared to be named Tom. One had eventually turned out to be a town.

“That sounds difficult to dramatize,” she said.

“There were clues. I was making dinner.”

When his half hour was almost over, he checked the time and stood. Eve didn't turn the end into evidence that he wanted to leave her. He had told her how long he could stay, and they had spent that time together.

Dumpling occupied the jacket as soon as Julian tried to lift it. Eve gently encouraged him onto the adjacent cushion with a familiar toy, then put the toy away when he lost interest. No garment needed to become a hostage to prove they had enjoyed tea.

At the door Julian thanked her for the invitation.

“Next time I'll bring a less geographically confusing story,” he said.

“I can't promise the same.”

After he left, Eve washed the cups and opened the cupboard with unnecessary satisfaction. The hinge held. She put them back without having to support the door with her shoulder.

Two work inquiries had arrived while her phone was silent. Each concerned a possible weekend event. She read them, marked the requested dates, and left the replies for the morning, when she could look at her calendar properly.

Dumpling settled on the chair Julian had used.

Eve didn't ask whether that was approval. She sat in the other chair, opened her book, and discovered the final chapter depended on a bicycle nobody had previously mentioned.

Some uncertainties were more irritating than others.
