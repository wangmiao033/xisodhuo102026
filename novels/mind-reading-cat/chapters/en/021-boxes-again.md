# Chapter 021 — Boxes Again

The folding table had solved one problem and quietly become another.

Eve had rented it to keep her laptop, camera, and notebook away from her dinner during the first few jobs. It had a broad white surface, adjustable legs, and a promotional photograph featuring a woman who appeared to own no cables. Now that she knew which equipment actually needed to stay within reach, she could arrange the smaller desk more sensibly.

In Eve's flat, it blocked half the route to the window and collected things she intended to put somewhere else. One corner held the printer she rarely used. Another supported a mug containing rulers, two dead pens, and a spoon whose presence could not be explained by any current project.

Returning it early, before the monthly rental renewed, would prevent another charge and restore a clear path across the room. She checked the collection terms rather than assuming that simply folding it away would stop the bill.

This was sensible. She had done the sums.

Dumpling disagreed with the process before she had even touched the table.

Eve brought the flattened delivery carton out from behind the wardrobe and laid it on the floor. The cat, who had been washing his face, froze with one paw raised.

“It's furniture,” she told him. “Not a rival.”

He lowered his paw and came forward, sniffing along the folded edge. That seemed ordinary enough. Everything entering the flat had to undergo a nose inspection, and cardboard generally received a longer interview than people.

She unfolded the carton. It sprang into a long, awkward shape and knocked a shoe off the rack.

Dumpling retreated beneath the chair.

“Fair. I didn't like that either.”

Eve set the box flat again and moved on to clearing the table. By the time she had found a home for the printer, the spoon, and eleven sheets of paper she did not need, the cat had disappeared.

So had her gloves.

She noticed when the return company called to confirm the collection window. The courier would come the following afternoon. She had to buy a little more packing material before then, and her thin gloves were usually tucked into the side pocket of her outdoor bag.

The pocket was empty.

Eve checked the coat hooks, the shoe rack, and the table she had just cleared. The gloves were not among the dead pens. They had not transformed into a spoon.

“Dumpling?”

An orange ear appeared beneath the sofa.

She crouched. One glove lay beside him. The other was farther back, caught against a dust ball that had been building a quiet civilization.

“You could have asked for softer bedding.”

He put a paw over the nearest glove.

She almost took a photograph. Cat steals owner's winter accessories was harmless enough, certainly more appealing than five practical reminders about checking captions. Then she remembered she actually needed to leave the flat.

She drew a toy along the edge of the rug, waited for him to follow it, and collected the gloves without turning the retrieval into a chase. The dusty one went into the laundry. She went out with bare hands.

At the shop, she bought the smallest roll of protective wrap she could find and no new storage containers. This felt like a significant act of maturity until she realized she had left her shopping list on the folding table, which was precisely the kind of object she was trying to stop organizing her life around.

When she returned, Dumpling waited behind the door.

He did not rub against her ankles immediately. He looked past her, into the corridor, then up at the roll under her arm.

“Only me,” she said.

He followed her to the table.

By evening, one leg had folded properly and the other had trapped the hem of her sleeve. Eve freed herself, said something unhelpful to the hinge, and decided to finish after dinner.

The courier's return label sat on the counter. Her tote bag, emptied for the next morning, hung from the back of a chair.

Dumpling climbed onto the seat, gripped the loose fabric corner, and tugged.

The bag slid down. He startled at its fall, then seized the handle and dragged it toward the sofa.

Eve stood very still.

It was not an impressive theft. The tote was larger than he was, and every few steps he had to stop and rearrange his grip. Had she been looking for a funny clip, the material would have offered itself generously.

Instead she watched him abandon the handle near the sofa and sit beside it, ears moving toward the half-open carton.

She thought of the gloves. Then of the way he had stopped washing when the cardboard came out.

“Is it the noise?” she asked.

He looked at her.

“Or do you just hate an efficient household?”

No answer. Of course there was no answer. A cat watching a box was not a statement, however insistently her imagination tried to supply punctuation.

She picked up the tote and put it inside the wardrobe. He followed her, then returned to the carton.

Eve left the table alone for the night.

She had planned to use the evening for editing. Instead she sat on the rug and sorted a small stack of papers into a rigid folder. Dumpling watched from the chair. He did not move when she shuffled documents. He did move when she lifted the cardboard flap to tuck the return label inside.

His body lowered. He came down from the chair and pressed himself against the side of her leg.

She rested a hand near him, letting him choose whether to lean into it.

The gesture made her throat tighten before she understood why. Packing her belongings after losing her job had made ordinary objects feel painfully temporary; she still remembered trying to fit a working life into things she could carry. Perhaps this carton meant nothing to him beyond a loud, changing object. Perhaps he was responding to the way she kept moving things. Perhaps she was borrowing her own dread of being told to pack and fitting it to his face.

She did not know.

She wrote three plain lines in her notebook: Watched when carton opened. Gloves under sofa. Dragged empty tote after packing started.

Then, underneath: No idea why yet.

The last line felt important enough to underline.

Sophie called while Eve was making tea.

“Did you return the table or did the table return you?”

“Tomorrow. It's a drawn-out separation.”

“Do you need a hand?”

“I think I can manage. The cat is stealing my luggage.”

“Your luggage?”

“A tote bag and some gloves. I'm using the dramatic term.”

Eve explained the order of events. Sophie listened without offering a diagnosis borrowed from a video she'd half watched.

“Would you want me there for the collection?” she asked. “I'm nearby after a meeting, but only until two.”

“That would help. Mainly with the door and the table. Not as an unpaid cat therapist.”

“I was going to put that on my invoice.”

They agreed a time. After the call, Eve moved Dumpling's familiar things into the bedroom, leaving the door open while he inspected them. She did not suddenly shut him away to see whether a theory worked. She wanted a quiet space ready before a stranger arrived carrying furniture through the entrance.

He sniffed the water bowl, scratched once at the edge of the mat, and returned to the living room.

Apparently the arrangements were acceptable, but not sufficient to distract him from management.

The next day, Eve finished packing early. She worked slowly, avoiding a great performance of reassurance every time Dumpling glanced at her. When he went into the bedroom to settle, she let him settle.

Sophie arrived with a bakery bag and the brisk air of somebody determined not to become part of a furniture rental dispute.

“You've made it bigger,” she observed.

“The packaging adds gravitas.”

“The packaging has its own postcode.”

They wrapped the table, secured the carton, and propped it where the courier could lift it without crossing the whole flat. Eve made sure Dumpling was comfortable in the quiet room before closing its door for the short collection. He complained once, a small, sharp sound that made her hand pause on the handle.

She stayed beside the door until the courier rang, then went to meet him.

The collection took six minutes.

Six ordinary minutes of checking a label, turning a long box sideways, and apologizing to Sophie when one end brushed her sleeve. Nobody carried Eve away with it. Nobody changed the locks. The corridor returned to its usual smell of old paint and someone's lunch.

She opened the bedroom door.

Dumpling came out cautiously, stopped at the empty patch by the window, and inspected the floor where the table had been. He circled it once, then looked behind the curtain, as though the missing object might have become embarrassed and hidden there.

Sophie put two pastries on plates.

“How much room does one cat need to conduct an investigation?” she asked.

“All of it, apparently.”

Eve sat at her smaller desk. It was crowded but usable. The printer fit on the lower shelf, the cables had a hook, and the spoon was back in the kitchen where it had always belonged.

The collection receipt arrived by email while she was untangling the last cable. She opened it, checked that the return had been recorded, and saved it beside the original rental agreement. The empty space would be less comforting if the company charged her for keeping a table she no longer had.

Avoiding the next rental charge would not rescue her finances. It did mean she wouldn't keep paying for extra space once she'd found a workable way to use what she already had.

Dumpling approached the chair. He sniffed her shoe, then settled beside it rather than on top of the bag now safely stored away.

Eve wanted to declare that he understood. The temptation was almost physical. A neat ending: frightened cat learns his person will stay.

But one quiet afternoon could not tell her that. She had seen a pattern, not read a story written inside him.

Sophie finished her pastry and checked the time.

“I have to go.”

“I know. Thanks for the actual help.”

“As opposed to the imaginary help?”

“The kind I invent for people before asking.”

Her friend raised a crumb-covered finger. “A dangerous industry.”

After Sophie left, Eve washed both plates and found the second glove in the laundry basket where she herself had put it. She had already started looking toward the sofa.

That was worth remembering too.

She added one more line to the notebook: Don't blame him for every missing thing.

Then she put the notebook away and sat by the cleared window. Dumpling came over, considered her lap, and chose the warmer square of floor instead.

Outside, a delivery van stopped. He lifted his head at the sound of its doors, watched Eve for a moment, and settled again.

She stayed where she was—not to prove anything, not because he had commanded it, but because the tea was still hot and she had finally made room to sit.
