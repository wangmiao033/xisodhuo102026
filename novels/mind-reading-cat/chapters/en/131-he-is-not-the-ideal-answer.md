# Chapter 131 — He Is Not the Ideal Answer

The cake box would not fit between Julian's knees and the seat in front of him.

He tried turning it. Eve moved her bag. The passenger beside the aisle stood to let somebody through, and for a moment all three of them appeared to be participating in a poorly rehearsed delivery.

“Your mother said not to bring anything,” Julian said.

“She says that when she means nothing enormous.”

“You selected the cake.”

“I underestimated its transport requirements.”

They finally put the box on Eve's lap. Through the little window in the lid she could see one strawberry leaning against the cardboard. It would not survive the journey in the position the baker intended.

She found this oddly comforting.

Her mother knew who was coming. Eve had asked whether she would like to have lunch with them, confirmed that no one else was invited, and told Julian the return train she hoped to catch. The arrangements had been made days ago. This morning her mother had sent a photograph of the table, asking whether the flowers were too tall.

Eve had replied that they would probably be able to recognize each other around them.

Now, approaching the familiar station, she wished she had brought a smaller cake and worn something less susceptible to creasing.

“Anything else I should know?” Julian asked.

“The rice corners are good. If she offers you the last one, take it.”

“I meant about the visit.”

“So did I.”

He looked at her until she smiled.

“She'll ask about work,” Eve said. “Possibly housing. You don't have to tell her anything you don't want to.”

“What have you told her?”

“That we're dating. That I like you. That you're employed and capable of buying your own lunch.”

“An ambitious introduction.”

“And that you aren't coming to explain my accounts.”

He nodded. She reached across the box and put her hand over his. For the remainder of the journey, they watched the buildings change without rehearsing answers.

Her mother opened the door wearing an apron Eve remembered from years ago. She greeted Julian by name, then looked at the box.

“Oh, you shouldn't have.”

“It's suffered enough already,” Eve said. “Let us bring it in.”

The house smelled of baked rice. The flowers had moved to the sideboard, and three places were set at the table. Her mother took Julian's coat and asked whether the journey had been all right.

“Fine, thank you. The cake may disagree.”

Her mother laughed. Eve felt her own shoulders lower by a fraction.

In the kitchen, the tap ran without its former irregular knocking. Her mother had finally had it repaired. Eve noticed Julian looking toward the sound and almost supplied its entire history, including the wrong people who had been asked to examine it. Instead she accepted the jug her mother handed her.

“Water on the table, please.”

Eve carried it through. Julian was studying a small framed photograph on the shelf: Eve at school age, glaring at a paper crown that had slipped over one eyebrow.

“An early management role?” he asked quietly.

“I was a tree.”

“An angry tree.”

“They gave another girl the watering can.”

Her mother came in behind them. “You refused to wave your branches.”

“My artistic judgment remains sound.”

They were all laughing when they sat down. For the first several minutes, lunch was simply lunch. Julian accepted the corner piece. Her mother asked how he had chosen veterinary work, and he described liking science and finding the practical training more interesting than he had expected. He did not produce an account of a childhood animal whose noble suffering had determined his destiny.

“Were your family pleased?” her mother asked.

“Pleased when I finished studying. They'd heard quite enough about examinations by then.”

Eve watched him pass the vegetables. He was being polite without putting on the professional voice he used for recorded introductions. She wanted to touch his knee beneath the table and resisted, partly because the table was small enough for mistakes.

“And the clinic is yours?” her mother asked.

“No. Dr. Clark runs it. I'm employed there.”

“Oh. Eve said you had a lot of responsibility.”

“I do. Ownership is a different responsibility.”

“Yes, of course.” Her mother served herself more rice. “But you have a regular salary.”

“I do.”

“That must be a relief.”

Eve knew the next glance before it arrived. It touched her face and moved away.

“The regular part helps,” Julian said. “There are still costs to plan for.”

“Professional fees?”

“Those, training, ordinary living expenses. I save what I can.”

Eve had expected a question about hours, perhaps an anecdote about a neighbor's dog. The conversation had arrived at his savings before the vegetables were finished.

Her mother looked genuinely interested. “Do you rent near the clinic?”

“Yes.”

“Not buying yet?”

“No.”

He said it so simply that Eve nearly laughed. Her mother waited for the qualifying clause. None came.

“Prices are difficult,” she supplied.

“They are.”

“Sometimes it's worth getting somewhere small before they rise further.”

“Sometimes. It depends on what you can afford and whether you want to stay.”

“Do you not want to stay?”

Eve put down her fork. “Mom, he's describing a decision. He hasn't announced he's moving away.”

“I was asking.”

“I know. Can he finish eating before the housing survey?”

Her mother's mouth tightened. Julian looked down at his plate, which did indeed still contain half his lunch.

“It's very good,” he said.

“The rice?”

“Yes.”

Her mother offered him more. He accepted a small helping, and the conversation turned to the oven's unreliable timer. Eve was grateful, then annoyed with herself for being grateful to an appliance.

After lunch, she helped clear while Julian carried the jug into the kitchen. Her mother directed him to the sitting room with his tea and opened a cupboard for the cake plates.

“He seems sensible,” she said quietly.

“He is.”

“A little cautious.”

“He's meeting you.”

Her mother gave her a look. Eve dried the serving spoon rather than apologize for a joke that had come too easily.

“I only wondered whether he had plans,” her mother said.

“You can ask about his work. You don't need to establish what property he might supply.”

“That isn't fair.”

Eve looked up. Her mother's face had changed, and the change was enough to make her slow down.

“I worry about you having nowhere secure,” her mother said. “Both of you paying rent, both of you working so much. Everything keeps costing more.”

“I worry about those things too.”

“Then why do you sound as though I'm insulting him?”

Because Eve had been listening for an insult since the question about the clinic. She could admit that much without handing over the rest.

“I expected to have to defend him,” she said. “I started before you finished.”

Her mother set the plates on the counter.

“I don't dislike him.”

“Good. I wanted you to meet someone I like. I wasn't bringing an answer to the rent problem.”

From the sitting room came the quiet sound of Julian setting his cup down. Eve was suddenly conscious that quiet voices were not invisible ones.

She picked up the cake knife.

“Let's feed him the collapsed strawberry.”

Her mother opened the box. The strawberry had fallen onto its side but done little damage.

“That's easily fixed.”

“Please leave it. We've become attached.”

They carried the plates through. Julian stood to help, and her mother told him to sit down with a firmness Eve recognized from every visiting adult of her childhood.

Tea was easier. Her mother asked about Dumpling, who had remained at home with his normal afternoon arrangements. Eve showed her a recent photograph of him lying with one back foot against the wall. Julian explained nothing extraordinary. The cat looked entirely capable of creating his own minor problems.

Then her mother brought out a folded property supplement.

Eve stared at it.

“It came with the paper,” her mother said. “I'm not suggesting you buy anything today.”

“No one has brought a pen,” Julian said.

Eve laughed before she could help herself. Her mother smiled reluctantly and opened the supplement on the low table.

A development near a different station advertised small flats beneath a picture of a room containing almost no possessions. Eve wondered where the imaginary residents kept their vacuum cleaner.

“They say the commute is reasonable,” her mother said.

“For whom?” Julian asked.

“Well. Into the city.”

“That could mean several things.”

“Would you look at it properly? Just see whether it's worth keeping in mind.”

Eve felt the old reflex to agree so the paper could be folded and the afternoon could move on. Julian had not answered. She could make the whole question disappear with a cheerful promise to look later.

Instead she said, “You can keep the leaflet, Mom. We aren't looking for a home together.”

Her mother glanced between them. “I didn't say together.”

The picture on the front showed a couple in a kitchen. Nobody mentioned it.

“I'm not looking to buy on my own either,” Julian said. “I haven't budgeted for it.”

There was a short silence. Eve's mother folded the supplement less neatly than she had opened it.

“All right. More tea?”

“Yes, please,” Julian said.

He neither softened the answer into a future commitment nor made the refusal sound like a rebuke. Eve found the steadiness attractive in a way that had nothing to do with a salary.

Before they left, her mother packed rice into a container for Eve and cake into a smaller one for Julian. She asked him whether he preferred the piece with fruit. He said yes. She gave him that piece and told him to come again when his hours allowed.

At the door, she straightened Eve's collar, then stopped herself from doing the same to Julian.

“Safe journey,” she said.

They walked toward the station with one container each. Eve's face felt tired from all the expressions she had prepared and not used.

“How was that?” she asked.

Julian considered. “The cake traveled less well on the way there.”

“That's not an answer.”

“The lunch was good. Some questions were difficult.”

“I'm sorry about the flat.”

“You didn't print the supplement.”

“I nearly volunteered us to examine it.”

“I noticed you didn't.”

At the crossing, she reached for his free hand. He took hers, shifting his cake to the other side.

“I didn't need you to sound richer,” she said.

“I couldn't have sustained it beyond a second visit.”

She smiled, but continued seriously. “Or more certain. I liked having you there.”

“I liked seeing the angry tree.”

“There are other photographs.”

“I hope so.”

The signal changed. They crossed together, and Eve chose the correct end of the platform this time. On the train, Julian's smaller container fitted easily on his lap.

Her mother's message arrived before they reached the city.

He was very polite. Tell him the container can come back whenever.

Eve showed him the screen. Then she answered that she would, and put the phone away before turning a tolerable afternoon into a request for a final verdict.
