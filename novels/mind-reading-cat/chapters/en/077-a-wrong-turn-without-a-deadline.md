# Chapter 077 — A Wrong Turn Without a Deadline

The entrance was easy to find. Getting through it was another matter.

A sheet of paper had been taped to the glass door at eye level. Beneath the printed exhibition dates, somebody had written that the building was closed for the afternoon because of a plumbing problem. A blue bucket stood behind the door, doing little to improve the explanation.

Eve looked at the leaflet in Julian's hand.

He looked at the bucket.

“I checked the opening hours yesterday,” he said.

“Apparently nobody consulted the pipes.”

He took out his phone. “There may be another—”

“Wait.”

Eve surprised herself with the word. A minute earlier, she had been reaching mentally for the same solution: locate an equivalent experience, calculate walking time, rescue the empty afternoon before it could be judged a failure.

Julian lowered the phone.

“Did you particularly want the exhibition?” he asked.

“I would have liked it. But I don't need an emergency replacement drawing.”

His mouth moved into a smile.

“Good. I'm not qualified to provide one.”

They stepped aside so an approaching couple could read the notice. The woman sighed, the man proposed coffee, and both left with an efficiency Eve admired. Nobody stood beneath the sign apologizing for having believed in Sunday opening hours.

“Would you rather walk?” Julian asked.

“Yes. Somewhere without a booking.”

He put away the phone and folded the leaflet along its existing crease. Eve liked that he didn't throw it away as though the afternoon had personally betrayed him.

There were two possible directions. One led back toward the restaurant and the streets they knew. The other sloped gently downhill past a row of small shops. Eve chose the slope. Julian fell into step beside her, and neither announced a destination.

This felt agreeable for approximately forty seconds.

Then she noticed a street sign and began estimating how long it would take to reach the river. There was probably a footpath. There might be a café. A café might close early, which would make it sensible to check now rather than discover another locked door.

She laughed at herself.

“What?” Julian asked.

“I'm arranging our unarranged walk.”

“Is it going well?”

“We have a proposed timetable and an unresolved refreshments issue.”

“I still have most of that stew inside me.”

“So the catering can wait.”

“For some time.”

They passed a hardware shop with an entire window devoted to door handles. Eve stopped at one shaped so oddly that she couldn't tell which end she was supposed to press.

Julian leaned nearer the glass.

“That would catch a sleeve.”

“You sound personally offended.”

“I've met its relatives.”

She remembered his cupboard-handle opinions from the evening they had moved the cabinet in a circle. Here was an opportunity to hear them without a screwdriver waiting to make the conversation useful.

“Go on,” she said.

He glanced at her. “Really?”

“I've been promised this explanation for days.”

He described a cupboard in a previous flat whose handles were exactly far enough apart to catch both pockets of a coat. Eve imagined him stranded in a narrow kitchen and began to laugh before he finished.

“I had to take the coat off.”

“No.”

“Yes.”

“Did anybody see?”

“Unfortunately, the person helping me move.”

“Did they help?”

“After an unacceptable delay.”

She leaned against the wall, laughing in a way that made her earrings move. He watched her with an expression she wasn't going to label for him, though she hoped he was enjoying it.

“I think the delay was reasonable,” she said.

“You weren't there.”

“That's the worst part.”

A customer came out carrying a broom. They moved away from the door, still smiling. The story had taken three minutes and changed nothing practical. Eve would have been sorry to lose it.

Farther down, a narrow alley seemed to offer a shortcut toward the water. They followed it until it ended at a delivery yard with no public exit.

A man unloading cartons shook his head before Julian could ask.

“Back the way you came. Everyone tries that.”

“Thank you,” Eve said.

They turned around. Julian lifted the folded leaflet as if it were a white flag.

“Our navigation has a consistent theme.”

“I chose this one. We can distribute the blame fairly.”

“Shall we?”

“No. I'm enjoying not having an audience for it.”

He looked at her as they emerged onto the pavement.

“Is that what work feels like? An audience for the wrong turns?”

“Sometimes. Sometimes the audience is only me, but she is extremely persistent.”

They waited at a crossing. A bicycle passed, its front basket full of something green and leafy. Eve watched a loose leaf flutter onto the road and resisted making it an image about freedom. It was probably somebody's dinner.

“I used to go places and think about what I could make from them,” she said. “A post. A pitch. Something to show I hadn't wasted the day.”

“Used to?”

“I'm trying a more ambitious tense.”

He nodded without letting her pretend the habit had vanished.

“I do something similar with time off,” he said. “If I don't catch up on everything, I think I haven't used it properly.”

“Did you catch up this morning?”

“No. We have established the washing powder.”

She laughed. “A serious unresolved matter.”

The crossing signal changed. They walked across together without touching, close enough that she noticed when his sleeve moved beside hers.

A secondhand shop had put a box of postcards outside. Eve stopped to look while Julian examined a chipped model lighthouse on the sill. Most of the cards showed places she had never visited, their colors brighter than any weather she remembered. She selected one with a crooked little street and turned it over.

“Already written on,” she said.

Julian kept his eyes on the lighthouse rather than leaning over her shoulder.

She read only enough to recognize a private holiday greeting, then put the card back without showing it to him. Another card was blank. She bought that one for a small coin, not because it was the best image in the box but because she liked its absurdly red doorway.

“Who is it for?” he asked when she returned.

“Possibly my own wall.”

“A demanding recipient.”

“She has very little space, but excellent taste in doors.”

The river was farther away than she had thought. When they finally reached the path, the water was brown rather than picturesque, with a floating stick making steady progress toward somewhere neither of them needed to go. A child in bright boots counted ducks while an adult tried to count the child's remaining gloves.

Eve sat on the end of a bench. Julian asked whether the space beside her was dry before sitting, which seemed a sensible refinement of romance.

“It is,” she said. “I conducted a brief inspection.”

“Any findings?”

“One old leaf. No major defects.”

He sat down. They watched the ducks without naming them or assigning them their own relationship problems.

After a while, Eve took out her phone.

The view wasn't remarkable, but a low branch crossed the water in a shape she liked. She framed it automatically, adjusted the angle to exclude a rubbish bin, and noticed Julian waiting while she searched for a better version of the afternoon.

She lowered the phone.

“You can take a picture,” he said.

“I know. I'm deciding whether I want this picture or whether I want to prove I went somewhere.”

He looked toward the branch. “It is a good branch.”

“Strong recommendation.”

“I don't know its other work.”

She laughed, took one photograph with the rubbish bin still at the edge, and put the phone away. She didn't check which account might suit it. She might delete it later. The branch would survive.

Julian pointed to a dark object in the water. “Is that another duck?”

“It hasn't moved.”

“Patient duck.”

They watched until the current turned it slightly and revealed a discarded ball. Neither was disappointed enough to require a better identification story.

Eve rested her hands in her lap. For the first time since leaving home, she forgot to wonder how she looked sitting there. Her shirt was creased where the strap of her shoulder bag had pressed against it. Lunch had left a small mark near one cuff. She rubbed it with her thumb, then stopped before spreading it.

Julian noticed.

“Sauce?”

“Possibly. I'm calling it evidence that lunch happened.”

“A stronger record than my leaflet.”

He unfolded it to look at the exhibition illustration. Eve leaned closer. The featured drawing was a row of rooftops, with one washing line running between buildings.

“I like that,” she said.

“So do I.”

“We could come another day.”

“Yes.”

He didn't immediately suggest a date or open a booking page. The possibility lay between them without becoming an obligation to repair today.

A gust of wind turned the leaflet inside out. Julian caught it badly, and Eve put her hand over one corner before it escaped. For a second their fingers pressed against the same thin paper.

She felt the contact far more strongly than the event deserved.

Then he took the leaflet, folded it, and put it away. No one made a joke about static electricity or the temperature. Eve looked at the river while her pulse returned to a less theatrical pace.

“Cold?” he asked after a minute.

“A little now.”

“Shall we keep walking?”

She nodded. They stood, checked the bench for forgotten things, and followed the path toward a footbridge visible around the bend.

Halfway there, Eve's phone vibrated with an ordinary work message. The preview showed a request about Monday's file. She checked enough to establish it wasn't a same-day problem, then slipped it back into her bag.

Julian didn't congratulate her for ignoring it. He asked whether she wanted to cross the bridge or stay on this side.

“This side,” she said. “I don't know where the other end goes.”

“I thought that was the point.”

She looked at him and smiled. “I can be spontaneous within a modest walking radius.”

“A sensible specialty.”

The path narrowed near a hedge. They walked one behind the other for a short distance, and Eve watched the back of his coat without trying to imagine it permanently in her wardrobe. She wanted another afternoon like this. That was already something she could want without building a household around it.

When the path widened, Julian slowed so she could come beside him again.

“Are you disappointed about the exhibition?” he asked.

“A little. Not about the afternoon.”

“Neither am I.”

She looked at his face, and he didn't look away to make the answer less plain.

Behind them, the closed gallery remained closed. Nobody had saved the booking, found an alternative attraction, or produced a story worth posting. Eve had learned about a trapped coat, mistaken a ball for a duck, and taken a photograph with a rubbish bin in it.

Ahead, the path bent out of sight.

She let it.
