# Chapter 018 — A Face Isn't an Answer

By breakfast, Julian had become the most successful thing Eve had ever put on the internet.

This was irritating for several reasons. He had contributed no dance, no confession, and no opinion about the texture of expensive coffee. He had merely looked up from a notebook and said, “You don't have to remember everything before you come in.”

The longer clinic video had been checked and approved. Its audience was modest. The short version on Cat Pays Rent, however, had escaped the neighborhood and found people who apparently had a deep interest in veterinary medicine, provided veterinary medicine had dark hair and rolled-up sleeves.

Eve ate toast over the counter while the numbers climbed.

One comment asked where the clinic was. Another said the speaker seemed kind. Three people asked whether he was married. Someone announced that they were about to adopt a dog for unrelated reasons.

“Please do not,” Eve said to her phone.

Dumpling, at her feet, looked up.

“Not you. You're already a bad financial decision.”

He turned his back and began washing a shoulder, unimpressed by a household whose jokes depended on him understanding bookkeeping.

She answered the practical question with the clinic's public information. She ignored the marriage investigation. Then she opened the editing project again.

There was nothing wrong with a handsome face attracting attention. A good entrance didn't invalidate the room behind it. At her previous job, she had spent entire mornings convincing people of that. Useful information could be appealing. Serious work did not have to arrive wrapped in gray cloth.

She made a second, faster clip.

At the beginning, an empty carrier sat beside her own anxious hands. She had filmed the shot at home, without Dumpling inside it. Then Julian glanced toward her, calm and attentive, while she laid words across the image: The vet isn't worried. You don't have to be either.

His answer about forgetting a question followed. She trimmed away her prompt and the middle of the sentence so the strongest reassurance arrived first.

It was neat. It was warm. It was eleven seconds long.

She told herself it was an introduction to the full video, which she linked in the caption. The clinic had approved the material. This was simply a different doorway.

She posted it before her toast went cold.

The second clip moved even faster than the first.

By late morning, she had stopped pretending not to refresh. She carried her phone to the window for better light, though the light had no effect on the numbers. She opened an old proposal, wrote half a sentence, and checked again. It was the same sick little pulse she remembered from campaign launches, except now the account belonged to her.

At twelve seventeen, Julian sent a message.

Could you take down the new short version while we check it?

She read it twice.

The first hot thought was that he disliked being called attractive. The second was worse: perhaps he disliked being called attractive on her account.

She typed, Is this about the comments?

His reply came a minute later.

No. The edit changes what I'm answering.

Eve set the phone down. Then she picked it up and watched the clip without looking at its view count.

The anxious hands. The carrier. His steady expression. Her headline.

The question he had actually answered was whether people sometimes forgot what they meant to ask. Without that question, his response could seem to concern whatever worried a viewer about an animal. Her link to the longer explanation was underneath the video. The eleven seconds had already traveled without it.

She removed the short clip.

The confirmation button was small. Pressing it felt absurdly like taking a chair away just as she had finally been invited to sit down.

It's down, she wrote. I see the problem. Can we speak for ten minutes when you're free?

He offered a time near the end of the afternoon, not immediately. She appreciated that in principle and resented it for the next forty minutes.

Sophie called while Eve was lining up the original footage and the new cut.

“I have twelve minutes before someone asks me where eighty napkin rings have gone,” her friend said. “Are you alive?”

“Professionally, in a disputed condition.”

Eve explained. She tried to make it funny, but the part about the numbers came out too sharply.

“So you cut out the question?” Sophie asked.

“Yes. For pace.”

“And put a different one on top?”

“Not a question. A line.”

“Does the punctuation matter?”

Eve stared at the caption. “No.”

“Then there you are.”

“It was doing well.”

“I know.”

That was more helpful than being told numbers didn't matter. They mattered. They could become work. They could become proof that her ideas still reached people. Knowing that did not make this particular edit accurate.

Sophie exhaled. Somewhere behind her, somebody called for a stepladder.

“Keep the nice face if the nice face agrees,” she said. “Just let it answer the question it was asked.”

At the clinic, Eve left her phone in her bag until Julian had finished what he was doing. Tessa directed her to the small staff table, where a box of tea bags occupied a space much larger than its importance warranted.

Julian arrived carrying a mug and the printout of an appointment list, which he folded before sitting down. She looked at the table rather than the paper.

“I took it down before more people saw it,” Eve said. “I should have sent that version separately.”

“Yes.”

The word landed with its full, unpadded weight.

“I know it isn't an excuse that the source was approved.”

“No.”

“Would you consider using a longer word? I'm getting very good at hearing bad news in one syllable.”

He put the mug down. “I'm not asking you to stop making the videos interesting.”

She had prepared a defense against exactly that accusation. With it removed, she had nowhere useful to put her hands.

“I thought the longer video made it clear.”

“It does. People don't necessarily watch the longer one.”

“I know.”

He nodded. “I thought you probably did.”

There was no triumph in his voice. That made the embarrassment cleaner and harder to avoid.

She opened the two versions side by side. “This is the part I changed. Here you were talking about people forgetting their questions. Here I made you sound as though you'd assessed what they should worry about.”

“Yes.”

“All right. That one can stay short.”

He almost smiled, but she did not reach for her camera.

They discussed a replacement. Not a lecture, not a wall of cautionary text no one would read. The question would stay in the frame. The cut would keep his qualification rather than borrow authority from his calm voice. Anything that rearranged the meaning would get its own review.

Eve wrote the new process on one page. She did not turn it into a ceremonial document that required everyone to sign a promise never to be human again.

“I'm putting a correction on the account,” she said. “The short version was misleading, so I removed it. The longer introduction is still available.”

“Thank you.”

“Not that you object to people finding you reassuring.”

“I'd prefer to be reassuring about something I've actually said.”

She laughed, unwillingly. There he was again, almost impossible to write for and quite easy to listen to once he started speaking.

As she packed up, Tessa appeared in the doorway with two mugs in one hand.

“Is he allowed to be handsome again?” she asked.

Eve closed her eyes.

Julian looked at the clock. “I have another appointment.”

“That wasn't an answer.”

“It was the answer I had time for.”

On the walk home, Eve stopped outside the bakery. She had meant to buy something comforting and unnecessarily iced. Instead she stood under the striped awning, watching a man struggle to fold an umbrella while carrying a box tied with string.

A woman held the door for him. He backed through it, thanked her, then had to come out again because he'd left his umbrella propped against the bin.

Eve smiled. She knew that little sequence. A person arrived with three things to manage and acquired a fourth because they were trying not to inconvenience anyone.

At home, she filmed her own hands attempting to carry a notebook, a coat, a carrier, and a phone at once. The carrier was empty. Dumpling watched from the sofa as she dropped her pen, retrieved it, dropped the notebook, and finally put the phone away.

The clip took twenty seconds.

She added a voice-over about the things she had been afraid of forgetting on her first visit, then showed the list she now made for herself. She did not give general instructions for every appointment. She said she checked the clinic's arrangements and wrote down what she wanted to ask.

It wasn't glamorous. Nobody in it had particularly interesting forearms.

She sent the revised Julian excerpt for its own approval and held it until the reply arrived. Meanwhile, she posted the correction and the small, separate video of her hands.

For half an hour, nothing happened.

Eve washed her plate. She put away the tripod. She played with Dumpling, who ignored the toy until she stopped presenting it as an important opportunity and let it disappear behind a cushion.

When she returned to the phone, the practical clip had a few comments.

One person said they always forgot the name of the thing they meant to ask about. Another admitted they rehearsed questions outside the door. A third asked whether she could show how she kept loose paperwork from swallowing her entire bag.

Eve looked toward the bag. Loose paperwork had already won.

That, at least, was something she knew how to film honestly.

The replacement excerpt received approval just after dinner. She checked the exported file, not merely the project, then checked the caption and the link. Only when all three matched did she publish it.

Its first hour was slower than the vanished clip's first ten minutes.

She let it be slower.

An hour later, someone shared the corrected version with a friend who had an appointment the next morning. There was no compliment for Julian in the message, no demand for more cat footage. Just a small note: This is what I meant about writing it down. Eve read that twice too, but for a different reason.

Before bed, she opened her planning notebook and drew a line through the phrase reassuring vet reaction. Underneath she wrote, Person arrives with too many things. What actually helps?

Dumpling hopped onto the chair beside her and leaned against her elbow. For a ridiculous moment, she wanted to tell him the answer: apparently not being beautiful on command.

But that was unfair. Beauty had not caused the problem.

She had made a cut, and she had made another one. The second was less dazzling. It left the question where it belonged.
