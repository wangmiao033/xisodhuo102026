# The Camera Can Wait

The light arrived early and made Eve foolish.

It fell across the park wall in a clear amber stripe, catching the leaves of the small trees planted along the pavement. The bakery window reflected it. Even the drain cover looked deliberate.

Eve checked the time. They had perhaps twenty minutes before the buildings swallowed it.

Mabel and Biscuit were due in five.

She framed the wall, moved two steps to avoid a parked scooter, and tried again. This could be the opening shot: the route ahead, the dog entering from one side, Mabel's hand loose on the lead. Not sad. Not sentimental. Simply a good picture of an ordinary walk.

When Mabel appeared, Eve was already holding the phone at chest height.

“Hello. Would you mind starting over there?”

Mabel followed the direction of her finger.

“We've just come from there.”

“I know. Just a little way. The light's really good.”

Mabel glanced at Biscuit, who was examining the edge of a tree pit.

“All right. Once.”

Eve heard the agreement more clearly than the last word.

---

The first take included a cyclist pushing his bicycle between the phone and Mabel. The second included Eve's own shadow. She had not intended to ask for a second. It had simply seemed wasteful to stop when the first did not work.

“Sorry. One more from the corner?”

Mabel did not move.

Biscuit stood beside her, looking toward the bakery rather than the stretch of pavement they had just covered twice.

“We're going to sit,” Mabel said.

“We can. If we get the turn first, then—”

“We're going to sit now.”

The voice was not loud. It made Eve lower the phone more effectively than shouting would have done.

Mabel turned toward the bakery. Eve followed, suddenly aware that she had greeted a picture instead of a person.

At the bench, Mabel settled herself and arranged the lead so it did not trail across the walkway. Biscuit stood for a moment, then lay down near her feet. She watched him, not Eve.

“Is he all right?” Eve asked.

“He needs the stop we came to check.”

Eve looked at the phone in her hand.

“I got carried away.”

“Yes.”

There was no gracious addition to rescue her from the answer.

She put the phone in her bag.

---

A bus went past, carrying the light away in its windows.

Eve sat at the far end of the bench, leaving Mabel room. The bakery door opened and closed. Someone bought a loaf and broke a warm end from it before reaching the pavement.

“I asked for once,” Mabel said.

“I know.”

“Did you?”

Eve swallowed the quick explanation about the cyclist. “I heard it. I acted as if a bad take made it not count.”

Mabel nodded once.

That was worse than an argument. In an argument, Eve might have found some useful work for the anxious energy gathering behind her ribs. Here she had to remain on the bench while the thing she had done stayed done.

“I won't ask you to walk it again,” she said.

“Not today.”

“Not today.”

Mabel took out Biscuit's water and bowl. Eve moved her bag to give her more room, then kept her hands to herself rather than offering to perform a task Mabel had already begun.

Biscuit drank and returned to his place. His ears moved when a paper bag crackled nearby.

“Will you be all right getting home?” Eve asked after a while.

“Yes. After this.”

Eve nodded. She did not say she had only been trying to make the film good.

Mabel already knew that. The problem was what she had made “good” mean.

---

The light disappeared while they sat.

Without it, the pavement became ordinary again. Eve found she could look at it more accurately. A loose paving stone near the rubbish bin. A narrow gap where two people could not pass comfortably. The patch of glare on the bakery window that would make small printed information hard to read from outside.

Mabel followed her gaze.

“That's the other thing I wanted to show you.”

“The paving stone?”

“The bin. On collection day, they put a second one there. You can't use the space beside the bench.”

Eve took out her notebook, not her camera.

“Which day?”

“Usually Thursday. Ask inside. Sometimes it changes.”

“Would you like me to check now?”

Mabel considered. “Yes. I'm staying here.”

The baker confirmed the usual collection day and showed Eve where they moved the bins if there was a delay. He was apologetic about the narrow pavement, but he could not make it wider by agreeing with her.

She returned with the information and a piece of scrap paper on which he had drawn the arrangement.

Mabel held it down with a finger while Eve copied the position onto their rough map.

“Not there,” Mabel said. “You've put the bin in the doorway.”

“That would be a different problem.”

“It would.”

Eve erased the square and drew it again. Mabel corrected the angle of the bench. Neither of them mentioned the film.

For ten minutes, the work was a pencil, an eraser, and two people looking at the same unremarkable corner.

---

When Biscuit stood, Mabel gave him a little time before collecting the bowl.

“We'll go back by the other side,” she said. “No filming.”

“All right.”

Eve asked whether she could walk as far as the crossing with them. Mabel said yes.

At the corner, a fallen branch lay partly across the pavement. It was small enough for Eve to move without dragging it into anyone else's path. She put it beside the tree, clear of the walkway, then stepped back rather than waving Mabel forward like a traffic marshal.

Biscuit stopped to sniff the place it had been.

Eve waited.

A woman walking quickly behind them slowed, went around, and continued. Nobody's afternoon collapsed.

Mabel glanced at Eve. “You're doing better.”

“I have discovered standing still.”

“Don't put it on your business card yet.”

Eve laughed, relieved by the sound without treating it as absolution.

At the crossing, Mabel said she would look at the revised map tomorrow. She did not offer another filming day.

Eve thanked her for the time she had already given.

Biscuit turned his head toward a passing dog, then followed Mabel across when the signal changed. Eve watched until they reached the opposite pavement and went home by a different street, because she had several measurements to correct and no reason to follow them.

---

The footage was beautiful for eleven seconds.

Eve watched it once at home. Mabel and Biscuit moved through the stripe of light exactly as she had imagined. Then Biscuit glanced toward the bakery and Mabel's hand shifted on the lead.

In a short cut, no viewer would necessarily know that Eve had asked them to repeat the walk. She could trim before the pause. The image would survive.

She opened the next clip. Her own voice came from the speaker.

One more from the corner?

Dumpling looked up from the window ledge.

Eve stopped playback.

She put both clips in a folder marked NOT FOR USE. Not because she could invent a universal rule about repeated shots, but because this one had been obtained by pressing past the limit Mabel had actually set.

Her planned opening was gone.

So was the easy shape of the afternoon. She had a reference picture of bins and an awkward sketch of a bench. She could not edit those into a gracious encounter without lying about how it had felt.

She did not need to make a film about her mistake, either. Mabel had come to find somewhere to sit, not to provide Eve with a public lesson in humility.

Eve closed the video timeline and opened the map.

---

The small arrows were the next problem.

On the screen, a narrow arrow looked elegant. Printed at the size someone might carry, it became nearly invisible. Eve made it thicker. Then the route line obscured a note about a step.

She printed another copy.

Dumpling watched the machine from a prudent distance, having apparently concluded that it was noisy but not food.

Eve took the page to the window. The words were too small. She enlarged them and lost a third of the space she had carefully arranged.

By the fifth version, the map was less attractive and much easier to read.

She folded it the way Mabel folded her original, then unfolded it while holding a shopping bag in her other hand. The most useful note disappeared into the crease. Eve put the bag down, moved the note, and tried again. It was an ungainly rehearsal, performed alone in her kitchen, but it told her something the beautiful full-screen version had not. She could make a page that worked without asking anyone else to repeat a walk for her.

She laid the copies on the floor to compare them. Dumpling selected the oldest, smallest-text version and sat in its center.

“Thank you. That one is retired.”

He tucked his paws beneath him.

Her phone vibrated. Julian had replied to the paragraph she had sent for wording only. He suggested one plain change and said the new title was clearer.

Eve thanked him. She started to type a description of the afternoon, then stopped. She wanted someone to say she had meant well. It would feel good, but it would not fix the page in front of her.

She sent the revised text to the café owner and checked that the bakery note did not mistake a usual arrangement for a promise. Then she messaged Mabel a photograph of the large-print version.

I've removed today's walking footage. This is the corrected bench and bin note. No rush to look tonight.

She put the phone down and cooked dinner.

Mabel answered while Eve was washing the saucepan.

The arrow is better. Put the collection-day note under the picture, not beside the road.

Eve dried her hands and moved the note.

Another message followed.

Tomorrow we can check it sitting at the bakery. Biscuit and I are taking our usual walk, not doing extra turns.

Eve replied, Understood. I'll bring the printout.

She did not add a camera emoji or a promise to make it up to them with something spectacular.

Before bed, she charged the phone because a phone needed charging. She left the small filming grip in the drawer.

On the table, the latest map lay flat beneath a mug. The broken bench was clearly crossed out. The bins were in the right place. The letters were large enough that she did not have to lean close to read them.

It was not the picture she had gone out to make.

She set a pencil beside it and turned off the light.
