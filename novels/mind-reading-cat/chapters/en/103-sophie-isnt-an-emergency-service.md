# Chapter 103 — Sophie Isn't an Emergency Service

Sophie arrived carrying a lunch box, not a toolbox.

Eve took this as a useful warning.

“I have forty minutes,” Sophie said. “Then I have to collect something before the shop closes.”

“Lunch first.”

“An excellent proposal.”

They sat in Eve's kitchen. Dumpling approached Sophie's bag, inspected its closed zip, and retreated to a chair when it failed to produce an opportunity. Eve put the market plan beneath a clean sheet so she wouldn't begin pointing at it while her friend was still taking off her coat.

Sophie opened the lunch box. Two pieces of pastry had survived transportation as one larger, less elegant piece.

“It was divided when I left,” she said.

“We can recognize their separate intentions.”

They ate. Eve asked about the equipment case with the loose handle. It had gone back to the shop, Sophie said, and returned with a new fastening and a warning not to carry it by the old one. She described the warning's position directly beside the tempting handle.

Only after the tea was poured did Eve uncover the plan.

“I want to ask whether you'd be interested in a paid role at the market. Not whether you can rescue anything I've forgotten.”

Sophie looked at the date before the drawing.

“I have the morning. I need to be somewhere else later.”

“The market runs from ten until one. Setup begins at eight. What time would you need to leave?”

“Two, with no hopeful interpretation of two.”

Eve wrote it down.

She had imagined Sophie staying until every last cable was packed. Not because Sophie had agreed to do that, but because the picture of a finished event in Eve's mind always contained her friend doing the difficult practical bits at the edge.

“Then cleanup needs someone else responsible after that,” she said.

“Yes.”

There was no accusation in the answer. Eve was grateful for that, and for the fact that gratitude did not erase the need to arrange another person.

She explained what she wanted: site setup coordination, stallholder check-in, and managing the ordinary equipment and layout issues during the event. Eve would handle the programme, client decisions, and content. The community committee would supply two named-on-the-day volunteers for short, defined shifts, and the caretaker retained authority over the venue.

Sophie listened, then put a finger on the drawing.

“Who moves the tables?”

“The supplier delivers them.”

“To this courtyard, or to the pavement?”

Eve stopped.

“I need to check.”

“Who counts them back?”

“That can be part of your role until two. After that, the organizer takes over.”

“If the collection time is later, I can't be responsible for the return count.”

“Right.”

Sophie took another bite of pastry while Eve wrote. The questions were not a performance of being difficult. They were the work Eve had asked her to do, beginning before anybody touched a chair.

“What if a stallholder wants to move into the walkway?” Sophie asked.

“You can refuse based on the agreed plan.”

“And if they say they've spoken to you?”

“They call me. I don't overrule you just because they're standing in front of me.”

Sophie raised an eyebrow.

“I'll actually say that in the briefing,” Eve added.

“Better.”

They discussed the fee. Eve had included a reasonable amount for the hours on the event day, but not the extra site visit she was now proposing. She could feel the familiar temptation to describe the visit as a quick look, as though an adjective shortened a bus journey.

“There's a separate preparation visit,” she said instead. “Can you quote for that too?”

Sophie did. The total consumed most of the decorative allowance Eve had already reduced.

“It's more than I put down,” Eve admitted.

“I can do fewer hours if you want a smaller role.”

“No. I need this role. I'll remove the extra print panels.”

“Check before deciding. I don't want you resenting me every time you look at a bare wall.”

Eve laughed, then looked again at the numbers rather than using the laugh as agreement. The panels were optional; the coordination was not. She could use the community noticeboard for the essential information and a small printed sign at each stall. The figures fit, narrowly, without borrowing from anyone's unpromised time.

“I'd rather have someone who knows where the tables are,” she said.

“A low but appropriate bar.”

Before Sophie left, they fixed the site-visit time. Eve promised a written scope that evening, not an affectionate message containing twelve new tasks in the form of questions.

At the door, Sophie paused.

“You can still tell me you're nervous, by the way.”

“I am nervous.”

“I know. You put a blank sheet over your plan as if it might start talking.”

Eve looked back at the kitchen.

“It has been unusually vocal.”

Sophie smiled. “Tell me about being nervous as your friend. Hire me for what you need done. Those can be two different conversations.”

After she left, Eve wrote the agreement.

It was not an impressive document. It specified the preparation visit, event hours, fee, payment dates, expenses requiring approval, and what Sophie could decide on site. The organizer's separate responsibilities sat in a box because mixing them into Sophie's list would make the page look complete while leaving the actual work ambiguous.

Eve sent it and received three corrections. One removed “as needed” from a sentence about photography assistance. Another clarified that Sophie would not supervise unattended animals. The last changed the handover time to allow her to leave at two rather than begin packing her own things then.

Eve accepted all three.

The site visit happened on a clear afternoon a week before the market. Sophie brought a tape measure, a short roll of removable marking tape, and a small notebook with corners softened by use. She did not bring a miraculous ability to stretch paving.

They laid out the table positions with the caretaker's permission.

“Stand here,” Sophie said.

Eve stood at the proposed check-in desk.

“Now greet someone carrying a carrier while another person wants to leave.”

The imaginary visitors immediately collided.

Eve moved the desk half a table-width to the side.

“Again,” Sophie said.

This time the exit remained clear. The organizer, arriving with the supplier's measurements, tried the route too and discovered that the noticeboard needed to turn toward the entrance rather than face the prettiest background.

Nobody took photographs until the route worked.

The table supplier had confirmed delivery into the courtyard. Collection would be at two-thirty, after Sophie's departure. The organizer wrote her own name beside the return count and asked the caretaker where the tables could wait without obstructing anyone.

Eve felt a small embarrassment that the answer had not been hers. It passed. A person accepting responsibility for a job was more valuable than Eve appearing to have thought of every job first.

They tested the owners' exchange seating with six chairs. Sophie sat in one, pushed her bag beside her feet, and tried to turn without striking the imaginary knees of the next person.

“Less intimate,” she said.

Eve moved the chairs apart.

“Now it's a neighborhood conversation, not an intervention.”

The organizer laughed hard enough to make the caretaker look over.

At the end of the visit, they had fewer chairs in the conversation area, a better entrance, and no spare decorative corner. They also had a usable map.

Eve sent the approved version to everyone involved instead of assuming Sophie would explain it all on the morning. The document included who to contact about arriving late and where animals could take a quiet break with their owners. It did not promise staff would provide pet care.

Before leaving the courtyard, they met the two volunteers the committee had arranged. One could stay through opening; the other would arrive later to take over. Eve began describing everything both might possibly encounter, then noticed the first volunteer looking at the map rather helplessly.

“Sorry,” she said. “I'm giving you the whole event instead of your part.”

Sophie tapped the entrance on the drawing.

“Show us where you'd stand.”

The volunteer walked to the wrong side of the desk. From there, the noticeboard hid the lane entrance completely.

They changed the position and tried again. Eve walked in carrying her bag in front of her as an imaginary carrier. The volunteer greeted her, indicated the route, then asked where to send a question she couldn't answer.

“To me for the programme,” Eve said. “To Sophie for the layout or equipment. You don't have to invent an answer to keep someone happy.”

The woman relaxed visibly. The second volunteer took a photograph of the marked plan, with permission, and confirmed the handover time aloud. Eve left with two people who knew what they had agreed to do, rather than two reassuring names in a grid.

That evening, a stallholder asked whether the setup team could assemble a complicated display delivered in separate pieces.

Eve forwarded the assembly instructions to Sophie and asked for an assessment, not a favor. Sophie replied that it would exceed the agreed setup time and need either paid additional help or preparation by the stallholder elsewhere.

Eve relayed the answer.

The stallholder was disappointed but chose to bring a smaller assembled version. Nothing collapsed. No friendship required repair. Eve had spent more time anticipating an unpleasant refusal than the actual conversation took.

On Friday she met Sophie for a brief final check at a cafe near the community room. They spread the papers between the sugar jar and a sandwich neither could identify without opening it.

“Mine is cheese,” Sophie said.

“Then this must be the one that was described as seasonal.”

“That doesn't narrow it much.”

Eve took a bite and discovered mushrooms. She stopped discussing the plan long enough to enjoy them.

They checked the supplier arrival, volunteer shifts, and the handover. Sophie pointed to the extra fifteen minutes Eve had inserted before opening.

“What's this?”

“Time for something ordinary to be wrong.”

“Good. Something usually is.”

Eve closed the folder.

“I keep imagining everyone arriving and discovering I'm only me.”

Sophie swallowed her tea.

“Who did they think they'd hired?”

“A person with an invisible department.”

“Then make the visible department clear. There are several of us. You don't have to pretend we're all extensions of your arms.”

Across the table, Eve noticed the faint dent in Sophie's finger where she had carried her equipment bag. She had spent so long thinking of her friend as capable that she sometimes forgot capability had a body and a schedule.

“Thank you for taking the job,” she said.

“You're welcome.”

“And for meeting me for lunch.”

“That part was voluntary.”

They smiled. Eve put the folder away properly, leaving the rest of the table to the sandwiches.

For the remaining ten minutes, Sophie told her about a display she had seen that appeared to advertise a shoe no human foot could enter. Eve offered an explanation involving a very ambitious pigeon. Neither of them checked the market plan again.
