# Chapter 009 — The Sandwich After Closing

By the time the last folding table was empty, Eve had collected three abandoned leaflets, half a roll of tape, and a sticker declaring her an excellent companion.

The sticker was on her shoe.

She scraped it off with a fingernail while Tessa counted the brochure holders. The dogs had left with their carers. A few consultations would continue by phone later in the week. The activity area smelled faintly of wet cloth and the coffee nobody had finished.

It did not smell like the triumphant final scene of a fundraising film.

Eve was almost grateful. She didn't have the strength to direct one.

“Do you need these separated by size?” she asked, holding up the leaflets.

“Only the ones without footprints.”

She looked at the top sheet and placed it in the recycling.

The clinic itself wasn't closed yet. The event had ended, but the last scheduled appointments were still being handled beyond the door. Every time it opened, Eve heard the ordinary sounds of a working place: a phone, someone asking for a towel, a drawer pushed shut with a hip.

Julian emerged carrying two empty mugs and stopped beside the box marked Cups.

“Those are pens,” Eve said.

He looked inside.

“Of course.”

“The cups are in the bag that says Extension Leads.”

“I'm afraid to ask about the extension leads.”

“Accounted for. I checked before anyone tried to drink out of one.”

His mouth moved at one corner. He looked as though smiling required a resource he was saving for an emergency.

Tessa glanced at him. “Have you eaten?”

“Yes.”

“When?”

He considered long enough to invalidate his answer.

“There was a biscuit.”

Eve knew that tone. She had used it to explain meals consisting of the almonds at the bottom of her work bag. Julian's particular version was more convincing only because he had a medical qualification unrelated to whether a biscuit counted as lunch.

She went to the camera bag behind the desk. Beside it was the insulated pouch she'd brought when she returned from taking Dumpling home. Two wrapped sandwiches remained inside.

The bakery had been selling them in pairs, and she had imagined a sensible afternoon in which she ate one at lunch and took the other home. Instead, she had eaten a banana while exporting a preview and called it efficient.

She checked the packaging, then held up one sandwich.

“There's an unopened one going spare.”

Julian glanced toward the consulting rooms. “You should eat it.”

“I have another. This is not a solemn donation.”

Tessa took the mugs from him. “Use the staff table. Not here.”

He opened his mouth, probably to explain why he couldn't, when the phone rang.

For half a second, his eyes went from the phone to Eve.

“Could you watch the desk while—”

“No,” she said.

The word arrived before she could polish it.

All three of them paused.

Eve felt heat climbing her neck. “I can leave you a sandwich. I can't cover reception. I don't know what I'm allowed to answer, and my work here is finished.”

Tessa had already reached for the phone. She held up one finger to Julian, took the call, and directed it through the clinic's normal process.

He looked back at Eve.

“You're right. I asked the wrong person.”

She had prepared a longer defense. It sat awkwardly behind her teeth, suddenly unemployed.

“I wasn't refusing to be helpful,” she said anyway.

“I know.”

“Just that particular version.”

“Understood.”

He didn't sound offended. He sounded tired, which was a different thing and none of her business to repair by becoming clinic staff.

Tessa finished the call and told him the remaining handover was covered for the few minutes he needed. He confirmed one detail with her, put his phone where he could hear it, and finally took the sandwich.

Eve followed him to the small staff table. She had been invited to eat, not to stay until everybody was finished. The distinction shouldn't have made the chair so much more comfortable.

There was a plant by the window with one determined leaf leaning toward the light. Julian moved a pile of forms off the free seat, leaving them facedown on a shelf.

“Do you always work through lunch?” Eve asked.

“No.”

She looked at him.

“Not successfully,” he amended.

She unwrapped her sandwich. The first bite reminded her that a banana was not, in fact, a strategy.

A slice of cucumber slid out of Eve's sandwich and landed on the wrapper. She attempted to retrieve it discreetly, missed, and caught Julian watching.

“Structural failure,” she said.

“Too much filling on one side.”

“Please don't improve my sandwich.”

He lifted both hands a little and returned to his own. She rescued the cucumber and decided there was something unexpectedly restful about being allowed to look mildly ridiculous without anyone turning it into a lesson.

For a while, they ate without making conversation. Through the wall came the muffled sound of Tessa laughing at something. A trolley wheel squeaked once, then stopped.

Julian examined the bakery label.

“What?” Eve asked.

“Nothing. I'm establishing which one I should buy you next time.”

“You don't owe me lunch. I said I had a spare.”

“I can buy you a spare.”

“That sounds like an accounting loophole.”

“Then we'll avoid the paperwork.”

“Do you actually like this filling?” she asked. “Or is hunger doing most of the work?”

“Both. Though I'd remove the pickle.”

“That's the best part.”

He looked at her sandwich as though reconsidering her qualifications. “We can disagree.”

“About veterinary medicine, yes. About pickles, this is serious.”

She told him the bakery sold out of the plain ones first, a local mystery she had investigated on several unproductive lunch breaks. He said he usually reached the place after everything had sold out, which seemed a poor basis for judging its menu. They agreed on nothing except that the bread was good.

She took another bite before she could turn his perfectly ordinary offer into something too large to sit at the table with them.

Her poster proof lay folded inside her notebook. One corner stuck out of her bag, showing the line she'd been reconsidering all afternoon.

Julian pointed at it with his sandwich wrapper.

“Did we keep that?”

Eve pulled out the sheet. “Ask us anything?”

“Yes.”

“It's an invitation. Not a binding promise to explain the universe.”

“Someone asked Wendy whether we could guarantee a dog wouldn't bark in a new home.”

“They would have asked that without the poster.”

“Probably. But it sounds as if everyone here answers everything.”

Eve put down the proof. “If I put every limitation in the headline, nobody will get as far as the table.”

“I didn't suggest that.”

“You were thinking it in a very small font.”

For the first time that evening, he laughed properly. It was brief, and she nearly missed it because she was looking at the paper.

Then he took the proof, turned it to the back, and read the information she had arranged.

“This part works,” he said. “It tells them who to speak to.”

“So perhaps the headline can sound like a human invitation, and the rest can be precise.”

“Perhaps the invitation can point them to the right human.”

Eve rested her elbow on the table. He was being irritating in a way that required actual thought, rather than the usual client demand to make something simultaneously warmer and more blue.

“Come with questions?” she suggested.

He considered. “That doesn't promise an answer to everything.”

“It also lets them arrive without pretending they're ready to take someone home.”

He nodded toward the proof. “That sounds like what happened today.”

She wrote the phrase in the margin, then paused before replacing anything.

“This goes into the next version. Today's approved poster stays as it was. I'm not inventing another unpaid revision tonight.”

“I wasn't asking you to.”

“I know. That announcement was partly for me.”

He passed the paper back.

There was a smear of mustard on the edge, well outside the text.

Eve looked at it, then at him.

“A rigorous review process,” she said.

“I can provide comments without condiments.”

“Too late. This is the approved mustard.”

He reached for a tissue, and she laughed before moving the proof out of danger. For a few seconds, neither of them looked toward the door.

Then his phone sounded.

Julian checked it. His shoulders shifted, preparing to rise, but he stopped long enough to read the whole message. He replied, set the phone down, and finished the last bite.

“Not urgent?” Eve asked.

“Not something I need to abandon half a sandwich for.”

She didn't congratulate him. One meal wasn't a personality transformation. It was simply nicer to eat with someone who was still at the table.

When they stood, Eve gathered her own wrapper and washed her hands. She left the poster in her notebook, mustard mark and all.

At the activity room, Tessa was finishing the inventory. Eve offered the tape she'd found, then stopped herself from asking whether she should stay and help count every remaining item.

“Is anything of mine still here?”

“Your camera bag. Your excellent companion sticker is gone, I see.”

“I failed to meet the standard.”

“It was on your shoe.”

“Difficult placement for professional recognition.”

Tessa handed over the bag. Eve checked the memory cards, battery case, and signed filming permissions. The day's footage was safely accounted for. The editing still belonged to her, but it belonged to tomorrow's work, not to an endless extension of tonight.

Julian walked as far as the entrance while she adjusted the bag strap.

“Thank you for the sandwich,” he said.

“You're welcome.”

“And for saying no.”

She looked up. “Don't make a habit of giving me things to refuse.”

“I'll try to ask the appropriate person.”

“Excellent. Very efficient. Almost like a button.”

This time she saw the smile arrive.

Outside, the air had cooled. Eve took out her phone, intending to tell Sophie that she'd survived her first event as a freelancer. Instead, she checked the time and put it away. Her friend might still be working. An update could wait until she had something worth saying beyond I did not collapse.

At home, Dumpling was exactly where she had left him, except that he had pulled one corner of the bedding over his face. When she spoke, he uncovered one eye.

“Everyone's very impressed with my availability,” she told him. “I'm trying to reduce it.”

He yawned.

She fed him, dealt with the ordinary evening tasks, and transferred the footage to her backup drive. Then she shut the laptop without opening the edit.

Her notebook lay on the table. In its margin, beneath a small yellow stain, she'd written Come with questions.

For once, it sounded like an invitation she might accept herself.
