# Chapter 247 — Two Different Cries, the Same Chaos

About four months after they moved the cabinet under the window, Nell and Theo had been home for several weeks, and Eve had become quite good at telling which one of them objected to the world.

Nell went up to the complaint and stayed there, with the extraordinary steadiness of a kettle. Her brother Theo started lower and put a little space between the first sound and the next. The space had misled both adults more than once.

What Eve could not always tell was whether she had already got out of bed.

She found herself in the upstairs passage one morning with her hand on the bathroom door, listening to Nell cry in one room and Julian in another say, with great conviction, “I've got Theo.”

“I didn't think you hadn't,” she called back.

He appeared wearing yesterday's shirt and holding their son. “I was telling Theo.”

Eve went to get Nell. Her slippers were both outside the bedroom, pointing in opposite directions. She slid one foot inside the nearer one and knocked the other away in the process. It looked, when she turned the light up, as if somebody had carefully aimed the second slipper at the top of the stairs.

She picked it up. Behind the bedroom door was the familiar padded tread of Dumpling jumping down from the wide chair. He had his grey blanket, water and a way to the other things he needed inside the adults' room. Beyond it, the stair gate kept what happened on the landing separate from anything downstairs. At this hour the door stayed closed and the cat could settle in peace while the adults took the children elsewhere. Eve could hear him scratch at the side of the upholstered chair. She resisted the urge to go in and explain to him that any cat would feel unsettled. Dumpling had never shown much gratitude for an explanation when there was a chair to scratch.

They got through the next hour in a sequence neither could have reconstructed accurately. Theo fell quiet just when Julian had prepared a full sentence to calm him. Nell wanted to be held, then cried while she was held, then stopped and stared at a patch of light above Eve's shoulder. Julian made toast and didn't bring a plate. They ate one piece each over the counter, on opposite sides of a yellow tea towel with a great crease down the middle.

“Can you go back to sleep after I’ve had my turn?” Eve asked.

“I thought I'd do the washing first.”

“I did it.”

Julian looked at the basket beside the stairs. “That's still full.”

“Those are clean.”

“Then what are the ones on the rack?”

“Also clean.”

He put the heel of a hand against the side of his face. Eve followed his gaze down to her feet and realized she was still wearing only one slipper. Neither of them laughed at first. It took them a moment to stop thinking that the other person had left a perfectly clear situation in an unreasonable state.

Nell inhaled sharply. Eve turned at the same time as Julian. There was no cry after it. Julian picked up the dirty plates and managed to set one on top of the other without a sound.

“I said I would do the washing,” he said quietly.

“You were asleep. We needed something that was in the basket.”

“Which thing?”

It was a fair question, and Eve was too tired to hear it as one. “I'll keep the exhibit next time.”

Julian set the second plate down. “I want to know if I should be making sure we have more of something upstairs. I did not say you had invented a reason to wash it.”

“I was the person who was awake. I did it, and now it has to dry. Can that be the end of it?”

He didn't answer immediately. She could see his thumb moving on the edge of the yellow cloth. When he finally said yes, he was still looking at it. Her arm had begun to feel the work of holding Nell. She changed how she was standing and asked him to take her.

He did. She filled a glass of water so close to the top that it wetted her lower lip before she meant to drink. At the counter she ate the small dry bit of toast that would otherwise have ended up in her pocket, or on the floor, or attached to something clean.

On the sheet they had put up in the kitchen, his name was first for the laundry and hers was first for rest that morning. Those were two things, not a test. Last night she had woken to a door click and discovered Julian put a warm washing cloth by her elbow every time he got one out for himself. She liked that he did it. She also wanted him to be able to sleep without taking a kind of attendance of the house when he came in.

Eve put the glass down. “I need you not to ask the follow-up question until after I've sat down, sometimes.”

He nodded once. “And I need to be able to do my things without guessing which ones you've already done while I slept.”

She found the peg from yesterday's folded laundry still on the counter and put it on the clean basket where it would show. The drying rack was empty of pegs. Julian could tell what was clean now without sending either of them looking for the item that had started it.

“Temporary technology,” she said.

He looked at the peg. “How much funding?”

“One peg.”

“I'm out.”

She took her glass with her and sat for a few minutes, while he held Nell and talked to Theo in the same dry voice he used with adults he liked. Eve didn't think he was trying to fix the morning. He wanted to know who had left a safety pin on the windowsill. It turned out to be him. He picked it up and put it away.

Late that morning Sophie rang from outside the bakery. She had a box file to drop at the stationer on their side of town and had asked the previous day whether they wanted her to come over after. Eve had said maybe in the afternoon and let the matter hang there. Now Sophie wanted to know whether quarter to two would work. She could stay forty-five minutes before going to meet someone who might hire her to run a small exhibition.

Eve almost asked her to stay as long as she could. Behind that request lay two hours of possible sleep, someone making tea, someone being pleased to see her, and a small hope that a visitor might know exactly where to put everything without being told. They were not forty-five minutes' work.

“Would you hold one of them here while we have lunch?” she asked instead. “I don't need you to bring lunch. And don't stay past half past two.”

“I can do that. If they both object, how do I choose?”

“Whichever one we're holding when you come in.”

“What if that's Julian?”

Eve leaned into the phone and laughed. She told Sophie there would be a place to put the box file if she wanted to bring it inside. She was glad to be able to make that small offer. The downstairs chair was clear except for her second slipper, retrieved at last.

When Sophie came, she had two buns in a folded paper bag. One for her, she said, and one to make Eve jealous. Julian cut the second bun in thirds. Sophie watched him do it and accepted the largest of the three pieces, fulfilling both purposes.

She washed her hands, sat where Eve indicated and asked to hold Theo, who was fussing in Eve's arms. Eve passed him over. Nell was with Julian and looking intently at a seam in his sleeve. The main lunch was soup that could be hot without everyone having to sit down at the same moment. Eve went to put a ladle in the bowl and caught herself staring at Theo from the kitchen doorway. Sophie had tucked a clear edge of cloth out of the way and was looking at him with a faint smile. Then she looked up at Eve.

“He can continue to disagree with gravity,” she said. “Go and eat your lunch.”

Julian sat down near Eve with Nell while his soup waited out of reach on the counter. Sophie held Theo. Eve ate without trying to finish every spoonful before somebody else asked for it. When her bowl was empty, she took Nell from Julian. He brought his bowl over once his hands were free. There was time, unexpectedly, to ask about Sophie's meeting. The exhibition had to move into a room that would still be in daily use until two days before opening, and Sophie wanted to get a real access date in writing before she offered a price. She used the last minutes of the visit to describe a display stand she had once seen committed to a freight lift with the wrong dimensions.

“What did they do?” Eve asked.

“They took it out again. It hadn't stopped being the wrong size.”

It was good to hear about a problem she didn't need to solve. At twenty past two Sophie asked to pass Theo back, got her box file together, and stood in the doorway with her coat open to make sure the pocket was free. By half past two she had gone. She didn't leave an offer for the following Tuesday on her way out. Eve missed her immediately and didn't wish she'd stayed.

That evening the house was loud again. Eve put a bowl on the counter too quickly and it knocked against a second bowl. Julian started to ask who was crying. Halfway through the question, Theo gave himself away with the pause, followed by a second indignant note. Then Nell started up and made the distinction much less important. Julian took Theo, who was nearest him. Eve went to Nell.

Later she carried the clean basket upstairs and left it where Julian would expect it. She went back down for her glass. On her way up again she heard Dumpling come close to the other side of their bedroom door. No one had taken the children in to meet him that afternoon. When they were settled apart from him and Julian was there, Eve opened the door, stepped inside and shut it again behind her.

Dumpling was on the chair, still on his blanket. The patch of upholstery where he'd scratched looked unchanged in the low light, although she doubted he would admire her for saying so. She lowered herself onto the edge of the bed. He began to wash a forepaw. When she said his name his ear twitched, but he didn't come to find out what she wanted. She had come for a drink of water and a few minutes in a different room, and he was already doing exactly what suited him.

Sometime after Julian looked in to say he'd take the next turn, she could hear Nell briefly and then hear his feet in the passage. From the hall Julian asked whether he should bring the empty basket down once he’d put the washing away. Eve said yes. He could leave the peg by the kettle. The next basket would be full again soon enough. She curled her empty hand against the bedspread. In the lamplight Dumpling stretched from one end of his blanket to the other, eased his chin down, and went on sleeping through everything he could hear.
