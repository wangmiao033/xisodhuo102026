# Chapter 148 — A Business Without a Window

Ruth had brought three document sleeves and a conviction that the smallest one was responsible for most of her troubles.

“It looked large online,” she said.

“Everything does when it's photographed alone,” Eve said.

They were at the borrowed room's table after the next session, with ten minutes left in the booking. Eve had asked whether anyone wanted to stay briefly to discuss possible future formats. Three people had accepted. Two others had sent answers in advance; the sixth had said she was happy with the existing arrangement and had no more opinions to offer.

Eve had thanked her for that answer too.

She placed three cards on the table. One described occasional sessions in hired rooms. One described a small regular venue. The third offered online materials and a short remote group session. Each included a likely price range and the time involved.

There were no drawings of plants, soft chairs, or attractive cups.

Ruth picked up the regular-venue card.

“Would you be there every day?”

“No. The schedule would be limited.”

“Then what makes it regular?”

“The place. Some recurring times, if enough people wanted them.”

Ruth put it down. “I see.”

Eve did not fill the pause by explaining how lovely the window was.

A man who had attended once before preferred a room he could reach after work, wherever it was. He would not travel farther merely because Eve could leave supplies there. The quiet participant from the previous session liked knowing the layout in advance, but she wanted fewer voices rather than more opening hours.

“I'd pay for a smaller group,” she said. “I wouldn't pay to sit somewhere all afternoon.”

“Would an individual appointment help?” Eve asked.

“Maybe. But I don't want you doing it for me. I need to understand what I've made.”

Eve wrote that down.

Ruth turned over the online card.

“This, if I could see it first.”

“A sample?”

“Yes. Some things say simple and then need another account and a password I can never find.”

They discussed what a file should do when printed and how someone would know it was worth paying for. Eve had imagined a small library of polished templates. Ruth wanted one useful example, legible on a phone, which did not demand that she move her household information into an unfamiliar system.

The man wanted a blank version he could change. The quieter woman wanted the chance to try an instruction on somebody who had not heard her explanation five times already.

None of those requests required a shop.

That fact should have comforted Eve. For a minute it felt like someone had quietly removed the floor beneath her favorite idea.

She asked which of the three they would actually book within the next month. They answered differently. Ruth might buy a sample-based pack if it addressed the task she was working on. The man did not need another session soon. The quiet woman wanted to know the date of a smaller group before deciding.

Eve gathered the cards.

“Thank you. This helps.”

“Will you still take the shop?” Ruth asked.

“I haven't decided.”

“Good. Don't take it on account of my sleeves. They're nearly sorted.”

After they left, Eve carried the box into the corridor and stopped to tie the loose strap. The unit next door lay just beyond the window. A room with its own address would let her answer the question of what she did with something people could walk past.

That was a real desire. It was not an answer Ruth had given.

At home, Eve put the three format cards on her desk and began to build the alternative as if she meant to use it.

She confirmed two available room slots at different times, holding neither beyond the center's normal policy. She checked a second venue's hire terms and travel time. The second room was farther away but offered a longer clearing period and a quieter entrance. Its apparent savings shrank when she counted transport, though they did not disappear.

The second venue sent photographs of its entrance at her request. They answered one question and raised another: the nearest bus stop required a longer walk than the map suggested. She added the actual approach to her comparison. A cheaper room was not automatically a better alternative for people already fitting a session between work and home.

She made a schedule that kept commission work in uninterrupted blocks and allowed one small group at a time. The cupboard remained uncertain, so the materials still had to travel. She reduced the kit until it fitted into the box without the lid bowing upward.

Then she worked on the sample Ruth had asked to see.

The first version was beautiful and useless on a small screen. The headings were clear, but the spaces beneath them demanded more typing than anyone needed for one visit. Eve deleted half the fields. She made an invented example about locating cleaning supplies and checking an agreed task, then wrote a short explanation of how to adapt it without sharing private household details with her.

She tested it on her phone.

The last line vanished beneath a button.

“Excellent,” she told Dumpling. “A product requiring psychic access to the bottom of the page.”

He was occupied with the shadow of a leaf on the floor and offered no assistance.

She repaired the layout and asked the three people who had agreed to review the sample to look at it when convenient. This was feedback on a free example, not an unpaid demand to build the product for her. They could ignore the message. One did.

Ruth replied the following afternoon with a photograph of the printed sheet. She had circled the place where the date should be larger.

Eve enlarged it.

The quieter participant wrote that the example helped, but she still preferred trying her draft with another person. Eve added that to the format comparison rather than treating it as rejection of the file.

On Wednesday, Claire invited her to share the last half hour of a cafe table before a shoot. Eve arrived with the draft on her phone and no intention of requesting a review. Claire was reading a brief beside a bag of equipment.

“Have you rented the glamorous headquarters?” she asked once the brief was put away.

“It has a sink with opinions.”

“That wasn't the question.”

“No. I'm trying to see whether I want the work or the photograph.”

“Could be both.”

“I'd like both.”

Claire nodded. “The photograph's usually easier to afford.”

Eve told her about the customer answers. Claire listened while removing an adhesive label from a lens cap.

“What did you want them to say?” she asked.

“That a reliable place would change everything.”

“And?”

“One wants a larger date box.”

“Powerful market research.”

“It is, annoyingly.”

Claire described a project she had nearly expanded into a recurring studio booking. In the end she rented only for the days she needed and kept the rest of the money for editing help. The decision had not cured every difficulty; equipment still occupied too much of her hallway.

“Do you regret not having the studio?” Eve asked.

“Some days. Especially when I fall over a case. That doesn't make the decision wrong.”

Eve watched a bus pass the cafe window. She had been waiting for one option to become free of regret so she could call it the sensible one.

Before leaving, she asked about Claire's shoot. It involved a reflective product and a room full of windows. Claire showed her a photograph of the setup, and they spent five enjoyable minutes locating accidental reflections of a lighting stand.

Back at her desk, Eve completed the no-shop plan.

It had income based on sessions she could actually offer, not a new audience assumed to appear because she wanted one. It had costs for room hire, transport, preparation, support when needed, and the modest online product she could make between commissions. It had inconvenient gaps when the rooms were unavailable. It also had money left when a month went badly.

She read the whole page aloud.

Dumpling woke at the sound of her voice, stretched, and moved to another patch of the sofa.

The business would continue if she did not take the unit. She could write that sentence without adding for now as an apology.

That evening Julian came over with the book she had asked to borrow. She made tea, and they sat on the sofa while Dumpling investigated the paper bag the book had traveled in. Eve folded the handles out of reach and left the open bag on the floor for a supervised inspection.

“What if I don't open the place?” she asked.

“Then what would you do?”

“I've worked that out.”

She fetched the page, handed it over, and waited while he read.

“This looks like your work,” he said.

“It is my work.”

“I mean it hasn't disappeared because there's no shop.”

She sat beside him again. Outside, someone was dragging a bin across the paving with tremendous determination.

“I think I wanted an answer for people who still wonder whether this is a temporary phase,” she said. “An address. A door they could understand.”

“Would they stop wondering?”

“Probably they'd ask whether I could afford the door.”

He smiled slightly.

She took the page back. “I still want it.”

“All right.”

“Not the whole thing I imagined. A place to test the smaller work properly. Somewhere the person who wants quiet can come back to the same room. Somewhere I can find out whether leaving the materials set up saves real time.”

“Those sound like questions you could test.”

She looked down at the alternatives. Borrowed rooms gave her a way to continue. That made the empty unit less necessary, but it also made her curiosity less frightened. She did not have to make every activity succeed there. She could discover that some belonged elsewhere.

They put the papers away and read for a while. Dumpling settled halfway inside the bag, leaving most of his body outside it. His arrangement appeared inconvenient but entirely chosen.

After Julian left, Eve wrote the landlord a proposal. She asked about limited, paid evaluation access for setup tests and a small number of separately approved sessions, with removable equipment only. She explained that she was not ready to enter the full lease and would need a clear way to end the trial.

She attached the proposed uses, not her life story.

Then she drafted a short description of the online sample pack and saved it. Whether or not the unit became part of her work, Ruth still needed to find the date.

Eve closed the laptop and carried the format cards to a folder. She kept all three. None had earned the right to vanish merely because one had a better window.
