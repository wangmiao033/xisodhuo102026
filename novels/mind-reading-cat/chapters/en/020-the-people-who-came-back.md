# Chapter 020 — The People Who Came Back

The weather forecast changed its mind three times before Friday lunch.

At nine, Saturday would be bright. At ten thirty, there might be showers. By noon, Eve's phone displayed a dark cloud so emphatic that it looked personally offended by her plans.

She stood in the courtyard behind the community rooms with a tape measure in one hand and a roll of paper signs in the other. A modest Saturday drop-off for clean animal-care supplies and an introduction to volunteering had seemed a sensible next step after the film. No animals needed to attend. People could meet the volunteers, ask what was useful, and learn why a bag of random cupboard contents was not necessarily a donation.

She had promised two tables, not a festival. Even so, she had spent an unreasonable amount of time choosing where the tables should stand.

“Could we put them under there?” she asked.

The volunteer coordinator followed her gaze to the narrow awning above the rear door.

“Then everyone would be standing in front of the emergency exit.”

“Right. No.”

“And the supplies would still get wet at the sides.”

“Also no.”

Eve reeled the tape measure in too quickly. It snapped against her finger.

The coordinator gave her a sympathetic look that did not include an offer to change the weather. The indoor room was already booked for a residents' meeting. Its chairs, projector, and several formidable opinions would be arriving at ten. There was no spare space into which a cheerful small event could quietly expand.

Eve checked the forecast again, as though it might become embarrassed and withdraw.

It did not.

They agreed to postpone.

The decision itself took less than five minutes. Everything around it took the rest of the afternoon.

She called the registered visitors, changed the notice on the account, asked the community office to update its board, and sent a clear message to the volunteers. She put the old date in large letters under the word POSTPONED, because a pretty replacement poster would not help someone who only remembered seeing blue and yellow somewhere online.

Then she checked that no one had been asked to travel with an animal. That part, at least, was simple: the event had never required it.

Mia answered from a bus, her voice competing with an announcement about the next stop.

“I've already got the clean cloths,” she said.

“Keep them dry. We can arrange the delivery separately. You don't need to bring them out in the rain just because the bag is ready.”

“I wasn't going to.” A pause. “I did wonder if it would be letting everyone down.”

“No. It would be a damp bag.”

Mia laughed. Eve heard herself and wished she were as sensible when speaking inside her own head.

The clinic's contribution had been limited to approved information leaflets and Julian's availability for a short session of general questions about arranging visits, not examinations or individual advice. Eve had put his name on the notice only after confirming the slot.

Now she had to remove it until she knew what he could actually do.

She sent a message explaining the postponement and the two possible replacement dates. Then she opened the event page.

Nine people had said they were interested. Four had confirmed they intended to come.

Four was a number that looked terribly small on a screen and surprisingly large when she imagined four separate journeys through rain because she'd been too proud to cancel in time.

She stopped refreshing the page and went to the community office with printed notices.

By evening, the sky still looked almost blue.

This felt like a personal insult.

At home, Dumpling had discovered that the cardboard tube from her signs could be rolled under the sofa and left there for a human to retrieve. After the third retrieval, she put the tube away and gave him a safer toy. He watched the cupboard door with the solemn disappointment of a customer whose favorite product had been discontinued.

“Everyone's plans are changing,” she told him. “You aren't special.”

He sneezed and went to drink water.

Eve cooked rice, answered one more message, and wrote a note to herself not to measure an event's success by whether the sky obeyed her. She crossed the note out. It sounded like something printed beside a sunset in an office she no longer worked in.

At two in the morning, rain hit the windows hard enough to wake her.

She lay still and listened. Dumpling was curled in his usual place, an orange shape in the dark. Somewhere downstairs, a loose gutter rattled in bursts.

Relief arrived first. She had made the right decision.

Then came the small, mean disappointment of having made the right decision and still not getting what she wanted.

On Saturday morning, she went to the courtyard without supplies or camera equipment, just to put a final notice at the entrance and check with the office. The rain had eased to a cold drift, but the paving shone. Water collected exactly where her second table would have stood.

A man in a green coat approached while she was smoothing the notice beneath its clear cover.

“Is this the collection thing?” he asked.

“Postponed. I'm sorry. Did you not see the message?”

“I did. I live upstairs. Thought I'd check whether you wanted these now.”

He lifted a small bag containing two washed towels, neatly folded.

Eve almost accepted automatically. Then she remembered that the volunteers weren't there to receive or sort anything, and the office had not agreed to store it.

“Could you bring them on the new date, once it's confirmed? I don't have the storage arrangements today.”

He nodded, entirely uninjured by this reasonable answer.

“Fine. They're not going anywhere.”

“You still want to come?”

He looked at the towels. “Well, I haven't found another use for them since yesterday.”

After he left, she laughed alone under the dripping entrance.

The first confirmation arrived before lunch.

Let me know the new time. My daughter still wants to ask about volunteering during the school holidays.

The second was from someone who could not attend either proposed date but asked whether there would be another opportunity later. Eve did not count that as a yes. She did, however, answer it without apologizing for the existence of calendars.

Mia sent a photograph of the laundry bag on a dry shelf, captioned: Not being heroic.

Eve replied with a thumbs-up and no request to use the photo on the account.

Julian called in the afternoon. She was struggling to peel an obsolete date sticker off a sign without taking the color underneath with it.

“I can do next Saturday from eleven to eleven thirty,” he said. “Not the following Sunday.”

“Only half an hour?”

The question escaped before she could soften it. She was used to people asking her for a little more time and calling it enthusiasm.

“I have something arranged afterward.”

“Right. Of course. Half an hour is what we originally discussed.”

He waited a beat. “Is that enough for the format?”

“Yes. I'll keep the general questions in that slot. The volunteers can explain their own arrangements.”

“Good.”

She pulled at the sticker. It tore diagonally.

“Are you fighting something?” he asked.

“A rectangle. It's winning.”

“Paper?”

“Adhesive. I underestimated its commitment.”

He made a sound that might have been a laugh.

She could have filled the quiet by saying how few people had registered, or how grateful she was that he was helping, or how she hoped he didn't think the whole project was pathetic. All three thoughts lined up behind her teeth.

Instead she asked whether his name should appear on the new notice with the exact half-hour slot.

“Yes,” he said. “That would be clearer.”

When the call ended, she wrote eleven to eleven thirty in her calendar. Not all morning. Not whenever she might need him. A real piece of time that she could plan around.

The next Saturday was bright in a restrained, untrustworthy way, but the courtyard stayed dry.

Four households came.

Eve recognized the man in the green coat before she recognized anyone from the comments. He brought the same towels. A woman came with her teenage daughter, who had prepared six questions and wanted to know whether doing unglamorous tasks still counted as experience. The coordinator answered that it was a very good place to begin.

Another visitor arrived carrying a bag of unsuitable items and looked embarrassed when some were declined. Eve helped separate them without filming the exchange. The useful things went onto one table; the others went home without becoming evidence that the woman was foolish.

Mia delivered the laundry, checked it off with a volunteer, and asked where the empty bags belonged. She didn't wait for applause. Eve noticed anyway.

Julian arrived at ten fifty-five carrying the information leaflets. He had not brought a rescue kit, a miraculous speech, or an invisible staff to make the morning run itself. He brought what he'd agreed to bring.

During the question session, a man began describing a particular problem with his dog. Julian listened long enough to understand what was being asked, then explained that the table wasn't a substitute for a proper discussion with the animal's usual clinic. He helped the man identify whom to contact rather than supplying an answer for the crowd.

Eve saw two people relax when he said that. Perhaps not having to discuss private worries in front of strangers was also a useful service.

At eleven thirty, she closed the session herself.

“That's all the time we arranged with Julian. The volunteer table will stay open until noon.”

He looked at her, then began gathering the spare leaflets.

She walked him to the gate because the fastening stuck, not because they had exhausted every excuse to stand together.

“Thanks for coming back,” she said.

“I said I could.”

“I know.” She lifted the latch. “That turns out to be helpful.”

He glanced toward the tables, where Mia was negotiating the lid of a storage box.

“Looks useful,” he said.

No promise of enormous things. No declaration that every future Saturday belonged to her. He went through the gate, and she went back to work.

After noon, she counted the supplies with the volunteers, returned the tables, and photographed the empty courtyard rather than the people who had come. The new image was mostly paving and a stubborn scrap of blue sticker on her thumb.

She posted a short thank-you, with the next contact details and no claim that they had changed anyone's life.

Four households had come back.

She also wrote down what had not worked: the gate fastening, the unclear sign for unsuitable donations, the need for somewhere dry to put bags while visitors asked questions. None of those details would make a dramatic caption. They would, however, make the next morning easier for whoever stood where she had stood.

This time, when she wrote the number down, it did not look small.
