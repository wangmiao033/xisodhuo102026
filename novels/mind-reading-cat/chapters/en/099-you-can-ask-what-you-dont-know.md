# Chapter 099 — You Can Ask What You Don't Know

On Wednesday, Julian arrived at the meeting place carrying a paper bag with one corner torn away.

“Don't be alarmed,” he said. “It's only bread.”

“I wasn't alarmed until you said that.”

“A fair criticism.”

Eve took the bag while he adjusted the strap of his work bag. Through the torn corner she could see a bread roll, flattened on one side. He had bought it on the way out, he explained, because the afternoon hadn't left time for a proper snack.

They had agreed to meet outside the little shop near the walking route. Eve was six minutes early; Julian was two minutes late. Neither fact needed to become an account of who had cared more.

“Do you want to eat before we walk?” she asked.

“Yes.”

There was a low wall beside the shop, dry under its overhanging awning. They sat there while he divided the roll. He offered her half. She said she had eaten but would like a small piece, and he tore off the amount she indicated rather than insisting she take an equal share.

The bread was warm at the center and extremely firm at the edge. A few crumbs caught on her scarf. She shook them into the paper bag before handing it back.

“A structural feature,” Eve said after a cautious bite.

“It survived my bag.”

“Your bag may not have survived it.”

He checked the torn corner and smiled. The familiar dry exchange made her shoulders loosen. She hadn't realized she had brought a little of Sunday's carefulness to the wall with her.

They disposed of the bag and started along the path. The evening was cool, but walking kept them comfortable. Beyond the railings, people were finishing the ordinary business of getting home: a bicycle being unlocked, a child objecting to a hat, somebody balancing two shopping bags while searching for keys.

Julian offered his hand. Eve took it.

For the first few minutes they talked about her edit. The changed lettering had forced her to adjust a frame, but the client had accepted the revised file and confirmed the payment date. Julian asked whether she had charged for the extra change. She explained that it fell within the agreed revision round.

“Not everything additional is a catastrophe,” she said.

“I'll try to remember.”

“I'm saying it to myself.”

“I suspected.”

She nudged his arm with hers. Then, near the crossing, his phone vibrated. He glanced at the screen, paused, and put it away.

The movement was so similar to the one over the tomato soup that Eve felt the old question arrive before she had formed any words.

This time there was no cat nearby to look at. That did not make her suddenly fearless. She could still invent a dozen explanations with only a pavement and a distracted expression to help her.

They waited for the crossing signal.

“Can I ask whether something's bothering you?” she said.

“Yes. Something at work.”

“Do you want to talk about it now?”

Julian looked along the path beyond the road. “I can tell you the general problem. No details about people coming to the clinic.”

“That's fine.”

The signal changed. They crossed before beginning, not trying to conduct a meaningful conversation while watching for turning traffic.

On the other side, he slowed.

“We're discussing how follow-up questions and appointment messages move through the clinic. Who receives them, when they reach the right person, what gets promised before anyone has checked the day's capacity.”

Eve nodded. She knew enough from making the preparation cards to picture a perfectly clear sentence encountering a very crowded morning.

“More messages than places to put them?”

“Sometimes. And different people assuming someone else has answered.”

“Is this the message from dinner?”

“Part of the same discussion.”

She let him continue.

“I had suggested a change that sounded efficient when I wrote it. Then someone pointed out where it moved the work rather than reducing it.”

His mouth tightened, and Eve couldn't tell whether he was embarrassed, frustrated, or merely remembering the wording. She didn't need to choose one to listen.

“Did they say what would work better?”

“Some of it. Other parts we still need to discuss with the people actually doing them.”

“That's uncomfortable.”

“Yes.”

The answer was so straightforward that she nearly missed it. He wasn't inviting her to admire a perfect professional who was reluctantly repairing everyone's mistakes. He was describing an idea of his own that needed work.

They passed a closed kiosk. Its metal shutter rattled when someone inside pulled it down the last few inches. Julian waited until the noise stopped before speaking again.

“I don't want to turn every gap into another thing I cover between appointments. I've done that before. But I also dislike leaving a problem unfinished.”

“So you keep thinking about it after work.”

“Yes.”

Eve looked at their joined hands.

“And I kept wondering whether your pause meant you regretted something about us.”

Julian stopped walking, not abruptly enough to pull her arm. She stopped too.

“I don't regret dating you,” he said.

“I believe you.”

“I was upset about what we discussed. That was real. The clinic issue was real as well. One didn't replace the other.”

She nodded. “I think I kept looking for one answer that would explain your whole face.”

“My face may not be organized that well.”

The laugh surprised her. It was a relief without being an escape.

He went on, “Sometimes I'll be preoccupied and still glad I'm with you. I can tell you that. I can't always finish the problem first.”

“I don't need you to.”

She considered the answer, then amended it before it became a polished lie.

“I may want you to. But I don't want us to arrange our life around that wanting.”

Julian squeezed her hand once. They started walking again.

Eve could already see three possible ways to help. A simple message flow, fewer different cards, a visible place for questions that needed a later answer. Her mind assembled them with the pleasing speed of a professional recognizing familiar material.

She opened her mouth.

Then she remembered that he had asked for a walk, not a consultant who appeared whenever he admitted difficulty.

“Would ideas be useful,” she asked, “or do you want me just to hear it?”

“Tonight, hearing it.”

“All right.”

“Some of it may need communication work later. If it does, the clinic can ask you properly. Not because I mentioned it on a date.”

“I wasn't about to invoice the pavement.”

“I appreciate that.”

She smiled and put the three possible diagrams away in her head. They would not become unpaid drafts on her laptop when she got home. A possibility was not yet a commission, and showing him she understood did not require producing something.

Ahead, a bench stood in the shelter of a low wall. They sat with enough room for a person to pass behind them without brushing their shoulders. Eve rested her hands in her lap while Julian took out his phone.

“I need to tell them I can read the revised note tomorrow morning,” he said. “Then I'd like to leave it until then.”

“Go ahead.”

He wrote a short reply. Eve looked toward the path rather than at his screen. A man was trying to persuade a folded shopping trolley to remain upright. It kept returning to the shape it preferred.

Julian put his phone away.

“Done.”

“Then tell me something entirely unrelated.”

He considered. “I killed a houseplant once by following the instructions too carefully.”

Eve turned toward him. “This is an excellent beginning.”

“The instruction said to water regularly. I had a very regular system.”

“How regular?”

“More regular than the plant wanted.”

She laughed. He described a little pot he had bought years earlier because it looked uncomplicated. The plant had looked worse, so he had become more diligent. By the time somebody explained the problem, diligence had done considerable damage.

“Did you save it?”

“No.”

“You could have made up a better ending.”

“You asked what happened.”

Eve liked him so much then that her chest ached a little. Not because the confession was grand. Because he was willing to tell her a small, unflattering story that did not need to establish either his competence or his pain.

“I once made a client presentation with the sample text still in the final slide,” she said.

“How much sample text?”

“An inspiring conclusion that said, Put strong ending here.”

Julian looked away, trying not to laugh.

“You can laugh. They did.”

“Was it a strong ending?”

“Memorable.”

They sat there trading mistakes until the cold reached Eve's fingers. Julian noticed her rubbing them together and asked whether she wanted to move. She said yes. He didn't turn the question into a demand that she wear his coat, and she didn't pretend to be comfortable to preserve the scene.

On the return path she asked, “When you said you wanted ordinary time on Sunday, is this what you meant?”

“Partly.”

“What else?”

“Being able to tell you about an annoying day without it becoming evidence of where we stand. Being allowed to enjoy this even if the day wasn't resolved.”

“I can do that.”

She stopped herself from making it a promise never to feel insecure. “I can practice doing that.”

“I can practice telling you instead of assuming my silence explains itself.”

The division of effort felt fairer than Eve alone becoming perfectly untroubled. Julian had not caused her wish to know everything, but he could still choose to speak more clearly. They were two people learning something, not one being evaluated by the other.

At her building, they lingered beneath the entrance light. She asked about Saturday. He had the afternoon free, provided an existing morning arrangement finished on time. They chose a meeting time that left a margin rather than stacking the date directly against work.

“A walk?” he asked.

“And something to eat. From somewhere with bread less suitable for construction.”

“I can investigate.”

“No professional report required.”

He smiled. Then he asked if she wanted a hug.

She did.

This one was not carefully shortened by the strain of a disclosure. Eve stood close, her cheek against his coat, until she wanted to look at him again. He let her move back without making release an abrupt ending.

“Saturday,” she said.

“Saturday.”

Upstairs, Dumpling came to meet her with a small questioning sound. Eve checked his water and sat on the rug while he rubbed his face against her wrist.

She did not tell him he had been right about Julian. The evening had belonged to two humans speaking imperfectly and listening long enough to hear the rest.

On the desk, her notebook remained in its drawer. Eve left it there and wrote Saturday's meeting time in her ordinary calendar instead.
