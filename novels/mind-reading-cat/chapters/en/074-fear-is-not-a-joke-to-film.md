# Chapter 074 — Fear Is Not a Joke to Film

The first time Eve watched the clip, she laughed.

It was an embarrassed, involuntary sound. On the screen, a yellow shopping bag slid toward the edge of the table with all the solemnity of a ship leaving harbor. Dumpling stood on the chair, teeth caught in a folded corner. His orange ears were level with the tabletop. Only the ears, the shifting fabric, and an absurd amount of determination appeared in the frame.

Twelve seconds. A caption practically wrote itself.

*My cat has canceled my social life.*

Then the bag moved another inch, and the chair leg scraped.

Eve heard her own voice from behind the camera: “Wait. Leave that.”

The clip ended in a close view of her sleeve.

She stopped smiling.

On Friday morning she'd been trying to film a quiet account post about what she actually carried for a small work errand. No sponsor, no medical claims, no promise to transform anyone's life: a notebook, water, a folded bag for bringing things home, and the surprising number of receipts a person could accumulate without becoming rich.

Her work bag had stayed inside the wardrobe until she needed it. The empty shopping bag was a prop she had placed on the table while the camera ran. She'd turned to fetch the notebook. Dumpling had climbed up before she returned.

She had put the camera down and removed the bag without pulling against his mouth. Nothing had fallen. No strap caught him. She checked the cloth, tucked it safely away, and let him go back to the rug.

Now, in the edit, the incident looked much less inconvenient than it had felt in the room.

Eve dragged the playhead back.

The tight frame hid his pause when the chair scraped. It hid the way he'd stayed low afterward, watching the cupboard where she'd put the bag. It also hid her immediate annoyance. Edited to music, it could become a perfect little story of a cat who loved her too much to let her leave.

The account had been quiet lately. Her useful posts received useful comments from a small number of people. Some days that satisfied her. Some days she looked at the numbers and felt as if she were throwing carefully folded paper into a cupboard.

This clip would probably do better.

Probably was not even a promise, yet she could feel herself beginning to bargain with it.

Dumpling slept beneath the window. Eve looked from him to the small figure on the screen. She knew the bag had moved. She did not know everything the movement meant. The line she had just imagined would turn that uncertainty into something people could enjoy demanding again.

She closed the caption box without typing it.

For several minutes she sat with the unfinished timeline, resenting how little satisfaction a good decision could provide when it left a blank space where the post ought to be.

Claire called at lunchtime about the owner-preparation film. The agreed corrections were done, and the remaining clinic check had come back. Mabel's wording stayed intact. They arranged the final delivery files and confirmed the credit text one last time.

“Anything else from your side?” Claire asked.

“No. That version is ready from mine.”

“Good. You sound as if you're fighting a different film.”

Eve looked at the paused ears on her screen.

“My own. I accidentally recorded Dumpling dragging a bag. It's funny in twelve seconds and less funny if you're here.”

“What happened after twelve seconds?”

“I stopped it. He watched the cupboard for a while.”

Claire was quiet for a moment.

“You don't have to send it to me,” she said. “What's the post supposed to do?”

“Originally? Show a sensible little work kit.”

“And does the cat help with that?”

“Only if the advice is never leave home.”

“An unpopular professional model.”

Eve laughed, then rubbed her eyes.

“I could make it into a completely different post.”

“You could. You also don't owe the first thing that happens in front of the camera a career.”

It was such a practical sentence that Eve stopped looking for an argument against it.

They finished the delivery conversation. Claire had another booking that afternoon and did not become a free counselor because she had listened for three minutes. Eve thanked her and let her go.

Then she moved the accidental clip out of the publishing timeline. She kept a private, plainly labeled copy with the observations she had already sent the clinic, in case the ordinary behavior discussion needed it. It wasn't an asset waiting for a more profitable caption.

The replacement was harder to film.

She secured the bags inside the wardrobe and used a plain tray on the table. Dumpling remained in the other end of the room with access to his usual resting place. Eve didn't call him over or recreate the theft to show viewers why she had changed her mind.

She filmed the notebook opening to a page with no client details. She placed the water bottle beside it, discovered that its cap rolled directly into the frame, and did the shot again. The spare cloth went into a closed case instead of being dragged across the table for a graceful movement nobody needed.

Her first voice-over sounded unbearably cheerful.

She deleted it.

The second began, “This is what I take when the job is too small to justify carrying everything I own.”

That was at least recognizable as her.

Halfway through, the washing machine entered its loudest cycle. Eve leaned back and stared at the ceiling while it completed an activity more committed to sound design than she was.

There would be no magical cat to rescue this post. There would be a woman waiting for household machinery to finish so she could explain why she brought an extra pen.

While she waited, she watched the silent cut. Her hand covered the notebook's closure at the very moment she was explaining it. She moved the camera a little higher, checked the frame with her own hand in place, and made a pencil mark on the table mat so she could repeat the position. This was the sort of work the twelve-second accident had offered to spare her.

She did it anyway. In the new shot, the closure could actually be seen. The pen rolled a shorter distance this time, stopped against the tray, and stayed there.

When the room quieted, she finished the recording.

Julian came by after work to return the food container she had sent home with bread. They had arranged the brief visit by message; neither pretended the empty container was urgent.

Eve opened the door with a smear of ink on her wrist.

“Have I interrupted a medical procedure?” he asked.

“A pen demonstration. The pen won.”

He handed her the clean container. Dumpling came forward, sniffed his trousers, and returned to the sunny patch, which had almost left the rug.

“Would you like tea?” Eve asked.

“Yes, if you're stopping anyway.”

“I am now.”

They sat at the table while she showed him the finished kit video. It was short, ordinary, and had no animal in it. At one point her spare pen rolled away and her hand entered the frame to retrieve it. She had kept the interruption because it was genuinely hers to be embarrassed about.

Julian laughed at that part.

“An effective demonstration of the problem.”

“I hope the pen company never sees it.”

“What happened to the bag video?”

She had mentioned the accident in the message arranging his visit. She told him where the clip was and why she wasn't using it.

“I almost made him responsible for whether people watched,” she said. “Again. Just in a different outfit.”

“You stopped.”

“Yes. I also spent five minutes wishing I didn't have to.”

He took a sip of tea. “That sounds possible.”

She watched him put the cup down carefully on the coaster rather than turn her admission into a compliment.

“Would you like to see the private clip?” she asked. “Only as part of what I noticed, not to tell me it's harmless.”

He nodded. She played it once with the sound on and included the end where she stopped. Julian asked what happened before it, and she explained the filming setup. His questions stayed with what she had seen. He didn't announce a diagnosis from twelve seconds of video.

“I've kept the appointment channel informed,” she added. “I won't make you be the clinic every time you come here.”

“I appreciate that.”

She closed the file and turned the laptop away.

For a moment neither spoke. Outside, someone struggled to start a motorbike. It caught, stopped, and caught again. Julian looked at his cup, then at her.

“I'm relieved you didn't put it out with the caption you mentioned.”

“Because of Dumpling?”

“Yes. And because I sometimes worry about becoming a funny explanation for things too.”

Eve's hand went still on the table.

“What things?”

He took a moment before answering.

“If I'm quiet, I'm the serious vet. If I don't know what to say, it's because I'm charmingly terrible with words. Sometimes I'm simply tired. Or I don't know what to say.”

She looked down at the ink on her wrist. She had done that, privately at least: made his awkwardness into a consistent character she could understand without troubling him for more.

“You don't have to be interesting every time,” she said.

“I know. I don't always behave as though I know.”

He turned the cup a little.

“Yesterday I nearly said I could help with something else this weekend. Not because anyone asked. Because it was easier than leaving an evening unaccounted for.”

“Did you?”

“No. I checked what I actually wanted to do first.”

Eve felt a warm, dangerous desire to make that sentence entirely about herself.

She left room for the possibility that he had other reasons too.

“I'm glad,” she said.

He looked at her and smiled. “So am I.”

There was no confession tucked behind the cup. It was still a conversation she wanted to remember.

They finished their tea without opening the laptop again. Julian asked whether she had a preference for the weekend's meal, and Eve admitted she did, but she wanted to look at the menu properly rather than choose whatever sounded easiest for him.

“Tell me tomorrow,” he said. “We can decide then.”

After he left, she washed the container and set it back on its shelf. Dumpling had moved to the cushion, where the light was better. He showed no concern for the missing public career of his twelve-second performance.

Eve watched her own video once more, checked that no private note or address had slipped into a frame, and posted that version.

The first response asked what size notebook she used.

It was not a wave of applause. It was a question she could answer.

She found the notebook, measured it, and wrote back. Then she put the phone down and washed the ink off her wrist before it could become another permanent part of the story.
