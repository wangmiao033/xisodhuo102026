# Chapter 218 — The Thing That Was Not Read Out of Him

Julian had drawn three circles around the same paragraph.

Eve noticed because he had left the page beside the fruit bowl while looking for a clean chopping board. She could see the circles from her chair, but not the words inside them.

“Have you found a particularly dangerous sentence?” she asked.

He looked over.

“No. A poorly placed price.”

“Those can be dangerous.”

He handed her the board instead of the page. “Could you do the bread? I want to tell you about that after we've eaten.”

“All right.”

It was Wednesday evening. Eve had delivered the new editing job that afternoon and left the business laptop closed. Dumpling lay along the back of the sofa, facing the wall with the confidence of someone whose future did not depend on understanding the room.

Eve cut the bread.

Julian heated soup. They disagreed briefly about whether a loaf could be sliced too thickly, then agreed that the evidence was already on the table and would have to be eaten.

The page remained beside the fruit bowl.

Eve didn't pick it up.

That small fact should not have felt like an achievement. He had explicitly said he would tell her. There was no suspense he had asked her to solve. Nevertheless, she had grown familiar with the temptation to read a person's mood before they chose their words, especially when the person was Julian and the mood seemed concentrated.

Dumpling yawned.

“You're not contributing,” she told him.

Julian glanced toward the sofa. “To the bread argument?”

“Yes. He could have been an impartial witness.”

“He's never been impartial about bread.”

They ate. Eve described a moment from the editing job when the proprietor's hand had covered the label throughout his most enthusiastic explanation. Julian told her that somebody had mistaken a clearly labelled staff cupboard for the exit, then admitted he had once made the same mistake in another building.

By the time they cleared the table, the circled paragraph had stopped looking ominous.

Julian brought it over with two mugs of tea.

“I've been looking at a course,” he said.

Eve sat down.

“What kind?”

“Teaching practical skills to other clinicians. A short course, not another qualification I can complete by refusing to sleep.”

“That sounds promisingly specific.”

“One weekend in person, with preparation before it. There are later sessions online. I haven't applied.”

He put the page between them, right way up for her.

The price was indeed badly placed. The first large number covered only part of what a person would have to pay. Travel and accommodation were separate, and the page used a surprisingly cheerful color for this information.

“I wanted to mention it before I worked it into a private plan,” he said.

Eve looked from the page to him.

“You mean before it became a thing you were already doing?”

“Yes.”

“Have you talked to Dr. Clark?”

“Only about wanting to improve that side of my work. Not this course. I need to ask whether there'd be protected time for any teaching afterwards. Otherwise I'd be buying a more sophisticated way to stay late.”

She smiled.

He didn't smile immediately. His thumb pressed the edge of the paper flat.

“I'd like to do it,” he said.

The plainness of the sentence made Eve stop thinking about the small print.

He had not framed it as something the clinic desperately needed from him. He hadn't brought her a schedule and asked whether she could fit herself around it. Here was a thing he wanted, before usefulness had been put around it like packaging.

“What part?” she asked.

“Learning how to explain without taking over.”

“You're making that sound personal.”

“It may have uses outside the clinic.”

She laughed, then let him continue.

He described a recent moment helping a colleague work through an ordinary task. There were no patient details in the story, only his own impatience with leaving enough time for somebody else to think. The colleague had understood the problem. Julian had supplied the next sentence before they could say it.

“I don't want to be the person everyone needs in the room because I never let them finish learning without me,” he said.

Eve thought of Robin moving the blank cards back to the end of the table. She could have told the story again and made the conversation hers. Instead, she asked what the course actually taught.

He showed her the outline.

“This is different from the time away before,” he added. “That was about developing my own clinical work. This is about what happens when I try to pass something on. I don't want to assume being able to do a task means I know how to teach it.”

Eve read the first section again. There was no promised new job, no demand to relocate, and no grand announcement concealed in the small print. There was a limited next step he wanted to investigate. She could be interested without turning it into the next crisis they would have to survive together.

They read together for several minutes. Eve asked whether the supervised practice happened during the weekend or depended on arranging it afterwards. Julian checked the notes and found that it was one of the questions he still needed answered.

“So the three circles weren't three decisions,” she said.

“One decision, not to pretend I understood the fee.”

“Very advanced.”

The dates were far enough ahead that they did not conflict with a booking already in their shared calendar. They still affected two days he would otherwise be home. The travel would have a real cost, and the preparation would need time somewhere other than the unclaimed edges of every evening.

Eve felt the beginning of an answer rise automatically: she could manage everything while he went.

She paused.

“What would you need from me?”

“Initially, to tell me what I'm overlooking about our plans. Not to organize it.”

“I might need a reminder about that distinction.”

“I can provide one.”

She checked the calendar on her phone. There was no appointment on those dates, only an empty weekend that belonged to both of them in different ways.

“I don't have something booked,” she said. “That doesn't mean I can promise a whole arrangement now. If you apply, let's check the ordinary home things once the dates are actually confirmed.”

“Agreed.”

“And I don't want you not to apply because you're waiting for everything to be effortless.”

He nodded, looking at the outline rather than immediately replying.

Eve realized she was waiting for a sign that he felt relieved. In another week she might have glanced at Dumpling, who sometimes paid disconcerting attention to a held breath or an abruptly quiet room.

Tonight the cat had slid off the sofa and was drinking water.

“How do you feel about telling me?” she asked Julian instead.

He blinked.

“Less foolish than I expected.”

“Why foolish?”

“Because I don't know whether I'll do it. I usually prefer to bring you an answer.”

“I live here. I'm likely to encounter the middle.”

“That is becoming apparent.”

She reached across the table. He turned his hand over beneath hers.

They didn't leave the page as a romantic object between two mugs. After a while they worked out the questions he needed answered and stopped before the conversation became an application completed by Eve.

Julian wrote them himself: what the fee included, what practice arrangements were expected, what time the clinic could realistically provide. Eve saw him add travel underneath, then a line about preparation hours.

“Do you want me to read the message?” she asked.

“After I've written it. For clarity, not to make it charming.”

“An enormous limitation on my craft.”

He smiled at last.

He drafted a short inquiry to the course organizer. Eve read it when he passed it over. One sentence contained three different questions, and she suggested separating them so the middle one wasn't lost. He changed it and sent the inquiry himself.

Then he put the page into a folder.

He had not enrolled, paid, or accepted anything. The kitchen table looked less dramatic without the three circles, but the evening felt changed to Eve.

“Shall we go out?” she asked.

“For what?”

“The library books. They're by the door, and we keep stepping around them.”

“The library's closed.”

“The return slot isn't.”

They checked the books, put them in a bag, and left Dumpling asleep on the sofa after his water and a brief inspection of the hallway. Nobody needed to carry him through the evening for the walk to count as family life.

Outside, the pavement still held warmth from the day. A shopkeeper was drawing in a stand of postcards, having discovered that one wheel preferred a different direction from the others. Eve stepped aside rather than volunteer an entire redesign.

At the library, one book refused to fit through the return slot.

“It went in the bag,” Julian said.

“A less demanding aperture.”

They checked that they were using the correct slot, then turned the book the way the sign indicated. It slid through with an undignified thud.

Eve kept the final book in her hands for a moment. It was the one Julian had been reading in pieces between shifts. A folded scrap marked a page near the middle.

“Have you finished?”

“No. Someone else has reserved it.”

“We could borrow it again later.”

“Yes.”

He took out his scrap of paper, noted the page number, and returned the book. Eve watched it disappear. There were things he was willing to leave unfinished now without making unfinished mean lost.

They walked the longer way home, not because either had prepared a date, but because Eve said she wasn't ready to go back yet and Julian said neither was he.

At the crossing he asked about the new product clip. She told him she had liked making the simple comparison clearer. She also admitted she was worried about the next month's bookings.

“Do you want to go through them?” he asked.

“Not tonight.”

“All right.”

“I just wanted you to know the worry was there.”

He squeezed her hand once as the light changed.

At home, Dumpling had spread across both sofa cushions. He opened his eyes when the door closed, looked at the empty book bag, and went back to sleep. Eve put the bag away and set the kettle on for one more small cup of tea.

The course might not happen. Its cost might be wrong, or the time unavailable, or Julian might decide a different way of learning suited him better. None of that would make tonight's conversation unnecessary.

He had brought her a wish while it was still a wish.

She hadn't found it in a hidden message or guessed it from a cat's paw.

When Julian came into the kitchen, Eve moved toward him and kissed him, briefly enough that he looked surprised afterwards.

“What was that for?”

“An unfinished answer.”

He considered this, then pulled her gently closer.

“I have several.”

“Don't turn it into a reward scheme.”

His laugh warmed the side of her face. Behind them the kettle clicked, and from the sofa came the soft thump of a cat changing position without providing commentary on either of them.
