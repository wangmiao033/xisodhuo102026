# Chapter 126 — Ready at Last

Mia brought a folder instead of a photograph, which was how Eve first suspected something had changed.

They met outside the neighborhood center after Eve's paid session. Four people had come, one had borrowed a pen permanently, and the room was already being rearranged by a committee whose members appeared to own more coats than bodies. Eve held her single box of materials against her hip.

“Have you got ten minutes?” Mia asked.

“Yes, if you don't require furniture.”

“There’s a bench.”

“There’s rain on the bench.”

Mia opened the folder, removed a plastic sleeve, and spread it on the wet wood. “Now there's administration on the bench.”

They sat. Inside the folder were a tenancy letter, a household schedule, and several pages filled in by hand. Eve recognized Nora's adoption application before Mia said anything.

“Again?”

“Again. For a dog this time.”

Eve looked up.

“I haven't chosen one from a photograph,” Mia said quickly. “And I haven't bought a bed. You don't need to make that face.”

“What face?”

“The kind where you're delighted but also remembering a drying rack.”

It was an uncomfortably accurate description.

Mia still lived with Rachel and Leah. Her work hours had become more predictable, and the evening class had finished rather than been sacrificed to an imagined pet. She had spent months doing the laundry route, then handed one collection day to another volunteer when work changed. In the course of those visits she had met foster carers and accompanied some of their arranged dog walks.

“I like going out,” she said. “Even when it's horrible. I hadn't thought about that before. I liked the picture of having a cat.”

Eve remembered white paws on Mia's phone, the unused yellow blanket, and the furious dignity with which she had returned the toys.

“Have you spoken to Nora?”

“She told me to fill this in. Fresh application. The old one doesn't carry anything over.”

“Good.”

Mia glanced at her.

“Good that she's told you what happens next,” Eve added. “I wasn't awarding points for paperwork.”

“I know.” Mia rubbed a damp mark off the sleeve. “Would you come to the household conversation if everyone agrees? You don't have to say I'm wonderful.”

“I could probably manage a whole evening without saying that.”

“I've noticed.”

Eve laughed, and Mia closed the folder before the rain could reach its contents.

The conversation took place the following week. Nora had read the application, spoken separately with the housemates, and checked the landlord's written permission. Eve attended at Mia's invitation, with Rachel and Leah's agreement. She brought biscuits and no camera.

Gerald, Rachel's bicycle, no longer blocked the corridor. A wall hook had been approved and fitted. The clear floor looked startlingly large, as if someone had added a modest extension by moving two wheels.

“Don't praise it,” Rachel said, catching Eve looking. “Mia will become unbearable.”

“I've always believed in Gerald's potential.”

Leah took the biscuits into the kitchen. The three milk cartons still wore different colored dots. Eve felt reassured by this survival of ordinary territorial conflict.

Nora began with the schedule. Mia had arranged leave for the initial transition, then shorter shifts temporarily. A professional dog walker had confirmed availability for two workdays, subject to meeting the particular dog and reviewing what care was suitable. Mia had included that cost rather than writing friend in the gap.

Rachel would remain unavailable after night shifts. Leah could offer occasional help when asked but would not become the regular weekend carer. Both had agreed to the dog living there, with their bedroom doors kept closed and shared areas discussed in advance.

“And if the walker is ill?” Nora asked.

“I've spoken to a second service. I still have to meet them. I wouldn't just send a stranger a key.”

“What if your work changes again?”

“I need to ask for notice. If I can't get it, this plan changes.”

Nora wrote something down.

Eve watched Mia's fingers flatten against her knees. Questions still cost her something. The difference was that she answered them before trying to discover whether the answer had pleased anyone.

They walked through the flat. The proposed resting area was in Mia's room, away from the route between kitchen and bathroom. There was space to move without stepping over a bed. Nora checked the doors, talked through access to the building, and asked about the shared outside area. Permission to use it did not make it private or secure.

At the front door, Leah demonstrated the awkward latch.

“It looks shut when it isn't. We've been pulling it twice.”

Mia's expression changed. “I forgot to put that down.”

“Then put it down,” Rachel said. “It's why we're here.”

They arranged to report the latch to the landlord before any placement. Nobody offered to solve it with constant vigilance and a hopeful sign.

Back in the kitchen, Mia took a small envelope from the folder. It contained her cost calculations, which she handed directly to Nora. Eve looked at the biscuit plate instead.

“I can leave for this part,” she said.

“Please,” Mia replied, then looked apologetic.

Eve took her tea into the living room. Leah joined her and switched off a presentation she had been reviewing.

“She’s actually listening this time,” Leah said.

“So are we.”

Leah considered that, then nodded.

When Eve left, there was no approval to celebrate. Nora had follow-up questions, the latch needed repairing, and a suitable match had not been decided. Mia walked her downstairs without the old slam of disappointment.

“I thought I'd hate doing all this again.”

“Do you?”

“Some of it. The printer especially.”

“A reasonable enemy.”

“But I know what I'm asking now.”

Nine days later, Eve met Toby.

He was a medium-sized brown adult dog with a white patch beneath his chin and ears that seemed to have agreed on different careers. One stood halfway up; the other folded. His foster carer, Denise, brought him to an arranged meeting in a quiet outdoor space with Nora and Mia. Eve had been invited for the latter part, after the people who actually needed to assess the match had time together.

Toby sniffed the edge of Eve's shoe, found it unremarkable, and turned back toward Denise.

“I'd prepared to be charming,” Eve said.

“He likes a hedge better than most people,” Denise replied.

Mia held the lead only after Denise explained the arrangements and stood beside her. This was their second meeting. Rachel and Leah had attended the first; another visit to the flat was scheduled. The repaired latch clicked properly now, Mia reported, with the solemn satisfaction of someone announcing a qualification.

Toby stopped beside a drain cover. Mia stopped too.

Denise talked about the routines she had observed, the things Toby found difficult, and what they still didn't know. He was settled in her house, but that did not predict exactly how he would respond to a different one. Mia asked about being left, noise in the stairwell, and what the first nights had been like in foster care.

Eve listened to the questions without adding her own reassuring guesses.

At one point Toby leaned briefly against Mia's leg. Mia's whole face changed. Eve felt the familiar urge to lift her phone, save the instant, give the story a picture.

Then Toby moved away to investigate a crushed leaf.

Mia did not call him back to repeat it.

On the walk home, Eve received a message from Julian asking whether she wanted dinner on Saturday. She said yes and proposed her flat, then told him she had met a dog with a complicated ear arrangement.

His reply came while she waited at the crossing. Most ears are administratively independent.

She smiled at her phone. There was plenty she could say about Mia, but the application was not theirs to discuss in detail. At dinner she told him only that someone she knew was going through an adoption assessment and that she had managed not to volunteer an unsolicited character reference.

“Difficult?” he asked.

“Terrible. I had several excellent adjectives.”

Dumpling occupied the chair Julian had moved closer to Eve's. Julian sat on the other side instead, leaving the cat to discover that controlling one chair did not control the evening.

“Did you want me to say anything?” Julian asked.

“No. I wanted credit for shutting up.”

“An underrecognized skill.”

They ate the dinner Eve had made slightly too salty and talked about his unsuccessful search for a replacement saucepan. Later he went home, carrying the container she had explicitly offered him rather than leaving it as another object with uncertain residence.

The adoption decision came after the flat visit, the remaining checks, and nearly three weeks of conversations and meetings. Nora called Mia directly. Eve found out when Mia asked whether she could help carry a bag on the agreed arrival day.

“Only the bag,” Mia said. “I've got the transport arranged with Denise. Rachel will be home. I don't want a welcoming committee.”

Eve arrived with empty hands and found the yellow fleece folded on a shelf.

“You kept it.”

“It survived my terrible judgment. It deserves a second career.”

Mia had bought a suitable bed after discussing Toby's needs, not tried to squeeze the old cat bed into a new story. The supplies were plain and ready. Denise brought the familiar items they had agreed upon, and Nora reviewed the written handover, contact arrangements, and follow-up. Mia asked one question twice because she wanted to be sure she'd understood.

There was paperwork to finish. There were ordinary costs to settle. There was a dog drinking water while the humans tried to keep their important voices quiet.

Eve carried the bag to Mia's room and left it where directed. Toby investigated the doorway, then withdrew toward Denise. Nobody treated the retreat as a rejection.

“Do you want a picture before I go?” Eve asked Mia, holding up her phone without opening the camera.

Mia watched Toby sniff the same chair leg again.

“Later. I want to see what he does.”

So Eve put the phone away.

At the front door, Rachel tested the latch after Eve stepped through. It caught with a solid click. Through the closing gap, Eve saw Mia sitting on the floor at an angle to the new bed, leaving space rather than filling it with her hands.

The scene was less triumphant than Eve had imagined on the wet bench. No animal had leaped into a person's arms and certified the future. A young woman had chosen, been assessed, asked again, and brought home someone whose preferences she was still going to learn.

Downstairs, Eve found a folded tissue stuck to the sole of her shoe. She peeled it off beside the bin and checked her messages. Her next client wanted a caption shortened. The community center needed the room restored differently at her next booking. Life had not paused for the occasion.

Mia sent one message before Eve reached the corner.

He has chosen the rug next to the bed.

Eve replied, An independent design decision.

Then she walked home to the cat who had never once consulted her about furniture.
