# Chapter 154 — The Argument About Five Outlets

Sophie stood in the proposed quiet area and could not sit down.

“Where's the chair?” she asked.

“Represented by the blue square.”

“The blue square is under a light stand.”

“The light stand isn't usually there.”

“Where is it usually?”

Eve pointed at another piece of tape.

Sophie looked at it. “That's a person.”

It was, according to the key on Eve's drawing, a person waiting to collect a photograph. According to the actual floor, it was a strip of blue tape folded back on itself because the adhesive had caught Eve's sleeve.

They were forty minutes into Sophie's paid layout session. The unit was almost empty again. Only the temporary table, a few chairs, and Eve's two equipment bags remained. The landlord had let them in for their booked period and asked them to remove the tape before leaving.

Eve had chosen low-tack tape, checked a small patch first, and brought enough colors to make a railway map. She had neglected to bring a layout that worked.

“The lights can go closer to the wall,” she said.

Sophie moved the folded stand without opening its legs. “Like this?”

“Yes.”

“And when it's being used?”

Eve opened the legs.

One crossed the edge of the blue square. Another occupied the route to the rest corner.

Sophie looked at her.

“I see it,” Eve said.

“Good. I charge extra for becoming a haunting.”

The photography area had seemed a sensible addition. Owners had asked about simple portraits before, and a plain background could serve both commissioned work and a few carefully arranged sessions. Eve already owned some of the equipment. Natural light came from the front window. A fixed place for the camera would reduce the time she spent setting up at home and then clearing everything away so she could eat.

She had drawn the lights, backdrop, subject position, camera, and a little waiting station. Then she had fitted the rest area into the remaining corner.

Sophie put her bag down at the edge of the room and walked to the plan.

“Your camera has more room than your people.”

“It needs a working distance.”

“So do people.”

Eve felt a small, stubborn resistance rise. She had already lost the sofa. She had already moved the welcome table, reduced the chair count, and kept the porch as an unresolved question rather than a ready-made attraction. The camera area was the part of the room she knew how to use.

“If I squeeze the equipment too much, I can't do the job properly.”

“Then don't squeeze it. Choose when the job happens.”

Eve looked toward the window.

In the drawing, everything happened at once because that was the only way the imagined business seemed busy enough. Someone sat for a photograph while someone else waited, two people talked quietly, and a workshop used the table. All these activities paid in her forecast. None of them had to hear the others in the forecast.

Sophie knelt and placed a yellow strip across the floor.

“What is that?” Eve asked.

“The thing you keep putting in your way.”

“My personality?”

“The table. We can assess your personality after lunch.”

They began with a normal owners' exchange. Eve moved the photography equipment aside and put four real chairs around the table. Sophie took the part of an arriving participant, carrying her coat, a bag, and an empty folder.

“Where do I go?”

“Welcome table first.”

“Then?”

“Choose a chair.”

“Can I put my coat here?”

“No, that's the materials.”

Sophie put the coat back over her arm. “Can I put my bag down?”

“Under your chair.”

She did so, catching the folder on the table edge. Papers scattered across the floor.

They were sample pages, but the annoyance was genuine.

“I meant to keep those in order,” Sophie said.

Eve bent to help, then stopped with one page in her hand. “This is your programming rota.”

“A copy. I brought something with weight.”

“Have you decided to keep the new rota?”

“Yes. The shared closing shifts worked during the trial. I accepted the longer-term role.”

Eve looked up. “You didn't tell me.”

“I did. In the message with the dates I could do today.”

Eve remembered the message. She had read the dates twice and replied to them immediately.

“Oh.”

Sophie took the page without making a speech.

“Congratulations,” Eve said. “Properly. Tell me about it when we stop.”

“I will. At lunch, when I'm no longer pretending to be your customer.”

They gathered the pages and tried the arrival again. This time Eve gave the guest somewhere stable to put the folder before asking her to remove her coat. The change required moving the materials box, which meant finding an actual place for the materials box.

By the third attempt, Eve had stopped calling objects temporary when she meant inconvenient.

Next they tried a photography booking.

Sophie stood on the subject mark holding a rolled blanket as an imaginary pet. Eve positioned the camera, then looked through the viewfinder.

A corner of the welcome table appeared behind Sophie's shoulder.

“That needs to move.”

“Where?”

Eve pointed.

“Quiet area,” Sophie said.

“Only while we're taking the picture.”

“Then I'm the person resting there.”

She stepped off the mark and sat in the quiet-area chair. The rolled blanket remained in her arms.

“I arrived with someone. I don't want my picture taken. I want to sit for ten minutes.”

Eve moved the table to another position.

It appeared in the frame again.

Sophie began laughing first. Eve tried to remain offended and failed. Every time she found the perfect camera angle, a necessary part of the room entered it like a badly behaved extra.

“The table is committed to a public career,” Sophie said.

Eve lowered the camera. “More committed than Dumpling.”

“The table can consent by staying still.”

“It hasn't.”

They laughed until Eve had to sit on the floor beside the tape.

Then she looked up at the wall outlets.

There were five places she had marked on the drawing. She had distributed tasks among them with optimistic arrows: lighting, laptop, charging, proposed drinks counter, miscellaneous. Miscellaneous had swallowed half the room without needing a shape.

Sophie followed her gaze.

“The outlets didn't make the room bigger.”

“No.”

“And five outlets don't tell you what the wiring can take.”

“I know. Nothing high-load until it's checked.”

“Good. But you keep arranging your people around places to plug things in.”

Eve stood and picked up the plan. A dark arrow led from the proposed camera position toward an outlet beyond the quiet corner. Even before an electrician considered capacity or any fixed changes, the cable route crossed where someone should walk.

She crossed out the arrow.

A reel would not make it a good route. Neither would asking every visitor to be careful.

They put the powered equipment away and continued using shapes on the floor. That made it easier to discard an arrangement without first winding a cable around her elbow.

By lunchtime the photography area had changed from a permanent part of the shared room to one possible use of the main space during a separate booking. Equipment would be brought out for that purpose, then cleared. The quiet corner stayed available and outside the shot. General activities would not share the room with an active shoot.

The change cost her two of the simultaneous services on her original page.

It also meant that a person could wait without being told to shift whenever Eve needed a better angle.

Sophie wrote the change on the sketch and put down her pencil.

“Lunch,” she said.

They ate sandwiches on the two chairs that had survived every simulation. Eve turned the plan facedown and asked about Sophie's new programming role.

This time she heard the answer.

There would be more responsibility for choosing activities and fewer last-minute calls asking her to make an impossible event appear competent. The pay was not extraordinary. The hours mattered. Sophie was pleased about one particular evening she could now keep for herself.

“What will you do with it?” Eve asked.

“Nothing you've commissioned.”

“An excellent program.”

“Possibly a class. Possibly sitting down. I'm leaving it undecided for a week.”

Eve liked the visible pleasure Sophie took in not having to know yet.

After lunch they tried the room once more, this time with no photographs involved. They used a folded chair for a person who had arrived early and a box for the supplies someone would need during the activity. Sophie insisted they count clearing time inside the booked slot, so Eve had to get the box out without making everybody stand.

They also tried ending the activity. Sophie put her coat on, picked up the folder, and immediately had nowhere to stand while Eve carried the supplies box to storage. They reversed the order. That left Sophie waiting beside the door with a chair in her path.

On the third attempt, Eve left the box where it was until the guest had gone, then began clearing. It took two minutes longer than her optimistic version and involved no negotiation over who should squeeze past whom.

She added those two minutes to the changeover time. The timetable would have fewer tight edges if she stopped counting a departing customer as an object already removed. Sophie checked the note, nodded, and finally allowed herself to stop being five different imaginary people.

The successful arrangement looked almost disappointing from above. A table, chairs, clear routes, a place for bags, somewhere separate to pause. There was no impressive diagonal feature. No clever corner serving three different purposes depending on where the photographer stood.

Eve photographed the tape for her notes, then photographed the room from the position of an arriving visitor.

The second image was better.

“How much did we throw away?” she asked.

Sophie compared the two plans. “About half.”

“Only half?”

“The most argumentative half.”

They removed the tape carefully and checked the floor for residue. Eve packed the equipment into its bags. Until she had suitable storage and the future arrangement was settled, it would go home with her rather than become the landlord's problem.

Sophie checked the time against her agreed session and added a final note about the next test. No extra afternoon appeared merely because they were friends.

At the door, Eve asked whether she could book another consultation after the remaining premises questions had answers.

“Send me the dates. If I can't do it, I can recommend someone.”

Eve nodded. The room would have to make sense to people who had not watched her invent it.

After Sophie left, Eve folded the discarded plan. One piece of yellow tape clung to the back. She peeled it off and rolled it between her fingers.

On the new page, the five outlets were small marks along the walls. Nothing stood in the center just because a wire could reach it.

She added the revised photography schedule to her calculations and reduced the income estimate accordingly. Then she made one last note beside the still-open corner.

No table stored here between bookings.

She underlined it before she could think of an attractive exception.
