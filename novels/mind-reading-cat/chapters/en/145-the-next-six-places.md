# Chapter 145 — The Next Six Places

Eve removed the extra column before she removed anything else.

For a week, the quarter she could almost have afforded had remained beside the quarter she actually had. Every sensible decision looked meaner in its company.

She saved the old version, closed it, and widened the remaining sheet until it filled the screen.

The two Good Company projects were there. So were her other confirmed commissions, ordinary expenses, the booked room session, and the hours all of them required. The first scheduled brand payment had arrived. She recorded it against the invoice and left the later payments where they belonged, in the future.

There was enough work to continue. There was less spare money than she wanted.

She began with the things nobody else was waiting for.

The microphone replacement could wait while she used a tested borrowed one for an existing client's recording, with the loan arranged for that job. The paid booking-page review became a narrower check she could commission after the next trial had earned its costs. The imagined series of neighborhood features became one article she could fit between paid deadlines.

Then she changed the part she disliked most: two mornings she had wanted for developing the owners' exchange went back into the availability she offered clients.

She did not fill them with imaginary sales. She sent specific notes to two previous customers, naming the work she could take and the weeks she had free.

One replied with a possible assignment. The other had no budget until later.

Both answers went into the proper places.

Dumpling watched from the arm of the sofa while Eve crossed the deferred microphone off a shopping list. His tail moved once over the edge.

“You're being very restrained about this,” she said.

He yawned and climbed down toward his food bowl. There were priorities that required no spreadsheet.

The confirmed room session had five paid bookings by the cancellation deadline.

Eve checked the number against her revised costs. With the existing materials, the simpler setup, and preparation kept inside its planned block, five would cover the room, supplies, charges, refund allowance, and the time she had counted. It would not cover a morning spent inventing decorative extras.

She looked at the half-finished welcome card on her desk.

The card was attractive. It also repeated information already in the confirmation.

She put it away.

At the center, the language class finished exactly when its booking ended. Eve waited outside with her lidded box instead of treating the previous group's last five minutes as part of her setup time.

A woman emerged carrying a stack of notebooks.

“Are you next?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“We've left the tables together. Is that useful?”

“Very.”

“We only did it because we couldn't agree how to separate them.”

“I accept the benefit without investigating the motive.”

The woman laughed and went down the corridor.

Inside, Eve arranged the chairs, checked the route around the table, and placed a sheet at each occupied place. The materials box went out of the way. There was no display to assemble, no camera to connect, and no animal hiding behind the furniture.

She had three minutes left to drink water before the first person arrived.

Ruth came without a cabbage this time. She had brought the feeding card from the first session, folded inside an envelope.

“It's changed,” she said.

Eve looked at the crossed-out wording and the much larger replacement beneath it.

“I can see.”

“We kept turning it over at the wrong time. Now it says the day as well.”

“Does that work better?”

“Usually. When it doesn't, we speak to one another. Annoying but effective.”

She put the card away. Eve had not asked for a testimonial, and Ruth had not arrived to supply one. She was here to work on instructions for a relative's occasional visits, which had become complicated enough to need sorting.

The other four participants arrived over the next few minutes. One was new to pet ownership. Another had lived with animals for decades and immediately asked whether she had to contribute a charming story.

“No,” Eve said. “You can be completely uncharming.”

“Excellent.”

The topic was making a short handover note useful to the person receiving it. Eve started with an invented example containing no private details: a long page whose important change for that particular visit was buried in the fourth paragraph.

She gave them a minute to find what the visitor needed to know today.

Two people found it. One found the wrong date. Ruth circled three paragraphs and wrote WHICH ONE? in the margin.

“That was my response when I made it,” Eve said.

The group laughed, and the work began.

They separated information that stayed the same from instructions that changed. They talked about where a note would be seen and how the person receiving it could confirm they understood. Eve kept the example about locating supplies and recording an agreed household task. When someone began comparing feeding quantities, she brought the conversation back to recording the instructions already given for that individual animal.

At the far end of the table, the quietest participant crossed out nearly everything on her page.

“Starting again?” Eve asked.

“I keep writing it for someone who lives in my head.”

Ruth looked up. “Do they pay rent?”

“No.”

“Then they can leave.”

The woman laughed and explained that her friend would be coming directly from work. The note needed to answer the first practical question without requiring the friend to read several pages while standing in the hall.

Eve asked what that first question would be.

“Where I've put the things this time.”

They worked from there.

During the break, one man asked whether there would be another session about organizing information received from different services. Eve wrote down the subject without announcing a new event across the room.

“Would you want that as a group, or a template you could use yourself?” she asked.

“A group once. Then the template. I don't want to come every week to be told I've misfiled something.”

“Fair.”

By the end, each person had a small next step. None was required to show the group a private phone number, household schedule, or care record. Ruth had a short draft she would test with the relative before turning it into the official instructions.

Eve finished at the advertised time.

This time, when the chairs scraped back, she did not feel obliged to ask whether everybody had enjoyed themselves. She asked them to send the two feedback answers if they wished, reminded them of the stated refund window, and helped one person find the exit that avoided the busy front corridor.

Then she cleared the room.

The coordinator arrived as Eve checked beneath the table.

“How long?” she asked.

“Eleven minutes, including the cup I nearly forgot.”

“That's usually where the extra minute goes.”

Eve returned the last chair and carried the box out before the next group's booking began.

At home, she entered the actual time before exhaustion could improve the figures. Preparation had taken slightly longer than planned. Clearing had taken less. After including follow-up and a small allowance for worn materials, the session still paid her something beyond the immediate costs.

A modest amount. A real one.

She kept the refund provision untouched until the window closed. Nobody requested one. The feedback included one person who wanted a quieter room, one who had used the example with a friend, and one who liked the session but did not expect to need another soon.

Eve saved all three.

She booked one more available date, not a series. Six places went on sale for a narrower information-organizing topic. Five sold over the following week. The sixth sold after someone asked whether listening without sharing personal details was acceptable.

It was.

Over the next six weeks, the work found an unremarkable rhythm. Eve finished the branch guide, corrected a measurement label with the product team, delivered the second commission, and recorded the payments when they arrived. Two ordinary clients gave her further work. The extra development mornings did not return by magic.

The next room session ran with six paid participants. The one after that had five, including two people who had come before and wanted the new topic. Eve reused the materials that still worked and changed the parts the feedback showed were unclear.

No session became an unofficial clinic. Nobody bought access to Dumpling. A person who wanted a different service received a clear answer before paying.

The results stopped looking like one unusually encouraging evening.

They also stopped fitting neatly into the room calendar.

When Eve asked about another early-evening slot, the coordinator showed her three dates. One conflicted with paid client work. One was too late for the people who had already asked to attend. The third had a committee booking immediately afterward, leaving exactly the same tight clearing window as before.

“I can book the third,” Eve said.

The coordinator wrote it down.

“Do you need something more regular now?”

Eve looked at the schedule.

“I need to find out what more regular would cost. And whether people want it often enough.”

“Tell me when you know. We can discuss what the center actually has.”

That afternoon, two people joined the notification list for the next suitable topic. Eve told them there was no extra session yet and took no payment. One wanted an afternoon. The other wanted the early evening she had just booked.

Demand, it turned out, came with a calendar of its own.

On Saturday she met Sophie for lunch. Sophie had received the written hours for the newer programming role and was comparing them with the rota she had been shown.

“One closing shift too many,” she said. “I've asked whether it's fixed or shared differently after training.”

“What would make you say yes?” Eve asked.

Sophie answered. Eve listened, enjoying the fact that she knew enough now to understand why one particular evening mattered.

When they discussed the trials, Sophie asked whether Eve had counted the time she spent carrying the box.

“Yes.”

“Both ways?”

Eve gave her a look.

“Professional suspicion,” Sophie said.

“Both ways.”

“Good. How heavy is it getting?”

“Lighter. I've stopped bringing things because they look like a business.”

They finished lunch without drawing a floor plan on the napkins.

That evening Julian came to Eve's flat at her invitation. She showed him the small record of completed sessions, then closed the laptop before dinner.

“People keep coming,” she said.

“Different people?”

“Some different. Some returning for a particular topic. Enough to ask what I do next.”

“What do you want to do next?”

Eve looked toward the lidded box beneath her desk. For the first time, wanting a place where it could stay between sessions had evidence beside it. The evidence was still small and rather inconveniently specific.

“I want to find out whether more room would help the work,” she said. “Or just give me more room to worry in.”

Julian smiled. “A useful distinction to investigate.”

They went out for a short walk after eating. Dumpling lay in his familiar bed while Eve put on her shoes. He lifted his head when Julian picked up his coat, watched them for a moment, then lowered his chin again.

Eve left his water where it belonged and checked the door as usual.

In her bag was one confirmed room booking. On her desk were the figures for a quarter smaller than the one she had imagined, but still moving.

For once, the next question had arrived after people did.
