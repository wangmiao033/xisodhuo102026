# Chapter 221 — Not Because Things Are Going Well

The chair made a noise whenever Julian leaned forward.

It was a small noise, a wooden complaint at the kitchen table. Eve had ignored it for three dinners. On the fourth, it interrupted him so precisely that she laughed before hearing the rest of his sentence.

“I was asking about the shopping,” he said.

“I thought the chair had answered.”

He stood and examined it. One fastening had loosened. They put it aside until they could deal with it properly and used the spare chair, which was comfortable enough but too low for the table.

Now, on Sunday afternoon, Eve sat on the floor counting the pieces while Julian consulted the fitting instructions he had found in the drawer. Dumpling remained in the bedroom, where sunlight and a folded towel offered a better arrangement than their company.

“Nothing missing,” Eve said.

“Good.”

“Nothing extra either. That's usually where confidence begins to fail.”

Julian looked up. “We're tightening a chair.”

“I have experience of ambitious chairs.”

They worked with the room closed to the cat, keeping the little fittings in a shallow dish. It took less time than finding the right tool. Julian checked the joints before setting the chair upright, then sat carefully and shifted his weight.

Silence.

Eve applauded once.

“A remarkable performance. Will it be touring?”

“Kitchen only.”

She put the tool away while he collected the instructions. The afternoon had no larger purpose. She had delivered a client revision on Friday, checked the next week's small sessions, and left the work phone in her bag. The new format still needed time to prove itself. She could give it that time without spending every Sunday imagining a different business.

When she returned, Julian was sitting in the repaired chair with both feet on the floor. The instructions were folded beneath his hand.

“Can we talk about something?” he asked.

Eve paused in the doorway.

“Have we repaired it incorrectly?”

“No.” He smiled, but the smile did not quite settle. “About getting married.”

She stayed where she was.

Through the bedroom door came the muffled thump of Dumpling changing his mind about the towel. Somewhere downstairs, a radio rose and fell as someone crossed a room.

The kitchen looked stubbornly ordinary.

“Now?” she asked.

“If you want. Not necessarily all of it now.”

Eve looked at his hands. Nothing small or glittering appeared between them. She felt relieved and, immediately, faintly disappointed by her relief.

“I don't mean that you need to answer a proposal this afternoon,” he said. “I'd like us to talk about whether we want that. And what we'd be agreeing to.”

“We've talked about the future.”

“Yes. I haven't said this part plainly enough.”

He lifted his hand from the folded paper.

“I want to marry you. I'm asking whether we can talk seriously about it before I ask you to marry me.”

“That is an extremely careful sentence.”

“I practiced a worse one.”

She laughed, and the laugh made it possible to leave the doorway. She took the other chair. For a moment she considered making tea. It would occupy her hands and grant the kettle responsibility for any silence.

Instead, she sat.

“What was the worse one?”

“It included circumstances.”

“Oh, no.”

“I removed it.”

“Thank you.”

He watched her without reaching across the table to make the moment easier. Eve was grateful for that too. She needed to discover what she was feeling before being comforted out of it.

Happiness was there, unmistakably. Beneath it lay something tight and familiar, a fear with surprisingly poor manners.

“Is it because everything's calmer?” she asked.

His eyebrows drew together.

“The shop isn't flooding. We've stopped arguing about every evening. We're managing the house better.” She touched the chair's edge. “The furniture has become cooperative.”

“I don't think a quiet fortnight qualifies us for marriage.”

“I know. I just—”

She stopped. That was not the whole question.

When she had been dismissed from her old job, people had asked what she would do next with the bright, expectant faces of people watching someone step off a broken escalator. Later, the question had changed. Was the business stable? Was Julian serious? Each answer seemed to lead toward another gate where someone else would decide she had finally arrived.

She did not want him to become the final gate.

“I don't want getting married to mean that everything I was struggling with turns into a charming story about before I found you,” she said.

Julian's expression changed. He put the folded instructions aside.

“Do you think I'd tell it that way?”

“No. Not deliberately.”

“That isn't quite no.”

Eve looked down at the grain in the table.

“I think I might let other people tell it that way. Because it's easier than correcting them. Then I'd begin acting as though it were true.”

She remembered the investor who had wanted Julian to approve her decisions after examining the plans she had prepared. The loss of that offer still had a place in the accounts. It had not become imaginary because refusing it was the right choice.

“I like being your partner,” she said. “I don't want that to become the only useful thing anyone knows about me.”

“I don't want that either.”

“I need more than us meaning well.”

He nodded slowly.

“What would you want us to do differently?”

The question was practical without feeling like a request to present a finished solution. Eve let herself think.

“Don't describe the venue as ours when you're trying to make me sound supported. Don't offer my time because we're going somewhere together. If a relative asks you whether my work is going well, you can say they should ask me.”

“I can do those.”

“And I need to do my part. I can't keep joking that I've acquired a sensible adult to justify what I'm doing.”

“You haven't.”

She looked up.

“Acquired one,” he said. “The evidence is mixed.”

She smiled. “The ears did considerable damage to your application.”

He glanced toward the jar where the cardboard ears still leaned, too large and too ridiculous to have found a proper home.

For a while they spoke about small things: what each wanted to retain, what already felt shared, how easily an invitation became an assumption. Eve wanted work she could describe without apology and evenings with Sophie that did not require arranging everyone else's dinner first. Julian wanted to keep learning, to spend occasional time alone without being accused of withdrawing, and to stop postponing difficult conversations until he had designed a perfect answer.

“You don't have to promise you'll never do it again,” Eve said.

“I'd prefer to promise that. It sounds efficient.”

“It also sounds unlikely.”

“Yes.”

He rubbed a thumb over a mark on the table.

“There are things I want to keep because they're mine. There are things I'd like to change because I'd be making a life with you. I don't think those have to cancel each other.”

“Neither do I.”

She meant it. That was what made the fear worth speaking about rather than treating it as evidence against them.

The bedroom door received one firm scratch.

Eve checked that all the tools were packed away, counted the fittings again, and opened it. Dumpling walked past her without looking pleased or offended. He went straight to the water bowl.

Neither human asked him to settle the question.

Julian stood to stretch. Eve joined him at the sink, rinsing two glasses while he cut the loaf they had bought that morning. The ordinary movement helped. Marriage had entered the kitchen, but they could still be hungry.

“There's something else,” he said.

She handed him a plate.

“I don't want to wait until your work is completely settled before I ask. Or mine. I don't mean ignore the money. I mean I don't want you to hear this as a reward for becoming easy to live with.”

Eve held the second plate against her stomach.

“I'm still fairly demanding.”

“I had noticed.”

“Your bedside reading has annexed part of the floor.”

“That's a separate matter.”

“No, it isn't. That's the whole matter.”

They laughed, then he took the plate from her and set it down.

“I want you,” he said. “Including a life that will sometimes make me uncomfortable. I don't want to use that sentence to make every problem romantic.”

Eve leaned against the counter. She had imagined, at various times, that certainty would arrive in one beautiful statement and make every question beneath it unnecessary. His statement had done almost the opposite. It made her willing to ask the questions without hiding the wish.

“I want this too,” she said. “Not just the discussion.”

His face softened so quickly that she wondered how long he had been holding it still.

“But I don't want to say yes to a version neither of us has described.”

“Then let's describe it.”

“Over more than one afternoon.”

“Yes.”

“With food.”

“An important condition.”

They ate by the window. No ring appeared in the bread basket. Eve was able to enjoy that fact without pretending she would never want one.

Afterwards, she took a scrap of paper from the drawer. She wrote three things they still needed to discuss: where they might live if work changed, what financial risks belonged to both of them, and whether they wanted children. Underneath she added family care, then stopped before the page became an instrument for eliminating every uncertainty from the next fifty years.

Julian read it.

“We won't know all the answers.”

“I know. I want to know where we don't have them.”

He picked up the pen and added a question about what they would do when their wishes changed.

Eve looked at the words, then at him.

“That's annoyingly good.”

“I've had a productive day. The chair and this.”

She folded the paper loosely and left it on the table. It was not an agreement, a wedding plan, or a test one of them could pass alone. They chose an evening in the coming week when neither had promised their attention elsewhere. They would begin with the work and money questions, and stop if they were too tired to hear each other properly.

When Julian took her hand, she moved closer.

“Can I kiss you during preliminary discussions?” he asked.

“That depends on whether the minutes are complete.”

“They aren't.”

“Good.”

She kissed him. The repaired chair stayed quiet beneath them when she sat sideways across his knees, an arrangement he accepted with more enthusiasm than structural caution. They moved to the sofa before testing its repair further.

Dumpling was already there. He opened one eye, judged the available space by ordinary cat standards, and refused to surrender the cushion.

Eve sat beside him, with Julian close enough for their shoulders to touch.

Her work would still need doing tomorrow. There would be difficult conversations after the ones they had planned. She did not feel transformed into a person with a completed life.

She felt wanted, and able to say what she wanted back.

On the kitchen table, beneath the unromantic light, the unfinished questions waited for both of them.
