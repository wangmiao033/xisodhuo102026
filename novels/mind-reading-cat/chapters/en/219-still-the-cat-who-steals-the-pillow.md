# Chapter 219 — Still the Cat Who Steals the Pillow

The toy came in a box with more instructions than Eve's first paid camera.

Sophie placed it on the table and announced that she had brought an intelligent gift for an intelligent household.

“That's a dangerous assumption,” Eve said.

“About the household?”

“About all of it.”

It was Thursday afternoon. Eve had finished her work early enough to meet Sophie for a late lunch at home, something they had arranged without attaching a venue task to it. Sophie had brought bread, a pastry each, and the elaborate toy. Her own production job had finally given her an afternoon without travel, and she seemed determined to use it in the company of somebody who didn't own a headset.

Dumpling sat beside the sofa, inspecting the box from a distance.

“He likes things he can chase,” Sophie said. “The shop said this was good.”

“Did they say it was good, or did it cost enough that you felt they ought to?”

Sophie winced.

Eve turned the package over and found the price on a label underneath.

“Oh.”

“Yes.”

“For that, it should do my washing.”

“Only if your washing meets its enrichment criteria.”

They laughed, then read the instructions properly before opening anything. The toy was a large, solid track with an enclosed moving ball and removable covers to vary the openings. No batteries, strings, or tiny dangling pieces were required. Eve checked the parts as she assembled it, set aside the packaging, and placed it on a clear part of the rug.

Dumpling approached.

Sophie held her breath in a way she had never done during any of Eve's public events.

The cat sniffed one opening, watched Eve move the ball once, and stepped over the track on his way to the sofa.

He settled on the cushion Eve usually used.

“It's a slow reveal,” Sophie said.

“Very sophisticated.”

“Perhaps he needs to work out the concept.”

“He's worked out where I sit.”

They left the toy alone and ate lunch.

Sophie talked about a colleague who kept sending revised arrangements as photographs of handwritten pages, each photograph slightly less readable than the previous one. Eve offered sympathy rather than a free redesign. Sophie described the disastrous sandwich she had sent a picture of last Saturday, then admitted the replacement hadn't been structurally much better.

“What did you do with your free day?” she asked.

“We played a word game badly.”

“How badly?”

“Julian couldn't identify an umbrella.”

“What clue did you give him?”

“A portable roof.”

Sophie stared at her.

“I'm beginning to understand.”

Eve protested while cutting the pastries. Dumpling opened one eye at the sound of paper, saw nothing intended for him, and closed it again.

“He seems very ordinary today,” Sophie said fondly.

Eve's knife paused.

Sophie was commenting on a familiar cat habit, not assessing his ability. There was no accusation in the remark. Still, Eve had spent much of the week bracing for the possibility that somebody would notice what she couldn't explain.

“He's allowed,” she said.

Sophie looked up from her plate.

“Of course he is. I meant he's being rude to my expensive purchase in a very recognizable way.”

Eve laughed, a little late.

“Sorry.”

“No need. But if the track doesn't suit him, tell me. I kept the receipt. The shop said we could bring it back within their stated trial period.”

“We'll follow their instructions. I won't pretend he used it enthusiastically.”

“Please don't. My feelings can survive one orange critic.”

After lunch they cleaned the table together, each taking the part nearest her without turning it into a test of friendship. Sophie had another appointment later and left at the time she had said she would. Eve walked down with her carrying the bread wrapper for the recycling.

Two children were standing with their father near the front of the building. One pointed toward the broad, closed sitting-room window. From that angle the children could see Dumpling on the low interior perch beside it.

“That's the clever cat,” the younger one said.

Eve stopped a comfortable distance away.

“That's Dumpling.”

“Can he do something?”

“He's sitting very well.”

The child considered this inadequate but not entirely false.

“Can you make him wave?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“He doesn't have to do tricks for people. Sometimes he wants to play. Sometimes he wants a quiet afternoon.”

The father nodded. “Same as you when I ask you to show Grandma your dance.”

“I don't know the ending yet,” the child said indignantly.

“Exactly.”

Eve smiled, but she didn't ask for the dance either.

The older child wanted to know whether the cat had gone to sleep because he was bored.

“He may just be comfortable,” Eve said. “We can look from here for a moment, but let's not tap on the glass.”

The father repeated the last part when the younger child stepped closer. They watched quietly. Dumpling stretched a foreleg, then changed position so only his back was visible.

“He's finished,” Sophie said.

“That was very short.”

“He has a strict contract.”

The children laughed. Their father led them away without a promise that the cat would be more entertaining next time. Eve and Sophie walked to the corner together, talking about a film Sophie wanted to see when her schedule eased.

“You were serious upstairs,” Sophie said before they parted. “About him being allowed to be ordinary.”

“Yes.”

“Good. The intelligent household can return the intelligent toy if necessary.”

Eve hugged her. Then she went home, where Dumpling had moved from the window perch back to her sofa cushion. Apparently his strict contract covered several excellent locations.

She sat at the other end and read for twenty minutes. The toy remained on the rug under her supervision, available but not advertised. Once Dumpling glanced at it. Eve managed not to reward the glance with an encouraging speech.

When she needed to go into the kitchen for longer, she put the track away. A new object did not have to remain out merely to defend the money spent on it.

Julian came home in the early evening carrying a small bag of groceries and wearing the expression he got when he had made several decisions and did not want another one presented as a surprise.

“Soup or eggs?” Eve asked.

He shut his eyes briefly.

“Is there an incorrect answer?”

“No.”

“Eggs.”

“Good. I wanted eggs.”

“You could have led with that.”

“I was practicing hospitality.”

They made an uncomplicated dinner. Afterwards, Julian changed out of his work clothes while Eve put away the groceries. From the bedroom came a small, startled complaint.

“Eve.”

“What?”

“I have been robbed.”

She went to the doorway.

Julian stood beside the bed with one bare foot and one sock on. Dumpling was retreating toward the hallway with the other sock dragged behind him, held by its folded edge.

“Don't chase him,” Eve said, then realized she had already begun to follow.

Julian stopped. The cat stopped too, looked over his shoulder, and continued at a deliberate walking pace.

“That's a particularly insulting getaway,” Julian said.

Eve fetched an appropriate cat treat from the closed cupboard rather than try to pull the fabric from Dumpling's mouth. By the time she returned, he had dropped the sock near the sofa and was inspecting an invisible point on the rug. She redirected him with the treat and picked up the sock, checked that it was intact, and handed it to Julian to put away.

“No chewing,” she told the cat. “You may submit a complaint in writing.”

Julian emerged wearing a different pair. He had put the remaining loose sock on top of the laundry basket while changing, intending to gather both afterwards.

Or so he thought.

Ten minutes later, when he went to collect it, the basket top was empty.

“You're sure you put it there?” Eve asked.

“Yes.”

“Not in the basket?”

“I am aware of the distinction.”

“Just establishing the scene.”

They checked inside the basket anyway. No sock.

Dumpling was not visible either.

Eve felt laughter gathering behind her teeth. Nothing about the cat's recent behavior had suggested a complete commitment to dignity. Ordinary did not mean sensible.

They looked under the bed, behind the chair, and beside the sofa. Julian checked the place where he had put the first recovered sock and found that he had laid it on a low folded blanket rather than the high shelf he meant to use.

“That one is gone too,” he admitted.

Eve looked at him.

“I was distracted.”

“By your other foot?”

He made a face at her and moved the blanket, revealing only blanket.

They found both socks in the shallow open box beside the bookcase.

Dumpling lay on top of them with his front paws tucked beneath his chest, looking neither guilty nor particularly proud. One gray heel protruded beside his shoulder. The other sock had been flattened into an unconvincing pillow.

Eve crouched at a distance and laughed until she had to sit down.

Julian examined the arrangement.

“Apparently none of the perfectly adequate cushions were suitable.”

“Perhaps they lacked a personal grievance.”

“I was looking forward to owning my socks.”

Dumpling closed his eyes.

They didn't leave fabric available for chewing merely because the discovery was funny. Once he stood to follow Eve toward his ordinary evening routine, Julian collected both socks, checked them again, and put them directly into the closed laundry container. Eve placed the cat's familiar small blanket in the box instead.

Dumpling returned, turned twice, and lay down.

The substitution apparently met his standards.

Later, Eve took out the new toy for one more brief, supervised opportunity. He watched the ball move and pushed the edge of the track away with his foreleg. Then he walked past it to reach the box.

“That's clear,” Julian said.

“Fortunately the receipt still exists.”

She put the track away carefully, ready to ask the shop about the return rather than manufacture a story of delight for Sophie.

Before bed, Julian sat on the sofa and reached for the cushion behind him. Dumpling, newly arrived, leaned firmly against it.

“You've had my socks,” Julian told him.

The cat stayed where he was.

Eve switched off the brighter lamp, leaving enough light for them to put away their cups. Her worry hadn't vanished entirely. She still couldn't explain the missing precise interruptions, and a normal examination had not explained the ability in either direction.

But tonight she knew several things without interpretation. Dumpling disliked the new toy. He liked a familiar box. He was capable of making two competent adults search a small apartment for laundry.

And he still believed every good pillow belonged to him.

Eve sat close to Julian on the remaining cushion. His arm came around her, warm and unhurried.

Between them and the lamp, the cat settled into his stolen comfort without earning it by doing anything clever at all.
