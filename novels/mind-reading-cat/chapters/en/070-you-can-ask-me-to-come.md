# Chapter 070 — You Can Ask Me to Come

Julian was carrying a loaf of bread when he arrived.

Eve had prepared several possible opening sentences. None involved bread, and the unexpected object gave her a moment in which to forget them all.

“For me?” she asked.

“For my breakfast.”

“Oh.”

“I can make it a gift if this is becoming awkward.”

“No. Keep your breakfast. I wouldn't want to start something.”

He glanced at her, and Eve immediately wished she had chosen a less interesting verb.

It was Tuesday evening. They had agreed to meet near the garden after his shift, with the understanding that he would message if he was delayed. He had been delayed by twenty minutes and had said so before she left home. Eve had used the time to feed Dumpling, wash a cup, and change her mind about her jacket twice.

Now Julian stood in front of her wearing an ordinary coat, holding a paper bag, and looking as if he had come because she had asked.

“Walk?” he said.

“Yes.”

The garden was busier than it had been on Thursday. A group near the gate was attempting to photograph a toddler who preferred the back of the bench to every proposed background. Eve and Julian took the other path, leaving the photographer to negotiate without professional assistance.

“How was your day?” he asked.

“Good. I delivered an edit that had the correct opening hours in every version.”

“Ambitious.”

“I try.”

“The cupboard is also still attached.”

“We agreed not to discuss it.”

“I thought you might want closure.”

Eve laughed. The sound helped more than rehearsing had.

They passed the herb beds and found the bench farther down the path empty. Julian put his bread on his own lap when they sat. Eve was relieved he didn't leave it between them, then embarrassed to notice herself awarding meaning to the seating arrangement of a loaf.

“You said you wanted to talk,” he said.

“I did.”

“Is everything all right?”

“Yes. Not an emergency.”

“That wasn't a complaint about being asked.”

She looked at him.

“I know,” she said, though she had needed to hear it.

A woman went past with a small dog who inspected the same patch of grass three times. Julian waited until they had moved on, giving the conversation room without manufacturing privacy they didn't have.

Eve told him about the meal at her mother's house.

She began with the three glasses on the table, the lemon in the jug, the moment Owen emerged carrying water. Julian listened with an expression that grew stiller as she spoke, though she could not tell whether he disliked the arrangement or simply understood that she did.

“Owen was nice,” she said. “That's made it harder to explain.”

“To whom?”

“My mother. Myself, for a while.”

“What do you need to explain to yourself?”

“That I can like someone well enough and still not want another afternoon with him.”

Julian nodded.

“Did you tell him?”

“Yes. Properly. He asked again when he brought my scarf back. I said no.”

“Your scarf?”

“My gray one. Yours is still yours.”

“Good. I was beginning to think scarves had developed an independent social life.”

Eve smiled, then looked down at her hands.

“My mother asked whether you'd made any promises.”

The words altered the quiet between them.

Julian shifted the bread bag on his knee. “What did you say?”

“That we hadn't agreed to anything.”

“All right.”

“I didn't tell her you were my boyfriend.”

“Thank you.”

His answer was immediate enough to sting, even though it was precisely the answer she had earned by telling the truth.

He saw something in her expression.

“Not because I object to being mentioned,” he said. “Because I would rather know what part I'm being asked to play.”

“I almost asked you.”

“To pretend?”

“To say something convincing. I didn't get as far as deciding what.”

He looked toward the path for a moment. The toddler near the gate had finally agreed to be photographed, apparently only if the photograph also contained a large, unremarkable stick.

“I wouldn't have wanted to lie,” Julian said.

“I know.”

“But you can ask me to come if you want company. That doesn't have to be an assignment.”

Eve felt her throat tighten unexpectedly.

She had spent Monday determined not to require rescuing. Somewhere in the process, she had almost turned the ability to manage her own conversation into an obligation to manage every uncomfortable feeling alone.

“I did want company afterward,” she admitted.

“You could have said.”

“You were working.”

“I might have said I couldn't. Or asked whether later would help.”

“That would have helped.”

“Then next time you can ask.”

She nodded, looking at the bread instead of at him because it was considerably easier.

Julian went on more quietly.

“I've had family conversations I didn't handle particularly well. I wouldn't assume I'd improve yours by being in the room.”

“That's a disappointing service description.”

“Yes. Very limited warranty.”

She laughed again. He smiled, and the tension eased without disappearing.

For a while they talked about the café. Eve described the shiny replacement button, her mother's stubbornness at the tailor, and the surprise of realizing the same woman who wanted Eve to accept a good-enough future refused to accept a good-enough button.

Julian considered that.

“Was the button wrong?”

“Yes.”

“Then perhaps she knew what she wanted.”

“So did I.”

“Yes.”

He said it without congratulating her for becoming an adult. Eve found herself sitting a little more comfortably.

A breeze came through the trees. She pulled the sleeves of her jacket down over her wrists. Julian noticed but did not automatically offer a coat or diagnose her choice of clothing.

“Want to keep walking?” he asked.

“Yes.”

They stood and took the path toward the far gate. The bread bag rustled every few steps. Eve wondered how she would ever describe this evening to Sophie without making it sound like a conversation she had imagined because she wanted it too much.

Then she remembered she did not need to describe it yet.

At the gate, Julian stopped to let a cyclist push a bicycle through ahead of them. Eve stepped beside him rather than behind. The path outside led toward the row of small shops, most of them beginning to close.

“Have you eaten?” she asked.

“Not yet.”

“Neither have I.”

“We could find something.”

They approached the takeaway counter they had used on Thursday, then found the shutter half lowered and a handwritten note about an equipment problem. Eve read it twice as though the second reading might produce noodles.

“Alternative plan?” Julian asked.

“There is a place around the corner.”

“Good?”

“The chairs are comfortable.”

“A stirring recommendation.”

“I haven't tried the food.”

They continued. Outside the next shop, a rack of postcards turned in the breeze. Eve paused at a photograph of the garden they had just left. The photographer had managed to make the ordinary low wall look like part of an estate people paid to enter.

“I could have done that,” she said.

“Bought a postcard?”

“Made a bench look important.”

“It was doing its job.”

“That doesn't usually impress an audience.”

“It impressed my knees.”

Eve looked at him and laughed hard enough to lose the sentence she had been trying to find all evening.

Perhaps that was why the next one came out without a proposal attached.

“I want to go on a date with you.”

Julian stopped walking.

Eve stopped too. A man coming out of the shop had to step around them with a parcel. She moved closer to the wall, suddenly aware of the precise size of her body and every possible place to put her hands.

“A real one,” she added. “Not to answer my mother. Not to make the introduction seem like a mistake because I've found someone else.”

“Eve.”

She looked at him.

“Yes,” he said.

The word was wonderfully brief. She distrusted her ability to leave it alone.

“Yes as in—”

“Yes, I'd like that.”

“Oh.”

He smiled. “Unless the chairs are the entire attraction.”

“They're only part of my criteria.”

“Reassuring.”

Her face felt hot. She could have laughed the request back into a joke, restored the comfortable ambiguity of a walk with someone who might simply be kind. She stayed beside the wall and let him see that she was pleased.

“This weekend?” she asked.

“I think so. I need to check the final rota before promising a particular day.”

“All right. We can choose something when you know.”

“Somewhere we both actually want to go.”

“Yes.”

“No reason involving borrowed clips?”

“Absolutely none.”

They began walking again. Nothing else had been settled. He had not asked to become her boyfriend in front of a postcard rack. She had not promised that a date would solve every uncomfortable conversation they might have later. The next step was small enough to recognize and large enough that she could feel it in her knees.

At the restaurant around the corner, the promised chairs stood behind a glass door beside a sign saying they were reserved for a private event.

Eve stared.

Julian looked at her.

“Your research was extensive.”

“The chairs remain comfortable.”

“From a distance.”

They ended up buying hot filled rolls from a bakery with two narrow seats by the window. Julian's bread sat beside his shoe. Eve chose the filling she wanted rather than the one easiest to eat gracefully, then lost a piece of cabbage onto the plate.

He passed her a napkin without comment.

They talked about food while they ate, and about the book he had abandoned halfway through because every character seemed unwilling to ask a simple question. Eve accused him of using literature to criticize her. He said he was hardly qualified to stand outside the charge himself.

“What else happened to you today?” she asked when they had finished eating. “I've taken most of the evening.”

He considered. “I spent ten minutes looking for my phone.”

“Where was it?”

“In the pocket of the coat I was wearing.”

“Did anyone see?”

“Tessa helped me look.”

“Oh, that's expensive.”

“She has already mentioned it twice.”

Eve laughed, then listened while he described the moment he had actually heard the phone vibrate against his own side. He told the story without making himself cleverer in the ending. She liked being trusted with something that could only reduce his dignity.

When they parted, he said he would tell her once his weekend hours were confirmed. She said she would think about somewhere to go.

At home, Dumpling greeted her by standing in the exact place she needed to put her bag. She moved it to the table instead and crouched to stroke him.

“I asked,” she said.

He leaned into her hand, interested only in what she was doing now.

Eve stayed with him for a moment. Then she stood, hung up her jacket, and left the coming weekend unwritten until two people could arrange it together.
