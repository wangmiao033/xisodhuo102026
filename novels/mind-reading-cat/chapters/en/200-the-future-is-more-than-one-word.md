# Chapter 200 — The Future Is More Than One Word

The walking book opened to a photograph of the sea, and Eve immediately wanted a different life for approximately four seconds.

There was a white path above dark water, a row of wind-bent trees, and nobody carrying three bags of venue supplies. She stood behind Julian's reading chair and rested her chin on his head.

“We could live there.”

“On the path?”

“Near the path.”

He looked at the small print. “The path is closed during nesting season.”

“My dream already has administrative difficulties.”

He tilted the book so she could see. A folded map slipped from the back and landed beneath the chair. Eve retrieved it, smoothing the soft creases on her knee.

Someone had marked two routes in faded pencil. One ended beside a station. The other appeared to stop abruptly in the sea, which suggested either a damaged map or an unusually decisive walker.

“Would you?” she asked.

“Walk into the sea?”

“Live somewhere else.”

Julian turned in the chair. “Possibly. Would you?”

She had expected him to continue the joke. Instead, she found herself looking around their newly settled room, as though asking about other places might offend this one.

“Possibly,” she said.

It sounded larger when she returned it.

They had planned to walk to the market and buy something for dinner. Eve folded the map along the wrong crease, unfolded it, and tried again while Julian found their shopping bags.

“Bring it,” he said. “We can look while we have coffee.”

“It's a map of somewhere we're not going.”

“That hasn't stopped it being interesting.”

She put it in her bag.

Outside, the weather was mild enough for open coats. The trees along the main road held only a few stubborn leaves, and the pavement smelled of a recent shower. They passed the bus stop that had become part of Julian's new commute. He checked the display out of habit, then laughed at himself because they weren't catching anything.

At the market, Eve bought peppers and argued gently with a stallholder about how many onions counted as a small bag. Julian found bread. They met beside a crate of apples, compared purchases, and discovered they had both bought lemons.

“We could open a very narrow business,” she said.

“Six lemons won't last long.”

“It would be a brief but focused career.”

They carried the bags to a cafe near the small public gardens. Their table was outside, sheltered from the wind. Eve spread the map between the cups and weighted one corner with a lemon.

Julian traced the coast with his finger without touching the old pencil line.

“During training, I liked being somewhere unfamiliar,” he said. “I missed home. Both things happened.”

“You liked that nobody expected you to know everything.”

“Among other things.”

She remembered seeing him listen in that unfamiliar team. She had wanted to be the person he turned toward first, then discovered that learning from other people did not remove her from his life.

“Would you want a longer period away?”

“I might want more training at some point. I don't have an offer sitting in an email this time.”

“Good clarification.”

“I'd tell you before I had built an entire decision around what I imagined you'd say.”

She nodded. The old argument had left something useful behind. It did not need to be held again in miniature over coffee.

“What about working somewhere else?” she asked.

“I don't know yet. There are things I want to develop at the clinic first. I wouldn't want to leave just because a new place sounded impressive.”

“Or stay forever because you've become good at finding the towels.”

“That would be a powerful reason.”

Eve lifted her cup. Steam fogged the lower edge of her glasses before she remembered she had put them on to read the map.

“My work could move more easily than the venue,” she said. “The content work, anyway. I keep saying I'm building something here, and then I hear myself as if I've poured concrete around my shoes.”

“You've signed a lease, not a declaration that you'll never travel.”

“I know. There are people and bookings and costs. I would need time to change things.”

“Yes.”

She liked that he didn't make it sound effortless. She could imagine going somewhere else without pretending her current life was a suitcase she could shut in five minutes.

Across the path, a child attempted to steer a small scooter through a puddle. An adult called for a slower approach. The child accelerated, splashed both of them, and stopped in astonishment at the consequences.

Eve smiled despite herself. Julian watched the adult inspect a wet trouser leg with an expression of weary disbelief.

“Would you want a place near your family?” she asked.

“Closer than now might be useful. But I wouldn't choose only on that basis. What about your mother?”

“She has opinions about where I should live. She also has her own life, which I occasionally remember.”

Julian smiled.

“If she needed more help later, I'd want to talk about what she actually needed,” Eve continued. “Not move first and find out afterward that she wanted somebody to fix a cupboard.”

“Mine would ask for the cupboard first.”

“Lucky you.”

They drank their coffee while the wind lifted one corner of the map. Eve moved the lemon. She felt a strange pleasure in discussing a possibility that neither of them could yet turn into a sensible plan.

When their cups were empty, they walked through the gardens. The market bags swung between them. A bench near the playground stood in a patch of weak sunlight, and they stopped to divide the weight of the groceries.

The child with the scooter was now negotiating with the adult over whether wet socks required going home. Both seemed sincerely convinced.

Eve watched them for a moment, then looked at the lemons in her bag.

“Do you think you'd want children?” she asked.

The question came out quietly enough that she could have pretended she had asked about dinner.

She didn't.

Julian sat beside her. He took a little time before answering.

“I think I might.”

Eve waited.

“I've imagined it sometimes,” he said. “Not very thoroughly. Usually the parts where everyone has slept.”

“That seems selective.”

“Yes. What do you think?”

She pressed her palms against the bench. The wood was cool and slightly rough.

“I don't know. Sometimes I see a family doing something completely ordinary and want to know what that would feel like. Sometimes I see the same family and want to go home to a locked bathroom.”

Julian nodded as though both were reasonable observations.

“I don't want children to be the next task I'm supposed to complete,” she said. “And I don't want saying maybe to become a promise somebody keeps in a drawer.”

“I won't keep it as one.”

“What if you decide yes and I decide no?”

He looked toward the path, then back at her.

“Then we'd have something difficult to talk about. I can't make that easy by answering for a version of myself I haven't met.”

The answer hurt a little, simply because it didn't erase every possible future difficulty. She was also relieved. She would have distrusted a cheerful claim that any choice would fit them equally well.

“I'm not asking for a decision now,” she said.

“I know.”

“Right now, I don't want to try. I want to learn how to live in the home we've just moved into.”

“So do I.”

He shifted one of the bags away from her feet.

“And if we ever did choose it, I'd want us to work out how both our lives changed,” he said. “Not quietly assume yours would absorb everything.”

Eve pictured her desk disappearing under things she was expected to need more. She didn't want that picture to be inevitable. She also knew a sentence on a bench couldn't establish every future arrangement.

“We'd have to be more specific then,” she said.

“Yes.”

The scooter passed again, now pushed slowly by its owner. The adult carried the wet shoes in one hand. Apparently a settlement had been reached that satisfied nobody completely.

Eve leaned against Julian's shoulder.

“I like that we can ask,” she said.

He rested his hand over hers. “I like that you asked.”

They stayed until the cold reached through the bench. On the walk home, the conversation moved to whether the sea photograph was taken at sunrise and how much money a short trip might cost. They did not book one. Julian suggested checking a few routes when they actually had dates available, and Eve put the map back in her bag without making a project out of it.

At home, Dumpling sniffed the groceries and lost interest when nothing emerged for him. Eve put the vegetables away. Julian washed the lemons, all six of them, and lined them along the counter.

The kitchen looked prepared for a small citrus emergency.

While they cooked, Eve thought about the word later. She used it constantly. Later she would finish the shelf. Later they might travel. Later their business and work and families would presumably settle into some arrangement she had not yet been shown.

“I used to think the future would arrive as a question someone else asked me,” she said.

Julian turned from the chopping board.

“A job offer. Or whether I'd marry someone. Then I'd say yes or no, and that would decide what my life was.”

He listened, knife resting safely on the board.

“I don't want to wait for one question to do all of that. I want to ask things too. Where we'd live. What work we'd keep. Whether the life we were making still felt like ours.”

“You can ask me.”

“I know. Today I actually believed it.”

He crossed the small kitchen and kissed her, tasting faintly of the apple he had stolen while cutting vegetables. When he stepped back, the pan was ready and the onions needed adding. Their future could wait while they prevented dinner from burning.

They ate beside the walking book. Eve had tucked the map inside without drawing over the previous owner's routes. On a scrap of paper, she had written only two things: a place she'd like to visit, and a question about work she wanted to think about herself.

Julian read it when she showed him, then added a walking route he wanted to try nearby.

That one might happen sooner. The distant coast remained possible. Children remained a real question with no settled answer, and there was no new promise hidden beneath either subject.

After dinner, Eve stood at the window while Julian cleared their plates. A few drops of rain marked the glass. Across the road, someone hurried to retrieve washing from a balcony.

She closed the walking book and put it on the shelf where either of them could reach it.

They had somewhere to begin, and enough blank space to keep talking.
