# No Sob Story This Time

The agency's reception desk was made from an old door.

Eve knew it was an old door because the receptionist told her so, with the faint exhaustion of someone required to explain an interior-design choice to every visitor. The handle had been left on. Eve spent the first minute trying not to catch her bag on it.

“Water?”

“Yes, please.”

“Still or sparkling?”

“Still.”

It arrived in a glass heavy enough to make her reconsider everything she had ever paid for drinking vessels.

Through a window she could see six desks, two ring lights, and a rack of brightly colored clothes. A woman at the nearest computer was editing a video of someone opening a packet. The same two seconds played repeatedly. Rip. Smile. Rip. Smile.

Eve put her portfolio on her knees.

The salary in the invitation still existed. She reminded herself of that. An old door and a repetitive soundtrack were not reasons to reject stable work.

A man in a soft gray jacket came out to meet her. He introduced himself as Martin and shook her hand while looking at the portfolio.

“Excellent. You've brought material.”

“Two campaigns and a recent community project.”

“Wonderful. And the cat?”

“At home.”

His hand paused on the meeting-room door.

“Oh. We thought you might bring him.”

“The invitation didn't say to.”

“No, of course. Not a problem.”

He smiled and ushered her inside.

Eve placed the portfolio on the table with its non-animal project uppermost. She had been pleased with the decision last night. Now it felt like a small flag planted in uncertain ground.

---

For the first twenty minutes, the interview was almost exactly what she had hoped for.

Martin asked how she planned a production day when several people needed the same location. Eve described an old campaign involving one tiny kitchen, three competing schedules, and a refrigerator that made a noise every time anyone spoke.

He laughed at the right part. He asked a sensible follow-up about approvals.

A producer called Naomi joined them and looked properly at the work, not just the pictures. When Eve explained why a beautiful shot had been left out of a campaign, Naomi nodded before she finished.

“Wrong promise,” Naomi said.

“Exactly.”

Something in Eve's shoulders loosened.

She could do this. She remembered the shape of a meeting in which her experience had weight. She could imagine returning on Monday with lunch in a bag and knowing which desk was hers.

Then Martin opened a presentation.

The first slide showed Dumpling.

It was a screenshot from a clip in which he had put his paw on a folded towel while Eve tried to count the stack. Someone had added a speech bubble: MY HUMAN IS HOPELESS.

Eve smiled automatically. “He does have objections to arithmetic.”

“The engagement is good for a new account,” Martin said. “More importantly, there's a hook. You're struggling. He rescues you. It's human, but the animal gives us a repeatable format.”

She waited for the production role to return.

The next slide contained sample headlines.

FIRED, BROKE, AND SAVED BY MY CAT.

HE KNOWS WHO YOU CAN TRUST.

THE CAT WHO READS THE ROOM.

Eve set her glass down carefully.

“I don't make claims about what he knows.”

“Not literally,” Martin said. “It's storytelling.”

“Some of those are claims.”

Naomi glanced at the screen, then at him. “We haven't tested language.”

“Obviously. It's early thinking.” Martin clicked again. “We'd build around your account first, then develop other personalities. You'd be both on-camera and production. A lovely combination.”

The salary was still there, like a person waiting outside a room after an unpleasant conversation had begun inside it.

Eve opened her notebook.

“The listing said in-house content planner.”

“It is. With an ambassador element.”

“How much of the week would be on-camera?”

“We'd stay flexible.”

“How much would involve Dumpling?”

“Initially, quite a bit. We'd need enough material to establish the format.”

Naomi answered more concretely. “Probably three shoot days a week during the first month. Some short setups, some location work.”

“Would that be written into the role?”

Martin smiled again, less comfortably. “We can send over the scope.”

“Yes,” Eve said. “I'd like that.”

---

They asked for a small exercise before she left: sketch three opening sequences for a pet-food partnership. No brand had been named, and Naomi explained that the exercise would not be used commercially.

Eve agreed to describe an approach, not produce finished copy.

She suggested a practical routine filmed around ordinary feeding preparations, with the animal free to leave the shot. Product claims would need to come from approved information, not her own invention. The person on camera could explain what they had actually used.

Martin listened, tapping one finger against his notebook.

“Could the cat choose between two people?” he asked. “One holding the product, one without? We could make it seem as if he knew.”

“We could film what happens,” Eve said. “We couldn't guarantee which person he approaches.”

“We can use different takes.”

“Different takes of a routine are different from making the animal endorse an idea he doesn't understand.”

For a moment she heard herself from outside: earnest, difficult, possibly unemployable.

Naomi closed her laptop halfway.

“Would you be interested in the production side if the personal-account element weren't part of it?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“That isn't the opening we currently have,” Martin said.

Eve nodded. It was useful information. It also hurt.

She asked her questions about hours and approvals anyway. Some of the answers were perfectly ordinary. Others returned to flexibility, ownership, a shared journey. She wrote down the phrases without supplying the missing details herself.

When the meeting ended, Martin said they could probably move quickly if she was excited.

“I'll read the scope,” she said.

Outside, the receptionist offered to take her glass. Eve thanked her and managed to leave without catching the bag on the door handle.

It felt like a very minor achievement.

---

The written scope arrived before her bus.

Eve stood beneath the shelter and read it while traffic pushed damp air against her ankles.

On-camera work was required. Use of her existing account was part of the arrangement. Animal availability was described as a core production resource. Hours included evenings and weekends according to campaign needs, with the method of compensating extra time still to be agreed. The agency would expect approval over commercial posts made through the account during her employment.

None of these things had to be wicked to be wrong for her.

That was harder than discovering a simple villain. She had liked Naomi. She had enjoyed answering questions about work she understood. The salary would have solved several immediate problems. If the terms had been closer to the original listing, she might have accepted.

She opened a reply, then closed it when the bus came.

A child across the aisle was attempting to put a sticker on the window. His mother negotiated a transfer to a notebook. Eve watched the sticker move to its new home with rather more attention than it deserved.

At her stop, she bought a small bag of oranges because she needed something to carry besides disappointment.

---

Dumpling was asleep when she came in.

He lifted his head, blinked at her blue shirt, and lowered it again.

“No verdict?” she asked.

His ear moved.

“Fine. I didn't bring the case files.”

She put the oranges in a bowl and hung up the shirt before it could become the uniform of an entire failed future.

Then she sat at the table and wrote a short email.

Thank you for meeting with me. Having reviewed the scope, I don't think the combined creator-and-production role is the right fit. I applied for an in-house planning position rather than a role requiring my personal account and my cat's regular participation. I would be glad to be considered for future production-only work with a defined brief.

She read it three times. The fourth reading added nothing. She sent it.

There was no liberating rush. Her bank balance did not applaud.

She remained unemployed.

An hour later, Naomi sent a brief acknowledgment. She thanked Eve for being clear and said she would keep the production portfolio on file. There was no secret better offer, no hurried bid to win her back. Eve saved the message with the rest of the correspondence. A civil ending was still an ending. She allowed herself to check the jobs board once, then set a limit on how long she would scroll before dinner.

For a while she allowed herself to be unhappy about that without turning it into proof that she should have said yes.

When Sophie called after her shift, Eve told her the whole interview, including the parts she had liked.

“That makes sense,” Sophie said.

“I wish they had been awful from the first minute.”

“More convenient.”

“I could have come home outraged instead of doing this complicated thing where I still want their money.”

“You're allowed to want the money.”

Eve peeled an orange. A fine spray landed on the notebook, making one of her carefully written questions smell cheerful.

“I asked for the scope,” she said.

“Good.”

“And then I actually read it.”

“Revolutionary.”

Sophie had another early start. Eve did not keep her on the phone until the disappointment had been entirely used up. After they hung up, she ate the orange and opened a fresh document.

She wrote three services she could offer without promising to become a performing household: short-form editing, small-event content planning, and practical interviews. Under each, she listed what a client would receive, what they would have to supply, and where extra work began.

It took longer than writing a dramatic account of the interview would have taken.

She nearly opened the camera app anyway.

There was a familiar version of the story ready to go: a desperate woman, an unreasonable employer, a wise cat who somehow saved her. It would have had an excellent opening line. It would also have left out Naomi, the sensible questions, and the fact that no cat had attended the interview at all.

Eve put the phone facedown.

Dumpling woke, stretched, and came to investigate her chair. He stepped onto the empty one beside it instead, turning twice before settling.

“Very good,” she told him. “You can be head of not attending meetings.”

He closed his eyes.

Eve returned to the service list. At the bottom, below the three modest offers, she added a sentence for herself rather than a customer.

A clear no does not have to become a story about being rescued.

Then she deleted the sentence from the business document and wrote it in her notebook instead. The clients did not need that part.

They needed to know what she could do.
