# Chapter 119 — The Cat Only Owns His Bed

At five seventeen, Eve closed the last work file and found her left slipper in Dumpling's bed.

The right one remained beneath her desk, where she had abandoned it during a call. She put it on, crossed the room with one bare foot, and looked down at the cat.

Dumpling lay with his chin on the stolen slipper. Beneath his shoulder protruded a dark piece of fabric.

"That had better not be something expensive."

She crouched and lifted the edge. A knitted hat came free, slightly flattened and distinctly covered in orange hair. Julian's. She remembered him taking it off on one of the rainy evenings, then couldn't remember seeing it again.

The gap between the sofa cushions seemed the likely source. She looked there and found a coin, a crumb, and the folded receipt from the noodles. An entire archaeological period of a very short domestic history.

Dumpling reached one paw toward the hat.

Eve held it away.

"No. This belongs to someone who probably wants a warm head."

He climbed out of his bed and followed it as she stood. She put the hat on the shelf, retrieved her slipper, and returned the cat's small soft toy to the bed. Dumpling sniffed it, apparently unimpressed by the substitution.

Her phone lay beside the lamp, charged and quiet. She could send Julian a photograph of the hat. He might laugh. He might say he could collect it on his way home. She could tell him that wasn't necessary, or invite him in for ten minutes, which would become dinner because there was still food.

She put the hat inside a clean paper bag and wrote his name on the folded top.

It would keep.

For the first few minutes after finishing work, the flat felt less like freedom than a venue where nobody had arrived. She carried her mug to the kitchen, looked at the clock, and wondered whether five twenty was too early to decide what to eat.

There was no reason to decide now. There was no reason not to.

She opened the refrigerator and took out an egg.

Then she put it back.

"This is going extremely well," she told Dumpling, who had come to inspect the refrigerator from a safe distance.

He looked toward his bowl. His own dinner would occur at its familiar time, whatever crisis of choice she was having. Eve checked his water, cleaned the little patch around it where he had splashed, and opened the curtains wider so the last daylight reached his usual resting place.

He went to the window instead of remaining beside her.

She was absurdly relieved not to be the only available attraction.

The drawing book waited on the shelf. The paperback Sophie had lent her waited on the table. A basket of clean washing waited beside the bedroom door. Eve considered all three and recognized the danger of making a triumphant itinerary for her first deliberately solitary evening.

She chose the washing because she wanted the basket empty.

The fitted sheet resisted folding in the familiar way. She held two corners, inserted the wrong one into the other, and produced a shape suggesting an animal that had failed to evolve. Usually she would have laughed and called somebody to witness the failure. There was nobody to call from the next room.

She folded it badly and put it away.

At the bottom of the basket were the comfortable trousers with the hole near the knee. She changed into them, found a clean top, and tied her hair back without checking the mirror. The lack of an audience was becoming pleasant in small, physical increments.

Dumpling returned carrying a sock.

"We've just completed that department."

He dropped it beside her foot, then walked toward his bed. Eve followed, curious despite herself.

Her other slipper had reappeared there.

She looked from the bed to the cat. His tail lifted. Near the table, a pale scrap of fabric lay half tucked beneath the chair: a clean hand towel she had left on the low shelf. He had not managed to move it far.

Maybe he wanted familiar smells together. Maybe the rearranging was play. Eve had seen enough odd responses to intense feelings to know there might be another layer, but she could not extract a tidy explanation from a sock.

Last night, when Julian had put on his shoes, she had strongly wanted him to stay and strongly wanted the evening alone. If Dumpling had caught anything of that nearby tangle, it would hardly have supplied instructions even to a human.

Today she was not going to turn the results into instructions for herself.

She returned the sock to the drawer, hung the hand towel out of reach, and put the slipper on her foot.

Dumpling sat beside his bed, looking up.

"You have a bed," she said. "A very nice bed. I have the rest of the flat to make mistakes in."

She checked the stitching on his soft toy before offering it again. This time he hooked it with a paw and drew it under his chest. Eve sat on the floor nearby, legs comfortably folded, and watched his ears while a scooter went past outside.

She did not summon Julian to repair the cat's disappointment. She was not even certain disappointment was the right word. Dumpling blinked, lowered his head, and began washing one forepaw.

After a few minutes she stood and made dinner.

The egg became fried rice with the remaining vegetables. She used the pan she preferred even though it was slightly too large for one portion. At the last moment she added more pepper than usual, because she wanted it and no one else would be eating any.

The first mouthful made her cough.

"A successful act of independence," she said to the empty kitchen.

She added more rice.

At the table she discovered that she had put out two sets of chopsticks. The second lay neatly beside the empty place where Julian had sat the previous evening.

For a moment the sight made her sad.

She picked them up and returned them to the drawer. It was possible to miss him without finding the evening wrong. She had missed Sophie after their trip too, had wanted to continue a joke after they parted at the station. Missing a person did not always require retrieving them.

She ate the rice while reading the back cover of Sophie's paperback. Halfway through, she put the book aside because she preferred watching the changing square of light on the opposite wall. It made no useful picture. The room was untidy just outside it.

After dinner she fed Dumpling, washed both bowls, and left the pan soaking while she made tea.

Her phone lit up.

Julian had sent a photograph of the container she had given him, now empty except for a fork.

Cabbage resolved. Thank you.

She smiled.

Hat discovered. Safe here. No urgent collection required.

A reply arrived almost immediately.

I wondered where it had gone. How's your evening?

She looked at the table, the pan in the sink, the pencil she had not yet picked up.

Good. Pepper-related setback. Otherwise good. Yours?

Quiet. Found the floor beneath the laundry.

Congratulations to both of you.

He sent a laughing face. Eve put the phone down without inventing another subject. She had enjoyed the exchange. She wanted to return to what she was doing, which at that moment was nothing more demanding than holding warm tea.

The feeling was new enough that she sat with it.

From the neighboring flat came a burst of music, followed by the muffled sound of someone moving furniture. Eve had heard these sounds before without really listening. Tonight she found herself guessing which piece of furniture had caused the scrape, then gave up when a second scrape contradicted her entire theory.

She put on music of her own, quietly. A song she liked came up, and she sang the one line she knew with confidence, replacing the rest with sounds that could not reasonably be called words. Dumpling lifted his head. She continued until the chorus ended, then turned the volume down because she preferred the room quieter after all.

Later she opened the drawing book at an exercise about shadows. The example involved a bowl, an apple, and a cloth arranged with an attractive carelessness that had probably required several attempts. Eve had a bowl and a cloth. She substituted a potato for the apple.

The potato had a stubborn shape. She turned it twice, decided one side looked more interesting, and began.

At first she tried to copy the example's soft shading. Her pencil produced a dark patch resembling a bruise. She erased it, made it worse, and turned the page.

Dumpling jumped onto the empty chair.

"You may observe. There will be no feedback form."

He watched the pencil tip until she put it down. When he stretched toward the table, she moved the supplies away and offered him a short play session with his usual toy instead. They crossed the rug in several undignified bursts. He made one ambitious leap that landed well short of the target, then sat down as if the jump had been a private matter she had misunderstood.

Eve laughed. There was no one to tell the joke to, and still it was funny.

When he lost interest, she put the toy away and returned to the drawing. The potato had rolled to a new angle. She let it stay.

By the time the tea was cold, she had made something recognizably solid, though not unmistakably a vegetable. The shadow was better than the object casting it. She wrote the date in the corner and left the page unsigned because nobody needed to establish ownership of this particular potato.

She did not send a photograph.

Instead she put the pencil down, leaned back, and felt how tired she was.

Not exhausted. Not dissatisfied. Just ready to stop doing things.

The pan was still soaking. She washed it before the rice could become a problem for morning, wiped the counter, and checked that the paper bag with Julian's hat was closed on the high shelf. Nothing in it needed to become tonight's reason for a visit.

In the bedroom she chose which side of the bed to lie on and then lay diagonally, reading Sophie's book with a pillow under her knees. The dog appeared on page seven and immediately showed better judgment than the people. Eve composed a remark to Sophie in her head but did not reach for her phone.

She could remember it until their next conversation. Or she could forget it, and the evening would still have happened.

Dumpling came in after a while, inspected the space beside her, and returned to his own bed in the other room. Eve heard the faint creak of the floor, then nothing.

She read three more pages.

When she turned out the light, she wanted Julian's arm around her for one clear moment. Then she stretched both legs into the cool part of the sheet and wanted exactly this.

She let both wishes exist without asking either to win.
