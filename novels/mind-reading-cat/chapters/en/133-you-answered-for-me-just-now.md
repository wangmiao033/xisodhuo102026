# Chapter 133 — You Answered for Me Just Now

They reached the end of the street before Julian spoke again.

“Do you have something on the first Sunday?”

Eve looked at him. The question was so close to the problem, and so determined to miss it, that she had to take a breath before answering.

“I don't know. I haven't opened my calendar.”

“Then we can check.”

“You told your mother I'd keep it clear. Every month.”

“I thought it would be easier than trying to arrange each visit from nothing.”

“For whom?”

He stopped beside the bus shelter. A woman already sitting inside glanced at them, then returned to her phone. Eve lowered her voice.

“I don't want to have this conversation here.”

Julian looked along the road. “There's a café around the corner.”

“Can we just walk a little farther first?”

“All right.”

They continued without holding hands. The dessert container knocked softly against the side of Eve's bag. She had enjoyed that pudding. She had liked his mother's joke about the plant. Those facts remained annoyingly intact beside her anger.

At the small park beyond the shops, they found an empty bench facing a closed bowling green. Eve sat down. Julian stayed standing until she moved her bag from the other end.

“You answered for me just now,” she said. “I was going to say I needed to check. You made it sound as though we'd already agreed.”

“I didn't mean to commit you to something you couldn't do.”

“You said I could keep the afternoon clear.”

He looked at the grass.

“Yes.”

“And when I said I hadn't checked, you said there would be time to sort it out.”

“I meant we could change it if necessary.”

“So I get to cancel a promise I never made.”

The sentence came out sharper than she intended. She did not retract it. Across the green, someone wheeled a bin along the path, its lid bouncing with every uneven paving stone.

Julian rested his elbows on his knees.

“I was trying to stop the questions,” he said.

“About whether we see each other?”

“About everything. Your work, my hours, whether we can arrange an ordinary lunch without it becoming a project.”

“We had arranged this lunch.”

“I know.”

“Then why did we need to promise twelve more?”

“We didn't.”

She had expected him to argue longer. The admission left her with all the heat she had gathered and nowhere useful to put it.

For a while neither of them spoke.

Eve watched a bird walk beneath the neighboring bench. She wanted Julian to understand not only the sentence but the strange shrinking feeling that had followed it. At his parents' table she had become a person whose work could be moved around because someone else described it as movable.

“My weekends aren't blank because they don't follow a rota,” she said. “Some of the paid work is there. And sometimes the free time is something I've kept for myself.”

“I know that.”

“You knew it at my flat. Today you forgot it in front of your mother.”

He turned toward her. “That's fair.”

She looked down at her hands, disappointed that being right had not made her feel close to him.

“I liked them,” she said.

“I know.”

“No, you don't. I haven't told you yet.”

His mouth tightened. Then he nodded.

“All right.”

“I liked talking to your father about the printer. I liked your mother showing me the postcards. I would have liked being asked back.”

“You were asked.”

“And then you accepted for me.”

He closed his eyes briefly.

“Yes.”

That was the part she wanted to keep plain. There was no reason to make his parents into people she could not bear to visit. His mother had even said if that suited. Julian had supplied the certainty, and then Eve had sat silently while his family began using it.

She rubbed the side of her thumb where a paper edge had caught it earlier.

“I wish I'd spoken sooner.”

“You did speak.”

“After the calendar was already coming out.”

“I should have stopped then.”

“Yes.”

A bus passed beyond the railings. Eve could see its upper windows moving behind the bare shape of the shelter. She had an absurd urge to catch it, go home, feed the cat, and postpone this until she could present a calm version of herself.

Instead she stayed.

Julian sat back. “At home I answer things quickly so we can get past them. I know that's not an excuse.”

Eve thought of breakfast, the coat pocket, the shelf. He had sounded irritated by a tape measure and somehow become more agreeable when the answer involved her time.

“You said no to your father about measuring the shelf,” she said. “Then yes for me about lunch.”

“I said after dessert.”

“You negotiated it.”

He looked at her, then down again.

“I did.”

She waited. For once she did not help him complete his own explanation.

“I wanted them to see that this is serious,” he said finally. “That you aren't someone I see when there happens to be space.”

The words touched her. They also made her angry in a quieter way.

“You could have said that.”

“I could.”

“I'd have liked hearing it. I don't want seriousness demonstrated by how many Sundays you can book me for.”

“No.”

He turned his hands over on his knees, palms briefly open, then put them together again.

“I'm sorry. I made an arrangement using your time. I should have asked you, and I shouldn't have brushed past you when you tried to answer.”

Eve nodded. The apology was specific enough to leave her no argument with its edges. She still needed the afternoon to change outside this bench.

“What will you tell her?” she asked.

“That I answered without checking with you. There isn't a monthly arrangement. I'll ask about a single date when we both know whether we want it.”

“When we know whether we're available,” Eve began, then stopped herself.

He noticed. “Want it as well.”

“Yes.”

“I'll tell her that.”

“Don't say I'm upset about coming.”

“I won't.”

She could imagine the softened explanation already: Eve was sensitive about her work, Eve needed flexibility, Eve had asked him to tell them. Each would put her at the center of a problem he had created.

“I don't want to be the reason you withdraw your own answer,” she said.

Julian met her eyes.

“I'll say I got it wrong.”

They sat quietly. The bird had found something beneath the other bench and was attacking it with fierce concentration. Eve felt her shoulders beginning to hurt from holding themselves so still.

“I did something similar last week,” she said.

“With the flat?”

“Almost. I wanted to tell my mother we'd look at the leaflet so she'd stop asking. You'd have had homework because I didn't want another difficult minute.”

“You didn't say it.”

“No. But I understood how easy it would be.”

She did not offer that as a discount on what had happened. She needed to know she could recognize the impulse without agreeing to its result.

Julian looked toward the park gate. “Do you want to go home?”

“Soon. I'd like water first.”

“We can get some.”

At the café, the only empty table was small and badly positioned beside a display of greeting cards. They took it anyway. Eve bought a sparkling water; Julian ordered tea. Neither of them was hungry.

A card at eye level depicted a cat wearing a tie. Eve turned the rack a few inches because the expression was irritating her.

Julian watched.

“Personal disagreement?”

“He looks as though he's about to ask about my business model.”

Julian laughed once, then stopped, as though unsure whether the laugh had arrived too early.

Eve smiled. “It's allowed.”

They drank in silence for several minutes. Other conversations filled the room. Someone wanted to know whether a cake contained nuts. Someone else had misplaced a glove. Ordinary concerns continued without requiring them to finish being upset.

Julian took out his phone and set it beside his cup.

“I can call her now.”

“Do you want to?”

“I want to correct it.”

“Not from this table. I don't want to sit here while you manage her reaction for my benefit.”

He put the phone away.

“Tomorrow, then. I'll call from home when there’s time to talk.”

“That would be better.”

Eve checked her own phone. Her mother had sent a photograph of a pair of curtains and asked whether the color was too dull. There was no hidden property supplement in the message, no request for Julian to judge them.

She answered the question about the curtains. It took half a minute.

Then she remembered that her mother had asked her, during a later phone call, whether Julian might look at that development when he was next free. Eve had said they weren't house hunting. Her mother had said looking wasn't hunting, which left the request hovering exactly where she liked to keep inconvenient answers.

“I need to finish something with my mother too,” Eve said.

“The leaflet?”

“Yes. She keeps leaving a small opening in the answer. I've let it stay because I don't want to sound harsh.”

Julian nodded. He did not tell her what to say.

Outside the café, they checked the bus information. Eve could take the next service toward home. Julian needed one going the other way.

Usually she would have suggested another ten minutes together. Today she wanted to get back to her own room, change out of the careful blouse, and sit somewhere she did not have to work out her expression.

“I want to go home on my own,” she said.

“All right.”

“I'd still like to talk tomorrow.”

“So would I.”

Her bus appeared at the lights. Julian moved aside so she could stand near the stop.

She touched his sleeve.

“I'm glad you told me what you were trying to say.”

“I'm sorry I said something else.”

“I know.”

They kissed briefly, with none of the relief she might once have demanded from a kiss after an argument. Then she boarded the bus and found a seat by the window.

At home, Dumpling was asleep on the sofa. He woke when she put down her bag, stretched, and wandered toward his bowl without inspecting her mood.

On the desk, her paper calendar lay beneath a client folder. Eve uncovered it. The first Sunday of the following month had a penciled editing block, movable but real. The month after that was empty.

She looked at the empty square longer than the occupied one. She could have defended the editing hours with a deadline. She could even have moved them and attended lunch willingly. The blank square offered no evidence beyond the fact that she hadn't chosen what to do with it yet.

She left both months as they were.

Julian sent a message while she was hanging up her blouse: Home. Thank you for talking to me before we let it become the arrangement.

She answered that she was home too. Then she added that she would be free for a call after her delivery the following afternoon.

He replied with a time, as a question. She said yes.

Eve changed her clothes and gave Dumpling his dinner. Her own dessert waited in the refrigerator. She could still eat it later. Liking the pudding had never been the problem.
