# Chapter 183 — The Cheapest Traveling Companion

The client wanted photographs of a room before anybody had been invited to make it untidy.

“Saturday morning,” she said. “Tables laid out, materials ready, then a few with our two organizers. We won't have guests there yet.”

Eve checked the written brief against her calendar. A small craft business was opening a teaching room in the next city over. She had quoted for the shoot before Julian's course had been confirmed. The client had now approved the fee and the ordinary return train fare.

On the map, the teaching room and Julian's temporary accommodation appeared tantalizingly close.

On the rail website, they were separated by a transfer, a ticket supplement, and several opportunities for her to behave foolishly.

She wrote down the actual times. Then she opened a second tab for somewhere to stay.

The cheapest room was no longer the cheapest once cleaning charges appeared. The next one required a bus that stopped running before her intended arrival. The attractive little guesthouse she had saved in a burst of anticipation cost more than she could spare after putting aside money for venue rent.

She closed it, opened it again, and stared at a photograph of a breakfast tray.

Dumpling jumped onto the chair beside her. His tail brushed her arm, and he peered at the empty plate on the table.

“You're not included in the breakfast,” she said.

For one ridiculous moment, she imagined taking him. A cat peeking from a travel carrier, a reunion at a station, a little sequence of photographs about love making a journey. The thought had the glossy finish of an idea she would once have followed until the exhausted creature inside it became somebody else's problem.

Dumpling lowered his head to sniff a crumb and sneezed.

She moved the plate away.

“No. You get the boring weekend.”

The client had paid for professional photographs, not a trip involving her pet. Julian's room was temporary accommodation, not a suitable holiday arrangement for a cat. And nothing about the train prices became kinder if she put an animal through an unnecessary day of travel.

She opened her contacts instead.

The paid carer whose details Mia had passed on could do two Saturday visits. Eve sent the times she needed, checked their experience with cats, and asked for the price. Then she checked with Robin about the afternoon booking at You, Me & Paws.

Robin's answer was prompt.

*I'm already down as host. Are we changing anything?*

*No. Checking I haven't invented an extra version of you.*

*The current version would like the materials left out Friday.*

Eve put that on her list.

She had not yet asked Julian about the visit. The uncertainty made her absurdly careful with every small choice, as though arriving with a perfectly engineered plan could prevent him from being unavailable.

At last she called during his lunch break.

“I have work near you on Saturday.”

“How near?”

“Near on a map. Less near according to the railway.”

She heard him shift something, then the soft thud of a door closing. He had found somewhere quieter.

“I'm still free after the morning session,” he said. “Unless they've changed something without telling us. Are you thinking of coming?”

“I'd like to. I can't afford the overnight version.”

“Then don't do the overnight version.”

The simplicity of it stopped her rehearsed explanation.

She told him her last reasonable train home. He checked the route from the training building to the station. They would have a few hours, not a weekend. His morning work might overrun a little. She would still have photographs to back up and would not be able to pretend the client had paid for her romantic detour.

“I can buy my own extra ticket,” she said.

“I know.”

“I was saying it to the tab with the guesthouse.”

“Close the tab.”

She did.

He offered to meet her at the station if he could get away in time. Otherwise he would send her the way to the public entrance of the training building. There was a small seating area where visitors could wait. No one needed to escort her through a treatment area or abandon a class.

“Would you rather do something special?” she asked.

“I would rather see you.”

It was a good answer. She still looked at a page advertising a spectacular riverside lunch before closing that too.

Friday became an exercise in leaving things where other people could find them.

At the venue, Eve and Robin laid out the session materials together. She began explaining the order of the activities, caught herself halfway through a sequence Robin had already hosted, and stopped.

“You know this.”

“I do.”

“The spare cards are—”

“Under the table. In the box that says spare cards.”

“I may have learned too enthusiastically from the biscuit tins.”

Robin smiled and checked the booking list. One visitor had asked about bringing a dog. The reply already explained the session and the available arrangements; Robin knew to refuse an unsuitable last-minute change. Eve did not add three emergency instructions to compensate for being absent.

They agreed when Robin would send the ordinary closing update. Eve would not watch the group chat from a train as though distance entitled her to supervise more closely.

At home, the carer arrived for a brief paid handover. Dumpling investigated the visitor's bag, then retreated beneath the table when the bag failed to provide dinner.

Eve showed the food portions, water bowl, litter supplies, and where she had left the carrier. The emergency contact details and usual veterinary practice were written down, not hidden inside a conversation about how unlikely emergencies were. The spare key was tested in the lock while Eve stood inside.

“It sticks a little at the end,” she warned.

The carer turned it gently, opened the door, and tried once more.

“Like that?”

“Yes.”

They did not stop at instructions. Eve stood back while the carer found a clean dish and prepared the small portion due that evening. Dumpling remained under the table until the dish touched the floor, then emerged with the air of someone making an exception for poor service.

The carer moved aside instead of reaching down to touch him.

“He can come over if he wants,” they said.

Eve liked that more than any enthusiastic assurance that all cats loved them. They checked where the used litter went and asked which windows stayed closed. At the front door, they rehearsed entering slowly and watching for a cat near their feet. Dumpling, uncooperative as a teaching aid, washed his chest several yards away.

Only after the trial did Eve confirm the booking. She would leave the spare key by the agreed handover method, with its return arranged after the evening visit. Nothing depended on a stranger guessing that the unmarked tub contained food or that a cat near an open door would understand a human's good intentions.

When the carer left, Dumpling approached Eve and rubbed against her shin. She stroked him once, grateful that he had no opinion about train supplements.

Before parting they had confirmed two visit windows and what to do if Eve's return train was delayed. A photograph after each visit would be welcome; Dumpling did not have to pose. Eve put aside the agreed payment and felt the day shrink into something she could actually manage.

Later, she packed the camera equipment first. Lenses, batteries, spare card, written brief. Then a sandwich, a water bottle, and a clean scarf. She picked up a second outfit, looked at it, and returned it to the wardrobe.

She was going to work and then to see her boyfriend for an afternoon. She did not need to dress three different imaginary versions of herself.

On Saturday morning, the train was quiet enough for Eve to hear the wrappers of everyone's breakfasts. She ate half her sandwich early and tried to judge whether Julian would notice that she had brought the scarf he liked.

Then she opened the client's brief.

The teaching room was above a shop, reached by a staircase that looked much shorter in the location photographs. Eve carried her equipment up in two trips. Inside, long tables held neatly arranged tools and folded aprons. One organizer stood beside the window, fighting the urge to rearrange everything she had already approved.

“The room feels small now you're here,” she said.

“It felt small before. Now there's a camera to blame.”

The woman laughed, which gave Eve her first usable portrait.

The second organizer arrived with a vase of tall branches. They cast dramatic shadows over the demonstration table and covered half the person standing behind it.

“They looked so good downstairs,” he said.

“They still do,” Eve said. “Perhaps downstairs is their career.”

They moved the vase. She photographed hands arranging materials, the clear route between tables, the hooks where coats would go, and the two organizers standing in a room that looked ready for actual people rather than an advertisement for perfection.

A request for several extra product pictures came near the end. Eve checked the time, explained what their existing booking covered, and offered to quote for a separate shoot. The client chose two quick detail images within the remaining time instead.

Eve was glad she had not made Julian's afternoon the excuse. Her work had a finish even when there was no boyfriend waiting after it.

She packed, confirmed the delivery schedule, and backed up the photographs before leaving. The train connection was still possible. She bought the additional ticket herself and saved the client's original travel receipt separately.

On the platform she received a photograph of Dumpling's shoulder disappearing behind the sofa. Below it, the carer had written that he had eaten, his water was fresh, and he had declined a portrait.

Eve grinned at the screen.

*Entirely normal. Thank you.*

A family nearby was arguing amiably about which bag contained their lunch. Eve ate the other half of her sandwich and discovered that the filling had collected entirely at one end. This was not the sort of travel content anyone was likely to save.

She took no photograph of it.

Julian messaged as her train approached the city. The session was running a little late. He sent the entrance information they had discussed and apologized.

*I'm on my way. Finish what you need to finish,* she replied.

At the next station she stepped onto an unfamiliar platform with one shoulder tired from her equipment bag. There was no grand surprise in her hands and no cat in a carrier at her feet. Her bank balance had survived the decision. Dumpling was cared for. Robin had the afternoon they had agreed to run.

She checked the route and began walking.

For once, the cheapest traveling companion was herself without all the extra plans she had almost brought.
