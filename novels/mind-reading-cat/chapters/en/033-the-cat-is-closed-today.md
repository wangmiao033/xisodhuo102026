# Chapter 33: The Cat Is Closed Today

Dumpling entered his carrier willingly at home.

Eve would remember that later, when she tried to work out exactly where the morning had changed. He had sniffed the familiar blanket, stepped inside, and turned around once before settling. She hadn't chased him or put food beyond his reach or told herself that resistance was part of the process.

Before leaving, she had put a handwritten card in the front pocket of her camera bag. It said when the trial ended, who was paying which cost, and what would happen if there was no footage. She had felt faintly ridiculous writing down a conversation she could remember perfectly well.

Now she was glad she had.

The attendant had wanted to show them a second lamp. Felix had wanted to move the table nearer the window. Eve had wanted, for one bustling minute, to say yes to every improvement because saying yes made everyone look pleased with her.

She had checked the card instead.

They kept the single light and the layout Dumpling's carrier had been brought into. The second lamp went back into the cupboard. Felix moved his phone to silent, and she did the same. Nobody objected. The room became less busy, though the expectation inside Eve remained very much awake.

She had dressed for competent work: clean shirt, practical shoes, hair clipped back. She had imagined leaving with a small, successful piece of footage that would justify the careful preparation. It was surprisingly difficult to stop imagining that just because the morning had started differently.

At the photo room, he did not come out.

That was also information.

The room looked as it had on her inspection: pale walls, a small table, a low backdrop, one lamp angled away from the carrier. Felix had removed the product packaging. The attendant had taken the unused stool into the hall.

There were only two people inside, and nobody spoke loudly.

Eve set the carrier in the quiet corner they had chosen and opened the door. Then she sat on the floor some distance away, not directly in front of it.

Dumpling watched from behind the edge of his blanket.

Felix stood by the table with the tote.

“Should I put it closer?”

“No. Let's leave everything where it is.”

He nodded and set it down.

For a few minutes the most noticeable sound was the soft hum from the ceiling vent. Eve had asked whether it could be switched off, but it served the whole suite, and the attendant said no. It hadn't seemed loud on her first visit. Now she could hear every uneven breath it made.

She kept her own hands still.

Dumpling's ears shifted toward the hall, where somebody passed without stopping. He lowered his head.

Eve checked the time without lifting her phone into his line of sight.

Felix sat on the far chair. He had brought a notebook instead of a camera, as agreed. They weren't secretly filming a reluctant animal in the hope that reluctance could be made entertaining later.

“Do you think he's tired?” he asked quietly.

“I don't know.”

She almost added that he had eaten normally, that he had played with a paper ball, that he had been perfectly fine in the carrier before they left. All true. None of it entitled her to a different cat in this room.

“He can stay there,” she said.

Felix looked at the tote again.

Eve could imagine what he was thinking, which was not the same as knowing. The booking. The fee. The bright little film that had seemed simple before she began turning its parts into questions.

Dumpling looked in his direction too.

For a moment, the old temptation returned: perhaps the cat knew something about Felix. Perhaps this was a verdict, clean and convenient, on the person offering the job.

Then a trolley rattled somewhere beyond the wall, and Dumpling pulled farther back.

A room could be too much without anyone in it being bad.

Eve shifted her legs, which were beginning to ache. She didn't call him. She didn't pat the floor. Her bag contained a familiar toy, but taking it out now felt less like offering comfort than making one more attempt to obtain the result she wanted.

After several minutes, Dumpling leaned forward far enough to sniff the edge of the door. Eve held her breath and immediately wished she hadn't. Her own anticipation had become another thing in the room.

He put one paw on the threshold.

Felix straightened.

The paw withdrew.

Nobody had done anything dramatic. Nobody needed to for the answer to become clear.

“We're stopping,” Eve said.

Felix looked at his watch. “We still have time.”

“Yes.”

“He nearly—”

“I saw. He's staying inside. That's our stopping point.”

The sentence came out steadier than she felt.

Felix glanced between her and the carrier. “All right.”

She moved slowly to close the door, checking that Dumpling was clear of it. He remained curled against the blanket. She draped the light cover over part of the carrier as she usually did for travel, leaving the ventilation unobstructed.

Only then did she stand.

Her knees objected. She had to hold the chair for a moment, which made the entire decision feel less dignified than she had hoped.

Felix folded the unused backdrop.

“I wasn't going to make you keep trying.”

“I know.”

She did know. He had asked for more time; she had said no; he had accepted it. She didn't need to turn him into a villain in order to justify leaving.

“I'm disappointed,” he said.

“So am I.”

He looked surprised by that.

Eve picked up the camera bag she hadn't opened. “I wanted it to work.”

Outside the room, the attendant checked the booking slip. Their unused time didn't change the minimum setup charge. Eve paid the thirty-six they had agreed she would cover and asked for a receipt.

The small paper felt unpleasantly concrete between her fingers.

She had known the amount. She had budgeted for it. It still hurt to pay for a morning that had produced no film.

Felix paid his share and returned the spare key.

“Do you want to keep the tote for a couple more days?” he asked. “If you still want to pitch the other version.”

Eve looked at the sample bag beside his shoes.

A sensible person, she thought, might go home, sleep, and decide later whether she was still a sensible person.

“Until Monday?”

“Monday.”

“Please send that in writing.”

He gave a tired smile. “You really mean that every time.”

“I'm trying to.”

They separated in the hall. Felix had deliveries to receive. Eve had a cat, a carrier, a camera bag, and a borrowed tote she had rather optimistically assumed she could manage together.

At the bottom of the stairs, she set the carrier in a quiet recess while she rearranged the straps. The taxi she had requested was delayed. A delivery van blocked the curb, and the driver wanted to meet her on the next street.

She typed that she needed the safer pickup point they had booked, then waited for a reply.

Julian's message arrived while she was looking at the screen.

*How did the room work out?*

She had told him the trial time when she asked her practical questions. Apparently he had remembered.

*We stopped. He stayed in the carrier. Going home now. Too many bags, no footage.*

She nearly added a joke. Instead she asked whether he happened to be nearby and free to carry one bag to the pickup point. If he wasn't, she would manage after the taxi arrived.

*Just finished. Five minutes. Don't move him into the street to wait.*

She thanked him, then told the driver she would stay where she was.

Julian arrived in an ordinary dark jacket, without the brisk entrance of someone responding to an emergency. Eve was grateful for that before he said anything.

“Which one?” he asked.

She handed him the tote and camera bag.

“Not heavy. Just badly distributed.”

“Like most committee work.”

She laughed once, too sharply, and then found she was close to crying.

Julian didn't lean over the carrier or ask Dumpling to greet him. He stood beside Eve while the driver confirmed a new arrival time.

“I paid to learn he doesn't want to do advertising,” she said.

“You paid for a trial in one room.”

“That's less satisfying to complain about.”

“Sorry.”

She looked down at the receipt still crumpled in her hand.

“Thirty-six.”

He didn't offer to replace it. He didn't say it was only money. He waited while she folded it and put it in her wallet.

“I knew the cost,” she said. “I'm allowed to dislike it anyway.”

“I would.”

The taxi pulled in. Julian held the loose bags while Eve secured the carrier for the journey, then passed them through. He was heading in the other direction and didn't turn helping her into an uninvited visit.

“Let him settle somewhere familiar,” he said. “If you're worried about anything beyond the behavior you described, call the clinic. Don't decide it's only the shoot.”

“I won't.”

At home, she carried Dumpling into his quiet corner, opened the door, and left him alone with the room he knew.

He stayed inside for a while.

Eve put the sample tote in the cupboard and washed her hands. She ate toast over the sink because she couldn't face preparing anything that required deciding whether it looked successful.

Then she opened the expense sheet.

Thirty-six went into the correct column. Transport went beneath it. The larger fee remained in neither income nor expected income, because there was no agreement for a finished project.

She hated the emptiness of that cell.

For a minute she hovered over the draft folder, considering whether to make a little post about the failed morning. A clever caption. A principled explanation. Something to turn the cost into attention.

Behind her, the carrier blanket rustled.

She closed the folder.

Dumpling didn't need to earn back the morning by starring in its aftermath.

By late afternoon he had come out, drunk some water, and settled on the rug. Eve watched without following him around. When he approached her chair, she lowered her hand and let him choose the contact.

He rubbed his cheek against her fingers, then moved on.

That was enough for now.

Felix sent a message confirming the tote's return date. No complaint. No accusation. Below it he wrote that he still needed a campaign concept by the following week and would look at a short proposal if she had one.

Eve didn't reply immediately.

She took the tote out later, when Dumpling was sleeping elsewhere, and emptied her own work bag onto the table. Keys, notebook, loose cable, a crushed packet of tissues, one receipt with a damp corner.

The pocket she needed was never the pocket she reached first.

She stood there for a while, looking at the ordinary mess.

Then she picked up a pencil.
