# Chapter 194 — The Third Day of a Trial Stay

On the third morning, Eve discovered that love could make a noise very much like a spoon falling into a metal sink.

She opened one eye. The room was still gray. Julian's side of the bed was empty, which was expected, and his alarm had been quiet, which was considerate. Somewhere beyond the door, a cupboard shut with the finality of a small verdict.

Dumpling raised his head from the end of the bed.

“No,” Eve whispered. “We are not starting a committee.”

The cat stretched, stepped across her ankle, and went to investigate breakfast.

She remained still, trying to recover the sleep she had lost to an evening of sorting photographs. At home alone, she sometimes worked late and started later. Today was one of those mornings. She had told Julian that. He had said he would try not to wake her.

The kettle began.

Eve pulled the duvet over her ear.

When she finally got up, he was gone. A clean mug waited beside the kettle, and a note said he had left her some bread. His plate was in the sink with an egg yolk drying on it. A pan sat on the cooker, filled with water.

She looked at the pan for longer than it deserved.

There were reasons a person might leave a pan soaking. She had left pans soaking. She had occasionally developed a close working relationship with a soaking pan over several days. Nevertheless, this one felt different because she had not put it there.

Her own two mugs from the previous night stood beside the sofa.

She looked away from them.

At the venue, Robin was changing a booking on the shared schedule when Eve arrived. They glanced up from the screen.

“Morning.”

“Is it?”

“That was the working assumption.”

Eve put down her bag, checked the day's preparation, and caught herself setting a stack of cards down harder than necessary.

Robin waited.

“I didn't sleep very well,” she said.

“Do you want me to ask anything else?”

“No, thank you.”

“Excellent. The printer is making a personal statement.”

It had swallowed one corner of a sheet. Eve dealt with that instead. Paper was an excellent immediate problem: she could see where it had gone wrong, and no one would be hurt if she opened the side panel.

By lunchtime, she had decided that the pan was a tiny thing and she should stop being ridiculous.

By late afternoon, she had thought about it six more times.

Julian messaged to ask what time she expected to finish. She almost wrote something bright and unhelpful. Instead, she gave him the time and asked whether they could talk about mornings after dinner.

His reply came during a gap in her work.

Yes. And evenings, if that's okay.

Eve stared at the word evenings.

Apparently the pan had retained counsel.

She bought vegetables on the way home. Julian arrived shortly afterward, looking tired rather than ominous, and took off his shoes beside the door. His overnight bag was still in the space they had cleared for it. Nobody had quietly expanded into a second drawer.

“Hi,” he said.

“Hi.”

He kissed her, briefly. She was glad he did. Being annoyed had not made her want to become two distant people who happened to possess a pan.

“I was going to wash that when I got back,” he said, seeing her glance toward the cooker.

“Okay.”

“That's not the whole thing, is it?”

“No.”

“Let's eat first.”

They cooked together without the easy efficiency they sometimes had. Eve reached for the chopping board while Julian was using it. He moved it toward her with the knife still on it, then stopped himself.

“Sorry.”

“I can use a plate.”

“I'll be finished in a minute.”

They waited that minute. It felt longer than any minute involved in chopping a courgette had a right to feel.

Dumpling arrived in the doorway and sat down, watching hands. Eve fed him at his usual time, measured his portion, and returned to the human dinner. She refused to ask him to diagnose the atmosphere.

After they ate, Julian picked up the plates.

“Leave them for a second,” she said. “Otherwise I won't say it.”

He sat again.

“The noise woke me this morning. I know you were trying. But once I was awake, I couldn't get back to sleep.”

His expression changed. “I thought I'd been quiet.”

“You were quieter than yesterday.”

“Oh.”

She rubbed a thumb along the table edge. “I don't want to spend the trial pretending I'm delighted by every sound you make.”

“I'd find that quite worrying.”

“And the pan wasn't really about the pan.”

“It was a little about the pan.”

“Yes. I needed the cooker when I got up, and I didn't know whether leaving it was a plan or whether it had become mine.”

He nodded, slowly.

“I should have said I'd do it. Or put it somewhere that wasn't in your way.”

She waited for the but. He looked toward the sofa instead.

“Last night,” he said, “I was trying to sleep while you were working.”

“I wore headphones.”

“The desk lamp was on. The bedroom door was open. And you came in three times looking for things.”

Eve opened her mouth, then closed it.

“I didn't think I was that loud.”

“I know.”

It was an irritatingly useful answer.

She pictured the evening from his end: the bright rectangle beyond the door, her footsteps, the drawer she had opened for a charging cable she normally kept elsewhere. She had thought of him as a considerate addition to her existing night. He had been trying to have a night of his own.

“I needed to finish those images,” she said.

“I wasn't asking you not to work.”

“But this is when I sometimes work.”

“Yes. And this is when I sometimes sleep.”

Neither sentence cancelled the other. Eve wished, briefly, that one did. Then there would have been a clear winner and they could have stopped talking.

Dumpling jumped onto the empty chair beside her. She moved a napkin before he sat on it.

“He's not voting,” Julian said.

“I wasn't going to ask.”

“You were looking hopeful.”

“I always look hopeful when someone else might solve a practical problem.”

He laughed, and she felt the table become a table again instead of a place where a relationship was being marked.

They took their mugs to the kitchen and actually looked at the room. Julian demonstrated his morning route: cupboard, kettle, fridge, cutlery drawer. Eve stood at the bedroom door and listened while he opened the cupboard normally.

“That,” she said.

He tried again, holding the handle until the door met the frame.

“Better?”

“Yes. You don't have to stop drinking tea.”

“I was hoping to retain tea.”

He would put his mug, spoon, and breakfast things out before bed. He could prepare something quieter on the mornings she was sleeping later. If he needed a full cooked breakfast, the bedroom door could be closed and the pan dealt with before he left, or at least moved to an agreed place with an actual return time rather than a mysterious soak.

Eve returned to the sofa and retrieved her two abandoned mugs.

“These are not decorative,” she admitted.

“I wondered.”

She washed them while he washed the pan. The egg came away reluctantly. He did not ask her to admire the effort.

Then they inspected her evening arrangement. She moved the charging cable and a spare notebook into the living room before bed instead of planning to retrieve them by moonlight. The lamp could face away from the bedroom. Closing the door meant checking where Dumpling was and leaving him access to his usual water and litter outside the sleeping room, not shutting him into a place where he would scratch to be released.

The new apartment's separate workroom looked more useful than ever. It still would not make every sound disappear. Eve wrote that beside the measurements, because she did not want to sell them both a fantasy of perfect walls.

“What about dishes in general?” Julian asked.

She resisted saying they could simply both notice. That sounded lovely and had produced a pan with its own emotional history.

They divided the immediate jobs around actual shifts. Whoever finished breakfast at home would clear what they had used before starting work when possible; if a morning went wrong, they would say so. Dinner cleanup would be decided when they decided dinner, rather than awarded silently to whoever became uncomfortable first. They would try it for the remaining days, then change what failed.

They tried the new evening arrangement before congratulating themselves. Julian went into the bedroom and closed the door; Eve turned on the lamp and opened her laptop. From behind the door came his voice.

“The light's better.”

“Can you hear this?”

She tapped the keys in an exaggerated flourish.

“Yes. But I don't think you usually type with your elbows.”

She typed normally. He said that was fine, then came out to find her looking at the narrow strip of floor between the sofa and table.

“What?”

“I thought needing changes would mean we were bad at this.”

“We've only had three days to be bad at it.”

“That isn't reassuring.”

“I mean we can learn.” He sat beside her without closing the laptop. “You didn't tell me to give up mornings. I didn't tell you to give up your work.”

She leaned against his shoulder for a moment.

“And nobody has declared the pan evidence of a fundamental character defect.”

“Please don't. It was a perfectly ordinary egg.”

She laughed, then stood up to move the cable where they had agreed it should go. The conversation needed to become a changed room before they forgot how helpful it had felt.

It sounded almost embarrassingly ordinary.

Eve felt better anyway.

The following morning, she woke once to a faint click and then fell asleep again. When she got up, the pan was clean on the rack and Julian's note said there was bread, followed by a small drawing of a very carefully closed cupboard.

She photographed it and sent him a picture of her own two washed mugs.

An achievement, he replied later.

A major cultural shift, she wrote.

That night she worked late again. Before he went to bed, Julian paused beside the sofa.

“Everything you need?”

“Cable. Notebook. Water. Unreasonable confidence.”

“Good.”

She kissed him and let him go. Dumpling chose the living room chair; Eve left the bedroom door closed and turned the lamp toward her work.

The trial had not become effortless. She still had to remember. So did he.

Near midnight, she reached automatically for a mug that was not there because she had already washed it. The small inconvenience made her smile.

They were learning a home that did not exist yet, using the imperfect one they had. For tonight, there was enough room in it for his sleep and her unfinished picture.
