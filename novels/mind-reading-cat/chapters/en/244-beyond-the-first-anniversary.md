# Chapter 244 — Beyond the First Anniversary

Six weeks after their first wedding anniversary, Eve put on one walking boot and discovered that Julian had given away their Sunday morning.

He was standing in the kitchen, holding a saucepan lid up to the light. The lid did not fit the saucepan. He kept trying it as if somebody might have changed the measurements overnight.

“I told my mother I'd go round,” he said. “The cupboard door has come off.”

Eve held the other boot by its heel. “When?”

“Half past ten.”

“It's ten past.”

“I only need an hour.”

Through the kitchen window she could see the pale side of an enormous cloud. They had intended to catch the quarter-to-eleven bus and walk back along the river before the weather came in. Eve had left a paper bag with two slices of ginger cake beside his keys. The walk had already waited through two Sundays.

She set the boot down.

“What did you tell her about the walk?”

“Nothing. I didn't think it would matter if we went a little later.”

“The next bus is at twelve forty-five.”

Julian finally lowered the lid. She could see him working through the timetable he usually knew by heart.

“We could walk the other way from here.”

“Yes. We could also go the way we meant to go, if you hadn't promised our morning to someone else.”

“I promised mine.”

The reply came quickly. He looked at her as soon as he'd said it, which meant he had heard it too.

Dumpling, who had been licking the inside of his elbow on a dining chair, stopped with his back foot in the air. Nobody had opened a cupboard that interested him. He put the foot down and resumed washing.

Eve pulled off the boot she had managed to put on.

“You did promise yours,” she said. “After you and I had already made plans with it.”

Julian set the lid on the counter with excessive care.

His mother hadn't asked him to bring Eve. She hadn't claimed an emergency. The door was an ordinary wooden door, now leaning beside the cupboard that held her pans. She had asked him because he had fixed one before. Eve knew all this because he told her, filling in details that might have been persuasive if she had objected to the cupboard itself.

“Would you like me to say I can't go?” he asked.

She considered saying yes. For a moment she wanted the exact inconvenience returned to him, including having to explain it to his mother. Then she thought of the two of them jogging for the bus with the argument still sitting between the slices of cake.

“I want us to stop treating plans with each other as the easiest thing to move.”

He leaned against the counter. “You moved last Sunday.”

Eve began to say that last Sunday had been different. The precise reasons lined up in her head: a paid session, a delayed supplier, the keys to the venue. Of those, only the paid session had been truly fixed, and it hadn't been hers to run. She had gone in because she suspected the replacement easels would arrive flat-packed and she was good at assembling them. Robin had sent a picture of six assembled easels before Eve had finished buying her bus ticket.

“I did,” she said.

“And the Sunday before that I got called to the clinic.”

“You went because the new rota had left a gap nobody had noticed. I wasn't cross about that.”

“I was,” he said. “I wanted the walk too.”

It hadn't occurred to Eve that he had been counting the Sundays. She had thought he was pleasantly indifferent, content to go whenever she eventually got around to making it happen. She looked at the ginger cake. When she had put it beside his keys, she had been annoyed that she had to think of the cake as well as the bus.

“You could have told me,” she said.

“I think I wanted to be the person who didn't mind.”

“Did you manage it?”

“No. I managed to look like him.”

The kitchen filled with the sound of a passing van. Eve bent to collect the boot before she stepped on it in her socks. The laces trailed across the floor. Julian moved the saucepan out of the way and came to sit at the table.

Their actual anniversary had been easy. They had agreed on fish and chips in the warmest place they could find beside the river. A gust had carried off two napkins and a corner of the paper. Julian had walked halfway to the next bench to retrieve it while Eve defended both portions from a gull. She had liked that evening so much she hadn't wanted a photograph. But that had been an evening they had set aside weeks in advance, when everyone understood it had a name.

The Sunday walk had no name at all.

“I don't want to schedule an official marriage event every time I'd like to see you in daylight,” she said.

“No.”

“And I want you to see your mother. I don't want to act as if I have to grant you a pass.”

“She can call me as often as she likes. I'm the one who has to be able to say I'll check when I'm free.”

Eve turned one of his keys so the teeth pointed away from the cake bag.

“If it's urgent, call it urgent. If it isn't, can we check before changing a plan that involves both of us? Whoever wants to change it asks. And finds the next possible time.”

“That would have meant me looking up the bus before I said yes.”

“Yes.”

“I thought you might say it meant talking about my mother.”

“I like your mother. The bus is going to be much harder to train.”

That made him laugh. He took his phone out, but didn't make the call until he'd asked what Eve actually wanted to do with the remainder of the morning. She looked outside again. The cloud had grown an untidy gray fringe.

“I still want to go out,” she said. “I don't want to wait around in my boots in case you're back at eleven thirty. I'll go to the river market on my own. We can see about the walk later, if we still want to.”

He nodded, called his mother and admitted he had made two plans. They settled on an afternoon visit. His mother said, loudly enough for Eve to hear across the table, that the saucepan she wanted was already out of the cupboard and she wasn't going to cook lunch in it simply for the sake of a hinge.

When he hung up, Julian began, “So we could still—”

Eve raised an eyebrow.

“Would you like to go together after all?” he asked.

She thought about it. She had been looking forward to a walk with him. She was also looking forward, now, to buying one pastry of her own choosing and reading the back of every battered book she came across without trying to tell if someone was bored.

“I think I'll go by myself this morning.”

“All right.” He slid the bag towards her. “Take both slices. One of them is very small.”

“They're the same size.”

“I made an allowance for what's happened.”

She snorted. She took one slice and left the other wrapped, without deciding that she had to be angry for another hour to make her point.

At the market she found a book about a woman who rebuilt a boat and didn't enjoy owning one. She read four pages standing beside the stall. Then she bought it. Rain started while she was eating her cake under an awning. Across the pavement, a fruit seller pulled his chalkboard out of the wet and propped it on a crate. The top arrow now pointed straight up.

Eve liked being out without anybody. She liked thinking about telling Julian about the arrow when she got home.

The two things went together better when she wasn't giving up either of them.

Julian was putting on his coat when she let herself in. He had taken the old hinge from the cupboard under their own sink to compare it with the one that had broken, then remembered they needed that hinge exactly where it was. She found him putting the screws back. He left with a pencil, a small screwdriver set, and an expression of modest ambition.

He came home after four having fixed the cupboard and eaten something his mother insisted was a very late lunch. He'd made no promise about the remaining hinges. He was proud of that.

“Two of them showed signs of aging,” he said.

“Did she consult you about the other signs?”

“She said I ought to go home before she gave me more dinner.”

They found a Tuesday after work that suited them both, with a short walk from the end of a bus line rather than the whole planned route. Julian put it in the calendar while they were standing there. They went that Tuesday. A week later Eve got as far as composing a message agreeing to take an evening delivery, remembered they had tickets, and offered the supplier the next available slot instead. It cost her a return trip the following morning. Nobody from the venue suggested it proved anything about her commitment.

On a Thursday with no plans at all, Julian came home with a brown paper parcel tucked under his arm. Eve moved a cup off the sofa arm to make room for him.

“What have you bought us?”

“Not us.”

Inside was a linen-covered notebook with a slightly uneven spine and thick cream pages. Eve took it by the spine in case there was a letter inside. There was nothing.

“Is it for the venue?” she asked.

“No.”

“For the walking routes?”

“It doesn't have an assignment.”

She looked at him.

“I saw you pick it up two weeks ago,” he said. “You put it down after checking if it opened flat. I liked it too, but I thought I'd leave it in the shop if you didn't actually want it.”

“I was working out what I'd do with it.”

“I suspected that.”

She set it on her knees and opened it. It lay across them perfectly flat. She could picture bookings on the right side, expenses on the left. She could imagine an unusually tasteful photograph of the first page from above, with something green in the corner. She felt the urge to look for the address of the maker so that the picture could earn the cost of the notebook.

Across the room, Dumpling had fallen asleep upside down on the low footstool. His hind legs were spread with no discernible regard for proportion. His right lip had caught on a tooth. One ear was folded underneath him, giving the alarming impression that he had misplaced it.

Eve went to get a pencil.

The first time she drew the leg that stuck straight into the air, it looked far too much like a leg. She tried again. It came out as a small, furry exclamation mark, which was considerably closer. The belly occupied most of the page. She emphasized the tooth.

Julian leaned over for a look.

“Does he know about this?”

“I won't be issuing proofs.”

Dumpling shifted his enormous hindquarters an inch to the left, spoiling the composition and improving the pose.

Eve laughed, drew the second fold beneath his chin, and closed the notebook without signing or dating the picture. She put it on the shelf below the book about the boat. Then she went away to make tea and left the pencil exactly where it was, so she could come back and do something else with it that nobody needed.
