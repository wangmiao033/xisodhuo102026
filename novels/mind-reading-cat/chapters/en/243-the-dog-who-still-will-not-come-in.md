# Chapter 243 — The Dog Who Still Will Not Come In

Mabel came round on a Thursday with a short purple zipper in her coat pocket and Biscuit on the end of his usual lead.

Eve saw them from the front window. At the pace they were travelling, she had time to put the kettle on before they reached the doorway. She met them there with the brown chair ready in the recess.

“Good,” Mabel said. “You haven't painted it.”

“I could have. I own several colors.”

“Brown doesn't come off on my coat.”

Biscuit stopped at the boundary between pavement and doorway. Some months earlier Eve had offered him a clean room and the encouragement of everybody in it. Today, the room was warm, there was nothing on the floor to make the route awkward, and he was free to see as much of it as he liked.

He looked past her legs for half a second and turned toward his chair.

“Very consistent,” Eve told him.

He investigated the foot of the chair. She fetched a bowl of fresh water while Mabel sat down and arranged the lead away from their feet. He drank, walked a little circle on the dry tiles and lowered himself beside her shoe.

The porch was narrow enough that Eve could hand Mabel a cup from the doorway. It was also far enough from the neighboring passage that a person could use the chair without being in anybody's way. Mabel had drawn the first version of that arrangement in Eve's notebook. There was still a hard pencil mark on the page where she had pointed out that the person sitting down would want somewhere to put her cup.

The cup went on the little shelf beside the chair now, not on a table with open legs to catch a bag. Mabel didn't comment on the shelf. She used it.

Eve drew up the second chair briefly, clear of the passage. Robin was fitting the last tray of supplies for an indoor session and had agreed to call her when it was time to begin.

“What have you brought?” Eve asked.

“A perfectly good zipper. The blue pouch you sent over last week has lost its pull.”

“I was going to ask somebody to look at it.”

“You did. You sent me a photograph and asked how much I'd charge.”

“I meant I was going to ask somebody to look at it this week. The week has approached far more quickly than I thought.”

Mabel slipped the zipper back into her pocket. She would take the pouch home to repair, as agreed, and Eve would pay the small quote for the work when it was ready. They weren't starting a sewing job beside the dog. Eve had already packed the blue pouch into a bag for her, along with the spare fabric from the inside pocket in case it was useful.

“What goes in it?” Mabel asked.

“Practice information. Somebody can find an emergency contact, a key instruction, that sort of thing. No real details. The idea was to put the cards where they'd actually be found.”

“Behind a broken zipper.”

“That's the advanced course.”

Mabel snorted and set her cup down. Biscuit's ear shifted at the sound. His chin remained on the toe of her shoe.

There were two other practice pouches inside, one with a button and one with a single wide flap. Although they'd planned to try all three types, Robin's class that morning would make do with the two that worked. The group had booked indoors with no animals coming to the activity. Mabel had arranged a separate quick stop on her walk to collect the repair, not a lesson she now needed to complete.

Eve had about another five minutes before she was due inside to lead the exercise on writing a single clear line of directions. She sat with her hands around her cup, which meant she didn't have to find anything to do with them.

A delivery trolley went by in the neighboring passage, its front wheels hitting the seam between one surface and the next. Biscuit lifted his head long enough to see it go.

“I tried the corner shop with him the other week,” Mabel said. “He doesn't go into that either.”

“Did he used to?”

“He used to stop by the newspaper rack and eat things he shouldn't. I have no plans to revive it.”

Eve laughed. They talked about the shop instead. It had moved the rack to the other side of the door. Mabel liked the new arrangement because there was room to stand with a shopping basket without shutting the door on the end of her scarf. She had told the shopkeeper so, and he'd been surprised to find that someone noticed.

Inside the venue, a chair scraped. Biscuit put his head down again.

A woman arriving for the session stepped toward them, then stopped when she saw the dog. She held her bag against her ribs.

“Do I have to go past him?” she asked.

“No,” Eve said. She stood and indicated the open line straight through the doorway. “Biscuit is staying over here. There's room to use the entrance.”

The woman looked, took the route to the door, then paused to ask whether there would be animals in the room.

“Not for this session.”

“Thank you. I meant to ask before I booked.”

She went in. Eve took a closer look from where the woman had been standing. The A-frame sign was now inside the entrance in the proper position, but from the path the two chairs immediately caught the eye. The clear route only became obvious when somebody was close enough to feel she was already approaching the dog.

Mabel had noticed her looking.

“Can't see the door from the corner yet, can she?”

“You can see it. It doesn't look like the part you're supposed to go to.”

“Put the arrow before the chairs.”

Eve considered where an arrow could go without interfering with the neighboring entrance or leaving an object in the walkway. There was a sign holder on the inside face of the window. It pointed toward the booking table on a busy day. Turned out toward the approach, it could make the route clear. She went inside, checked it could be read without needing to move the furniture, and replaced its old directions with a short paper insert.

Inside for the booked group. Porch seating to the side.

From the corner she could read both lines. From Mabel's chair she couldn't see the words at all, which Mabel said was exactly the right amount of advertisement.

There were only a few minutes before the booked start. Eve was about to carry her cup in when an early burst of rain hit the street. It angled under the front roof, darkening a strip of tile near the edge. Mabel drew one boot back automatically. Biscuit moved his paws, then settled on the sheltered side of the chair.

“Want me to get the bag now?” Eve asked.

“In a moment. I'd prefer not to get my socks wet between here and the next awning.”

Eve went in and told Robin the group could start without her, if they were still happy to handle the introduction. They had already agreed either of them could run that part and the material was ready. Robin checked that she wouldn't be long, then carried the practice samples to the table.

“Leave me the button one,” Eve said. “I may want to time myself with it later.”

“Nobody is paying for a demonstration of you getting impatient with a button.”

“It's a specialized service.”

She brought her cup back from the doorway and sat with Mabel for a few minutes more. The rain rattled on a loose wrapper in the gutter. Somewhere beyond it a bus slowed, then moved away. Inside, Robin got the group talking about where they'd put one piece of information another person might need.

Mabel raised her cup to see if there was anything left.

“Do you know,” she said, “I can hear your table from here, and nobody's asking me to make a folder.”

“Would you like to make one?”

“I'd rather make a zipper work. Then I'd like to finish my walk.”

Eve felt suddenly grateful that she hadn't invited Mabel to teach. She'd had the thought while preparing the activity. Mabel's real life contained old tins and mending, small work she'd been doing well since before Eve knew her and no particular desire to run it past an audience. The porch was giving them somewhere to share a morning without Eve asking her to turn any part of it into a service.

Mabel scraped a thread off the knee of her trousers and caught it before Biscuit could inspect it. Eve put it away for disposal. Neither of them had anything to add for a while. In the room behind them, three people started laughing at once.

The nearest bit of pavement began to lighten. Mabel tested it from under the cover with one foot. Then she asked for the bag, took it by both handles and rested it on her knees to see that Eve really had remembered the fabric. She had. Mabel pulled out the blue pouch and worked its opening with her thumb before putting it away.

“It may only need a pull,” she said. “I'll see once I've looked at it under a light. Don't pay for a whole new zipper if you've still got one.”

“All right. Tell me what it needs when you know.”

Mabel stood and waited without tightening the lead. After a moment Biscuit got up too. He gave the doorway one last sniff at a slight distance before looking out in the direction they were going. Mabel said goodbye, tucked the bag away from his side and took the level stretch toward the main road. Biscuit went with her. Eve watched him stop at a puddle, make the small calculation it required, and go around it.

She dried the seat for the next visitor and put the water bowl away after cleaning it. When she got back inside, Robin was listening to a participant describe a place where instructions routinely disappeared. It turned out six people in that person's household used the same drawer, none of them to keep instructions. They tested a card on the front of a practice pouch instead.

The woman who had asked about the entrance was trying the wide flap with one hand. She looked up when Eve sat down.

“Is the dog gone?”

“Yes. He went past the window just now.”

“I do like them,” the woman said. “I just don't like finding out I'm sharing a doorway with one while I'm in it.”

“That's useful to know,” Eve said, and meant it. “The route should be obvious before you get that close.”

They tried the rest of the activity. The button took Eve too long, though not long enough for Robin to dispute the official result. At the end they sent the participants home with a plain sheet for a single household instruction and a discussion about where it could reasonably go. Nothing depended on opening the practice pouch nobody had repaired yet.

Before closing, Eve sat for a minute in the brown chair. From there she could see the arrow's white paper from behind and the dry edge of the small, unlovely shelf. She could see people passing without having to move her feet under the chair. Someone who wanted to sit and talk could do it right there, without having to take up a place at the table.

Biscuit had used none of the new trays and attended none of the class. He had lain down in the place he liked, listened to the same rain she did, and gone away at the end of his visit. Nobody needed to offer him a second attempt.

Eve finished her tea outside. It was cold, but she wasn't ready to go in yet either.
