# Chapter 234 — Leave the Back of the Photograph Blank

The printer made Nora's photograph look warmer than it had on the screen.

The old towel became almost golden. The shadow under the chair softened. Dumpling, who had probably been thinking about nothing Eve could identify, acquired the expression of a small survivor awaiting discovery.

Eve held the print at arm's length.

“No,” she said.

Julian looked up from his book.

“The printer?”

“The story it's trying to tell.”

She checked the settings and found an automatic adjustment. With it turned off, the next copy was less flattering and closer to the original. The towel looked like a towel again. The shadow remained a place the photograph had not illuminated.

“That one,” she said.

Julian nodded when she showed him both. She put the inaccurate print aside to dispose of privately rather than keeping it where the softened version might later become the one she remembered.

It was Wednesday evening. Yesterday's visit had not supplied another urgent task. The agreed copies were saved with their sources in the private history folder, and Nora had taken her originals home. Eve had finished her paid work before opening the printer tray.

Tonight she wanted one picture for the family album.

That was all she had intended. Nevertheless, while the printer worked, her mind had begun supplying a sequence: the indistinct cat beyond the cups, the blue door, the sleeping figure beside Nora's basket, and the box in their sitting room now.

She could make the transitions beautifully.

That did not mean she should.

The first caption formed itself almost without permission. From room to room, he was waiting for—

Eve stopped there.

He had not been waiting for her by name. She had no evidence that every quiet moment had been filled with loneliness, and no right to turn another person's temporary care into an inadequate prologue to her own arrival.

She closed the empty editing window on her laptop before importing a single picture.

“What happened?” Julian asked.

“I nearly gave him a narrative structure.”

“Would he have cooperated?”

“He'd have had no opportunity to object. That's the problem.”

Julian closed his book around the bookmark she had made him. The cardboard ears rose above the pages, absurd and reassuring.

“Do you want to talk about it?”

“A little.”

She sat beside him with the accurate print.

“I know how to make people feel something,” she said. “Not always. But I can see where the cut would go. Which detail makes the next one hurt.”

“That can be useful work.”

“Yes. It doesn't make every part of our life available for it.”

They looked at the photograph. It was not even a particularly compelling image without the words she had imagined putting around it. A cat lay near a basket. A cloth occupied the edge. The picture showed a quiet moment during a short stay.

That modest purpose deserved to survive her knowledge of what happened later.

“The searches matter too,” she said. “People were looking. I didn't know that.”

“Do you want that in the album?”

“I don't think I want the whole record in it.”

She had the dates and the messages elsewhere. She did not need to turn a book of pictures they might browse on a quiet evening into a case file. Nor did she want a smooth story that pretended the gaps had vanished.

On the table lay their private photograph from Saturday, Dumpling asleep in the paper after declining to become part of the arranged portraits. Eve put it beside the old image. The two sleeping shapes resembled each other. Everything surrounding them differed.

She could love the second without making the first merely a picture of what he had escaped.

She first placed the old print at the front, ahead of the picture from her own rescue period. Then she paused. Putting it first made it look like an origin, when it was merely the earliest clear moment this particular visitor could show her. There might have been other homes, other people, or a life none of their papers could reach.

She moved it to a separate page near the back, beside a small divider for pictures received later. The order of their discovery could remain honest even where the cat's complete chronology could not.

Julian helped hold the sleeve open when its edge stuck. Their fingers met over the stubborn plastic, and Eve laughed at how much practical work could be caused by a decision to leave something simple.

The album had several unused pages. Eve slid the older photograph into a sleeve and wrote a small factual note on a separate card: provided by Nora, during a temporary stay before the rescue. The source and supported period remained in the records. She did not write a feeling she could not possibly know.

On the back of the photograph itself, she wrote nothing.

The blank reverse looked unfinished when she turned it over. She left it that way.

Then she put the newer picture on another page, with the date she actually knew. Between them was room for what she did not.

Julian watched her close the album.

“Better?”

“Less impressive.”

“That's not what I asked.”

She smiled. “Better.”

The following afternoon she met Sophie at a small cafe they had chosen because it was convenient for both, not because it had a background suitable for announcements. Sophie arrived carrying a folded sheet of paper on which somebody had drawn a delivery route in three incompatible colors.

“No,” Eve said immediately.

“I haven't asked you anything.”

“I'm refusing the urge in myself.”

Sophie laughed and put the paper away.

They ordered lunch. For the first part of it, Eve listened to her friend describe a staffing problem on a current production. Sophie had declined a proposed change that would have made her responsible for another team's missing preparation. The decision had not been greeted with applause.

“I was polite,” Sophie said.

“Very polite?”

“An irritating amount. It left them no useful quotation.”

Eve smiled, then asked what the next practical step was. Sophie explained the revised arrangement she could actually cover. She did not need Eve to devise a better version, and Eve managed to hear the whole answer without reaching for a pen.

Only when they had finished eating did Sophie ask about Nora's visit. Eve had mentioned that someone from the cat's earlier care was coming, without turning the appointment into an event her friend needed to attend.

“Do you want the short version or the complicated one?” Eve asked.

“Whichever you want to tell. I've got half an hour.”

“The true version will fit.”

She described the matching photographs, the temporary stays, and the limited messages showing confusion during a handover and a search afterwards. She did not share unrelated names, addresses, or the private exchanges themselves. The documents belonged in the record, not spread across a cafe table.

Sophie listened without suggesting that the cat must finally understand they would never abandon him.

“At least somebody remembered him,” she said.

“Yes. And I nearly disliked her for it.”

Eve told her about Dumpling leaning into Nora's hand. The admission was easier here than it had been while the woman sat opposite her, but it still made Eve look at the crumbs on her plate.

“I kept thinking he'd recognize a better home.”

“Did she ask him to choose?”

“No.”

“Did you?”

“No.”

“Good. That would have been a difficult afternoon for a cat.”

Eve laughed, relieved by the absence of solemn judgment.

“He went back to his box after eating. I was absurdly grateful.”

“You can be grateful. You don't have to award yourself a trophy for it.”

“I considered a small one.”

“Keep it off the accounts.”

They laughed together. Outside the window, somebody wrestled with a folding umbrella that wanted to remain half open. Neither woman offered a theory about the stranger's life.

Eve showed Sophie the new picture from Saturday, the one she and Julian had kept privately. Her friend leaned closer, smiling at the undignified sleeping posture.

“That's an excellent decision by the model.”

“He chose the paper over the portrait.”

“Good materials department.”

She handed the phone back without asking for a copy to post.

Eve was grateful for the ease of that too. Her private life did not need a dramatic refusal every time she shared one small part with someone who cared about her.

“I nearly made a video out of the old photographs,” she said.

“Nearly?”

“I opened the editor. Then closed it. I could hear the caption becoming a story I couldn't prove.”

Sophie nodded. “You don't owe everybody an update because you once told them you found a cat.”

“I know. I think part of me wanted them to tell me I had done enough.”

“Have you asked the cat?”

Eve looked at her.

“I'm joking,” Sophie said. “He'd ask for the good cushion.”

“The feedback is remarkably consistent.”

They spent the remaining time discussing a film they might see when their schedules matched. Sophie had to leave for her next appointment. Eve did not keep her at the table until the subject of the cat felt neatly resolved.

On the walk home she stopped at the venue to collect a folder needed for tomorrow's client work. Robin was finishing the agreed preparation for a small session and asked whether Eve had enjoyed lunch.

“Yes,” she said. “We actually managed to eat most of it while it was hot.”

They discussed the sample quantities and the locked supply cupboard. Eve did not retell Nora's visit to make the ordinary exchange more meaningful. Robin had work to finish. The cat's missing years were not a subject the whole team needed to carry.

At home, Dumpling sat on the chair she had used for printing. Eve moved the remaining blank paper into the drawer and checked that no small offcuts were on the floor. He watched the operation, then abandoned the chair now that it had lost whatever had interested him.

She opened the album once more.

There was a temptation to fill the blank space with a line about belonging. She had plenty of lines. Some were even good.

Instead, she placed the separate source note securely beside the old photograph and left the space beyond it empty. If a verifiable detail arrived later, she could add it to the records. If nothing did, the blank would not become a debt somebody owed her.

Julian came home while she was putting the album on the shelf.

“How was Sophie?” he asked.

“Busy. Cross about something entirely justified. Interested in a film with terrible reviews.”

“That sounds promising.”

“It does.”

They made dinner. Dumpling investigated a closed cupboard, found it still closed, and went to his bed. Eve did not photograph the disappointment.

The album stayed on the shelf, holding what they knew and leaving room for what they did not.
