# Chapter 082 — Why Is the Camera Still On?

The chair squeaked whenever Julian leaned toward the table.

On Wednesday afternoon, Eve moved it six inches, checked the microphone, and discovered that the chair considered the new position an invitation to squeak more clearly.

“It wants a speaking role,” she said.

“It should have submitted a question.”

They were filming a short, paid addition to the clinic's existing preparation information. The room was closed to visitors during the booked slot. The papers on the table were demonstration sheets, not patient records. Dumpling was at home, unneeded by a film about writing down questions before an appointment.

Eve exchanged the chair for another one from the corner. Julian sat carefully.

Silence.

“An excellent audition,” she said.

Tessa came to the doorway with the wording Dr. Clark had approved. She checked that the demonstration form was the current version and pointed out an old copy somebody had left under a folder.

“Please don't make that one famous,” she said. “We finally stopped people printing it.”

Eve set the old version aside for disposal and held up the correct page to check the frame. Tessa had other work. They did not ask her to stay simply because having a receptionist in the background would make the film look more authentic.

The first take went well until Julian said the same sentence twice.

He stopped, closed his eyes briefly, and started to apologize.

“That's why there is editing,” Eve said. “Take a moment.”

“I know what I want to say.”

“I know. You already said it successfully once.”

His mouth shifted at the corner. She stopped recording, marked the usable section, and gave him the next prompt only when he asked for it.

On the third take, a delivery trolley rattled along the corridor. Eve lowered her hand before he could speak over it. They waited through the rattle, a polite conversation about where the boxes belonged, and the return journey of the trolley apparently carrying every loose wheel in the building.

Julian looked toward the ceiling.

“Would you like to include that in the question?”

“Only if your answer concerns suspension.”

He laughed, then rubbed his forehead.

Eve noticed how slowly his hand came down.

They had booked forty minutes. They had usable answers for the two essential prompts. The optional closing line remained, plus a close shot of the blank form.

“We can leave the last line,” she said.

“I can do it.”

“I didn't say you couldn't.”

He looked at her properly then, and nodded. “Yes. Leave it.”

She turned the camera toward the table for the form insert. Julian stayed beside the wall, out of the intended shot, while she adjusted the angle. She pressed record, moved the page into place, and held it still for several seconds.

The paper curled at one corner. She reached for a small weight from her bag.

Behind her, a cup knocked against the edge of the counter.

She turned. Julian had put it down too close to a stack of leaflets. Nothing spilled, but he stood looking at it with the concentrated annoyance of somebody whose body had made a very minor mistake on a day with no room for another one.

“Do you want to sit down?” she asked.

“I think so.”

She moved the squeaking chair toward him, then remembered and moved the quiet one instead. He gave a tired smile and sat.

Eve fetched the water he had left on the far end of the counter. She asked whether he wanted it before handing it over. Then she took the paper cup out of the small space beside his elbow so neither of them would knock it again.

“Thank you,” he said.

“You don't have to finish the insert. It's a piece of paper.”

“I wasn't going to argue with the paper.”

“You looked ready.”

He laughed softly, looking up at her. Eve rested her hand on the back of his chair for a moment, closer than she would have stood when they first began working together.

It was a small, warm pause. She liked that he had admitted wanting to sit rather than pretending the cup had offended him on principle.

Then she saw the red recording mark.

The camera had stayed on.

Her stomach tightened. She crossed to it and stopped the recording immediately.

“What is it?” Julian asked.

“The insert was still running. I forgot to stop it when I turned around.”

He looked at the camera, then toward the chair.

“Did it get that?”

“I don't know how much. I'll check with you.”

She did not say probably not. The frame had been wider than the sheet of paper. Guessing would only make the next part worse.

They played the file on the camera screen. The blank form filled the foreground. Beyond it, Julian sat in the corner of the image, accepting the water. Eve's shoulder obscured part of his face, but his smile and the brief touch against the chair were visible. Their voices were clear.

It looked tender.

That was Eve's first involuntary thought, and she was ashamed of how quickly her trained eye supplied a closer crop.

Julian watched without speaking. When the file ended, he said, “I don't want that used.”

“It won't be.”

“I wasn't doing a scene about being looked after.”

“I know.”

She put the camera down between them, screen still visible.

“I should have stopped it. I'm sorry.”

He nodded, but did not hurry to reassure her that forgetting was harmless. She was grateful for that and uncomfortable at the same time.

“The form shot is in the same file,” she said. “I can record it again. I haven't copied this anywhere.”

“All right.”

She deleted the file from the camera and showed him the remaining thumbnails. The earlier planned takes remained; the accidental insert no longer appeared. She checked the slot indication too. This camera was recording to the one card she had inserted, not quietly making a second copy in another slot.

Then she turned it fully off.

For a moment the room felt bare without the practical business of filming.

“I saw how it could look,” Eve admitted. “For a second. That doesn't mean I was going to publish it.”

“I believe you.”

“But you shouldn't have to wait while I decide whether a nice-looking picture is more important than what you said.”

“No,” he said quietly. “I shouldn't.”

She accepted the answer. There was no useful defense concealed inside her lack of bad intention.

Julian took another drink of water. The quiet chair made a small click as he shifted, as if it had reconsidered its professional standards.

“I am tired,” he said. “That's most of the story.”

“Then we can stop making the story.”

He looked at her, and his expression softened a little.

“Thank you.”

Eve asked whether he needed to return to a particular task or could take the rest of the booked time as a break. He said he would check with Tessa about the next appointment; he did not need Eve to reorganize the clinic for him.

She packed the microphone while he stepped out. When he returned, he said he had ten minutes and would use them in the staff room without the camera.

“Good,” she said.

He paused at the door. “Wednesday hasn't become a disaster.”

“I wasn't going to call it one.”

“You looked as if you were preparing an extensive report against yourself.”

She let out a breath. “A short report.”

“Keep the useful part.”

He left. Eve stood in the quiet room, then put the correct blank form back on the table. The shot still needed doing. Respecting his answer had not finished the job for her.

She framed tightly, recorded the page without any person in the background, stopped the camera, and said the word stopped aloud. It felt ridiculous. It also prevented her from treating a tiny red symbol as something she could notice later.

Tessa returned near the end of the booking. Eve explained there had been an accidental personal recording, that it had never been copied or shared and had been deleted at Julian's request. No visitors or records were involved. The necessary prepared material was complete.

Tessa asked whether they needed another slot.

“No. I have the essential takes and the replacement insert. I'll leave out the optional closing sentence.”

“Fine. Send the cut through the usual review.”

Eve did not recruit her to say Julian had overreacted. Tessa checked the room for the demonstration sheets, and together they restored the furniture.

At home, the memory of the little smile followed Eve into the edit.

Dumpling inspected the closed equipment case, decided it contained nothing immediately edible, and walked away. She left it secured rather than opening it on the floor while distracted.

The remaining files copied normally. She compared the file list with the camera thumbnails and checked that only the planned takes and the newly recorded form insert were present. The accidental recording was not among them. There was no secret private version she could later be tempted to rescue.

For the first few minutes, every ordinary cut looked dull beside the scene she no longer had.

Then Eve listened to the actual answer. Julian explained that writing down a question could help somebody remember to ask it, without promising that a prepared list would remove every uncertainty. She shortened the pause before his second example, kept his complete meaning, and placed the form insert where it clarified the sequence.

The film worked.

It worked because somebody had said something useful, not because the audience got to watch him being tired and loved into recovery.

Before sending it, she changed the little card she used beside the tripod. The old version reminded her to check sound, framing, and spare battery. She added a plain instruction to stop recording before leaving the camera, then tried the sequence with the camera switched off: finish the take, touch the stop control, confirm, move away. It was not an apology Julian needed to applaud. It was something her hands could learn.

She also moved the card where she would see it without turning away from the screen. The earlier position had looked tidy in a photograph and been nearly useless while she worked. This time she checked from the place she actually stood.

She sent the review copy through the agreed channel and put the equipment away.

Later, Julian messaged to say he was home and had eaten. No request for a grand reconciliation appeared beneath it.

Eve wrote that the cut was ready for clinic review, then added that she hoped his evening contained nothing requiring a professional expression.

*The washing machine is being difficult,* he replied.

*You don't have to charm it.*

*I wasn't planning to.*

She smiled and put the phone beside her book.

She still remembered the moment by the chair. Deleting a recording had not deleted the fact that she had been there, or that he had accepted the water.

For once, the part she couldn't show anybody was allowed to remain enough for tonight.
