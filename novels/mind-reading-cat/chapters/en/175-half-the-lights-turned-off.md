# Chapter 175 — Half the Lights Turned Off

With half the lights turned off, the unfinished corner finally looked intentional.

Eve stood beside the switch and considered this discovery with the seriousness it deserved. In daylight, the corner contained two plain chairs, a small surface for setting things down, and the empty pot Julian had given her. At this hour it appeared to be a deliberate exercise in restraint rather than the part of the room whose budget had run out.

She would have liked to remember that trick before paying for anything else.

Robin had finished their clearing time and gone home. The day's record was saved, the remaining stock counted, and the public door locked. Eve had checked Dumpling, who was awake now and grooming one shoulder with the concentrated resentment he brought to personal maintenance.

She had also packed the little tray of opening treats left from the guests' individually wrapped refreshments. No food service had materialized around them. The unopened cakes and crackers were simply extras she had bought for the occasion, fewer than her first enthusiastic estimate and still more than people had eaten.

Julian arrived while she was deciding whether to carry them home or leave the shelf-stable packets for tomorrow.

She let him in and locked the door behind him. He looked around the quiet room, then at her face.

“Have you stopped?”

“Most of me.”

“Which part is still working?”

“The part that thinks these crackers could become a useful spreadsheet.”

He took off his coat. “Can it have a break while we eat them?”

Eve led him to the unfinished corner. He sat in the brownish indoor chair she had kept after rejecting the long sofa, and she took the one beside it. Between them, the wrapped treats made a small, uninspiring feast. Eve put drinking water on the table and felt the effort in her shoulders as she lowered herself down.

Julian waited until she had opened a packet before asking how it had gone.

She gave him the version she had prepared for everyone who cared: the first session worked, the later demonstration went well, a few people misunderstood the offer, the plain labels sold out, and the figures needed more than a day before they could mean much.

He listened without interrupting. When she finished, he looked at the cracker in his hand.

“And how did it feel?”

Eve had no tidy version of that.

“Loud. Even when nobody was speaking.”

He nodded.

“I kept looking for the next thing. Someone wanting the toilet, or a price, or the cat, or a different answer from Robin. I knew we'd planned for most of it. My head still treated every person standing up as an incident.”

“Did every person become one?”

“No. One was just looking for a scarf already in her own bag.”

“An excellent outcome.”

“She announced it to the room. I nearly applauded.”

Julian smiled. Eve leaned back and let the chair hold more of her weight. There was something almost indecent about sitting in the room without trying to make it look welcoming to someone who had paid to be there.

He asked about Mabel. Eve described Biscuit declining to come inside and Mabel declaring the visit successful anyway. She told him Alex had moved a cluster of people with a complaint about vegetables, and Robin had stopped a scrape from becoming an entire afternoon's background noise.

“The boring parts worked,” she said.

“They were the parts you worked on.”

“I worked on the pretty cards too.”

“Did the pretty cards misbehave?”

“They remained aggressively available.”

He laughed. Eve found that she could laugh with him now without feeling she was undermining the dignity of her own enterprise.

She reached for another packet, then stopped. “Are these your dinner?”

“No. I ate before I came.”

“Actual dinner?”

“With a plate.”

She gave him a look. He returned it calmly, having earned the quotation through long practice.

“I ate earlier,” she admitted. “I may need something proper again when I get home.”

“Then let's not make these responsible for feeding you.”

He set the remaining packets where she could reach them and took a drink. No lecture followed. Eve had spent so much of the day explaining what things were and weren't that a conversation without an explanation felt like stepping out of tight shoes.

For a while they listened to the ordinary sounds beyond the window. A bus stopped somewhere nearby. A door closed upstairs. Someone walked past laughing into a phone. The neighborhood had continued its own day around the one Eve had made so important.

She looked toward the sign, visible in reverse through the glass.

“I love it,” she said.

Julian turned his head toward her.

“This place. The actual one. Not the picture I wanted before we moved the furniture. I like that somebody can sit outside. I like hearing people laugh about their terrible cupboards. I even like that the sign is wrong.”

“Repaired,” he said.

“Still wrong underneath.”

“Most repaired things are.”

She smiled, then felt the smile fade.

“I don't know if I can keep it.”

The words landed between them with less drama than she had expected. She had imagined crying if she said them. Instead she sat very still, holding an unopened packet that had become warm in her palm.

“I know the trial has an end date,” she continued. “I know I have the client work, and the numbers aren't terrible just because they aren't wonderful. But I love having a key. I love that people found something useful here today. And I keep thinking that if it doesn't work, I'll have to tell all of them I couldn't do it.”

Julian put his cracker down.

“Would that be the whole truth?”

“It would feel like it.”

“I know.”

She looked at him. He wasn't reaching for the notebook, asking for the figures, or preparing an optimistic calculation he could perform on her behalf. For a second that made her feel exposed. There was nothing practical to hide behind.

“I don't need you to say it'll succeed,” she said.

“Good. I can't know that.”

“I might still want you to.”

“I can want it to succeed.”

She let out a breath. “That helps more than it should.”

He moved his hand onto the edge of her chair, palm open. She put the packet down and took it.

“I can listen,” he said. “I can do the particular things we agree I can do. I can't become the answer to every gap without losing the rest of my life. I don't think either of us wants that.”

“No.”

It was not the first time they had said some version of this. Tonight she heard it differently because the room was no longer an imagined future. It had been open. People had come. The bills and the pleasant moments now belonged to the same real thing.

“What do you want?” she asked him.

Julian looked at their joined hands, then back at her.

“One evening a week that isn't this.”

She almost withdrew her hand.

He felt the movement and didn't tighten his grip.

“I don't mean an evening where you sit here looking guilty about your phone,” he said. “Or one where I come to help close and we call it a date because we're tired together. I want time with you when we aren't recovering someone else's schedule.”

Eve looked around the room. The corner had begun to feel less like a refuge and more like evidence.

“We've had dates.”

“We have. I like them. I'd like us to keep having them.”

The answer was so unaccusing that her defensive reply had nowhere to stand. She thought of the bookshop, the ridiculous picnic dessert, and the story collection she had barely opened since buying it. She had liked that evening too. She had been treating another one as something the venue would eventually earn for her.

“What if a good booking comes in?” she asked.

“Then you decide whether it really needs that evening. If we change our plan, we change it together and choose another. I don't want a rule neither of us can keep. I also don't want every week to become the exception.”

She nodded slowly.

“And your shifts?”

“I need to look at them before I suggest a day. My time isn't infinitely flexible either.”

“That sounded suspiciously reasonable.”

“I've been rehearsing.”

They took out their calendars only long enough to find the next evening they could both protect. Eve chose one that did not already contain a guest commitment. She resisted adding a small task before dinner or proposing they meet here because it would be convenient.

“Somewhere else,” she said.

“Strong opening.”

“And no discussing how to make this place better over the first course.”

“What if you ask?”

“Remind me what I asked for now.”

He smiled. They put the phones away. The plan was modest: dinner and a walk if the weather allowed, an ordinary evening that didn't require tickets, a camera, or a cat's approval. They would choose later dates when their schedules were clear enough, rather than pretending one calendar entry had solved the next three months.

Eve rested her head against his shoulder.

“I thought asking for something like that would feel like another demand,” she said.

“Does it?”

“A little. Also like you want to see me.”

“I do.”

She closed her eyes for a moment. His sleeve smelled of the outside air and the soap he used, not of a promise she had to turn into a business plan.

A soft noise came from the nonpublic room. Dumpling was awake and moving about. Eve sat up, checked the time, and went to see him. Julian stayed where he was rather than following as an automatic second carer.

The cat came toward her when she opened the door, rubbed against her leg, and inspected the carrier she had set ready for the journey home. She gave him time, gathered his things, and secured him comfortably for the short trip. No guests were left to misunderstand his appearance now. He was simply going home after an afternoon of being allowed to rest.

Julian carried the bag of unopened treats when she asked. Eve carried Dumpling, then set the carrier safely down while she completed the last checks. Lights, water, door. The room did not need an emotional farewell every time she left it.

At the switch, she looked once more at the unfinished corner.

“Keep the pot empty?” Julian asked.

“For now.”

“It suits the space.”

She turned off the remaining lights. Outside, he waited while she locked up, then took the spare bag from beneath her arm so she could hold the carrier securely.

They would part at her door tonight. He had his own home, his own morning, and things she had not yet asked him about. She could ask on their evening together. There would be room for an answer.

Eve checked the lock once and turned away from the window.

The place was closed. She was still allowed to have the rest of the night.
