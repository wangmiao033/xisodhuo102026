# Chapter 167 — Pet Food in a Biscuit Tin

There were three red tins.

Mia stood in Mabel's kitchen with her hands held carefully away from all of them.

“You didn't mention there were three.”

“I said the biscuit tin.”

“They all say biscuits.”

“One of them is taller.”

“Am I feeding a dog or choosing a successor?”

Eve turned her laugh into a cough and failed. Mabel gave her a look over the top of her glasses.

“You can laugh when you have a kitchen worth organizing.”

“I was defeated by a drawer yesterday.”

“A drawer is easier.”

“That is what the drawer wanted me to think.”

Mabel's kitchen smelled faintly of toast. Its windowsill held a row of jars that had each acquired a second life, and a tea towel hung from a hook positioned exactly where Mabel reached for it. Everything suited the person who had lived there long enough to stop noticing the arrangement. Eve had the uncomfortable feeling that her own flat would present a similar puzzle, with worse handwriting.

They had agreed on a short, pet-free rehearsal while Biscuit was out with a familiar helper on his ordinary slow outing. Mabel remained in the room. Nobody would feed him, change his routine, or try opening anything without asking. Mia's task was to use the written directions to find a clean bowl, the usual food, and the measuring item Mabel used under Biscuit's existing care plan.

They had reached the second step and were already in difficulty.

“The one on the right,” Mabel said.

“From where?” Mia asked.

“From here.”

Mia looked over her shoulder. Mabel was facing the counter from the doorway, while Mia stood on the other side of the small table. Their right sides pointed toward different tins.

Eve saw the exact moment Mabel noticed.

“Oh.”

“An important geographical discovery,” Mia said.

Mabel came closer, then stopped herself. She folded her hands in front of her as though physically restraining the urge to finish the task.

“You're supposed to work it out from the paper.”

“The paper says red tin.”

“Yes, all right.”

Eve asked whether she could shift the tins onto the table so they could compare them without reaching over one another. Mabel agreed and moved them herself. The tallest carried a faded painting of a country house. The other two had nearly identical roses. A small dent on one lid was the only distinction Eve could see.

“Do you remember what is in each?” Mia asked.

“Obviously.”

“May we open them with you?”

Mabel lifted the first lid.

Buttons filled the tin in a bright, complicated heap.

Mia nodded solemnly. “High in fastenings.”

“Don't start.”

The second tin contained wrapped biscuits. The third held a properly closed original packet of Biscuit's usual food. Mabel took it out far enough to show its label, then put it back without disturbing the contents.

“That one keeps the packet tidy,” she said. “I haven't poured mysterious brown things into a museum exhibit.”

“Nobody said you had,” Eve assured her.

“You both had the face.”

Eve could not deny that she had briefly acquired the face.

They closed the tins again. Mia tried to find the measuring item. According to the map, it lived with the cups. According to Mabel, that meant the small cupboard near the back of the table. According to Mia, cups were where the mugs hung.

The measuring item was neither in the cupboard nor with the mugs. Mabel had washed it that morning and left it on the drying rack.

For several seconds they stared at it.

“That is temporary,” Mabel said.

“Temporary things are very powerful,” Eve replied.

She thought of her own keys, temporarily put beside a book until a temporary bag covered them. Sometimes a perfectly ordinary morning could dismantle instructions before anyone arrived to follow them.

Mia sat down. “I was going to write 'same amount as usual.'”

“For someone who doesn't know usual?”

“Yes. I hear it now.”

She did not look stupid. She looked annoyed by the discovery that knowledge could disappear in the distance between her head and a piece of paper.

Mabel drew the map toward herself. “I should rewrite the whole thing.”

“Maybe first we find which bits worked,” Eve suggested.

“The bowl,” Mia said at once. “You described the shelf and said the blue one, and there was only one blue bowl there.”

“And the towel?”

“Also fine. The hook was by the door.”

Mabel's shoulders eased. The map had not been a ridiculous failure from beginning to end. It had simply assumed that whoever read it could borrow her eyes.

Eve took three blank cards from the small packet of spare materials she had brought with permission. “We could try names instead of positions for the tins. Temporary labels, then you decide what you actually want to keep.”

Mabel stared at the roses.

“I don't want enormous notices all over my kitchen.”

“They don't have to be enormous.”

“And don't put glue on those lids. It takes the paint off.”

“No glue.”

They used little paper sleeves around the containers, loose enough to remove. Mabel chose the wording. Buttons. Human biscuits. Biscuit's food, original packet inside. On the care page she specified the correct measuring item and the current written amount, checking it against her existing instructions rather than letting anyone guess from the scoop's size.

Mia picked up the tin marked HUMAN BISCUITS.

“The dog has your name on his food and you have your species on yours.”

“That's because the humans are the ones causing trouble.”

Eve laughed properly then. Mabel joined in, with a grudging shake of her head that did not disguise her pleasure.

They tried again from the kitchen entrance. This time Mia found the right food without opening the other tins. She found the bowl. She looked for the measuring item in its newly agreed home and stopped when it wasn't there.

Mabel had left it on the table during their discussion.

“I did that deliberately,” she announced.

“No, you didn't,” Eve said.

“No. But it was educational.”

They wrote one short instruction about contacting the owner if the expected item was missing, rather than substituting a household spoon. The rehearsal wasn't testing Mia's ingenuity. It was testing whether she could carry out what Mabel actually intended.

At the bottom of the page, Eve noticed a little drawing of a house with a crooked arrow.

“What is this part?”

“That's for later.”

“Later when?”

“The door. And things downstairs.”

Mia looked at the drawing and then at the three tins. “We may require a second expedition.”

Mabel folded that section under. “One household crisis at a time.”

Mia copied her own revised sentence onto a card and read it aloud. Halfway through, she stopped at the word there, crossed it out, and replaced it with a description of the shelf. Mabel watched, then silently changed an over here on her own page.

Eve pretended to be occupied with putting the unused paper away. Neither woman needed an audience for every small correction. They had already provided each other with enough of one.

The bell sounded before they could continue. Mabel checked who was there and went to receive Biscuit from his familiar helper. Eve gathered the loose cards and moved her bag out of the route. The old dog came in at his own pace, paused to inspect Mia's shoes, and went toward his usual resting place without performing gratitude for their organizational achievement.

“We haven't touched your dinner,” Mia told him.

Biscuit lowered himself with a sigh.

“Wise,” Mabel said. “He would ask for references.”

Their agreed half hour was nearly over. Eve checked the time. She could stay, take photographs, make a clean digital version, and somehow arrive at her client deadline with none of the work done. The possibility offered itself with an attractiveness she now found suspicious.

“I need to go in five minutes,” she said. “Do you want me to take a copy of the practice page, or would you rather keep it?”

Mabel kept the household sheet. She allowed a photograph of the three tins with their harmless temporary labels, cropped to the table, for their own discussion at the next exchange. No home address, access information, or personal care details would go on a public screen.

“Not a post about an old lady who can't label things,” she added.

“Absolutely not.”

“People are forever discovering old ladies. We've been here the whole time.”

Mia laughed, then asked whether Mabel would like help making the labels neater after Eve left. Mabel said not today. She wanted to try them herself for a few days before deciding whether they annoyed her.

It was a reasonable answer. Eve liked that Mia took it as one.

At the door, Mabel handed them each a wrapped biscuit from the correctly identified tin.

“For successful return from the expedition.”

“We haven't solved the map,” Mia said.

“You found the food. Don't become greedy.”

Outside, they walked together as far as the crossing. Mia unwrapped her biscuit and then put it away again because she wanted to talk.

“I thought I had this part sorted,” she said. “Not the trip. Just being a competent person with a dog.”

“Your dog still needs the arrangements you've made. This doesn't erase them.”

“No. But I didn't think a different set of days would make everything feel new.”

Eve remembered the first time she had put rent into the venue budget. The sum was straightforward. The number of choices leaning against it had not been.

“Have you heard from the carer?” she asked.

“They can discuss the dates tonight. I need to know exactly what I'm asking for before they can tell me if it works.”

“And Leah?”

“Still waiting. I'll catch her when she's home instead of sending six more messages.”

The crossing signal changed. They crossed without finishing the conversation in the road. On the other side, Mia turned toward her bus stop and Eve toward the work waiting at her flat.

“I'm going to label the utility cupboard,” Mia said.

“Will the door still be called the back door?”

“Probably. We're not ready for that degree of change.”

At home, Eve put her own biscuit on a plate and opened the client document. Dumpling came to investigate the wrapper. She moved it out of his reach and offered him his familiar toy instead. He batted it once beneath the chair, then looked at her as if she had designed the failure.

She retrieved it before settling to work.

For a moment she imagined a photograph of the three tins on the venue wall: funny, colorful, easy to explain. Then she looked at Mabel's message, which had arrived while she washed her hands.

I've moved the buttons. Still keeping the tin.

Eve replied that the tin was excellent.

She saved the approved private photograph with the workshop materials and returned to her client's sentences. The useful part of the visit belonged to Mabel's kitchen. It did not need to become Eve's content before she could be glad it had happened.
