# Chapter 125 — A Room Borrowed for Now

The neighborhood center offered Eve two early-evening slots, and for nine seconds she furnished an entire building.

A shelf by the door. A kettle that didn't have to be located before every session. Chairs she could choose once and stop returning to a cupboard. Perhaps a window with something beyond it besides another wall.

Then she read the message again.

Two possible slots in the following month. Each would be booked separately. The room was used by other groups before and afterward, so setup and clearing had to fit inside the stated hours. No storage, no exclusive use, no promise of continuing availability.

It was an offer to try her revised activity in a familiar room. Eve had supplied the building herself.

She replied with the one date that fitted her work calendar and asked to visit briefly to confirm the arrangement. She did not take the second date merely to prevent somebody else having it.

The coordinator met her beside the reception desk two afternoons later. From another room came a burst of laughter, followed by the sound of chairs scraping.

"The reading group," the woman said. "They debate seating as vigorously as the books."

She showed Eve the room schedule. A language class would finish before Eve's proposed booking, and a committee would arrive afterward. The available window included fifteen minutes before the exchange and twenty minutes after it.

Eve counted backward from the next group's arrival.

"I can keep the activity to seventy-five minutes, as before. Would the tables be in the same arrangement when I came in?"

"Not necessarily. The class uses them differently. You can move them during your time, but you can't count on entering early."

They went inside. Today the chairs faced the whiteboard in two rows, and the table had been pushed against the wall.

Eve put her bag down and moved one chair. The second caught briefly against the table leg. She freed it, then looked at the clock above the door.

"May I try the change now? I'd rather know how long it actually takes."

The coordinator agreed. Together they watched Eve turn a classroom into a small discussion space using only the furniture she would be allowed to move.

It took six minutes. Eight when she restored the arrangement she had disturbed.

"That leaves time to check the room and welcome people," Eve said, breathing a little harder than she had intended. "If I stop bringing unnecessary things."

"What unnecessary things?"

"Mostly imaginary ones so far."

The coordinator smiled.

Eve had brought a sketch of a display board. She left it in the bag. The trial needed clear information and a host who wasn't sweating over decoration when participants arrived.

They checked the route to the toilets, the location of drinking water, and where the directional sign could stand without obstructing another group. Eve asked about the fifth chair against the wall, which she had kept available for anyone wanting more distance. The coordinator confirmed it could stay there during the activity as long as the exit route remained clear.

"Will animals attend this one?" she asked.

"No. Owners only, same as before."

"Put that prominently in the confirmation. People sometimes see a familiar face online and expect to meet the animal too."

"I've already changed the preview. I'll check the confirmation again."

At the table, they looked through the revised description. The coordinator didn't try to turn the exchange into a larger event. She asked how Eve would tell whether it was worth repeating.

Eve had brought an answer, then found she didn't like how impressive it sounded aloud.

"Attendance, feedback, engagement, and sustainability," she began.

The coordinator waited.

Eve lowered the page.

"Whether enough people pay for the thing I actually offer. Whether they use something afterward. Whether I can prepare and run it in the hours I've counted. And whether the room is handed back properly."

"That will tell you more."

"Less attractive as a headline."

"Most of our useful information is."

They agreed on one booking at the ordinary small-room rate. The center would include the activity in its listings once Eve supplied the final description, but no audience was guaranteed. The second possible date would remain available to others unless she later chose to book it and it was still free.

Eve read the hire conditions before confirming. The cancellation deadline went into her calendar beside the minimum attendance she needed for this version to run. She would decide while she still had a real choice, not after pride had spent the room fee for her.

When the coordinator left to answer a question at reception, Eve stayed to measure the table with her own folder. She didn't need an exact dimension for furniture she wasn't buying. She needed to know whether six sets of paper would fit without covering the water cups.

They would, if she used one sheet at a time.

She took the display sketch out at last. It showed three neat panels, a title, and a little ledge for cards. Attractive. Portable, supposedly. Another object to carry, assemble, clean, and store in her flat.

She turned it over and wrote the next session's narrower topic on the back: Making everyday care information easier to use.

That was something she knew how to facilitate. It connected the feeding card, visit instructions, and information folder without promising a solution to every difficulty in a pet household. People could bring a non-sensitive example or work from a blank template. She could show an example in the announcement so no one needed to guess what the hour contained.

At home, the money for the room came from the trial budget, not the account where she kept her rent and ordinary bills. The booking left enough for the printing she actually needed and a refund allowance. It did not leave enough to order beautiful folders.

Eve opened the folder supplier's page anyway.

She selected a color, compared two sizes, and looked at the minimum quantity. Fifty. The company was very keen for her to become a person who owned fifty folders.

Dumpling jumped onto the chair beside her desk and pressed his face against the closed packet of plain card.

"You prefer the existing inventory?"

He bit a corner.

She took the packet away, checked that he hadn't swallowed anything, and closed the supplier's page.

The plain card went into a lidded box with pens, the sign, and the printed sheets. Eve packed the box once, carried it to the door, and discovered it was too awkward to manage with her bag. She removed the spare display stand and tried again.

Better.

The entire activity now occupied one box she could carry and one booking she could afford.

A few days later the coordinator allowed her a short final check of the empty room before closing. Julian was meeting Eve nearby for a walk afterward. She asked whether he could join her for the last ten minutes, and the coordinator said that was fine.

He arrived while Eve was checking that the sample sheet printed clearly under the room's lights. He paused in the doorway, looking at the whiteboard, the chairs, and the window facing brick.

"This is it," she said.

"It has excellent walls."

"All four. Included in the price."

He came in and set his coat over his arm rather than on the materials. Eve showed him the table arrangement, then stopped herself halfway through describing the entire run of the session.

"You don't have to review it. I invited you because I wanted you to see where I'm doing this."

"Then show me the important part."

She thought for a moment and pointed to the chairs.

"Three people sat there and talked about things they actually needed. I kept imagining the empty places. I almost missed the occupied ones."

Julian looked at the table.

"Are you expecting more next time?"

"I hope so. I need enough for the figures to work. But six is still the maximum. More people would be a different activity, with different preparation."

He nodded. "Which chair did you have?"

She showed him. He sat in it, experimentally, and stood again.

"I can see the door from here."

"Exactly. I could notice if someone arrived or needed to leave without making everyone turn around."

It pleased her that he had noticed something practical without arriving as the expert on her room. She showed him the plain sample sheet and the place where each participant would write a next action.

Then she admitted the building she had furnished in her head.

"There was a kettle," she said. "A shelf. Good chairs. Possibly a window with an unreasonable amount of daylight."

"Ambitious. The sun is difficult to schedule."

"And I'd know where everything was. I wouldn't be borrowing an hour between a class and a committee."

The room was quiet around them. Without people, it looked smaller and less certain. Eve could hear someone at reception counting keys.

"Would you like your own place eventually?" Julian asked.

She leaned against the table, careful not to sit on it.

"Maybe. A small one. Somewhere people could do useful things without needing to be impressive first. But I don't know what enough people would come back for. I don't know what the quiet months would cost. I haven't even learned whether I can run this version twice without borrowing hours from everything else."

Saying the unknowns did not empty the room of possibility. It made the shelf in her imagination less likely to fall on her.

"So not yet," she said.

"Not yet," Julian agreed.

She looked at him. "That wasn't a request for you to make it happen."

"I know."

There was no injured pause, no proposal to find her cheaper premises through someone he knew. He simply stood with her in the room while she pictured a future too large to book that afternoon.

"Would you come to see it," she asked, "if there ever was a room that stayed mine after nine?"

"Yes. I'd like that."

"You could admire the kettle."

"Within my competence."

She smiled and reached for his hand. For a few seconds they stood beside the table, neither of them making a promise about a business that had not yet earned its next month.

Then the coordinator called that the building was closing.

Eve gathered the sample sheets, checked beneath the table, and put the chair Julian had tried back where it belonged. There was no key to take home. She handed the temporary visitor badge to reception and thanked the coordinator.

Outside, Julian asked which way she wanted to walk.

"Along the shops. I need bread afterward."

"I need to collect something before they close."

They chose a route that let them do both. Dinner was not assumed. He had plans at his own flat, and Eve wanted an hour to finish a client revision before feeding Dumpling. They would see each other at the weekend, as arranged.

At the corner, he kissed her and asked her to tell him how the revised bookings went when she had something to tell.

She said she would.

On the way home, Eve bought bread and no decorative kettle. The booking confirmation was in her bag, the next activity still small enough to fit inside the time she had.

For now, that was a room she could open.
