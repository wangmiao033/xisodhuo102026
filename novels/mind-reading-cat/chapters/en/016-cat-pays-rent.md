# Chapter 016 — Cat Pays Rent

The name Eve wanted was taken by a man who posted photographs of his boat.

The second name was taken by a stationery shop. The third belonged to an account with no posts, no picture, and the serene confidence of a person reserving a useful piece of language forever.

Sophie watched from the sofa. “Have you tried adding 'official'?”

“I refuse to be officially unemployed.”

“You're working.”

“I refuse to be officially between invoices.”

Dumpling's tail appeared from behind the curtain and vanished again.

Eve looked at the name field, then typed Cat Pays Rent.

The platform accepted it.

“There,” she said. “An achievable financial strategy.”

Sophie looked toward the curtain. “Has your business partner agreed?”

“He's contributing atmosphere.”

The joke made them both laugh. Eve saved the name before she could start another search. It was only a name, she told herself. A small account to gather her work and the ordinary experience of having acquired a cat while losing a job. Not a company, not a promise to become interesting every morning.

She still checked how it looked beside the profile image six times.

Sophie had come for a planned forty-minute test, after which she was meeting someone about a job of her own. She had brought a small phone clamp and refused to let Eve turn the visit into an unpaid production day. Eve had supplied coffee and offered to hold the camera whenever Sophie next needed pictures of an event setup.

“Ask me when you actually have the date,” Sophie had said. “Don't start a favor savings account.”

Now she tightened the clamp and studied the corner Eve had cleared beside the window.

“You've made it look as though no one lives here.”

“The real room contains washing.”

“Most real rooms do.”

“I didn't think that was my strongest selling point.”

Sophie moved the chair a little, then stopped. “Show me the script.”

Eve handed her the notebook reluctantly.

The first page contained an opening she had written at midnight. On the day I lost everything, I found someone who needed me. Beneath it were three sentences about rain, loneliness, and the healing power of an animal who had nothing to give but trust.

Sophie read without speaking.

Eve began defending it before she reached the end.

“It's just an introduction. People need a reason to watch.”

Sophie looked up. “Did you lose everything?”

“My job. My plan. A significant amount of dignity on public transport.”

“Did you lose me?”

“No.”

“Your home?”

“No.”

“Your enormous collection of mugs?”

“Unfortunately intact.”

Sophie put the notebook down. “Then maybe don't start by evicting the rest of your life from the story.”

Eve felt the criticism more sharply than she wanted to. The sentences had seemed good in the quiet, when she was alone and frightened about money. They made the sequence of events look purposeful. Losing the job was the necessary darkness before a small orange light.

Without that shape, she had simply lost a job and found an injured cat. The facts did not promise a happy ending.

“That version gets to the point,” she said.

“It gets to a point. I'm not sure it's yours.”

Dumpling emerged from the curtain, examined the tripod, and chose the narrow space behind the sofa. His tail remained visible for several seconds, then disappeared too.

Sophie glanced at the empty frame. “The orange light has gone out.”

Eve laughed despite herself.

They tried a simpler opening. Eve sat on the chair and explained that she'd recently taken in a cat while helping continue the search for anyone who knew him. She had done some work with an adoption group and wanted to show the less photogenic parts of learning to live with an animal.

On the playback, she sounded like she was opening a community funding application.

“Why do I say 'animal' like I'm uncomfortable admitting he's a cat?”

“Because you wrote a speech.”

“All right. No speech.”

Sophie asked her what she had done that morning.

“Put the kettle on. Moved his water bowl because the sunlight was bothering me in a shot. Realized that was a stupid reason to move his water bowl. Put it back.”

“Say that.”

“I can't build an account around admitting I'm ridiculous.”

“You have years of material.”

Eve threw a cushion at her. It landed nowhere near Sophie and became an immediate object of interest behind the sofa.

They recorded the story about the bowl. Eve showed the badly chosen camera angle, then the ordinary arrangement that worked better for the cat. Dumpling did not appear. A tail entered the edge of the picture during the last three seconds, looking less like a cohost than a passing objection.

Eve watched the playback and smiled.

It wasn't polished. She spoke too quickly at the beginning and said “actually” twice. But she sounded like herself, which was either encouraging or a sign that she needed a more marketable self.

Sophie checked the time. “Twelve minutes left.”

“Can we do one more?”

“One.”

Eve knelt beside the banana box and described how an inexpensive container had defeated everything more attractive she'd considered buying. She didn't pull Dumpling out of hiding or shake a toy to manufacture a reaction. He eventually approached the box because it was his box, sniffed the cushion she'd thrown, and walked past the camera.

“Excellent,” Sophie said. “A strong refusal to engage with the brand.”

Eve wanted to try again. She could imagine a cleaner version with the cat climbing into the box at the right moment. The temptation was small and entirely reasonable. One more take. Another five minutes.

Sophie began unclipping the phone.

Eve swallowed the request.

“Thank you. How's your meeting?”

“Not happened yet.”

“I mean, what is it?”

Sophie looked at her for a beat, then sat back on the sofa while winding the cable.

“A production coordinator role. Short contract. Bigger venue. Possibly more spreadsheets than sunlight.”

“Do you want it?”

“I want to know whether they mean coordinator or person who apologizes when everyone else ignores the schedule.”

Eve laughed. “Ask them what happened at the last event.”

“I intend to.”

“Will you tell me afterward?”

“If there's anything to tell.”

She didn't promise a report or ask Eve to help decide. Eve found she could be interested without immediately offering a solution.

At the door, Sophie gave Dumpling a brief wave. He sat beside the sofa and watched her leave without responding.

“He'll miss me,” she said.

“Deeply. Privately.”

Once she was alone, Eve opened the account settings again. The blank biography field seemed to demand a life plan. She typed one version that made her sound like a trained animal behavior specialist and deleted it before finishing. Another made the account sound like an official page for the clinic, which it wasn't.

In the end she wrote: A cat, a small flat, and the work of figuring things out. Pet-life stories and practical behind-the-scenes notes. Not a veterinary advice service.

She checked it once and moved on.

The group had already agreed she could share its finished short film, with credit and the same approved information. She posted that first, linking back to their page. Then she trimmed the water-bowl story to a minute and added captions without music dramatic enough to suggest a funeral.

The draft introduction still sat in her notebook.

On the day I lost everything.

She looked around. Her flat was crowded and not particularly clean. Her savings had a time limit. She was scared about next month's work in a way no witty account name could solve.

But there was a half-finished coffee Sophie had left on the shelf. A message from Beth thanking her for the respectful edit. A clinic brief in her inbox, specific enough to price. Those things were not everything. They were not nothing, either.

Eve crossed out the midnight speech.

Before posting the second video, she checked the background for mail, labels, or reflections that revealed more of her home than she intended. She cropped a delivery label from the corner, corrected a caption, and watched the full minute once without touching anything.

Then she pressed publish.

The account did not immediately become a place where people gathered. For several minutes, it looked exactly as empty as it had before. Her first follower was Sophie, who sent a message saying the cat's tail deserved a separate appearance fee.

Eve replied with a photograph of the abandoned cushion.

The next notification came from the clinic, but it was an email, not a follow. Tessa had sent the written brief and the proposed recording window. One short introductory FAQ. Clear approval before publication. No filming patients or visitors. Julian would be available for a limited session in an empty room, provided the clinic's day allowed it. The budget was modest but real.

Eve read it twice, then drafted a quote for exactly that work. She added a line separating material made for the clinic from anything she might share on her own account. Permission would need to be agreed rather than assumed.

There was one practical problem with accepting it: her table wasn't large enough for the laptop, camera, notebook, and dinner without at least one of them meeting a sauce. She measured the clear wall beside the window, then found a folding worktable available on a short rental. It was not a studio expansion. It was a place to set equipment down during the first few jobs, with a return date she put in her calendar before booking it.

She nearly ordered a bright backdrop too. The preview showed a room that looked cheerful, spacious, and owned by someone with matching storage baskets. Eve looked from the screen to the banana box.

The backdrop came out of the basket.

She sent the quote, wrote down the rental cost, and checked how much would remain after ordinary expenses. The number was less exciting than the inquiry had felt. It was still a number she could work with.

Dumpling jumped onto the chair beside her, turned, and settled against the folded yellow dishcloth she had left there. He looked comfortable enough to be entirely unconcerned about the new account named for his earning potential.

“You don't have a shift,” she told him. “Relax.”

He had already closed his eyes.

By evening, the bowl video had acquired a comment from someone who said their cat preferred a discarded shoe box to an expensive bed. Eve replied that she was considering asking the box manufacturer to design the rest of her furniture.

It was an ordinary exchange. No one called her inspiring. No one needed to be told that she had been saved.

She enjoyed it enough to forget the view count for several minutes.

When she remembered, she left the phone facedown and went to wash the two coffee mugs.
