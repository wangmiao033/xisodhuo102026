# Chapter 019 — The First Bad Comment

The first comment Eve wanted to answer with a legal brief arrived while she was eating noodles from the saucepan.

She had intended to use a bowl. Then she had intended to finish one small piece of editing before the noodles went soft. Now the noodles were soft, the saucepan was warm against her knees, and a stranger had discovered precisely where to place a finger.

Another animal account doing the sad rescue routine for attention. Must be convenient having a cat pay the rent.

Underneath that, the same person had written: And your clinic video says everyone should get there half an hour early. Enjoy the packed waiting room.

Eve put the saucepan on the floor, well away from Dumpling, who had shown no interest in her dinner until it became available at his height. She moved it to the counter instead.

Then she began to type.

She had not filmed Dumpling's injury for entertainment. She had not posted a crying rescue montage. The account name was a joke. She had paid for his care, sought the owner through the proper channels, worked for the clinic on an agreed basis, and corrected the misleading short clip the previous day. She could provide a list.

The list became a paragraph. The paragraph became four.

By the fourth, she was explaining what kind of person she was to someone whose profile picture was a badly cropped lamp.

Dumpling jumped onto the vacant chair. His tail brushed the screen, scrolling the page just far enough to hide her unfinished defense.

“You don't have to take sides,” she told him. “But if you are taking sides, mine has dinner.”

He settled down with his back to her.

She took a screenshot and sent it to Sophie with the words, Am I allowed one tiny murder?

Sophie's answer came several minutes later.

No. I need help choosing a printer next week and prison visiting hours are inconvenient.

Eve laughed, which made the anger less satisfying without removing it.

Before she could ask for a more emotionally useful response, another message arrived, this time from Tessa.

Can you check the subtitle around 0:42 on the clinic introduction? The spoken line is right. The subtitle isn't.

Eve stopped chewing.

She opened the published video. Julian was explaining that people should check the arrangements for their particular appointment. The next section showed her own phone and notebook while Julian's recorded voice described what to do if a delay was likely.

The spoken words were ordinary and clear: If you're going to be late, call us rather than waiting until you arrive.

The subtitle said: If you're coming in, arrive thirty minutes early.

Eve stared.

It was a line from an early placeholder card. She had used it only to test text size and had meant to replace it with the verified wording. She had corrected the working caption track, then exported the version that still contained the old graphic laid over it. On the small preview, the two layers had looked like one.

Yesterday she had been so proud of checking the revised short excerpt. Today the older long video was quietly displaying a sentence nobody at the clinic had said.

The comment was not entirely wrong.

This was a deeply inconvenient development.

She called Tessa.

“I see it,” Eve said. “I'm taking the copy on my account down while I replace it. Can you stop sharing the clinic copy?”

“Already paused on our page. Nobody's been told to change an appointment.”

“Has anyone arrived early because of it?”

“One caller asked. That's why I checked.”

Eve closed her eyes. “I'm sorry.”

“I know. Can you send the corrected export and a still of that frame?”

“Yes.”

“And keep the message simple. People need to know the instruction was wrong, not that you've spent the evening suffering over a font.”

Eve looked at her four paragraphs about integrity. “I might have needed that second part.”

“I had a feeling.”

She paused the video, opened the project, and inspected every text layer. There were two hidden placeholders and a title card with an old date. Neither appeared in the final export, but she removed them anyway, then renamed the project clearly enough that a future, tired version of herself might understand it.

The correction took nine minutes. The check took twenty.

Part of her wanted to send the replacement immediately, before anyone else could notice. Instead she put the phone on its stand and enlarged the picture. The old instruction had looked tidy because she had spent time making it look tidy. She had adjusted its margins, chosen its color, and carefully aligned the wrong words with the edge of the frame. Remembering that made her wince. It also made the next step obvious. She turned off the decorative layer altogether and rebuilt the captions from the approved transcript, one plain sentence at a time.

She listened with the captions off, watched without sound, and then compared both together. She wrote down every practical instruction and checked it against the approved material. No one had asked for a medical answer in the clip, and she did not add one in an attempt to compensate for the mistake.

Tessa reviewed the corrected frame and file. Eve waited for the reply before replacing anything.

While she waited, the saucepan cooled on the counter.

She deleted her long response to the commenter.

That hurt more than deleting the wrong subtitle. The subtitle had been a mistake. The response contained several excellent sentences, including one about confusing skepticism with insight. She saved none of them. Their excellence was not relevant to what needed fixing.

When approval came through, she replaced the file wherever she controlled it and sent the final version to Tessa. The notice she posted was short: An earlier version showed an incorrect arrival instruction in its subtitles. That line has been corrected. Please follow the arrangements given for your own appointment, and contact the clinic if you need to check them.

Then she returned to the comment.

You're right about the subtitle. It was my editing error, and I've corrected the video with a notice. Thank you for pointing it out.

She read it several times, looking for weakness.

There was no explanation of the cat. No defense of her character. No mention of the lamp.

She sent it.

Dumpling stretched across the chair as if one of them, at least, had made productive use of the evening.

The following morning, Eve brought the borrowed microphone back to Sophie, who was working at a café table beneath a plant with ambitious downward growth. A leaf rested on the lid of her laptop. She had moved the laptop rather than the plant.

“You look as though you argued with a vending machine and lost,” Sophie said.

“I made an error. Someone noticed it while being unpleasant.”

“Very poor manners of reality.”

Eve put the microphone pouch on the table. “I wanted them to be wrong about everything.”

Sophie unzipped the pouch and checked the contents. “Of course you did.”

“I don't want to become one of those people who says every criticism is hate.”

“Then don't.”

“I came for nuance.”

“You came to return my microphone. Nuance costs extra.”

The café door opened. A gust lifted a paper napkin from the next table, and Sophie caught it against her knee before it reached the floor. She returned it to its owner without interrupting her inspection of the cable.

Eve wished sorting comments could be that physical: catch the useful piece, let the rest blow away.

“What about the rescue part?” she asked.

“Do you think they're right?”

“No. Not about what I've posted.” She hesitated. “I did write a version that made me sound very noble. I didn't use it.”

“Then you know which choice you made.”

“But they don't.”

Sophie looked up. “You can't make a stranger watch all the footage you decided not to post.”

Eve sat with that while the waitress set down their drinks. Hers was too hot to hold. She curled her fingers around the saucer instead.

Sophie had to leave in twenty minutes for a site visit. They spent twelve of them talking about the printer and the astonishing range of ways a machine could fail to print black on white paper. Eve listened. It was a relief to occupy a conversation that did not concern whether she deserved to exist online.

On her way home, she stopped at the clinic to collect a blank appointment-information card Tessa had offered as a reference for future shoots.

Julian was coming out of a room, washing his hands at the corridor sink. Eve waited until he had finished rather than announcing her humiliation to his back.

“Tessa said the correction is in,” he said.

“It is. I checked the final file this time. All of it.”

“Good.”

“I might start printing that on my forehead.”

“Wouldn't you have to check it in a mirror?”

She laughed despite herself.

He dried his hands. “The caller who asked about the timing was given the right information.”

“I know. Tessa told me. I'm glad they asked.”

“So am I.”

That was all. He did not tell her the error proved she was careless, nor did he tell her it didn't matter. She found she could breathe in the space between those two possibilities.

At reception, Tessa handed over the reference card, then pointed at Eve's phone.

“Your account name is not an employment contract with the cat, is it?”

“No.”

“Good. Because his attendance record is terrible.”

“He's very senior.”

“That's usually the explanation.”

Back home, Eve wrote a small export checklist and taped it above the desk. Not an inspiring statement. Five unlovely things to verify, ending with the words WATCH THE FILE YOU ARE ACTUALLY UPLOADING.

She checked the comments once.

The original critic had not apologized for the rescue accusation. They had replied, Fair enough about the correction.

It was not warm. It was not friendship. Eve found she did not need to turn it into either.

Another viewer said the revised information had helped them decide which question to ask reception. Somebody else still thought the whole account was pointless. A third person wanted to know what brush Dumpling would tolerate.

Eve did not know the answer to that yet. She said so.

Then she put the phone in a drawer and fetched the toy Dumpling liked least, because it was the one she could find. He watched it travel along the rug with total contempt. When she dragged it behind the chair, he leaned forward, betrayed by curiosity.

She let him wait. She waited too.

The toy appeared at the other side. Dumpling pounced with a clumsy enthusiasm that would have looked excellent on camera, had the camera been out.

It wasn't.

For ten whole minutes, nobody was required to form an opinion about them.
