# Chapter 173 — The Chair by the Door

The chair outside was the least beautiful piece of furniture Eve owned.

It was solid, wipeable, and a shade of brown that seemed to apologize for itself. She had nearly covered it for the opening photographs, before remembering that nobody had promised photographs and the cover would only slide when someone sat down.

By early afternoon, she was grateful for every unlovely inch of it.

Mabel arrived with Biscuit at the end of a slow walk. The elderly dog stopped before the entrance, looked into the room, and planted his feet. Mabel didn't coax him across the threshold.

“Good,” she said. “We've agreed on something.”

Eve came out to meet them.

“The chair's here.”

“I can see that. I'm deciding whether its color will clash with my coat.”

“It clashes with everything. Very versatile.”

Mabel sat down with a sigh that made Eve wish she had put a second chair there weeks ago. Biscuit remained beside her on his lead, with enough space to settle without entering the shared walking route. The little outdoor area they had reserved was not grand enough to deserve the word porch on a property listing, but it was sheltered and separate from the neighboring entrance. For Mabel, that mattered more than the name.

Eve offered water for her and a clean bowl for Biscuit. Mabel checked what he wanted, accepted the bowl, and set it within his reach. He sniffed, drank a little, and lowered himself onto the mat she had brought.

“There,” Mabel said. “A successful visit.”

“You've only just arrived.”

“I intend to keep my expectations manageable.”

Inside, Robin was resetting the table for the next short activity. Eve had left a gap between the booked session and the open demonstration period. The gap was now occupied by cleaning, a visitor finding a missing scarf, and two conversations that had not obeyed their estimated length. It was earning its place on the schedule.

She crouched beside Mabel, keeping clear of Biscuit's resting space.

“Would you like me to bring the display out so you can see it?”

“Not the whole shop. Just tell me whether you've put a price on everything.”

“Yes.”

“Then you're already better than the place that made me ask about a brush last week. I felt as if I was negotiating for a car.”

Eve laughed. She had nearly hidden the prices beneath the display cards because they spoiled the arrangement. She wasn't going to confess that now.

A pair of visitors came through the outer gate and paused side by side, looking at the window. Behind them, a courier approached the neighboring entrance with a parcel held against his chest.

“Excuse me,” he said.

They moved half a step, still reading.

Eve rose, but someone had already spoken from behind them.

“If you stand over here, you'll see the whole sign. He needs that door.”

Alex came down the last step from upstairs carrying a shopping bag. They pointed toward the clear viewing space without touching either visitor. The pair moved, apologized to the courier, and resumed their discussion from somewhere that did not block a working entrance.

“Thank you,” Eve said.

Alex glanced at the sign. “You made the cat crooked.”

“It was a collaborative decision.”

“Looks awake, at least.”

Their tone was dry rather than hostile. Eve checked the time without making a production of it. The agreed opening hours were on the notice Alex had received. They weren't supposed to be sleeping now, but she had learned not to treat a timetable as permission to stop caring about the person living above it.

“Has the sound been all right?”

“So far. The chair feet help.”

“I'll pass that on to the chair feet.”

“Please do. They're the quietest members of your team.”

Mabel made a sound that might have been agreement.

Alex stayed long enough to ask what the demonstration involved. Eve described the ordinary household instructions exercise. They looked at the table inside and said that their workplace could use it, since nobody agreed which cupboard contained spare forms.

“Do you want to try a round?” Eve asked.

“Maybe later. I'm going shopping first.”

They went without being converted into a volunteer. Eve liked them more for that, then corrected herself: people didn't have to help her to become likable. It was an irritating lesson to keep discovering from different directions.

A child stopped near Mabel and asked whether Biscuit wanted to be stroked.

“Not just now,” Mabel said. “He's resting.”

The child's father began to apologize for the question. Mabel waved him off.

“Good question. Sensible answer. We're all finished.”

The child considered this, then asked whether he could look at the drawing of the cat instead. Eve showed him where he could stand with his father without leaning over Biscuit. Nobody needed to make the old dog friendlier for the occasion.

The afternoon grew busy in a way the morning hadn't. People came to look between other errands. Some stayed for ten minutes; some asked about a session and left. The table demonstrations had a limited number of places, so Robin invited people to wait for the next round rather than adding another chair to every available gap.

One man objected that he only wanted to watch.

“You can watch from this side if there's space,” Robin said. “We still need the route clear.”

The man looked around for Eve, apparently hoping for a different answer. She nodded toward the place Robin had indicated.

“They're right. The next round starts shortly.”

He stayed, shifted his bag, and watched. The entire disagreement lasted less than a minute because Eve didn't turn it into two competing sets of instructions.

Then the woman with the missing scarf reappeared.

“It was in my bag,” she announced.

Eve congratulated her with more sincerity than the discovery strictly deserved.

Outside, Mabel was telling a passerby that Biscuit wasn't available for a photograph. The passerby put the phone away at once. Eve returned to the doorway carrying the requested price card for the supplies display, and Mabel read it with the attention she usually gave complicated bills.

“These labels are useful,” she said.

“I hoped so.”

“The decorative cards are very pretty.”

The separation between the two remarks was precise. Eve felt it and pretended not to.

A sudden scrape came from inside. One chair's felt pad had caught and begun to peel. Robin stopped the activity long enough to exchange it with the spare rather than pushing through the noise. Eve fetched the replacement pad from the box labeled with a plain description. It was exactly where the label said it would be.

For a moment she thought of the expensive sign and nearly laughed.

The things she had been embarrassed to spend time on were working. A spare chair. A place for an umbrella. A gap in the schedule. A mat outside for a dog who had never agreed to cross the threshold. None would have made a convincing opening announcement on its own.

Together, they were keeping the room from becoming an obstacle course.

Alex returned while Eve was fixing the chair away from the guest route. Their shopping bag contained something long wrapped in paper, and they held it upright to avoid catching anyone.

A little cluster had formed at the entrance again. This time they were people leaving, talking to people arriving, each group certain it would only take a moment.

“Sorry,” Alex said. “Could you move that reunion two steps over? Some of us have vegetables to deliver.”

The group laughed and moved. Eve put down the pad and came out.

“I should have caught that.”

“You were repairing a chair.”

“I still don't want you doing crowd control for us.”

“Then don't put me on your timetable.”

“I won't.”

Alex nodded toward the opening notice. “The end time still the same?”

“Yes.”

“Good. I'm back on nights tomorrow.”

Eve confirmed it without offering a hopeful maybe about extra visitors. After Alex went upstairs, she moved herself to a position where she could see the entrance and asked Robin to call her before the next changeover. The neighbor's help had exposed a place her own arrangement could improve. It hadn't supplied a free permanent door attendant.

Mabel watched the adjustment.

“You're doing that thing with your face,” she said.

“What thing?”

“The face that says somebody helping you has become a moral emergency.”

Eve stared at her, then burst out laughing.

“I said thank you.”

“You did. You can stop there sometimes.”

Mabel had a point. Eve could accept a helpful sentence without turning it into a debt or a staffing plan. She brought the brown chair a little farther into the shade when Mabel asked, then left it alone when Mabel said that was enough.

Later, a woman who had attended the morning exchange came back with a friend. She began to explain the activity before Eve could start. Her version included the cupboard drawing, the chance to pass, and the fact that nobody had laughed at her handwriting.

Eve would never have thought to advertise that last part.

She listened instead of correcting the description into something smoother. The friend asked about the next date. Eve gave her the information card and pointed out the booking method, then returned to the door as a departing group stood up.

The passage remained clear.

Eve checked the number of people at the table against the available places, then put the spare invitation cards back in their holder. She could have encouraged the two women to stay for an extra round. She wanted their interest badly enough to hear herself beginning the offer.

Instead she gave them the correct next date. There was room for them then, with time for the activity they actually wanted. One put the card in her purse rather than promising immediately, and Eve let the conversation finish without trying to secure an answer.

Mabel eventually checked the time and gathered Biscuit's things. He rose slowly after her, stretched, and looked toward the street rather than the venue.

“Would you like a hand with the bag?” Eve asked.

“Just until I get his lead sorted.”

Eve held it, waited, and handed it back when Mabel was ready.

“I didn't come inside,” Mabel said.

“I noticed.”

“Still counts.”

“I know.”

This time she did. She didn't ask Mabel to stand beside the sign for proof of attendance, or coax Biscuit into a farewell photograph. She watched them take their familiar slow route away, then brought in the bowl and wiped the chair for whoever might need it next.

The brown seat had a small water mark on it. Eve dried it. Behind her, Robin announced the final demonstration before the planned closing period, and a guest asked whether she could come another day just to look at the supplies.

Eve answered yes, during the published hours, and found that she could say it without promising herself to the room every day forever.

She set the chair back in its useful, unphotogenic place.
