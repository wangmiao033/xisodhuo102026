# Chapter 136 — The Blue Door Behind the Cups

The woman who thought Dumpling was hers had photographed her cat asleep in a roasting tin.

Eve looked at the picture across the small table at the clinic. There was a folded towel beneath the cat, a kitchen chair behind him, and a white paw hanging over the metal edge. The resemblance made her grip her own folder harder.

“He liked it better than the expensive bed,” Marion said.

“I can believe that.”

It was the first thing Eve had managed to say without rehearsing it.

Marion had contacted the found-cat notice after an old post appeared in a neighborhood group. Tessa had arranged a short appointment to compare the supporting information. Dumpling remained at home. Nobody needed to bring two frightened creatures together merely because one of them could speak.

Marion placed another photograph on the table.

“This one shows his face properly.”

Her cat, Rusty, was looking past the camera. The white around his nose widened on a different side from Dumpling's. Eve noticed that immediately, then distrusted how pleased she was to notice it.

“May I put the current photograph beside it?” Tessa asked.

Marion nodded.

They compared the clear pictures rather than the small image in the old notice. Rusty's white markings ended differently above his front paws. The shape on his face differed too. In the roasting tin, the turned head and dangling leg had hidden both differences.

Marion leaned closer. Her finger stopped above the paper without touching it.

“Oh,” she said.

Eve wanted to offer an explanation that would make the sound less painful. She could find none that belonged to her.

There was another discrepancy. Marion had brought an original message containing the kitchen photograph, sent to her sister weeks after Eve's recorded rescue date. A routine veterinary receipt from the same period named Rusty, and the original appointment message helped Marion place the week. She had first supplied the month in which she posted a later search notice, not the month he disappeared.

“He was still with me then,” Marion said, looking at the date. “I kept thinking of when we moved.”

Tessa gave her time to check.

Eve remembered saying it had rained all day when she only knew about the rain after leaving work. Memory had improved her own story without asking permission. Marion had simply been trying to find a cat.

“So it can't be him,” Marion said at last.

“The dates and the clearer markings don't fit,” Tessa replied.

Marion sat back. For a moment she looked angry, though Eve couldn't tell with whom. Then she began returning the photographs to their envelope, aligning their corners with more care than the task required.

“I'm sorry I worried you.”

“You had a reason to ask,” Eve said.

“I thought I'd know immediately.”

“I thought I would too.”

Marion looked at her then, and the awkwardness between them changed. Eve had arrived afraid of what the woman might take away. Across the table sat someone who had brought an envelope because she hoped to carry an answer home.

Tessa checked that Marion had the appropriate current search contacts. She didn't promise a result. Marion thanked them, took her coat from the chair, and left with the same envelope.

Eve remained seated.

Tessa closed the comparison record. “Do you need a minute?”

“I feel relieved.”

“You're allowed.”

“She isn't.”

“She may feel several things.”

Eve looked toward the door. That was true. She had already begun deciding what the woman must feel, as though failing to identify a cat qualified her to understand a stranger.

She helped gather the papers. The appointment had answered one claim. It had not answered the empty space that had sent Marion there.

Outside, Eve bought a roll from the bakery and ate it on a bench before going home. She had nearly skipped lunch to reward herself with more searching, which would have been a peculiar response to a useful morning.

Her phone contained a message from Leanne.

The old charity-sale photograph had finally acquired an address.

There had been occasional exchanges since Leanne's first inquiry. A clearer scan had arrived; the date remained uncertain. Someone remembered a church room, someone else a shop basement. Neither had supplied enough to follow. This time Leanne attached a scan of the whole print, including the border previously cropped away, and a photograph of the writing on its back.

An address, a sale date, and a note about borrowed tables.

At the left edge of the wider image stood a blue door. Its paint was worn near the bottom. Beside it, a man's arm supported a stack of plastic trays. The orange-and-white cat still occupied the corner beyond the cups.

Leanne's message added that the man had agreed to speak with Eve. He ran a small repair shop on the same row and remembered feeding a cat outside. His name was Daniel.

Eve finished the roll before replying. She asked whether the date had been checked against anything besides the handwriting, and offered two times when she could take a call.

Leanne replied that her friend had found the sale leaflet tucked in the same album. It carried the same date and address. She sent a copy with permission.

At home, Dumpling was sleeping with one foot against the side of his bed. Eve crouched nearby and watched him breathe for a moment, grateful in a way that made the morning feel uncomfortably close again.

He opened his eyes.

“I know,” she said. “Hovering is irritating.”

He stretched the foot farther, leaving no room for disagreement.

Daniel called at the later time. Eve sat at her desk with a blank page, not with the elaborate family tree she could already feel herself wanting to construct.

“I should tell you I never owned him,” he began. “I put food down a few times. That's what I can tell you.”

“That would still help.”

“Leanne said you're looking for everyone who looked after him.”

“I'm checking what happened before I found him. I don't know how much we'll be able to establish.”

“Good. Because my memory's excellent right up until somebody asks for a date.”

Eve smiled. “We can start with something else.”

He described the shop frontage, a shared passage, and the small yard behind the building. During one period, people brought bags of pet food and folded blankets through the blue door. Daniel had sometimes put down an extra portion for a cat waiting near his back step. He had not asked who owned him.

“Orange and white?” Eve asked.

“Yes, though that's half the cats on the internet. I found pictures. I'm sending them now.”

Three images arrived. One showed a repaired trolley beside the blue door. An orange-and-white cat sat underneath it. The next was nearer, with the cat turning toward a shallow dish. The third caught the side of his face as he looked up.

Eve opened her own clear photographs beside them.

The uneven white border at the shoulder matched. So did the shapes around the face. She could see enough of the body now to compare what the chair leg had hidden in the sale picture.

Her chest tightened.

“This looks much more like him,” she said carefully.

“I thought so. Same expression, anyway. Like I was late with something.”

“When did you take these?”

Daniel read the dates shown with the original files. All preceded the rescue. The trolley had belonged to the sale organizers; an old repair invoice helped him confirm that part of the sequence. He would send only the relevant date and item description, he said, once he covered the customer's details.

Eve wrote down what the different records supported. There were several feeding visits within a short period. There was a location. There were clear photographs consistent with Dumpling. There was no continuous account of where he had lived.

“Did someone ask you to feed him?” she asked.

“Once. A person coming out of the back room said there was food in the tub if he turned up before they did. I can't give you a name. We knew people by which door they used.”

“Did the person say he was theirs?”

“No. I didn't ask.”

Eve looked again at the cat beneath the trolley. She wanted to know whether he had liked the person, whether he had waited for them, whether anyone had carried him inside when it rained.

Daniel was describing a damaged wheel. She made herself listen.

“The floor slopes. Things roll toward the drain. I remember because I kept moving the trolley back.”

“Is the blue door still there?”

“Yes. Room's used for storage now. I don't have the key.”

She asked whether the current occupier could be contacted through the shop, without expecting Daniel to let her into somewhere he didn't control. He offered to pass on her inquiry.

Then he said, “If you come to look at the yard, I can show you the step. It isn't much.”

“It doesn't have to be much.”

They discussed a quiet time later in the week, subject to the current occupier agreeing. Eve explained that she might bring Dumpling only if the journey and the space seemed suitable. Daniel said there was no need to bring him for his benefit. The photographs were clearer than his memory.

After the call, Eve sent the new material through the existing search channel, separating Daniel's firsthand account from what he had heard about the room. She did not write that Dumpling's first home had been found.

She got up to make tea and discovered she had left the kettle empty.

The practical failure steadied her. Water first. Switch second. There were still sequences she could complete.

Julian called that evening from his own flat, as arranged. She told him about Marion before she told him about the blue door.

“I was so glad she was wrong,” Eve said. “Then I felt awful.”

“Did you make her being wrong more important than checking?”

“No.”

“Then you did the uncomfortable part.”

She leaned against the counter. “I'd have preferred to become generous before the appointment.”

“That would have been convenient.”

She laughed softly.

He asked about the new photographs, and she described what they showed. She would send them privately if he wanted to see; he said yes. Neither looked to the cat for confirmation. Dumpling was washing a hind foot with an intensity that offered little assistance to historical research.

Eve told Julian about the proposed visit. He was free that afternoon and offered to come if she wanted company. She did. They agreed to decide about bringing Dumpling after checking the arrangements, not because a photograph demanded a reunion.

Later she filed the blue-door image beside the earlier cups.

The new picture contained more of the room, but its edges still excluded almost everything. Somebody had held the camera. Somebody had stacked the trays. A cat had waited near a dish.

Eve could finally attach a person's voice to one small piece of his life.

She closed the folder and went to fill the bowl that belonged to tonight.
