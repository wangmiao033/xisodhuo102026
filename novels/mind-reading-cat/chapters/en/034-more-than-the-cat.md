# Chapter 34: More Than the Cat

The first version involved a sock.

Eve would later prefer not to discuss that version.

She had stuffed the sock with a tea towel and made it emerge from an overcrowded bag while her other hand searched for keys. On paper, the idea had suggested the comic chaos of leaving the house. On camera, it suggested that her laundry was attempting to escape.

She watched the clip once.

“No.”

Dumpling, asleep beyond the table, offered no objection.

She deleted the test and took the sock back to the bedroom.

The second version was cleaner: a top-down view of everything arranged around the Pocket Path tote, each item entering its proper compartment.

It looked like every other advertisement for a bag.

More seriously, it concealed the thing she had liked about the product. A tidy person with all the time in the world could pack almost anything. The point was finding something again when a person had one hand occupied and had already decided they were late.

Eve put the camera aside and went downstairs with her ordinary bag.

At the building entrance, she tried to take out her keys while holding a water bottle and a folded towel. The bottle slipped under her elbow. The keys were beneath the notebook. The door began to close against her shoulder.

An elderly man coming in held it open.

“Thank you.”

“Going somewhere?”

“Research.”

He looked at the towel, then at her face, and wisely chose not to ask.

Back upstairs, Eve arranged the same objects on the table. No fake mountain of belongings. No hidden thread pulling anything into place. The actual bottle she used, the same notebook, the lead borrowed as a prop from her supplies box, and keys that were not suddenly attached to a secret mechanism for the useful shot.

She tried the tote's side pocket. Her fingers found the keys without disturbing the rest.

That was the moment.

Not a cat's approval. A hand finding what it had put away.

She wrote a three-part sequence on a sheet of paper: leaving, needing, finding.

Then she crossed out the dramatic opening she had added beneath it.

The story was small. That didn't mean she needed to apologize for it by pretending something enormous happened.

She sent Felix a one-page proposal with three rough drawings and a short, unpolished test of her own hand using the pocket. The message called it a concept test, not material available for publication.

He replied that afternoon.

*Useful, but not much of a hook. Can we still end on Dumpling reacting?*

Eve stood by the sink and read it twice.

The cat was in the window, watching a pigeon with a level of interest he had not shown in the tote. She could have filmed that look and attached it to anything.

A bag. A bowl. A declaration of professional competence.

She dried her hands and called Felix.

“If we end on an unrelated reaction, we're back where we started.”

“I'm not asking you to bring him to the studio again.”

“I know. But it would still imply he was responding to the product.”

“Could the caption make it a joke?”

“We could keep explaining what the cat isn't doing, or we could make a film about what the bag does.”

A pause.

“That sounded sharper than I meant,” she added.

“It was fairly clear.”

She took a breath. “The hook can be the key search. Not a fabricated disaster. The little frustration people recognize. Then we show the same things in this bag, in one continuous shot.”

“People won't watch a full packing demonstration.”

“Not full. Twenty-five seconds. Maybe thirty. One use case, not every feature.”

He asked her to come by with the sample when she returned it. She agreed to discuss the revision, not to arrive with a completed film.

Before the meeting, Eve sent Sophie the rough test and asked whether she could book half an hour of feedback on pacing and sound at her usual rate.

Sophie replied with a time and the cheerful warning that friendship would not improve the quality of the footage.

At the agreed hour, they spoke by video. Sophie was sitting on a stack of empty equipment cases, eating from a container balanced on her knee.

“You're between events?” Eve asked.

“Between loading and regretting loading. Show me the new cut.”

Eve did.

Sophie watched the key search without laughing. Eve's stomach sank.

“It's too long,” Sophie said.

“I thought the frustration needed to build.”

“It has. In me.”

Eve stopped the clip.

“The second part works,” Sophie added. “You hear the keys before you see them. Keep that. Lose the face you make at the camera.”

“My relatable face?”

“Your person-being-relatable-on-purpose face.”

Eve groaned and marked the frame.

They tested two shorter openings. Sophie pointed out that the movement was clearer when Eve's sleeve didn't cover the pocket, and that the sound of the zip should be recorded separately rather than boosted until the room hissed.

Useful, specific things. Work.

When the half hour ended, Eve stopped herself from sliding into five more questions.

“I'll pay you this afternoon.”

“Good. And you can send me something that isn't a bag later.”

Eve looked at the frozen frame on her screen.

“Have I only sent you work lately?”

“You've also sent me a photograph of a broken spoon.”

“It was a very strange break.”

“It was. I'll give you that.”

The call ended because Sophie needed to load the cases. Eve transferred the agreed fee before opening the edit again.

At Pocket Path the next morning, Felix had cleared a small space on the counter. Eve placed the returned tote beside its sample tag and showed him the revised sequence on her phone.

“I still miss the surprise,” he said.

She had prepared herself for that. Preparation did not prevent disappointment.

“Then don't buy a large campaign from me on the promise it'll surprise everyone.”

He looked up.

“Buy one small test,” she continued. “One finished film. Your channels, for the period we discussed. A limited paid run if you want it, with the amount and dates written down. No claim that it improves anyone's life beyond making these items easier to organize.”

“That last line isn't much of a slogan.”

“No. I promise not to put it in the film.”

He smiled, reluctantly.

She showed him the revised quote. It was lower than the original offer because the scope was smaller, not because the failed trial made her labor free. The film would use her hands, simple props, and the product. No animal performance. No studio day. No raw footage handed over afterward.

Felix ran a finger down the page.

“I can afford a test. I can't afford a beautiful failure.”

“I can't promise sales.”

“I know.”

She waited.

He turned the tote around, looking at the pocket she had used.

“This was the part I kept arguing about with the manufacturer,” he said. “They wanted to make it decorative. Too shallow. Things fell out.”

“That's the story.”

“Not me arguing with a manufacturer.”

“The pocket being deep enough. We can show that without saying it's the best pocket in the world.”

He let out a breath and reached for his laptop.

They changed the deliverables while sitting on opposite sides of the counter. Felix asked for two different opening captions. Eve included one alternate caption within the same edit rather than two separate films. They fixed the approval date and payment date. The sample would remain with her for the filming period, which he recorded in the loan note.

Only when they had both accepted the same version did Eve put the tote back in her bag.

Outside, she stood for a moment beneath the shop awning.

The original number had been much larger. She hadn't turned it into an equal triumph by finding a clever principle. She had negotiated a smaller job, one she could actually do, at a price that still paid her for doing it.

Her relief was quieter than the first rush of seeing the offer.

It also lasted longer.

Filming took most of the next morning. She borrowed no miraculous light; she moved the table toward the window and bounced daylight with the inexpensive board she had bought after the workshop. She checked the strap didn't obscure the pocket. She repeated the hand movement until it was clear without looking unnaturally smooth.

Once the keys caught on the notebook's elastic. She kept the take as a reference for what not to conceal with a cut, then repacked the bag honestly and tried again.

At lunch she discovered another problem. In the close shot, the bag looked larger than it was. The camera angle made the side panel appear deep enough for a folded coat, although the coat beside her would have filled most of the main compartment by itself.

She tried adding measurements to the caption, then took them out. A line of numbers racing across a phone screen wasn't the same as showing scale.

Instead she backed the camera away and kept her hand in the frame while placing the ordinary bottle in the pocket. The shot became less elegant and more useful. She checked the product sheet before naming the bottle size, then sent Felix a still to confirm the sample matched the version he was selling.

His answer came while she was eating an apple over the sink: it did, but the revised pull tab would be slightly longer.

They agreed not to feature the old tab in close-up. Eve made a note for the final description so the difference wouldn't look like a surprise to someone who ordered the bag.

It cost her another half hour to reset the angle. She wasn't delighted. She was also aware that these were the decisions she had wanted to be paid for: noticing, checking, and making the thing clearer before somebody else had to complain.

Dumpling woke midway through and approached the edge of the work area. Eve put the camera down before he could investigate the dangling strap.

“Not your shift.”

She moved the loose strap out of reach and waited while he crossed to the other side of the room. The film could stop for two minutes. That was one advantage of directing keys.

By afternoon she had the sequence she wanted.

The final shot showed her hand lifting the bag from its hook. The keys were already where she needed them. Nothing winked at the camera. No animal certified the purchase. The caption named the practical use and identified the film as paid work.

She watched it without sound, then with sound, then on the small screen people would actually use.

Twenty-eight seconds.

A small job that looked like a small job done properly.

Eve sent the first cut and closed the laptop before she could refresh for an answer.

On the table, the rejected sock lay beside her notes.

She finally put it in the laundry.
