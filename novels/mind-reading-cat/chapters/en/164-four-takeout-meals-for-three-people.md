# Chapter 164 — Four Takeout Meals for Three People

The fourth meal arrived with no fourth person to claim it.

Eve stood in her kitchen holding the delivery receipt while Sophie lifted one container after another from the bag.

“Did you invite someone and forget?”

“No.”

“Did you forget someone and order their dinner anyway?”

Julian checked the number of chopstick packets. “The evidence suggests a confident host.”

Eve looked at the order on her phone. She had added their three choices, then failed to remove the single meal left in the basket from an abandoned order earlier. Four dinners. Three adults. One cat sitting beneath the table with the expression of a creditor.

“It's tomorrow's lunch,” she declared.

“An excellent recovery,” Sophie said. “Was that in the original plan?”

“The plan has been revised.”

They put the spare meal aside. Dumpling sniffed the air, received none of it, and climbed onto the unused chair while Eve found bowls. The food stayed on the human side of the table, out of his reach. His own dinner had already been served in its usual place.

Eve had invited Sophie and Julian over to look at the next month's venue figures. Sophie had agreed to a specific paid planning hour; afterward they would have dinner as friends. Julian was there because two proposed expenses involved him, and because he had promised to tell Eve when her estimates depended on something he had never offered.

That last description had sounded affectionate when they arranged the evening. With the spreadsheet open beside four warm containers, it sounded much more like an audit.

“Food first?” Julian asked.

“Only a few figures,” Eve said.

Sophie set a timer for the planning hour. “Then we stop, whether your figures are behaving or not.”

The first page went reasonably well. Rent under the modest trial arrangement. Utilities estimated cautiously. Robin's booked preparation and hosting time. Cleaning supplies. The filming cancellation charge, which Eve read aloud without trying to make it sound smaller.

Sophie did not say she had told her so. She asked whether it had been paid.

“Yes.”

“Good. Put it in the actual column, then. It can't keep haunting the estimate.”

Eve moved the number. The total changed by exactly nothing, but the page looked less accusatory.

Then Julian pointed at a line called shared support.

“What is that?”

“Things we might all contribute.”

“Which things?”

Eve could hear Robin asking how short was short. Apparently vagueness had begun following her home.

“Some setup help. The little things people bring. Your plant pot, for example.”

Julian leaned back.

“The empty pot I gave you?”

“Yes. It might go in the venue.”

“It can go wherever you like. I gave it to you.”

“That's why I'm trying to account for it.”

“Are you trying to make it equipment?”

She looked at the unglazed pot on the shelf. It was still empty, still resting on its saucer, quietly surviving every opportunity she had offered it to become useful.

Sophie put down her pen. “You can own a present without making your business owe somebody for it.”

“I know that.”

The speed of her reply suggested that she hadn't known it as comfortably as she wanted.

She had dealt with budgets before. She had paid people, negotiated fees, and separated client costs from groceries. The venue had made the confusion larger because so many people she cared about were now standing inside something she hoped to earn money from. A gift could become a prop. A favor could become a weekly task. A meal could appear to thank someone for work nobody had priced.

She didn't want to take advantage. Somehow that had led her to a line in the spreadsheet where affection, wages, and a flowerpot all occupied the same cell.

Julian touched the edge of her notebook.

“Can we start with what you need people to do?”

She read the list. Welcome guests. Host the session. Set up and clear. Deal with bookings and refunds. Check the room before locking. Prepare content. Handle the accounts.

“Those are jobs,” Sophie said. “Some are yours. Some you're paying other people to do. If someone offers a one-off favor, record what they actually offered. Don't turn it into a permanent shift.”

“I wasn't going to.”

Sophie waited.

Eve looked down at the draft rota. In pale gray, she had written S? beside two future preparation sessions.

She erased both.

Sophie smiled, not triumphantly. “Thank you.”

“I thought the question mark made it a question.”

“It does, until your budget needs the answer to be yes.”

The words landed quietly. Eve picked up her food container, felt how much cooler it had become, and put it down again.

Julian's proposed involvement was on the next page. Eve had imagined an occasional booked conversation about what owners could expect from a veterinary appointment, if the clinic agreed and the scope was suitable. She had also, less clearly, imagined that his presence might make other events feel supported.

He drew a line under the first description.

“This is something we can discuss with proper arrangements. It's not confirmed.”

“I know.”

“The second one sounds like being available because I'm nearby.”

She looked at the phrase reassuring presence and wished the spreadsheet could swallow it.

“I did that at the rehearsal.”

“You corrected it there. Let's not put it in next month's figures.”

Eve deleted the phrase. No expected income would rely on an unconfirmed professional session. Julian would keep his clinic work, and any separate collaboration would need a defined date, scope, fee, and whatever workplace agreement was required. His coming to collect her after work would remain coming to collect her after work.

Sophie checked the next subtotal. “That leaves a gap.”

“I can see it.”

The gap had been there all along, disguised as people she loved saying yes.

For a moment Eve wanted to close the laptop. They could eat, laugh about the extra meal, and return to the numbers after she had become a person less susceptible to disappointment.

Instead she asked Sophie which part of the first month's plan they could reduce without selling something they couldn't deliver.

They cut a second preparation session that had no confirmed purpose. Eve moved the introductory video to a later decision, after the simpler written booking description had been tested. She kept Robin's paid clearing time. She kept the cleaning allowance. She didn't remove the quiet corner from the floor plan merely because it had no income line.

The gap became smaller. It did not disappear.

Sophie asked where Eve had put the cost of replacing the worn chair feet.

“In supplies.”

“And the refund allowance?”

Eve scrolled back. It was there, but she had calculated it against a hopeful number of bookings. They changed the attendance estimate to the smaller figure already supported by inquiries, then left enough room for a booking that didn't work out.

“That's uglier,” Eve said.

“It was already ugly. Now we can see it.”

Julian turned his container so the label faced her. “This one promised extra vegetables. There are two pieces.”

Eve gave an involuntary laugh. “Please don't make the dinner another forecasting failure.”

“Too late.”

“I can use part of the money from the existing client work,” Eve said. “Up to this amount. After that I need to reduce activity again, not assume the next booking will fix it.”

She wrote the amount in a separate contribution line and kept a minimum for her own living costs. The figure hurt because it was money she had earned, not money a fictional full room might bring next month.

Julian nodded. He did not reach for his phone to transfer the difference.

A small, ungracious part of her noticed. A larger part remembered how badly she would resent discovering that he considered a payment the right to settle every later decision.

“I don't want either of you to feel that saying no to work means saying no to me,” she said.

Sophie lifted her eyebrows. “I said no to two evenings. I'm still eating your dinner.”

“Technically, you haven't started.”

“That is becoming a serious concern.”

The timer sounded. Sophie finished the note she was writing, then closed the notebook.

“That is my paid hour. I'll send the short summary we agreed, within that fee. No more spreadsheet tonight.”

Eve began to ask one last question, saw Sophie's hand resting on the cover, and stopped.

They moved the laptop to the kitchen counter. Julian helped portion the food while Eve put the spare meal away promptly for later. The other containers had lost their inviting heat during the discussion. She reheated what needed it, bringing bowls back one at a time while Sophie complained that the most stable relationship in the room was hers with the microwave.

Dumpling had settled into the unused chair with his front paws tucked beneath him. Eve put an empty placemat on that side while making room, and he rested his chin against its edge as if dinner had been delayed through intolerable incompetence.

“No,” she told him. “You have eaten.”

He blinked slowly.

Sophie looked between the cat and the fourth place. “He thinks he's the investor.”

“Terrible terms,” Julian said. “Demands all the chair space.”

“And won't explain his contribution,” Eve added.

Dumpling shut his eyes.

They ate. For several minutes nobody discussed attendance, costs, or responsibilities. Sophie told them about a prop cupboard at her new project whose labels had been written by three different people with three incompatible ideas of what counted as fragile. Julian described finding a clean pen inside a box of examination gloves and spending longer wondering how it got there than retrieving another pen would have taken.

Eve talked about the camera operator's kindness after she canceled, and then admitted that she had wanted them to be less kind so she could be annoyed instead of embarrassed.

“That seems human,” Sophie said, stealing a piece of food from Eve's offered bowl. “Inconveniently.”

When they finished, Eve reached for the receipt.

“This dinner is personal,” she said. “My invitation. Not payment for the work.”

Sophie nodded. “And my invoice is still coming.”

“I expect it.”

Julian took the bowls to the kitchen. Eve followed with the cups, leaving Sophie to defend her chair from Dumpling, who had suddenly decided the occupied seat was superior.

At the sink, Julian nudged Eve gently with his elbow.

“Are you all right?”

“I'm disappointed by some numbers.”

“Reasonable.”

“And relieved I don't have to pretend a pot is a loan.”

“It's a very small pot. The interest would be ridiculous.”

She leaned against him, warm shoulder to warm shoulder. He dried a bowl without turning the moment into a solution.

Behind them, Sophie said firmly, “I am a paid professional, and this is my chair until I leave.”

A soft thump followed. Dumpling had returned to the fourth place.

Eve laughed into Julian's sleeve. The food had needed reheating, the budget still required restraint, and tomorrow's lunch existed because she had made a mistake.

None of those facts prevented her from wanting this evening again.
