# Chapter 188 — You Do Not Have to Be There for Everything

Eve woke on the sofa to the sound of her mother looking for a shoe.

Not both shoes. That would have suggested a general failure of memory. One shoe had been placed neatly beside the door. Its partner had somehow traveled beneath the chair where the suitcase stood.

“Don't get up,” her mother whispered.

Eve lifted her head. Her neck disagreed with the previous night's decision to use a decorative cushion as a pillow.

“Are you leaving?”

“For a while.”

“What time is your appointment?”

“It isn't an appointment.”

But Eve was already sitting up, pushing the blanket away from Dumpling, who had appropriated its lower third. Sunday light lay pale across the floor. Her mother wore the blue jacket she used for outings rather than meetings. There was a folded piece of paper sticking out of her bag.

“I can come with you.”

“You've only just woken up.”

“Give me ten minutes.”

Her mother straightened. “Eve.”

The voice was mild. Eve heard tiredness in it and immediately supplied the rest: unfamiliar streets, reluctance to inconvenience her daughter, determination to manage alone until managing became difficult.

“I don't mind,” she said.

“I know. I've arranged to meet someone.”

That should have been enough. Eve was still untangling her feet from the blanket when her mother found the shoe, put it on, and added, “I'll send you a message before lunch. Go back to sleep.”

The door closed gently.

Dumpling stood, stretched, and looked at Eve as if she alone had caused morning.

She fed him, checked his water, and made coffee. There was no reason to feel abandoned. Her mother was visiting for work, not offering herself as the central activity of Eve's weekend. Yesterday they had successfully negotiated cheese. Today should have been simpler.

On the table lay the scarf her mother had forgotten.

Eve picked it up. The weather was cooler than yesterday. A scarf was an excellent reason to be useful without admitting that usefulness had become an itch.

Her phone buzzed.

At the little bakery by the square. Found it easily. Don't rush your morning.

Eve looked at the scarf again.

I'll bring this, she typed, attaching a picture.

No need, came the answer. Warm inside.

She stood in the kitchen holding it for another moment, then put it on the chair.

Five minutes later, she needed bread.

The justification was not wholly false. The loaf was nearly finished. The bakery was within walking distance. Eve dressed, checked that her mother had her keys, and went out with a shopping bag she held a little too purposefully.

At the square, the wind lifted dry leaves against the bicycle rack. Through the bakery window she saw her mother sitting opposite a woman with silver-streaked hair and bright red glasses.

They were laughing.

Not the polite laugh Eve heard when her mother had to negotiate around a colleague. Her mother was leaning forward, one hand flat on the table, apparently preventing the other woman from finishing a story too loudly. The woman ignored her and continued with visible delight.

Eve slowed.

Her mother saw her through the glass and waved her in.

There was no stranded traveler to rescue. There was half a pastry, two coffees, and a familiarity that had existed before Eve became someone who arranged things.

“This is my daughter,” her mother said. “The one I've been telling you about.”

“Oh, I recognize you. You had the yellow boots.”

Eve stopped beside the table. “I'm hoping recently you've had newer information.”

The woman laughed. She had worked with Eve's mother years ago, before moving to this city. Their messages had continued through different jobs, a broken ankle, and an astonishing disagreement about whether a certain television ending had been good.

“We've been trying to meet for months,” her mother said. “This trip finally worked.”

“You didn't tell me.”

“We only settled the time this morning. She had to check something first.”

Eve looked at the two cups again. A complete arrangement had been made without needing to pass through her. She had somehow forgotten that was an ordinary possibility.

“Right,” she said. “Of course.”

The woman offered her a chair. Eve nearly took it out of politeness, then looked at the two coffee cups and the pastry they had divided without negotiation.

“I only came for bread.”

Her mother's eyebrows rose, very slightly.

“And possibly to be unnecessary,” Eve added.

“You can be unnecessary with bread,” her mother said. “It's a useful skill.”

Eve bought a loaf and two rolls. She resisted purchasing something for the table as a small tax on leaving. Outside, her mother caught up with her for a moment.

“I wasn't trying to be brave,” she said.

“I know that now.”

“I like visiting you. I also like having breakfast with someone who remembers how awful my old supervisor was.”

“Apparently the yellow boots were worse.”

“They were very loud boots.”

Her mother touched her arm, warm fingers through the jacket sleeve, and went back inside.

Eve walked home by the longer street. She noticed a new display of secondhand lamps in a window, a dog refusing to leave an interesting drain, a patch of sun on an otherwise unremarkable wall. The morning no longer needed to justify itself through an errand performed for someone else.

Julian arrived at her flat just before midday, carrying the clean clothes he would take back to the course. His aunt came with him. They had met at his parents' house, where he had gone for an early cup of tea before collecting her.

“I have a question about an address,” the aunt said, taking a folded card from her bag.

Eve had the beginning of an itinerary ready before the card reached the table.

“What time do you need to get there?”

“No fixed time.”

“Is it difficult to get back from?”

“Not especially.”

Julian, beside the sink, looked at Eve over his glass of water. He said nothing. That was worse than if he had smiled.

Eve took a breath.

“What would help?”

The aunt unfolded the card. It was for a small shop selling replacement fittings for old lamps. She had brought a broken knob from home and wanted to see whether they carried something compatible. The street name was printed in a pale decorative type that looked elegant until anyone tried to read it outside.

“I need this written larger,” she said. “And to know whether that's an eight or a three.”

Eve examined it. Julian leaned in too. Between the three of them, they established that it was an eight. Eve checked the shop's publicly listed address against the card before copying it onto a fresh piece of paper in large, plain letters.

“That's all?” she asked.

“That's all. I can use a bus. I just don't want to get off at the wrong end because somebody thinks addresses should resemble wedding invitations.”

Eve wrote the nearest stop beneath it after asking whether she wanted that too.

The aunt tucked the paper into her purse. “Lovely. I'll go after lunch.”

They ate bread, cheese, and some of the vegetables left from dinner. Dumpling stayed on the windowsill until the aunt moved her bag from his usual landing space. Then he jumped down, sniffed the edge of a shoe, and continued elsewhere without granting anyone special approval.

Julian's train back was later that afternoon. Eve wanted to walk with him to the station. She asked. He said yes, and the aunt said she would take her own bus to the shop.

The aunt asked about Eve’s photography while she finished her bread. Eve showed her one recent image on her phone: hands repairing a battered chair, the light catching a small curl of wood. The aunt enlarged it to examine the tools. Her husband had once attempted a similar repair, she said, and attached the seat at a permanent angle. They had kept the chair for years because everyone knew which way to lean. Eve laughed and showed her the next photograph. For several minutes she forgot to calculate when anyone should leave. She was being asked about work she cared about, and the aunt was interested in the answer rather than waiting for travel advice.

No one needed an escort assigned by a committee.

After lunch, the aunt left with the large address and the broken knob wrapped in tissue. Julian checked his train ticket while Eve packed the two rolls into a paper bag.

“Those are for you,” she said. “If you want them.”

“I do.”

“Good. I was worried I'd have to make a distinction between support and aggressive baking.”

“You bought them.”

“That makes it aggressive retail.”

He laughed, then caught her hand as she turned toward the counter.

“You all right?”

She considered offering an amusing summary of the bakery incident and nothing else. But his train would take him away again, and she did not want every difficult feeling polished into something convenient before it could reach him.

“I keep thinking that if I don't anticipate everything, someone will have a terrible time.”

“Did anyone?”

“My mother had an excellent time without me.”

“That sounds inconsiderate of her.”

Eve leaned her forehead against his shoulder.

“I know how it sounds.”

“It sounds tiring.”

He did not tell her she was secretly wonderful for worrying. She was grateful. She did care about everyone. She had also spent twenty minutes this morning inventing a need her mother had explicitly denied.

“At the bakery,” she said, “she looked like someone I didn't have to look after.”

“She usually is.”

“I know. I liked seeing it.”

On the walk to the station, they passed a pavement stall selling cheap umbrellas. Julian stopped to examine one because his had developed a bent rib. Eve held his bag while he checked the catch. It was such a small division of labor that she almost smiled at it.

He did not need her to solve rain. He needed two free hands for half a minute.

At the station entrance, they stood away from the moving crowd. Eve gave him the rolls. He put them carefully above his books so they would not be crushed.

“Video tonight?” he asked. “After I've put my things away?”

“My mother and your aunt are coming back for dinner. There's been talk of cooking.”

“Then I should probably supervise.”

“That confidence will be recorded.”

He kissed her once, then again because neither of them moved away at the expected moment. When he finally went through, she stayed until he looked back.

On the walk home, her mother sent a photograph of a pastry wrapper and two empty cups. The aunt sent a much less artistic picture of a lamp knob held beside its replacement.

Found it. Large writing excellent.

Eve replied to both without adding a new instruction.
