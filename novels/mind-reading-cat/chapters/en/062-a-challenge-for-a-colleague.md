# Chapter 062 — A Challenge for a Colleague

Claire's brief was better than Eve wanted it to be.

It arrived without a dramatic opening line, a promise of a million views, or a suggestion that a veterinarian's face could compensate for missing information. The clinic wanted a short film about preparing for an ordinary visit. Claire would handle filming and editing. Eve would conduct a paid preparatory interview, develop the owner's part of the story, and review the cut against the agreed account.

The clinic's general guidance would remain separate from the owner's experience. Nobody's pet would have to improve on camera.

Eve read the proposed fee again. It was reasonable for the hours listed, provided the interview stayed within scope and the review did not grow extra heads.

She asked about revisions. Claire answered that one consolidated round was included and additional work would be discussed before it began.

Eve had to stop looking for a convenient flaw.

They met on Wednesday at a café with small round tables that turned every folder into a territorial dispute. Claire put her bag on the floor instead of the spare chair. Eve noticed that too, then became annoyed with how many things she was noticing.

“I thought of Mabel,” Claire said. “But only if she wants to. I don't want to borrow her because you know her.”

“She's not an easy reassuring quote.”

“That's part of why I thought of you.”

Eve looked up.

Claire stirred her coffee once, then set the spoon down. “Your walking film didn't make her into someone waiting to be rescued. I'd like that level of attention here.”

“The clinic may want something more cheerful.”

“They want people to arrive less confused. Cheerful would be a bonus.”

There was no sarcasm in the answer. Claire turned the brief toward her and pointed to the intended outcome. Viewers should understand that preparing questions, discussing practical needs, and bringing relevant information could help them use an appointment. They should not infer that the featured owner's experience predicted their own animal's treatment.

Eve could work with that. She disliked the tiny disappointment she felt at how little remained to argue with.

“What do you need from me at the interview?” she asked.

“To notice what I won't.”

“That could be expensive.”

“It already has a line in the budget.”

Eve laughed before she could stop herself.

They settled the roles and fee, including the interview and one review session. Claire would obtain the clinic's approval for the final structure, while Eve would confirm the owner's words with the owner. Neither would sign for the other person's consent. They agreed that if Mabel declined, they would find a different approach rather than persuade her until no sounded like yes.

Eve called Mabel after the meeting.

“I've already become educational once,” Mabel said.

“You're allowed to retire.”

“I wasn't asking permission. What is it?”

Eve explained the idea, including who would see the film, what they hoped to ask, and the fact that Mabel could discuss it without agreeing to appear.

“Does Biscuit have to come?”

“No. We can talk without him.”

“Good. He's had a better offer from the sunny patch.”

They arranged an afternoon conversation at Mabel's flat. Claire would attend, but no camera would. Mabel asked for their questions in advance because she preferred to know what kind of nonsense to prepare for.

Eve sent three short prompts, then removed a fourth that asked for her most emotional clinic memory. It did not belong in this project.

On Thursday, Mabel met them at the door with Biscuit standing behind her. The old dog looked at the visitors, considered the distance to his bed, and went back to it.

“A favorable review,” Mabel said. “He didn't bother staying.”

Claire waited while Mabel closed the door. She introduced herself, asked where she should put her coat, and did not crouch toward the dog with a camera-ready voice.

Eve had expected to spend the first five minutes managing Claire's enthusiasm. Instead she found herself holding a notebook with nothing to rescue.

They sat at the kitchen table. Mabel put out three cups, then pointed at the sugar bowl.

“The lid sticks. Don't prove anything.”

Claire lifted it carefully. It stayed shut.

“I'll take it without.”

“Wise.”

Eve began by asking how Mabel prepared for an ordinary appointment. Mabel reached to a shelf and brought down a small notebook with a bent cover.

“I write things when they happen,” she said. “Otherwise I remember the important question halfway home.”

“Would you be comfortable showing a blank example page?” Eve asked. “Not these actual notes.”

“Why blank?”

“Because viewers don't need Biscuit's private details to understand the idea.”

Mabel nodded. “Good. Some of my spelling isn't fit for strangers either.”

Claire smiled but did not interrupt. Eve asked what made an appointment difficult before Mabel entered the consulting room.

“Waiting somewhere I can't sit comfortably. Carrying too much. Trying to keep track of what I've already told someone.”

“Would a film about preparation risk suggesting those are all your responsibility?”

Mabel looked at her more closely.

“Yes,” she said. “It might.”

Eve glanced at Claire. This was the place where she expected resistance: the tidy brief was becoming less tidy. A story about a well-prepared owner could not pretend that access and communication depended entirely on that owner's efficiency.

Claire wrote something down.

“We should include asking the practice what arrangements are available,” she said. “And make clear they vary. Not show Mabel doing all the work to deserve a chair.”

Mabel tapped one finger on the table.

“I like her.”

Eve laughed, then felt embarrassed by the relief inside it.

They talked for another twenty minutes. Mabel did not want her home filmed. She didn't want a sequence of herself struggling to carry a bag, followed by someone kind arriving to save her. She would be willing to demonstrate the blank notebook and explain one thing she wished she'd known earlier.

“Which thing?” Eve asked.

“That asking for something practical isn't the same as complaining about everyone.”

Claire set her pen down. “Would you say it that way on camera?”

“I've just said it that way without one.”

“We can keep it.”

Eve wrote the sentence exactly, marking it for Mabel to check rather than polishing it into a slogan. Beside the table, Biscuit sighed in his sleep. No one turned the sound into a cue to photograph him.

Halfway through the final question, Mabel stood to fetch another notebook. Eve rose automatically to get it for her.

“I know where it is,” Mabel said.

Eve sat down.

Claire looked at her cup, not at Eve's embarrassment. That small courtesy made it harder to keep thinking of her as a threat.

The second notebook contained old shopping lists. Mabel tore out an unused sheet from the back to show how large she preferred the writing. Eve asked whether she could keep the example page. Mabel agreed after drawing a line through an old note about washing powder at the top.

“There. All my secrets protected.”

Before they packed up, Eve asked Mabel to show them how she would place the example notebook if somebody were filming it from the other side of the table.

Mabel pushed it toward the empty chair.

“Like that.”

“The writing is upside down to the camera,” Claire said.

“Then the camera should come round.”

Eve smiled. “It can. That's a useful correction.”

They tried the position without equipment. Claire sat where the lens might be; Eve moved her own cup to stop it blocking the page. Mabel kept the notebook close enough to read comfortably. The result was less symmetrical than Eve's first sketch and much more believable.

“Will people see the question clearly?” Mabel asked.

“We can show a closer view of the blank example,” Claire said. “You don't have to lean across the table to serve the picture.”

Mabel tested the reach again, then nodded.

Eve drew a new little diagram. The first had put the owner opposite the camera, with both hands neatly centered. The second put an ordinary person beside her own notes, able to see what she was talking about. It was a tiny change until she imagined making Mabel repeat the uncomfortable version six times.

Claire looked over the sketch. “Can I use that for the shot plan?”

“Yes. I'll label the positions before I send it.”

“Please do. My memory for left and right becomes theoretical when I'm carrying a tripod.”

Mabel looked between them. “You can both come back when I need the sugar bowl opened.”

“We may need a specialist,” Eve said.

They finished at the agreed time. Mabel wanted to consider the appearance agreement overnight. She wasn't refusing; she also wasn't signing simply because the conversation had gone well. Claire sent the plain version with the intended use and the opportunity to review her portion before release.

At the door, Mabel asked Eve to stay a moment. Claire went ahead to the pavement.

“You were braced for her,” Mabel said.

Eve's fingers tightened on the notebook.

“Was I?”

“I've seen you hold that expression before. Usually when someone is about to tell me what's best for me.”

Eve looked down at the folded example page.

“I may have been.”

“Well, she asked.”

“Yes. She did.”

Mabel didn't demand the reason. Eve was grateful enough that it made her throat uncomfortable.

Outside, Claire stood beside the low wall, checking that her bag was zipped. She looked up as Eve joined her.

“That was useful,” she said. “I would have rushed past the chair.”

“You caught the part about it not all being her responsibility.”

“After you asked the question.”

Eve waited for the compliment to turn into a negotiation for extra work. It didn't.

They walked toward the corner. Claire asked whether Eve thought the story needed Biscuit present at all. Eve said Mabel's objects and words might do the work without asking the dog to endure a filming session.

“I agree,” Claire said. “Let's build that version first.”

At the crossing, she paused.

“One thing. If you think the brief is wrong, say so while we can change it. You don't have to fix it quietly and present me with a better project at the end.”

Eve looked at her.

It was an uncomfortable request because it described something she might actually have done.

“All right,” she said. “And if I ask a question that sounds like a criticism, you can ask which part I mean.”

“That seems manageable.”

They parted without becoming instant friends. Eve still remembered Claire moving the reflector and making Julian laugh. The memory had not become pleasant. It had simply lost the power to explain everything else about her.

At home, Eve placed Mabel's example page on the desk and read it again. Large letters, a crossed-out shopping note, one ordinary question about where to wait.

Her task was to help that question survive the camera.

She opened the shared outline and began with the chair.
