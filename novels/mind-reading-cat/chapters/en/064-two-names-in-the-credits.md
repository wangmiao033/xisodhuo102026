# Chapter 064 — Two Names in the Credits

The first thing Eve saw in the room was a stack of blue chairs.

The second was a man trying to reach the cupboard behind them without moving any of the chairs. He looked at Claire's folded tripod, then at Eve's bag, and gave up pretending they were merely early for a class.

“You're the filming people.”

“We are,” Claire said. “Tuesday morning, the side room. I spoke to the coordinator.”

“I've got you down for nine.”

It was twenty past eight.

Claire glanced at her phone. “Yes. We need to set up before Mabel arrives.”

The man rubbed his forehead. “That's not in the booking.”

Eve put down her bag without opening it.

They had chosen the hall's small side room after Tessa confirmed that the clinic's available space would not suit the owner's interview. The clinic would supply its own separately filmed general guidance. No patients would be displaced to produce a cheerful picture of a waiting room.

Claire had sent photographs of this room on Friday. There was a broad window, a plain wall, and enough space to place a chair without making Mabel climb around equipment. Eve had approved the arrangement. Mabel had read the appearance agreement over the weekend and confirmed what she was willing to discuss.

None of the photographs included the chairs.

“The exercise class finished late yesterday,” the caretaker said. “I can move them. But I'm booked elsewhere until nine, and somebody has to put the room back afterward. You have it until ten.”

Claire went very still.

An hour was enough for the quiet interview they had designed. It was not enough for clearing furniture, setting lights, welcoming Mabel, recording, packing, and restoring the room.

“There's a preparation and clearing charge for filming,” he added. “The coordinator should have sent the sheet.”

“She did,” Claire said after a moment. “I thought it was for shoots needing extra staff.”

“It is. Unless you can use the room exactly as it is.”

Behind him, a blue chair tilted at an angle that made Eve unwilling to test that possibility.

Claire thanked him and asked where he would be for the next few minutes. Then she stepped into the corridor with Eve.

“I missed it,” she said.

Eve had imagined, with considerable embarrassment afterward, how satisfying it would be to discover something Claire had missed. The actual experience involved a caretaker's schedule, an older woman's journey, and a clock that kept moving.

It was not satisfying at all.

“Can we extend the room?” Eve asked.

“There's another group at eleven. Possibly until half past ten.”

“And pay for the clearing?”

Claire looked down at the budget on her phone. “Not without changing something.”

For a moment Eve could see the old solution waiting between them: move the chairs themselves, rush the interview, stay late without counting it, and call the resulting exhaustion teamwork.

She also knew that declining that solution would not create money.

“What can go?” she asked.

Claire opened the shot list. They had planned a separate pickup of bags, notebooks, and an owner arriving outside the hall, with a hired assistant helping carry equipment between locations. The arrival image was decorative. The blank notebook could be filmed on the table already in this room.

“We can lose the exterior setup,” Eve said. “But the assistant is booked. We can't pretend canceling today costs nothing.”

“No.” Claire scrolled. “There's a minimum payment. We'd save the remaining time and transport, not all of it.”

She worked out the difference while Eve rang Mabel.

“We've found a room problem,” Eve said. “We're sorting it out before you leave. Could I call you back in ten minutes?”

“Have you discovered the room is imaginary?”

“No. Too real. It has chairs.”

“Most rooms do.”

“These are ambitious.”

Mabel agreed to wait for the call. Eve did not ask her to sit outside while they solved a production error.

Claire called the clinic contact responsible for approving the film's expenses. Eve could hear only Claire's side: the overlooked charge, the reduced exterior work, the remaining difference. No claim that an unforeseeable disaster had occurred. No suggestion that Eve's requirements had caused it.

The answer was not immediate.

While they waited, Eve took out the printed running order and crossed through the walking shot. It had linked two pieces of speech that no longer belonged together. She drew a new sequence using Mabel's blank example page instead.

“That needs another editorial pass,” she said. “Not a free extra after the one we agreed.”

Claire looked at the page. “Could we use part of your remaining review time for it now?”

“Yes, if the final cut still gets a proper check. Fifteen minutes here, then the balance on the cut. If the structure changes again, we discuss it.”

“Agreed.”

Eve wrote the change next to the time. Claire confirmed it in their project message, along with the expense revision still awaiting approval.

There was no ceremonial handshake. Claire's phone rang before she had put it down.

The clinic approved the small remaining increase and the simpler sequence. The caretaker could give them the extra preparation and clearing time, ending at half past ten, provided they left the access to the cupboard free. Claire confirmed his charge in writing. She called the assistant, explained the change, and kept the agreed minimum payment.

Then she looked at Eve.

“Thank you for catching the part where I was about to volunteer both of us.”

“I was about to accept.”

“That's a terrible system.”

“Very popular.”

The caretaker returned with a trolley. They waited outside the doorway while he moved the stack, instead of surrounding him with helpful bodies. Eve called Mabel with the revised arrival time. Claire adjusted the lights.

By the time Mabel arrived, there was a clear route to the chair and a place for her coat.

She surveyed the room.

“The ambitious chairs lost, then.”

“They've moved on to other work,” Eve said.

Mabel sat down, stood up again, and asked for the chair nearer the wall. The first one pressed against the back of her legs.

Claire changed the framing without a sigh.

Biscuit was at home with Mabel's neighbor checking in, an arrangement Mabel had made herself. There would be no coaxing an old dog into a brighter corner for an illustration of good care.

Eve reviewed the agreed prompts aloud. Mabel could stop or decline a question. The notebook page held invented example headings, not Biscuit's health record. They would show her proposed words in context before the film was released.

“Can we begin before I become a historical document?” Mabel asked.

Claire checked the sound. “Whenever you're ready.”

The first answer came out perfectly polite and completely unusable.

“I find it beneficial to prepare my concerns in advance of attendance,” Mabel said, staring just above Eve's shoulder.

Eve stared back.

Mabel's mouth twitched.

“Was that educational enough?”

“Extremely. I barely recognized you.”

Claire lowered her hand from the camera controls. “We can try again.”

“Please,” Eve said. “Tell me about the question you remember on the way home. Not what you think a sensible owner ought to say.”

Mabel looked down at the notebook.

“I remember things in the wrong order,” she said. “The appointment doesn't make me stop being myself. So now I write a few things before I go, and ask which matter first.”

Eve let the silence last long enough for the thought to finish.

“And if something changes?”

“I tell them. A list isn't a promise that the animal will behave according to the list.”

Claire stayed quiet. Outside, a delivery trolley rattled across the paving, spoiling the end of the answer. They waited for it to pass and asked Mabel whether she wanted to repeat the last thought.

“Will I be paid in another cup of tea?”

“You can have tea without repeating it,” Eve said.

“Then I shall repeat it after tea.”

They stopped the camera.

In the break, Claire showed Eve a frame on the small screen. The notebook's edge led toward Mabel's hands, but a strip of bright window pulled attention away from her face.

“If I move this,” Claire said, touching the edge of a reflector, “we keep her where she's comfortable.”

Eve had been about to suggest moving the chair. She nodded instead.

Claire changed the light, not the person. The frame improved.

Later, Eve caught a question that made Mabel sound as if she managed appointments alone without ever needing assistance. She asked it again without that assumption. Mabel described checking transport before the day, then made them remove the word always from her answer.

“Always is how people catch you out on a bad Tuesday.”

They finished with time to let Mabel gather her belongings unhurriedly. She asked to see the blank page once more, laughed at Claire's illegible sample handwriting, and left with a copy of the agreed review arrangements.

Eve and Claire packed their own equipment. The caretaker restored the room as booked. Nobody had to pretend the additional work had vanished merely because it happened outside the frame.

At the doorway, Claire offered Eve the last wrapped biscuit from the refreshments.

“Negotiation fee?” Eve asked.

“No. Biscuit.”

“Good. My rates are higher.”

They ate standing beside the cases, too tired to make either of them into a lesson.

A trolley wheel caught in the threshold as the caretaker took away the last stack. Claire set down her biscuit wrapper and reached toward the chairs, then stopped when he asked her to leave them alone. He backed the trolley up and changed the angle. The whole stack passed through smoothly.

“Experience,” Eve said after he had gone.

“Mostly listening when somebody says don't touch it,” Claire said.

Eve brushed crumbs from her sleeve. Her phone held a message from Sophie: the curtain they had hung on Sunday was still attached to the wall. A photograph showed it slightly crooked above a perfectly ordinary sink.

Eve sent back a congratulations that contained no advice about straightening it. Then she put the phone away.

Claire opened the draft end card on her phone before they left. Her name appeared beside filming and editing; Eve's appeared beside owner interview and story development. The clinic's contribution and Mabel's participation were identified separately, subject to the agreed final wording.

“Does that describe your work?” Claire asked.

Eve read it twice. Not because she was hunting for another slight, but because the question deserved an answer.

“Yes.”

“Mine too.”

Eve took a photograph of the corrected running order for their records. In the margin, two sets of handwriting crossed out the same unnecessary shot. Neither looked particularly elegant.

Claire lifted the lighter case before Eve could choose which one politeness required.

“Next time,” she said, “I read the room sheet properly.”

“Next time, I ask what is outside the photograph.”

They carried the cases to the door together. Eve still wanted to be good at her work. She still noticed when Claire was better at something.

There was room, she was beginning to find, for both facts without turning either woman into the mistake.
