# Chapter 162 — The Doctor Sits in the Audience

The chair Eve had saved for Julian was the least impressive one in the room.

Its seat was perfectly sound, but a pale scuff ran across the back. She had put it beside the table, where he could reach the object cards without leaning over anyone, then moved it twice because saving a particular seat for her boyfriend seemed faintly embarrassing at a rehearsal she was trying to run professionally.

Robin watched the second move.

“Is it migrating?”

“I was checking the route.”

“The route hasn't changed.”

Eve left the chair alone.

The invitation had been clear: a small opening rehearsal with a few people who had agreed to give feedback. No animals, no recording, and no public drop-ins. Julian was coming after work as an invited participant. He had agreed to try the activities, not to give a talk. Eve had even written that on the schedule.

Unfortunately, she had then told two guests that a veterinarian would be there.

At the time it had seemed relevant and reassuring. Looking at the chair now, she could hear the extra promise she had tucked inside the sentence.

Julian arrived with rain on one shoulder and a paper bag containing his own late snack. He checked that there was somewhere out of the way to leave it, greeted Robin, and asked Eve whether she had eaten.

“Yes.”

He looked at her.

“Something with a plate,” she added.

“Excellent progress.”

She resisted kissing him beside the sign-in sheet. Then she wondered why she was treating a brief kiss as more disruptive than the introduction she had prepared.

“Everyone, this is Julian,” she said when the last guest came in. “He's a vet at the clinic, and he's joining us today.”

Four faces turned toward him with immediate interest.

Eve's stomach gave a small, sinking tug.

A woman standing by the table took out her phone. “Oh, good. There's something I've been wondering about. It won't take a minute.”

Julian glanced at the screen without taking it.

“I can't assess an animal from a quick look at a photograph here,” he said. “If you're concerned, please contact your own clinic so they can ask the right questions and arrange an assessment.”

“I don't need a diagnosis. Just whether it's worth taking him.”

“That would still be advice about his health. I haven't assessed him.”

His tone was courteous. Eve could see the woman's thumb resting on a picture she had probably been worrying over all day. The refusal had not removed her worry; it had merely left it in her hand.

“I'm sorry,” Eve said. “My introduction made this sound different from what we invited you to. Today's a test of an owners' experience session. Julian isn't offering consultations. We won't be discussing individual medical concerns.”

The woman lowered her phone.

“The invitation did say that,” she admitted. “I thought, since he happened to be here—”

“I understand.”

Eve pointed her toward the quiet chair if she wanted to make a private call. There was no need to share the photograph with the group. The woman stepped aside and began looking up her clinic's number. Eve did not watch her screen.

Meanwhile, a man had asked Julian whether the old folding chair would hurt his back during a long presentation.

“I'm not presenting.”

“Oh.”

Robin caught Eve's eye across the table. Their colored cards remained safely inside their bag. On the table they had set a key ring without keys, a cloth shopping bag, a wooden spoon, and a clean empty storage tub. Ordinary things, chosen to invite stories about routines rather than competitive tales of disaster.

Eve took a breath.

“I need to start that introduction again.”

She waited until the woman returned. The call had gone to the clinic's line; she said she would follow their process and keep her phone on. Eve thanked her without asking for the details.

“Robin is hosting,” Eve said. “I'm testing whether this room and the activities work for you. Everyone else is a participant, including Julian. You can talk about your own everyday arrangements, or simply listen. We won't use anyone's animal as a case study.”

“Do I still get to complain about my cupboard?” asked the man who had tested the first session.

“Your cupboard has been specifically invited,” Robin said.

He sat down smiling.

Julian chose the scuffed chair. Eve decided not to tell him how far it had traveled around the room on his behalf. When Robin asked them to look at the objects, he looked at the objects. He didn't scan the group for somebody needing professional reassurance.

Eve found this strangely difficult to believe.

She was accustomed to seeing people turn toward him with questions. She liked his competence; she trusted it. Somehow she had started using the sight of him in a room as reassurance that anything could be handled, including things he had never agreed to handle.

Robin asked each person to choose an object that reminded them of a task they kept forgetting.

The man chose the shopping bag. The woman who had made the call chose the storage tub. Julian picked the key ring.

“Keys?” Robin asked.

“Laundry.”

Eve turned her head.

“I leave the keys by the machine to remind myself to take the clothes out before I leave,” Julian explained. “Then I spend ten minutes looking for my keys.”

The man with the cupboard laughed first.

“You need a better system.”

“I've gathered that.”

Eve pressed her lips together. She had watched Julian carefully weigh decisions at the clinic. She had never pictured him accusing a jacket of concealing his keys while his clean clothes sat three feet away.

“Does it work eventually?” the woman asked.

“It guarantees I remember the laundry while late.”

Robin let the conversation stay there. Nobody needed a lesson about perfect routines. The shopping-bag man admitted he kept buying bags because he forgot the collection already living in his hallway. A guest who had been quiet described tying a bright ribbon around the handle of a bag that needed to go out, then forgetting what the ribbon meant.

When someone suggested that everybody should set reminders on their phone, another said she found too many alerts so irritating that she dismissed them without reading. They compared what actually worked in their own houses, with frequent interruptions from things that didn't.

Eve wrote a note about giving people a place to set their belongings. She stopped before turning their remarks into a neat list of advice. A failed key trick was still a good contribution. It had given the man beside Julian permission to be fallible without performing a catastrophe for the room.

Halfway through, the woman received a message and stepped out to return a call. Robin paused the activity without announcing her reason. Eve opened the side of the chair arrangement so she could leave easily.

When she came back, nobody asked what the clinic had said.

They were trying a short pair activity. One person described where they would put an unfamiliar object at home; the other drew the location from the description. The results were deliberately low stakes. A wooden spoon acquired three drawers and a mysterious “there” floating above a rectangle.

Julian and the cupboard man produced a drawing that appeared to show a shopping bag imprisoned inside a refrigerator.

“Beside it,” Julian said.

“You said next to the cold thing.”

“Which I assumed was the refrigerator.”

“There's a window there.”

Robin leaned over the paper. “I admire the confidence of both architects.”

Eve laughed loudly enough that Julian looked at her with mock injury.

She was still smiling when the cupboard man turned to him and began, “Actually, while I've got you—”

For a moment the room tightened again.

Then he continued, “Do you think this line looks like a door or a radiator?”

“A radiator,” Julian said.

“It was a door.”

“Then I've been very unhelpful.”

They finished with feedback on the activity itself. Had the instructions been clear? Had anybody felt put on the spot? Was the room comfortable enough to stay in for the promised length of time? One guest said the introduction should mention the passing option sooner. Another wanted a firmer surface for drawing. The woman with the phone said she had liked being able to step out without explaining her situation to everyone.

Eve wrote that down word for word after asking permission.

At the end, Julian offered to stack chairs, then stopped when Robin said they had a clearing order and needed people to leave the entrance clear first. He put his own chair where requested and took his paper bag outside while Eve said goodbye to the guests.

No one followed him for an extra consultation.

The woman lingered long enough to say she appreciated the clearer introduction. “I was hoping for a shortcut,” she told Eve. “That wasn't fair to him. But you made it sound a little like there might be one.”

“I did. I won't use that introduction again.”

The woman nodded, apparently satisfied that Eve had heard the unflattering part, and left.

Later, after Robin had completed their paid time and confirmed the next arrangements, Eve found Julian outside under the shallow shelter. He was eating the last of his snack. She locked the door, checking it once rather than three times.

“I borrowed your job to make my rehearsal sound safer,” she said.

“You corrected it.”

“I'd like to correct it before people arrive next time.”

“That would save me from being introduced as a surprise service.”

She winced. He offered her the empty bag.

“Too late to share,” he said.

“I had a plate.”

“An impressive claim. No evidence.”

She leaned her shoulder against his for a moment. The rain had weakened into a mist that caught in the streetlights.

“Did you mind sitting there?” she asked.

“Was I meant to stand?”

“You know what I mean.”

He considered the question while folding the paper bag into a small square.

“I liked having a problem I could be wrong about without anyone getting hurt.”

“The refrigerator prison?”

“The door-radiator was a serious design flaw.”

Eve laughed again. Then she took his hand, this time without wondering whether a passerby would mistake it for part of the event.

They walked toward the crossing. His phone stayed in his pocket, hers inside her bag. She had notes to type, an introduction to rewrite, and chairs that still needed protective feet. He had a shift tomorrow and, she suspected, laundry waiting somewhere inconvenient.

A bus passed, sending a shallow fan of water along the gutter. Julian drew her a step farther from the curb, then resumed complaining about the portrait she had made of his laundry habits. She promised nothing about keeping it out of future private teasing.

At the corner she asked him where he had left his keys.

Julian stopped walking.

For one glorious second she thought she had caught him.

Then he brought them out and jingled them beside her ear.

“Rehearsal feedback,” he said. “Never trust the audience.”
