# Chapter 069 — Don't Call Him to Rescue Me

Her mother's coat had been repaired with the wrong button.

It was close. That seemed to be the problem. The replacement was dark, round, and perfectly capable of keeping the coat shut. It was also shinier than the other four, and once Eve noticed it she could see nothing else.

“They said nobody would know,” her mother said.

“People are usually optimistic about mistakes they don't intend to fix.”

“I told them I knew.”

“Good.”

“They're looking for another one.”

They sat in the café near the station, the coat draped over the empty chair between them. Eve had arrived on time and found her mother already there, examining the disputed button under the light from the window.

The woman behind the counter brought their tea. Her mother moved the coat carefully, then asked Eve whether the chair was too close to the door.

“No. This is fine.”

“You used to hate sitting in a draught.”

“I still do.”

Her mother looked pleased to have retained one accurate fact.

Her mother took a folded shopping list from her bag, checked something, and put it away.

“Have you finished everything else?” Eve asked.

“Not yet. I'm meeting a friend after this.”

“I thought you were going straight back.”

“We might have a look at the bookshop. She wants a present for her sister.”

Eve had already been calculating how to get her onto the earliest train. She stopped.

“What sort of book?”

“Something with no terrible ending. Her sister says she has enough of those on the news.”

“A reasonable specification.”

“I said a cookbook. Apparently that's an accusation.”

Eve laughed. Her mother smiled into her tea, and for a moment they were two people with an afternoon, not a problem and the person attempting to correct it.

For a while they discussed the repair shop. The owner had offered a discount, but her mother wanted the coat she had brought in, not a cheaper version with a different front. Eve listened and said she thought that was reasonable.

Then her mother said, “Owen was very gracious about yesterday.”

The tea had not yet cooled enough to drink. Eve set the cup down again.

“I'm glad.”

“He told his mother there was no need to make a fuss.”

“There isn't.”

“I haven't made a fuss.”

Eve glanced at the coat, whose button seemed to be following the conversation.

“You arranged an introduction without telling me. Then you kept asking me to reconsider after I answered.”

“I asked you to think about it.”

“I did think about it.”

“For one afternoon.”

“And then I told him directly.”

Her mother pressed the edge of a paper napkin flat against the table.

“I'm trying to understand what you're waiting for.”

Eve could have said she wasn't waiting. She had work, friends, a home, and a cat who could make a room feel occupied even when he was asleep behind furniture. But there was also Julian, and the unanswered shape of what she wanted with him.

Her mother's concern was not invented. It was simply arriving as a demand Eve could not satisfy without handing over the decision.

“I don't want to date Owen,” she said. “I don't need to be waiting for somebody better to know that.”

“Better isn't the word I used.”

“No.”

“You liked him well enough.”

“Yes.”

“And he wasn't rude.”

“No.”

“Then what was the harm in another coffee?”

Eve wrapped both hands around the cup. She could feel herself preparing a speech, polished enough to give her mother no opening. The idea was tempting and impossible. Her mother knew more openings than any client she had ever met.

“The harm would be telling him there might be something when I don't want there to be,” she said. “I don't want to spend time with him that way. It's not a punishment.”

Her mother looked out of the window. A bus rolled past, hiding the street for a moment.

“Is this about the veterinarian?”

Eve's fingers went still.

She had mentioned Julian before: the cat's treatment, paid clinic work, an occasional meal. Her mother had apparently kept the references in a more coherent file than Eve had intended.

“What about him?”

“Has he said anything?”

“He says a great many things.”

“Eve.”

She put the cup down.

“No. We haven't agreed to be a couple.”

“Then you could be refusing a good person for somebody who doesn't intend anything.”

The words found their mark. Eve felt Thursday's garden wall become suddenly embarrassing: her careful question, his truthful answer, all the hope she had carried home in a paper takeaway bag.

She could call him. Not necessarily from the table, but later. Ask for a message her mother would understand. A small borrowed certainty, enough to stop Owen's good manners being held up as a debt.

Her phone lay beside the sugar bowl. She turned it facedown.

“I refused Owen because I don't want to date him,” she said. “If Julian isn't interested, that answer stays the same.”

Her mother shook her head slightly.

“You make things harder.”

“Sometimes.”

“Do you think I enjoy worrying?”

“No.”

“Because it can feel as though you're determined to show me that everything I think is foolish.”

Eve looked at her properly. There were lines beside her mother's mouth she did not remember noticing on Saturday, though they could not have appeared in two days. She had been busy defending herself from the arrangement at the table.

“I don't think you're foolish.”

“You don't want a regular job. You don't want to meet the person I suggest. You don't want me to help.”

“I want help I can say yes or no to.”

Her mother made a small, impatient sound. Eve could hear how much that sentence resembled language borrowed from an article. She tried again.

“I wanted your rice. I wanted to see you. I didn't want to arrive and discover that the meal had another purpose.”

“You'd have said no.”

“Perhaps. That would have been an answer too.”

The server returned to ask whether they wanted anything to eat. Her mother declined automatically. Eve ordered a small plate of toast because she was hungry, then asked whether her mother wanted some rather than ordering for her.

“Half,” her mother said after a moment.

They sat quietly until it came.

While Eve spread butter, her mother said, “I remember you calling from that office with a fever. They still wanted you to finish something. You said you couldn't come home because they needed you.”

Eve paused.

“I remember.”

“And now you're on your own. Who tells you to stop?”

The question was different from the ones Eve had prepared to answer.

“Sometimes I have to tell myself,” she said. “Sometimes Sophie notices. I'm trying to get better at it.”

“Trying doesn't pay your rent.”

“No. The work has to do that.”

“Does it?”

“I'm paying my bills. I'm watching the numbers. If it doesn't work, I'll change what I'm doing.”

Her mother looked unconvinced.

Eve wanted to open the account sheet on her phone and lay each payment on the table like a character witness. She stopped herself, not because the numbers were shameful, but because a café argument was a poor substitute for the review she owed her own business.

“I can tell you if I need help,” she said. “I won't ask you to believe everything is guaranteed.”

“You used to say everything was fine at the company.”

“Yes.”

That answer cost more than the rest.

She broke a piece of toast, put it down, and continued.

“I was embarrassed when it wasn't. I didn't want to explain it until I could also explain how I'd fixed it. I understand why that makes this hard to trust.”

Her mother's expression softened a little, but she did not suddenly agree with the whole shape of Eve's life.

“I would sleep better if you had a salary.”

“I know.”

“And somebody dependable.”

“I want dependable people around me too. I don't want to choose one because he might make my finances easier to explain.”

Her mother ate half her toast. Then she asked, more quietly, “Do you like the doctor?”

“Yes.”

The answer surprised Eve by how little decoration it needed.

“Does he know?”

“Not properly.”

“Then you should find out instead of guessing.”

Eve almost laughed. After all that, her mother had reached advice she could actually use.

“I intend to.”

“You could ask him to come next time.”

“When there is something to tell you, I'll tell you. I'm not bringing him to answer questions we haven't answered ourselves.”

Her mother sighed again, but this time she reached for her tea.

Eve checked the time. She had twenty minutes before she needed to leave for home and the editing session. The conversation could consume all of them and still remain unfinished.

“Would you like me to walk back to the tailor with you?” she asked.

“I can manage.”

“I know. I'm offering company.”

Her mother looked at the coat, then at Eve.

“All right.”

They paid, each reaching for the bill before her mother said she had invited neither the café nor the argument and would pay for her own tea. Eve laughed despite herself and paid for the toast she had ordered.

At the repair shop, the owner produced a small tray of buttons. Eve stood beside her mother while she selected one close enough to be worth trying. When the owner addressed Eve as though she might approve the compromise, Eve pointed gently toward her mother.

“It's her coat.”

Her mother chose the less shiny button. The owner said he could fit it later that afternoon.

“I can collect it next week,” she said. “Please call when it's actually ready.”

Outside, Eve helped her fold the receipt into the pocket of her bag. The gesture was small and wanted. It did not decide anything else.

At the station entrance, where her mother was meeting her friend before their bookshop visit, her mother touched her arm.

“I won't invite somebody without telling you again.”

“Thank you.”

“But I still think you're being too quick about Owen.”

Eve nodded, disappointed by the addition and oddly relieved that the promise had not come with an impossible transformation.

“You can think that.”

Her mother frowned, then pulled her into a brief hug.

“Eat something decent tonight.”

“I will.”

Eve watched her join the woman waiting beside the ticket machines. They greeted each other warmly and began discussing where to go first. Eve raised a hand in goodbye, then turned toward home instead of following to supervise.

On the walk, she took out her phone. Julian's conversation was still near the top. She did not ask him to rescue her from the café after the café was over.

She wrote a different message.

*Would you have time for a walk tomorrow evening? I'd like to talk. No cupboard questions this time.*

Then she put the phone away and went home to do the work she had said she had.
