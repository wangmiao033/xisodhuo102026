# Chapter 224 — The Cat Cannot Choose for Me

The strip of paper lay beside the printer, marked with a row of small numbers.

Eve noticed it while collecting her own pages. At first she thought it belonged to a product measurement she had forgotten, and reached to straighten it with the rest. Then she saw the heading.

Ring size.

Her fingers stopped above the table.

Julian had used the printer that morning before leaving for work. He had mentioned needing a page, just as she had mentioned needing the last clean travel cup. Neither announcement had seemed likely to change the shape of her day.

Now the page occupied a great deal of the room.

She put her own printout in its folder and went to the kitchen. She opened the cupboard, forgot what she had intended to take out, and closed it again.

Dumpling watched from the floor.

“Don't start,” she told him.

He rubbed his cheek against the cupboard corner.

“Yes. An excellent contribution.”

They had talked about marriage plainly. She could hardly accuse Julian of introducing the possibility without warning. She had said she wanted it too. They had discussed work and money, difficult family possibilities, and the serious but undecided hope of children later.

A paper ring measure was not a proposal. It did not require an immediate answer, an outfit, or a list of people to notify.

Eve knew all those things.

She still went back to look once, from the doorway.

The paper had not moved.

On the low side table stood a small closed box beside Julian's book. It had been there since yesterday, or perhaps the day before. Eve could not remember noticing its arrival. She had assumed it held something ordinary, which it might still do.

Dumpling jumped onto the table, found that the box occupied part of the surface he wanted, and pushed it with one forepaw.

It slid toward Eve.

She caught it before it reached the edge.

For a moment she stood holding the box with the cat watching her hand. Its lid was closed. There was no invitation written anywhere on it.

“You don't get a vote,” she said softly.

The old temptation would have been to call the coincidence guidance. He had pushed it toward her; perhaps he knew she needed certainty, perhaps the house itself had arranged a clue. But the cat wanted the table. Eve wanted to know what Julian was planning. Those were sufficient explanations for everything she could actually see.

She set the box on a higher shelf, closed and safe from the edge. Then she moved the book to a place Julian could find without searching. Dumpling occupied the cleared table and began washing his chest, entirely satisfied with the practical result.

Eve sent Julian a short message explaining that she had moved his box up because the cat was pushing it off. She did not add a question mark shaped like an accusation.

His reply arrived while she was fastening her bag.

Thanks. I'll put it away properly tonight.

No revelation followed.

She went to work.

At You, Me & Paws, Robin was comparing two versions of the small-session handout. One used a cheerful headline Eve had approved. The other simply explained what participants would do.

“Which?” they asked.

Eve read both. The cheerful one sounded as though an afternoon of clear instructions might redeem a person's entire household.

“The plain one.”

“You're sure?”

“I'm experiencing increased respect for accurate descriptions.”

Robin looked amused but did not ask what had happened at home.

They settled the handout within the allotted review time. Eve checked the upcoming bookings, completed a client edit, and answered a question about access to the venue. The world remained busy with things that neither knew nor needed to know about the paper beside her printer.

That steadied her more than a morning devoted to interpreting it would have done.

At lunch, she called Sophie because they had arranged to speak about Sophie's week. Her friend was between project meetings and had strong opinions about a loading entrance that existed perfectly on the plan and inconveniently behind a locked gate in reality.

Eve listened. She asked how the team had handled it, and Sophie described a revised unloading order that had made one person very cross and everyone else less late.

“Are you listening unusually carefully?” Sophie asked at last.

“I am listening normally.”

“Suspicious.”

Eve laughed.

“We're talking about getting married,” she said when Sophie asked about her own week.

The pause on the line lasted just long enough for Eve to imagine her friend sitting down.

“Talking, or did somebody ask?”

“Talking seriously. Nobody has formally asked yet.”

“And what do you want?”

“I want to marry him.”

Saying it without a qualifying joke made her press her free hand flat against the desk.

Sophie was quiet for a moment.

“Well,” she said. “That's a useful thing to know.”

“I found a ring-sizing printout.”

“Was it addressed to you?”

“No. It was lying near the printer.”

“Then you have learned that a printer works.”

Eve laughed, too loudly for the empty office corner.

“I didn't open anything.”

“Good. You can still tell him what you want without conducting an investigation.”

“I know.”

“Do you want to be surprised?”

Eve considered that.

“A little. Not about whether we agree. About how he asks, perhaps.”

“That sounds compatible with speaking.”

Her friend's next meeting was beginning. They ended the call without planning a wedding or recruiting Sophie to produce one. Eve put her phone away and finished the client message she owed before closing.

On her way home, she passed a small stationery shop. In the window lay sheets of thick colored paper and a display of ordinary bookmarks, several with enormous metallic tassels.

Julian would lose patience with a tassel before reaching the second page.

Eve went inside.

She bought one sheet of cream card, a protective paper sleeve, and nothing designed to imply that affection required an expensive object. At home she took her drawing pencils from the drawer and cleared the kitchen table. Dumpling inspected the paper sleeve; she put it away before he could turn a gift into lunch.

The first sketch was too polished. It showed a book, two cups, and a small window, all arranged with the competence of an advertisement for a life nobody actually lived.

Eve turned the card over.

This time she drew Julian in the enormous cardboard ears, holding a grocery bag with his usual serious face. One ear retained the ridiculous price fragment. His proportions were wrong, but the expression was unmistakably his.

She laughed alone at the table.

Underneath she wrote a short line: You can stop here and come back.

She looked at it for a long time. It could mean a place in a book. It could mean an evening, a conversation, the home in which both of them were still learning to leave things unfinished without fearing that they would disappear.

It did not promise that she would always know the answer for him.

She trimmed the card, rounded the sharp corners, and let the ink dry where the cat could not reach it. Then she put the scissors away and checked the floor for scraps.

By the time Julian came home, the bookmark was in its sleeve inside her personal drawer.

He put down his bag.

“I left something by the printer,” he said.

“Yes.”

“I noticed after I got to work.”

“The printer's discretion is poor.”

He laughed once, looking slightly embarrassed.

“I wasn't arranging for you to find it.”

“I didn't think you were. I saw the heading and left it alone.”

“Thank you.”

Eve leaned against the kitchen counter.

“I don't need to know everything you're planning. But I don't want you to think I'm sitting here waiting to be persuaded.”

His expression grew still.

“I want to marry you,” she said. “I meant that. The things we haven't solved aren't a hidden examination you're failing.”

He came closer without touching her yet.

“I'm glad you said it again.”

“Would you like to go for noodles on Friday? The place we first went to together.”

“Yes.”

“Just dinner. No audience arrangement. No one coming out of the kitchen with a microphone.”

“That wasn't on my list.”

“I am removing it from every possible list.”

He smiled and took her hand.

“Friday,” he said.

She told him the time she could genuinely finish work. He checked his own arrangements before agreeing, rather than making the occasion important enough to ignore them. They would leave Dumpling at home with his ordinary evening needs met, not recruit him as a ceremonial witness in an unfamiliar restaurant.

Eve checked the restaurant's current opening hours rather than trusting the picture she carried of it. Friday dinner was possible. She called during their open period and asked about a table for two. The person answering offered a time slightly later than the one she had imagined.

She covered the phone and checked with Julian. He agreed, so she accepted it and gave the details requested for the booking. She did not mention a special occasion or ask anyone to keep a secret on their behalf.

When she hung up, she changed the time in her own calendar.

“Twenty minutes later,” she said. “Our grand future encounters restaurant capacity.”

“An early test.”

“Fortunately, I believe in eating.”

Julian went to put away the box she had moved. Eve stayed in the kitchen. She heard a drawer close and chose not to discover which one.

When he returned, he held the sizing page.

“Would you mind checking this with me?”

Her face warmed.

“That's very direct.”

“I've been encouraged to use words.”

They checked the printer scale against the page's guide first. The result was not quite right, and the small error made Eve laugh until Julian asked whether she wanted an approximate finger. They decided a proper measurement at a jeweler would be better than trusting a badly scaled printout. A ring could be adjusted or chosen together; neither needed an inaccurate number to make their intention real.

“Are we ruining the surprise?” she asked.

“No,” he said. “I still have to ask.”

She looked at him, suddenly quiet.

“Yes. You do.”

He put the paper down and kissed her.

Later, after dinner, Eve looked once at the bookmark in her drawer. She did not revise the drawing to make Julian more handsome. The slightly unfortunate face beneath the ears was the person she had watched seriously hold somebody's cardboard while wearing them without noticing.

She put the sleeve in the small inside pocket of her bag.

Dumpling had fallen asleep on the side table, now clear of the mysterious box. The cat had obtained exactly what he wanted. Eve had obtained no secret information at all, and felt more certain than she had that morning.

Friday would come because they had chosen a time and said yes to dinner.

Whatever happened after that would require words from them both.
