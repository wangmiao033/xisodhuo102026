# Chapter 179 — An Ordinary Day Before Leaving

The ladder would not fit through the gate sideways.

Eve discovered this before Julian did, mostly because she was at the front and felt the end catch her coat against the latch.

“Stop.”

He stopped immediately.

“Rotate toward you.”

He rotated toward her.

“No, your other toward you.”

“That instruction contains a problem.”

“My coat contains a latch. We all have problems.”

They backed out together. Julian took the weight while Eve freed herself, and the ladder's owner watched from the other side of the gate with the patient expression of a person whose property would eventually come home.

Julian had borrowed the short ladder from a neighbor in his building to reach a high storage shelf. Returning it had become one of those tiny jobs made larger by being postponed. Now it was Saturday, and he had decided not to leave it leaning behind his front door for three weeks.

Eve had offered to help because she wanted to spend the morning with him. She had pictured coffee. She had not pictured negotiating with a gate.

They got the ladder upright, walked it through, and lowered it where the neighbor indicated. No damage. Eve's coat had acquired a pale streak of dust and a minor grievance.

“Thanks for bringing it back,” the neighbor said.

“Thanks for lending it.”

A cat belonging to nobody in the conversation watched from an upstairs windowsill. Eve looked at it and said, “You could have supervised earlier.”

The cat disappeared behind a curtain.

“Management,” Julian said.

They finally got coffee from a bakery, where the last two seats faced a wall displaying a photograph of bread. Eve studied the photograph while he found a place for his knees.

“Do you think we should be doing something more interesting?” she asked.

“Than returning borrowed equipment?”

“Than this.”

He looked at the coffee, the wall, her coat.

“Your morning has already involved light danger.”

She smiled but didn't quite manage to leave the question behind.

She had seen photographs of farewell weekends that looked as if somebody had booked them through a travel company. A coastline. A perfect dinner. Hair that remained beautiful in a wind apparently trained to assist romance.

They had a ladder, and Julian needed to go to the clinic for the final handover. She had a client invoice to send before the end of the day and a laundry load occupying the only rack she owned.

“It's three weeks,” she said to the bread photograph.

“Yes.”

“Then why does everything feel like it should be memorable?”

He turned the paper sleeve around his cup.

“Because we're about to stop doing it for a while?”

That was a more inconvenient answer than telling her she was being silly.

“I don't want you to spend all day entertaining me,” she said.

“I appreciate that. My prepared material is limited.”

He moved his knee out of the way as somebody squeezed past with a tray. Eve drank her coffee and watched a little foam cling to the cup's edge.

“What do you actually want?” he asked.

“Lunch. Maybe a walk. To complain that you're leaving without making you regret going.”

“I think we can manage that.”

He had to be at the clinic soon. They finished their coffee, and Eve followed him as far as reception because she needed to collect the bag containing the sweater he had promised her. He had brought it in that morning before coming to meet her, rather than carry it around the streets.

Tessa looked up from the desk. “The traveler.”

“The ladder has been returned,” Julian said.

“An essential milestone. Your handover room is free.”

Her glance shifted to Eve.

“Only collecting a bag,” Eve said. “I am not joining the meeting.”

“Excellent. We have enough people who think they know what everyone else needs.”

Julian made no effort to defend himself. He fetched the bag from the staff area and gave it to Eve. On top of the sweater sat the borrowed cupboard handles she'd nearly asked about at their dinner.

“I remembered,” he said.

She looked at them and laughed. “I almost made those our first topic at the restaurant.”

“I'd have deserved it.”

Tessa took a ringing call, and Eve stepped aside without being asked. The waiting room smelled faintly of disinfectant and a damp coat. An owner sat with a covered carrier at their feet. Behind a closed door, somebody laughed at something Eve couldn't hear.

This place would go on without Julian. So would her room. Neither fact made his leaving meaningless. It just meant several competent people were not waiting for permission to continue their lives.

“Message when you're done,” she said. “I'll be at the stationery shop, then home.”

He nodded. No promise of a precise finish time.

At the stationery shop Eve bought one roll of plain tape and almost bought a notebook she did not need. It had a small train embossed on the front, which seemed less like coincidence than the shop exploiting her emotional condition.

She put it back.

Instead, at home, she wrote a note on an ordinary card while Dumpling inspected the bag Julian had sent. He sniffed the sweater, placed one front paw on it, and looked toward Eve as if waiting to see whether this interesting soft object might become available.

“Not all for you,” she told him.

She took the sweater out, shook it once, and folded it over the chair. Dumpling climbed onto the seat beneath it. A workable division of resources.

The note took three attempts.

The first sounded as if Julian were crossing an ocean permanently. The second congratulated him in language fit for a staff newsletter. The third said, Ask the obvious question. I want to hear the answer too.

She left enough space beneath it to draw a lopsided carrot.

Then she sent her invoice, hung the laundry, and washed the breakfast plate she'd left in the sink. Nothing collapsed because she spent an hour doing tasks that did not commemorate her boyfriend's departure.

His message came when she was matching socks.

Finished. Hungry. Are we still having noodles?

She put down two socks of different colors and said aloud, “Yes, we certainly are.”

They met outside a small noodle place whose window steamed over whenever the door opened. Julian looked tired in an ordinary, recognizable way. Eve passed him the card before they ordered.

“Don't read it in a ceremonial voice.”

“I wasn't going to.”

He read it silently. His thumb moved over the little carrot, and she watched the corner of his mouth lift.

“Thank you.”

“There's no useful information on the reverse. I've spared you a list.”

He tucked it into his coat pocket separately from the crumpled handover reminder.

They ordered two bowls and a plate of dumplings. Eve realized halfway through her first mouthful that she had been genuinely hungry all morning and calling it anxiety because anxiety sounded more narratively suitable.

“Better?” Julian asked after a while.

“More capable of generosity.”

“Food does that.”

He told her the handover was finished. There had been one appointment that needed more discussion, but it now had a named clinician and a plan in the record. No details about the patient. Tessa had made him check that the appropriate contact arrangements were clear before allowing him to celebrate his own efficiency.

“Did she confiscate your pen?”

“She considered it.”

“Bring a souvenir back for her.”

“Something she can use to threaten me?”

“A ruler. Multifunctional.”

Eve stole one of his dumplings and offered a piece of mushroom in return. He inspected the exchange and accepted it under protest.

Outside, the sky had darkened but held off raining. They walked without choosing an important destination. At one crossing they stood through an entire light cycle because they'd become absorbed in discussing why a shop sold individual dining chairs in colors no table could reasonably accommodate.

Julian pointed out a green one. Eve said it looked like a chair that would charge for overtime.

“Empty corner?” he asked.

“No shopping for the venue. I'm behaving.”

They moved on.

Eventually she took his hand and said the thing she'd been carrying since the bakery.

“I can make a perfectly nice day miserable by checking whether it's nice enough.”

“I noticed you assessing the coffee.”

“The coffee was innocent.”

He squeezed her fingers.

“Tomorrow will probably feel bad in places,” she said. “Even if we do everything right today.”

“Yes.”

“Don't you have anything more reassuring?”

“We can still have lunch tomorrow.”

She laughed, then shook her head. “That actually helps.”

A few doors farther along, Julian paused outside a small hardware shop. He'd remembered that the neighbor's ladder had lost a rubber cap before he borrowed it. The owner knew, but he wanted to check whether a replacement was easy to find.

Inside, a shop assistant asked for a measurement they didn't have. Julian took a photograph of the sizes on the display and bought nothing.

“That was impressively unsuccessful,” Eve said when they came out.

“I can send the options and ask. Someone may already have bought one.”

“Or leave it until you're back.”

“Yes.”

She liked hearing him say that. His absence did not require every loose screw in the neighborhood to be tightened before Sunday. The ladder could stand safely where its owner had put it, and a rubber cap could remain a small unfinished matter rather than a moral failure.

She hooked her arm through his again. He smelled of broth and the soap at the clinic. None of the farewell photographs she'd imagined had included either.

They stopped at a crossing. He looked at her instead of the lights for a moment.

“I'm not trying to leave so well that you won't miss me.”

“I'd object if you were.”

“I think missing you will be unpleasant. I also think I'll be glad I went.”

She nodded. Both things could fit in a sentence without either one apologizing for being there.

They walked back to her building. He came upstairs for a glass of water and to say hello to Dumpling, who had rearranged the sweater over the edge of his chair by methods neither human had witnessed.

Eve retrieved it before it slid to the floor. It smelled faintly of Julian's usual laundry soap. She put it on despite the room being warm enough, and he looked at the cuffs hanging past her hands.

“Very efficient sizing.”

“I'm planning for growth.”

He still had packing to finish at his own flat. She did not ask him to stay and do it at midnight. At the door, though, she kissed him twice because once had seemed inadequate.

“Lunch tomorrow,” she said.

“Lunch, then the station.”

“And don't pack my scissors.”

“I have no interest in your scissors.”

After he went, Eve set the cupboard handles beside her work bag for another day. She fed Dumpling, checked the laundry, and found one of the mismatched socks she had abandoned.

There would be no photograph of the whole day. The coat's dusty streak still needed brushing off. She hung it near the door and decided that, for tonight, it could wait.
