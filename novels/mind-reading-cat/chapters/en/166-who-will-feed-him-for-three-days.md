# Chapter 166 — Who Will Feed Him for Three Days?

The first door sign said PLEASE RING QUIETLY.

Robin held it at arm's length. “A challenge for the conscientious visitor.”

“It's for knocking,” its owner said.

“Then we may have found the difficulty.”

Eve moved the basket of felt-tip pens nearer the middle of the table and enjoyed the sound of people laughing without being encouraged. They had six booked places, five occupied chairs, and one message from a customer running late. Nobody was being filmed. The animals were at home under their usual arrangements; today's session was for the people who wrote things on their behalf.

The room still looked unfinished. The empty corner had acquired a plain chair rather than anything worth photographing. Under the window, a narrow shelf held the materials Eve had already paid for, which made every uncapped pen seem mildly expensive. Yet the route from the entrance stayed clear, Robin knew where the spare paper lived, and somebody had come in saying, “Oh, this is nice,” before Eve had explained what it was supposed to become.

That small, unprompted remark was keeping her upright.

The exercise was simple: make a useful sign for something that visitors regularly misunderstood in your home. Pet sleeping. Please shut this door. Towels here. Robin had brought examples without turning them into rules for everybody.

Mabel had written BISCUIT IS NOT DEAF. HE IS IGNORING YOU.

“Is that useful information?” Eve asked.

“Extremely.”

“Can we be sure?”

Mabel considered the card, then crossed out the first sentence. “There. No unsupported medical claim.”

Eve was still laughing when Mia arrived, caught her sleeve on her bag buckle, and nearly introduced herself to the coat hook instead of Robin.

“Sorry. Sorry. The bus and then my manager—”

“Take a seat,” Robin said. “The pens have stayed available.”

Mia sat beside Mabel. She looked at the blank card in front of her as though it might contain a message she had missed.

“Everything all right?” Eve asked quietly while handing her a pen.

“I've been asked to go away for work. Three days. Next week.”

“That sounds sudden.”

“It is. It's good, actually. They asked me, not the person who usually goes. I said I could probably do it.”

Her face lifted for a moment. Eve recognized the expression: the pleasure of being chosen arriving slightly before the calculation of what choosing back would cost.

“Congratulations,” she said.

Then Mia looked at the word DOOR on Robin's sample and asked, “How do you tell someone everything they need to know about your dog without sounding insane?”

A man across the table raised his marker. “How much time do you have?”

Mia gave a short laugh, but her hand had stopped moving.

Her ordinary care arrangements had taken months to settle. Eve knew there were limited visits, paid help, housemates with lives of their own, and a plan that had been adjusted with appropriate advice. A three-day trip was a different shape from the hours they had worked so hard to cover.

“Have you arranged the care yet?” Eve asked.

“Not the whole thing. I'm checking. I thought Leah might be there for some of it.”

“Has she said?”

“Not yet.”

Mia rubbed a thumb over the pen cap. “I know. I'm asking her. I just got off the phone.”

Eve wished she had left a breath between those questions. Mia had come carrying news, not an application to be judged.

“You don't have to solve it in front of us,” she said.

“I'm not sure I could solve it in private either.” Mia looked around the table. “Does anyone else have instructions? Actual ones? Not just a message saying, 'It's where it usually is'?”

Mabel opened her handbag.

“Of course.”

The speed with which she produced a folded sheet made Eve suspect the question had been waiting for her all morning.

Mabel unfolded it carefully. Lines ran between boxes, circles, a drawing of something with four legs, and an arrow that disappeared beneath another fold. There were several capital letters. One word had been underlined three times.

Robin leaned forward. “Is this a household, or are we recovering buried silver?”

“That's the kitchen,” Mabel said.

“Which bit?”

“The kitchen-shaped bit.”

The table laughed. Mabel smiled, pleased rather than offended, and turned the page toward Mia.

“I wrote it when my sister visited. She understood it.”

“Does your sister know your kitchen?” Eve asked.

“She helped me paint it.”

“Possibly an advantage.”

Mia traced a line without touching the page. “What does the star mean?”

“Don't use that.”

“Don't use what?”

“The cupboard.”

“For anything?”

“For the dog things.”

Robin slid a clean sheet beside the map. “Would anybody object if we tried something different with the last half of today? We can finish signs, or we can see what happens when someone else reads instructions for an ordinary household task. No animals involved, no actual feeding here.”

They waited for answers. One customer wanted to finish the sign for her balcony door. Robin said she could keep doing that and join only if she wished. The others were interested. Eve watched them make the change without apologizing for the original exercise or presenting the new one as a transformation of the entire business.

“I can put the paid session's spare materials to a useful purpose,” she said, gathering clean paper.

“Try saying that more warmly,” Robin murmured.

Eve set the paper down. “Please draw your confusing cupboards.”

“Much better.”

They paired people who did not share a home. Each person wrote directions for finding a harmless object: a spare towel, a scoop, an empty bowl. Addresses, codes, and keys stayed out of the exercise. If a detail belonged to an actual person's care arrangement, it stayed on that person's own page.

Mia wrote: Blue tub by the little shelf.

The man opposite drew a shelf above a sink.

“No,” Mia said. “The one near the back.”

“The back of the kitchen?”

“The back door.”

“I didn't know we had a back door.”

“We don't. It's what Leah calls the door to the utility space.”

He added a door with a question mark.

Eve could have explained the purpose again. Instead she sat down with a marker and wrote the instructions she would have given for Dumpling's spare blanket.

In the drawer.

She stared at the sentence.

Her flat contained more than one drawer. Several held things Dumpling would happily sleep on. The drawer that had become his at the beginning was obvious to her because she had lived every day since she gave it to him. Nobody reading four words on a page received those days along with them.

She crossed the sentence out and began again.

Mabel was making impatient noises beside Mia.

“The round tin. I've written round.”

“A lot of tins are round.”

“The red one.”

“You've drawn it in black.”

“I didn't have a red pen.”

Mia looked at the generous basket between them. “History has been unkind to this document.”

Mabel laughed so abruptly that her glasses slipped. She pushed them back up and borrowed a red marker.

By the end, the table held several less beautiful signs and a considerable number of arrows that had lost their destinations. Eve's favorite was a picture of a cupboard with an enormous eye drawn on its door. Its author had meant “at eye level.” The partner had understood “look inside.” Both agreed that neither interpretation helped a shorter visitor.

Mia had stopped clutching her phone.

“I can write the feeding routine,” she said. “Amounts from the existing plan, where the supplies are, the contact details. But writing it doesn't tell me who can actually be there.”

“No,” Mabel agreed. “And some of us can't do the stairs in other people's houses just because we like the dog.”

The words were practical, without any invitation to reassure her. Eve wrote a small note for the session feedback: try the task, not just the sentence.

Mabel tapped her map. “You could try this at mine tomorrow. Find the empty bowl and the things. While I'm there, obviously. Biscuit can be with his usual help for that little while so we aren't all tripping over him.”

Mia looked at Eve. “Would you come?”

Eve almost said yes without checking. Her afternoon contained a client revision she had already promised.

“I have half an hour after lunch. If that works for Mabel.”

“It does. I don't require an expedition.”

They made the arrangement. It was a private visit between willing people, not a new care service Eve was selling. Robin packed the cards while the guests finished talking. The woman with the balcony sign had written PLEASE CHECK THE LATCH, then drawn the latch itself so nobody had to guess which part she meant.

She held it up. “I feel I've attended a surprisingly serious craft class.”

“You may still add flowers,” Robin said.

She added one very severe flower.

After everyone left, Eve and Robin counted the reusable materials and recorded what they would change for another session. More room for drawings. A plain description of what the activity did and did not cover. An option to bring a harmless empty container next time, instead of believing people could explain a whole kitchen from memory.

“You looked worried when we moved away from the signs,” Robin said.

“I printed six pages of examples.”

“We used two.”

“I had developed an emotional attachment to the other four.”

“They can come to a future session.”

Eve laughed and put the unused pages in a folder, rather than in the bin. The afternoon had not followed the photographs in her head. The participants had done something she could describe clearly afterward, which seemed better than an attractive table with nothing changed around it.

At home, Dumpling greeted her from the very drawer she had failed to identify on paper.

“Yes,” she told him. “You know where you live.”

He climbed out, stretched, and followed her toward his ordinary dinner. She checked the supplies she would need over the next few days, noticing how many labels faced the wall because she recognized the packets by touch.

Her phone chimed while she was putting them straight.

Mia had messaged: Asked Leah properly. Waiting for her reply. Also contacted the carer about different cover, not assuming the usual visits stretch.

Eve sent a brief acknowledgment, then a photograph of her own discarded four-word instruction. The image contained no address or access detail, only the absurd confidence of IN THE DRAWER.

Mia replied with laughing faces.

A second message followed.

I really do want to go on this trip.

Eve looked at that sentence longer than the others.

Then she wrote: I hope you can make an arrangement that works.

She didn't offer her own three days. She had not got three days to give.

Dumpling pressed his head beneath her empty hand. Eve put the phone down and scratched the place he liked, while the drawer remained open behind them, perfectly understandable to its current resident.
