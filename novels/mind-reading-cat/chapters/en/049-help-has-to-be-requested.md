# Chapter 049 — Help Has to Be Requested

Sophie read the proposed duties while Dumpling examined the outside of her empty shoe.

“It's been to a garden shop,” she told him. “You're welcome.”

He pressed his nose to the sole, sneezed, and withdrew beneath the table.

It was Tuesday morning. Eve had set out two cups but kept the work papers on one side of the table, leaving room for breakfast. Sophie had agreed to look at the paid role, not yet to take it. Her own employer still owed her an answer after Monday's meeting. She was keeping that discussion separate from this one.

“Ten until twelve,” Sophie said. “And this preparation meeting is paid.”

“Yes. Half an hour at the rate you sent.”

“I'm handling arrivals, the six-person limit, materials, and closing the table. You're staying for both discussions.”

“Yes.”

“If the coordinator asks for another round?”

“We've agreed there are two.”

“That wasn't my question.”

Eve looked at the last line of the duty sheet. It said *Changes referred to Eve*.

She had included it because she was responsible to the client. Reading it now, she could also see a familiar bottleneck waiting to happen.

“You can refuse anything that changes the hours or exceeds the places,” she said. “You don't have to fetch me out of a discussion to ask whether the agreement still applies.”

Sophie nodded. “Put that in.”

Eve did. They added the coordinator's number, the venue's responsibility for the main entrance, and the fact that Sophie wouldn't be supervising anyone's pet. The discussion was for people bringing objects and ideas, not animals.

“What happens if it is cancelled?” Sophie asked.

They settled the cancellation payment before talking about anything more cheerful. Eve sent the revised terms and transferred the agreed booking portion of the fee. Sophie checked her phone, confirmed receipt, and accepted the role.

The sequence felt slower than putting a name in a box. It also left Eve with somebody who had actually agreed to arrive.

Once the half hour ended, she closed the document.

“Do you have time to finish breakfast as my friend?”

“Ten minutes. My friend has better bread than most clients.”

They ate. Sophie showed her a photograph of the mint, which had developed one leaf at an angle she considered ambitious. She didn't mention Monday's conversation again, and Eve didn't make breakfast into a follow-up meeting.

That afternoon, the hall coordinator confirmed the final proposal. The printed notice described two small sessions, six places each, with no filming of visitors. Eve checked the details before approving it.

Julian's message arrived while she was packing the first set of cards.

*Talked to Dr. Clark. Tuesday finish is six. Saturday afternoon remains off. Coverage is on the rota, not a possibility I'm supposed to keep open.*

She read it twice, pleased by something less glamorous than a heroic offer.

*How was asking?*

*Uncomfortable. Shorter than staying late.*

Eve smiled.

*I'm glad you asked.*

He didn't offer to attend the hall or help carry boxes. She didn't add him to anything.

By Saturday morning, the short shoot at Pocket Path was finished and the unedited files were waiting for their scheduled Monday session. Felix had his cue card. Eve had her own notes. No camera needed to travel between the two events.

She left Dumpling at home with his ordinary arrangements and the bedroom door open to his quiet corner. The bag he had tried to occupy contained no interesting animal smells, only card, pens, and a folded tablecloth. She let him inspect the empty space afterward, then left on time.

At the hall, Sophie arrived at ten exactly, carrying a water bottle and no expression of emergency.

“Where do you want the sign?” she asked.

Eve showed her the end of the table.

Sophie put it there, walked to the main entrance, and came back.

“Can't read it from the route people are taking. I'll turn it toward the door.”

“Fine.”

A surprisingly large part of Eve wanted to explain why she had chosen the original angle. She kept unpacking instead.

They set six chairs in a shallow curve. Eve placed three objects on the table: a folding bowl, a small pouch, and a card with the location of a public bench, checked that week. These were conversation starters, not recommendations for every animal or every household.

The coordinator came over with the printed attendance sheet.

“Five for the first round, four for the second,” she said. “A little less than we hoped.”

Eve felt the familiar urge to apologize for empty chairs.

“That's enough for everyone to speak,” Sophie said, checking the names against the times.

She didn't say it brightly. It was simply true.

Mabel arrived at ten twenty-five, alone, with a zippered pouch that looked older than Eve's camera.

“Biscuit has had his morning walk,” she said, taking the chair nearest the clear passage. “He's asleep. I thought I'd come while we both had a sensible schedule.”

“Would you like tea?” Eve asked.

“Later. First I want to know why everyone puts the thing they need at the bottom of the bag.”

It was an excellent opening question.

The other four participants included a man who had brought an empty water bottle, a woman with a folded rain cover, and two neighbors who had arrived together but disagreed immediately about whether a pocket could be too large.

Before they began, one of the neighbors held up her phone.

“Could I record the suggestions? I forget things by the time I get home.”

Eve glanced at the others. Mabel had gone still over the open pouch. The man with the bottle was looking at the sign that promised no filming.

“We're keeping the discussion off camera,” Eve said. “I'll collect the suggestions people choose to share in the summary. You can also write yours down.”

The woman hesitated. “My handwriting is awful.”

“Mine too,” Sophie said. “Would you like a larger card?”

She brought one over with a thick pen. The woman tried the pen, found that the letters remained distinguishable, and put her phone away. Eve waited until she was ready before starting. Nobody had to explain a private reason for not wanting to appear in a stranger's recording.

Eve began by showing the folding bowl.

“This isn't a test of who packs properly,” she said. “Tell us about one thing that made an ordinary outing easier. It can be a thing you stopped carrying.”

Mabel unzipped her pouch and took out a small cloth.

“For the seat,” she said. “Not for the dog. People think a bench is usable because it exists. Sometimes it's wet.”

Eve laid a blank card beside her. “Would you like that included?”

“Yes. But don't write that I carry a towel for every bench in the district.”

“What wording would you prefer?”

Mabel considered, then dictated a sentence about checking the actual condition of a place to rest. Eve wrote it down and turned the card for her to read.

Across the table, the man with the bottle held it up.

“I used to remember this and forget to fill it.”

One of the neighbors laughed, then confessed she had carried a spare lead for three months without noticing that its clip had jammed.

The conversation became less polished and more useful. Eve had prepared a question about organizing compartments, but when the woman with the rain cover described not having a free hand to open her own bag, Eve followed that instead.

They tried opening the pouch with one hand. Nobody needed to imitate handling a dog. Holding a mug was enough to expose the difficult zip.

Mabel watched Eve struggle.

“Now imagine you're also annoyed,” she said.

“I may not need to imagine.”

The group laughed. Sophie moved the spare cards closer without interrupting, then quietly asked two early arrivals to wait by the exchange table rather than stand behind the seated participants.

Eve didn't have to leave her question half-finished.

At ten fifty-five, she thanked the group and explained what would happen to the cards. One neighbor took hers home. The others checked the wording before leaving it. Mabel corrected *always* to *usually*, then underlined the change.

“You've met a few exceptions?” Eve asked.

“I've met a few people.”

Between rounds, Eve drank water and replaced the used pens. Sophie counted the clean cards and asked whether she wanted the table moved an inch away from the window glare.

“Please.”

They moved it together. Nothing else required redesigning.

The second round began on time. It had a different energy: fewer jokes, longer pauses, one participant who preferred drawing to speaking. Eve waited while he sketched the side entrance of a shop where he could stand out of the main queue. She asked whether the location was public and current before allowing it onto the possible-summary pile. He wasn't sure about the current opening hours, so they kept only his general suggestion about checking where to wait.

Halfway through, two more visitors approached. Eve saw Sophie speak to them, point to the notice, and offer blank cards. One shook her head and walked away. The other stayed at a neighboring table.

Eve nearly broke off to explain. Sophie caught her eye and gave a small, level nod. The arrivals were being handled. She could continue listening to the person in front of her.

At eleven forty, the second group finished. The coordinator returned while they were clearing the chairs.

“Could you do one more at twelve fifteen? A few people have asked.”

Eve opened her mouth.

“We finish at twelve,” Sophie said. “The two rounds are complete. We can leave the approved information sheet for the remaining exchange hours.”

The coordinator looked toward Eve.

She felt the pull of the people who had missed out, the unused chair, the possibility of being described as helpful.

“Yes,” Eve said. “That's what we agreed. Another session would need another booking.”

“All right. Leave me the contact details.”

There was disappointment in her voice, but no catastrophe followed it. She went to help somebody carry a box of books.

They photographed the empty table, packed the cards, and returned the chairs. At eleven fifty-six, Sophie checked the floor for dropped pens. At twelve, her part was done.

Eve paid the remaining fee and confirmed it against the agreed hours. The amount came out of the project's income exactly as the budget said it would. She earned less than in the fantasy where friendship covered the middle for free.

She also had a completed event and a friend who was leaving when she expected to leave.

Before leaving, Eve stopped at the exchange table. Beneath a stack of magazines was a small jigsaw box showing a seaside town. Someone had written *probably* over the promised number of pieces.

“Do you have time for that?” Sophie asked.

“Not as a deliverable.”

The volunteer offered a bag. Eve declined, tucked the box beneath her arm, and left the larger puzzle beside it for somebody else.

Outside, Sophie asked, “Lunch?”

“Only if you want lunch. It isn't the balance of your invoice.”

“I know. You already paid that.”

Eve laughed and picked up her bag.

They found a table at the bakery. Sophie ordered something hot and opened her gardening book while they waited. Eve sent the coordinator the promised confirmation of the summary delivery, then put her phone away.

Across the room, a clock moved past twelve fifteen.

Nobody at their table stood up.
