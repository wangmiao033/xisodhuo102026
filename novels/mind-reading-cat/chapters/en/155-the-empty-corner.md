# Chapter 155 — The Empty Corner

The cabinet would fit if Eve used the space she had promised not to use.

She held the tape measure against the wall, checked the figure, and checked it again. The supplier had a narrow unit available at a reduced price because the display finish differed slightly from the current range. It would provide a counter, closed storage, and somewhere for the drinks setup she had imagined offering between activities.

It would also fill most of the quiet corner.

On the phone, the supplier waited.

“Do you want me to hold it until this afternoon?”

Eve looked at the penciled rectangle on the floor plan. She could make it fit by removing one chair, shifting the remaining chair closer to the activity table, and deciding that anyone who wanted more distance could use the porch.

The porch was not hers to promise. It was also outdoors.

“No,” she said. “Please let it go.”

“Are the dimensions wrong?”

“They're right. That's the problem.”

After the call ended, Eve let the tape slide slowly into its case.

The room had become more expensive through subtraction.

Without the sofa, the permanent photography set, and the extra service points, she had fewer things to buy immediately. But the usable activity area was smaller than her first forecast assumed, and appropriate movable furniture cost more than the cheap fixed pieces she kept finding. Cleaning, safe storage, the access work still under discussion, and professional checks did not become cheaper because the room looked plain.

The drinks corner had been the part she could picture most easily. A warm cup gave people something to do with their hands. A small counter would make the place seem finished. In the first drawing, it looked like hospitality rather than another service she would have to supply, clean, and pay for.

She opened her working budget on the temporary table.

There were two columns now: required before regular use, and possible later. Even the first column depended on the landlord agreeing to the eventual arrangement. No final lease had been signed, no permanent work commissioned, and Eve still arrived only during booked access periods.

She moved the fitted drinks area into the later column.

The total fell far enough to leave room for the necessary items. It did not leave enough to feel carefree.

Eve wrote a note beneath it: no drink sales assumed in initial income.

That was the part she disliked. It was easy to defer a purchase while leaving the money it was supposed to earn in another calculation. She removed that money too.

Then she closed the budget before she could bargain with herself in a third column.

The quiet corner sat between the side-room wall and a part of the main room less exposed to the entrance. It could not make someone invisible, and it would not suit every person or animal. But with the movable screen positioned carefully, it allowed a little distance without shutting someone away. A chair could face the room or turn slightly aside. The route back out stayed clear.

For animal-appropriate visits, a household could have space there away from others, with its own familiar carrier or mat and the owner staying responsible. For human-only sessions, someone could pause without performing interest at the table. Eve had no intention of calling it a treatment room or promising calm on demand.

She was trying to preserve somewhere a person could be without being asked to buy or contribute.

She put her own bag in the corner and sat beside it.

From there, the unfinished room looked less like a photograph and more like a place someone might spend an hour. The welcome table was visible. So was the door. She could hear footsteps outside without sitting directly in their path.

She remained seated for five minutes.

At first she kept identifying improvements. A picture on that wall. A shelf above the chair. A plant to soften the edge of the screen. By the fourth minute she was simply aware that her shoulders had dropped.

The corner did not need her to finish it while sitting in it.

A knock at the front window interrupted her.

It was the center coordinator, who had agreed to stop briefly on her way back from an errand. Eve opened the door and offered the spare chair.

“I can only stay a few minutes,” the coordinator said.

“That's fine. I'm testing whether a few minutes can exist here.”

The coordinator looked around, amused. “Has it been difficult?”

“Surprisingly.”

She set down her shopping bag and sat in the corner. Eve started to explain the revised layout, then noticed the woman taking a slow breath and looking at nothing in particular.

Eve returned to the main table and sorted the pencils instead.

After a little while the coordinator said, “This is a useful chair.”

“The chair itself is borrowed.”

“I meant where it is.”

Eve came back with the plan only when the woman reached for it.

They discussed the transition from the center's borrowed room to these limited tests. Eve's existing center booking remained in place. If the unit did not work out, she would still need venues, schedules, and people who knew she finished when she said she would. The coordinator had no spare regular slot to promise, but she could tell Eve when the next calendar review happened.

Eve wrote the date down.

“Are you selling refreshments?” the coordinator asked, seeing the crossed-out cabinet.

“Not at the start. I was going to.”

“Then say so in the activity details. People hear community space and invent their own cup of tea.”

“I've recently discovered they can invent an entire cat cafe.”

The coordinator laughed, then looked at the plan more closely.

“Will someone need to book this chair?”

“Not separately. It isn't another product.”

“Good. Although you'll need to make clear when the room itself is open.”

Eve nodded. An empty corner could not turn her temporary access slots into all-day public hospitality. She added clear availability information to the list for later, beside the schedule she still had to negotiate.

When the coordinator left, she took her bag without having to ask Eve to move anything.

It was a small test. Eve wrote it down.

Julian arrived near the end of the afternoon carrying a paper bag that seemed too light for its size.

“Is this a business visit?” Eve asked.

“No. You said I could come before we went to eat.”

“I did. I've spent so long measuring things that I wanted to check.”

He kissed her, then looked at the tape measure in her hand.

“Am I the right width?”

“Your delivery packaging is excessive.”

He took a flowerpot out of the bag.

It was small, unglazed, and empty. A saucer rested beneath it. There was no plant label, gift ribbon, or soil waiting to spill across the temporary table.

Eve looked from the pot to him.

“Is there more coming?”

“No.”

“Have you killed the gift before giving it to me?”

“I bought it empty.”

“That was wise of the shop.”

He set it on the table, away from the edge.

“You said you kept wanting to fill every gap.”

She remembered saying it after Sophie's visit, half laughing and half embarrassed by how much of the floor plan had disappeared.

“So you brought another gap.”

“A portable one.”

Eve ran one finger around the rim. The clay felt slightly rough beneath her skin.

“Does it come with an instruction?”

“No. You can leave it empty.”

She liked it immediately, which was inconvenient for the joke she had been preparing.

“Thank you.”

Julian smiled. “You're welcome.”

She showed him the corner and the cabinet drawing she had crossed out. He sat in the chair she offered and looked toward the entrance.

“The drinks part has gone?”

“For now. The cabinet would fit only if I moved this chair into the busy area. And if I keep this space, the remaining setup budget needs to go toward things that make the actual activities workable.”

“Do you mind?”

“Yes.”

He waited.

“I imagined being able to hand someone a cup and have the place feel finished. Instead I'm going to hand them directions to the place nearby that actually sells drinks.”

“That could be a good cup.”

“It probably will be. They're better at it.”

She sat in the other chair and let herself be disappointed without reopening the supplier's number.

The loss was not enormous. It was one attractive part of an early plan. But she had imagined herself behind that counter, competent and welcoming, belonging in a business that looked like a business. An empty corner offered very little evidence for that picture.

Julian moved his foot so she could turn her chair without catching it.

They sat quietly for a moment.

Then Eve said, “The coordinator sat here today. She didn't want anything for about three minutes.”

“Did you give her anything?”

“I almost gave her a floor-plan explanation.”

“Dangerous.”

“I restrained myself.”

He laughed, and the room sounded different with laughter in that part of it. Less hollow than it had while she walked back and forth measuring.

Before they left, Eve photographed the pot on the temporary table for herself. She did not place it in the empty corner like a claim on the space. It would go home with her until there was a settled place for it.

She packed her notebook and checked the room against the access agreement. Removable items remained only where storage had been permitted. Equipment went into her bag. Nothing obstructed the door or the shared passage outside.

The landlord came at the arranged closing time, and Eve showed her the revised sketch. The quiet corner had no counter, shelf unit, or extra seat hidden inside its measurements.

“This is being left unused?” the landlord asked.

“Available,” Eve said. Then, because the word sounded like advertising, she explained. “For waiting or stepping out of an activity. I don't want the only options to be joining in or leaving.”

The landlord considered the drawing. She had further questions about regular hours and how activities would work alongside the other occupants. Eve noted them for their next discussion rather than trying to finish the whole arrangement in the doorway.

There was still plenty to settle.

Outside, Julian took the light paper bag while Eve adjusted the heavier strap on her shoulder. He did not take the folder from her arm.

At the corner, she glanced back at the darkened unit. Through the window she could see the chair and the clear patch beside it. The room looked unfinished because it was.

“Where do you want to eat?” Julian asked.

Eve named the small place they had passed on the way to lunch the week before. It had looked crowded, noisy, and entirely free of decisions she was responsible for.

They walked there together. The empty pot swung gently in its bag, requiring nothing of either of them yet.
