# Chapter 238 — The New Doctor at the Clinic Door

The new doctor opened the door for Eve with the heel of a shoe. Both her hands were occupied with a cardboard box that said BISCUITS in thick black marker.

“I'm looking for Julian,” Eve said.

“He's looking for me. This solves half my problem.”

She stepped backward to let Eve in. In the box, under a clean tea towel, were the two mugs Julian kept at work and the little plant he'd been claiming for months was about to recover.

“Are those his?”

“If he takes the plant home, yes.”

The doctor put the box on the end of the reception counter before introducing herself as Dr. Patel. Eve knew her name from Julian, who had been describing someone thoughtful, quick with the records system, and perhaps too optimistic about what could fit into the first afternoon. From the way Dr. Patel went back to straighten the box before doing anything else, Eve suspected they had each been saying roughly the same things about the other.

Eve had come with the flat shoes Julian had left by the door. They were going to walk from the ceremony room to the restaurant once more in shoes they could actually wear. Her mother had asked three separate questions about the walk, none of which were about the distance. Eve had begun to think that her mother wanted a role between the two buildings and wasn't sure how to ask for one.

Julian appeared at the far end of the counter with Dr. Clark and said, “I only need another fifteen minutes.”

Dr. Clark looked at him. “You don't have fifteen minutes.”

“I thought the room was free.”

“It is. You said that twenty minutes ago.”

“I haven't shown her the comments view.”

“I've used the comments view,” said Dr. Patel. “You watched me use it on Tuesday.”

Eve put the bag with the shoes under the bench. Julian had the look he got when he intended to keep a promise, but everything else in the world inconveniently also required him to keep a promise. She had more patience for it when she didn't have to be the one holding the shoes.

“I'm not here to hurry you,” she said. “But I'm also not going to do a third walk after today.”

“Fair.”

He went back through the staff door with the other two. Eve took a seat by the window and looked down at the toes of her own shoes, which already bore the scuff from the last time she'd tried one particular stretch of paving. She made a note to tell her mother about the bench halfway there, not because anyone had to stop but because knowing one could often made the whole walk easier.

A man across from her held a folded blanket on his knees. From underneath came a small, irritable movement. He rubbed the corner of the blanket between his fingers until he appeared to notice himself doing it.

“Is Julian here today?” he asked the receptionist.

“He's finishing up. Dr. Patel is seeing afternoon appointments.”

“Oh. I thought it was him all week.”

The receptionist checked the booking, then named the appointment time and asked if he wanted to talk about changing it. The man looked at the wall clock. Eve could see him calculating something, though she could not tell whether it was a parking limit or the last time he'd been able to take off work.

“I don't have to explain everything again, do I?”

“Dr. Patel has the notes. If there's something especially important you want her to know, you can say so.”

The blanket pushed upward. A pale snout appeared and was withdrawn with a faint offended noise. The man loosened his hold.

“She's been all right about coming in since he did it,” he said. “It's getting her back out again. That waits until the room empties. People always think it's coming in she's frightened of.”

Eve knew what it felt like to hear the wrong half of a problem solved. The man did not ask her for help, so she kept her attention on the window while the receptionist checked which room was available and said she would pass that detail on. A delivery cyclist outside made three attempts to fasten a buckle and then took off a glove.

When Julian came back to reception he had his coat over one arm. Dr. Patel was beside him, waiting for the printer. The man stood, then sat down again when he heard the dog shifting. Dr. Patel's gaze followed the movement.

“I'm Dr. Patel,” she said, stopping well back from the blanket. “I've read Julian's notes. Would you like to tell me which way you usually get her out of the building? We can talk in the room first, if that's easier.”

The man looked directly at Julian.

There was a very small pause. Eve realized she was waiting, too, for Julian to decide how much of the conversation he needed to take over in order to make everybody comfortable.

“Dr. Patel has your case today,” he said. “We went through the record together. If you'd rather change the appointment, reception can help you do that.”

The man looked down, pushed an end of the blanket away from the dog's nose, and asked Dr. Patel whether the side exit was ever used. She said she'd check who needed it that afternoon and let him know before they brought the dog back out. He followed her at his own pace, with the small feet just visible beneath the corner of the cloth.

Julian had picked up the box. Eve watched him put it down again.

“Does she need the comments view?” she asked.

“No.”

“Do you need the comments view?”

He studied her expression. Then he deliberately turned the word BISCUITS to face the wall. “It's where we used to bury things we didn't know who should deal with.”

“Ah.”

“We don't use it like that now.” He picked up the box again. “Sometimes a really persuasive part of my brain tells me nobody will be able to find something unless I point to it from a distance.”

Eve took the shoes out from under the bench. “Yes, I've encountered that department.”

Before they reached the exit the receptionist called his name. On the counter lay an old laminated card with his direct clinic extension on it, a reminder for the staff who were accustomed to putting certain callers through to him.

“Should I leave this up?” she asked him.

Julian set the box at his feet. “No. Put the new duty rota there.”

“You're sure about the repeat callers?”

“Offer them the duty clinician. If they specifically want to wait to see me, give them the next available slot and don't promise I can be reached at home.”

She removed the card. Behind it the counter looked fractionally less yellow. Julian bent to collect his box and allowed himself to be outpaced to the door.

Outside, Eve offered to carry the plant. He declined on the grounds that whatever life there was in it should spend the next few hours getting used to his company. They walked slowly because he had put one of the mugs against a corner and it clinked with each step. At the crossing he stopped to tuck the tea towel between them. Some of the cotton smelled strongly of the clinic laundry detergent.

“Is this the first time she's had the afternoon to herself?” Eve asked.

“It's the first full one with her name against it. Dr. Clark is on site if she wants a second opinion. We made sure the follow-ups, call backs, and things awaiting results were assigned by name. No one is covering by reading my mind.”

“How will she know when something comes in?”

“In the usual way. The staff will tell her.”

She gave him a look.

“Yes,” he said. “I heard it.”

They walked past a bakery putting out its second tray of rolls. The glass was warm when Eve tapped it to point out a particularly misshapen one. The baker saw, raised her tongs, and turned the roll so its worst side faced the street. Eve bought it for the walk. Julian found the other half of the roll pushed into the space between his hand and the box.

He ate it slowly. “It's a good thing for the clinic, having someone who does some of it differently.”

“Does she?”

“She's better at asking which part of an appointment someone's worried about before she goes over the plan. I tend to explain the plan first.”

Eve remembered the man watching the clock. “That was useful.”

“Yes. I thought so too.”

Near the ceremony room they reached the paving slab that had caught Eve's shoe. This time Julian saw it first and lifted his box so it wouldn't block her view. She stepped over, walked to the restaurant entrance, and asked him if he wanted to go back at the pace they would actually take with friends and family.

“What pace is that?”

“Slow enough to talk. Fast enough that my mother can't assign us another building.”

He nearly lost a mug laughing. On the way back she noticed a flat stretch on the other side of the street, where they could walk two abreast without making everybody behind them pass in single file. They tried the crossing, checked the bench, and chose it. She sent her mother a short message with the route. If her mother wanted something to do, Eve would ask if she felt like walking with the people who preferred to take it slowly. She would not take being asked as permission to usher the entire neighborhood into the restaurant.

At home Julian left the box by the cupboard. Dumpling came through from the sitting room without haste, smelled the shoes and the tea towel in turn, and went back to wherever he'd been. He had not been waiting for news of anyone's professional development.

Julian took the mugs out, washed them, and put them with the others. He carried the plant to the windowsill and looked at it in the light.

“I don't think this wants direct sun,” he said.

“Has it submitted a preference?”

“Parts of it have gone brown. That's fairly legible.”

His phone buzzed while he moved the pot a little way back. He read whatever appeared on it, waited a moment, and left the phone on the kitchen table.

“Something wrong?” Eve asked.

“No. I forgot I'd made myself an alarm to call and check that the cover was working.”

“Are you going to?”

“The clinic has my planned return date. They know what to do if the building floods.”

“What do they do if it floods?”

“Call somebody who knows how to stop it.”

He turned the alarm off. Eve saw him hesitate with his thumb over the screen, then put it down rather than finding a task to replace it. She took his hand before either of them could make this small thing larger by discussing it further.

They ate leftovers at the table. Later, she could hear him quietly asking the plant whether it had an opinion about being watered before he got up and fetched a saucer for it. He stayed where he was when Eve's phone lit up on the other side of the room. While he found a place for the saucer, she read a reply from her mother, confirming she would be delighted to walk with anyone who fancied a little extra time.
