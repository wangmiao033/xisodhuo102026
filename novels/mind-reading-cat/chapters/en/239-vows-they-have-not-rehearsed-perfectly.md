# Chapter 239 — Vows They Have Not Rehearsed Perfectly

“Together we will build a lasting home with room to grow,” Eve read.

Julian didn't speak at once. He was holding a teaspoon above a mug, from which the last drop of tea had already fallen.

“Is that where the living room comes in the brochures?”

Eve put the page down. “I knew it.”

“I was picturing those people smiling at a folded blanket they haven't bought yet.”

“There's a photograph of that on my work computer. I paid for the right to use it.”

He laid the spoon across the saucer. She looked at the top of the page again, then turned it over so she wouldn't have to see all the fine-looking sentences she'd left underneath the first one.

They had agreed to read their vows at the kitchen table where they could stop, speak badly, or ask questions that might be difficult to ask standing up with their families close enough to hear. Part of Eve had expected that the opportunity to change things beforehand would make the reading easy. Instead it meant that every time she disliked a sentence she saw a second one approaching.

“Your turn,” she said.

Julian brought his own page closer. His handwriting looked orderly until he needed to put another word in between two neatly spaced lines. Then it became a dangerous-looking patch of hatching.

“I will never leave you to face anything alone.”

Eve rubbed at a spot on the table. There was a little flour stuck there from dinner, white enough that she kept thinking she had removed it before noticing it again.

“You went to bed while I was dealing with someone else's quotation yesterday,” she said.

“You told me to.”

“It was the right thing to do.”

He glanced at the page.

“I could say something about the serious things.”

“What counts as serious?”

“Not the quotation.”

“What if I'm in a state about the quotation?”

“I'd probably offer you a cup of tea. From the bedroom, if it were late.”

She could easily picture it, which was more than she could say for the person capable of keeping his first promise. She got a cloth for the flour. When she returned, Julian had drawn a line through his last two words, realized the sentence was not thereby much improved, and struck out the rest.

They drank a little tea without talking. It had been easy enough to arrange the signing with the registrar. There were words required for that part, and a sensible person would indicate when they should say them. These were their own short additions. Eve had imagined people leaving the room with the impression that she'd said something moving. She had spent surprisingly little time imagining Julian hearing what she meant.

She turned the page face up. “May I read something worse?”

“I'd rather know now.”

“‘I promise I won't disappoint you.’”

Julian leaned forward to make sure she was reading the page and not inventing a joke at his expense. “Oh, Eve.”

“It's worse in context.”

“I found it quite bad out of context.”

She laughed, then found she needed a moment. The cloth was still in her hand. She spread it flat beside the mug and smoothed two corners.

“I didn't especially mean never making a mistake,” she said. “I meant not being the kind of person you're surprised to find out you've married.”

“Who do you think I'm expecting?”

She took too long to answer.

Julian moved his mug aside. “I'm expecting you. You occasionally tell me something is fine when you're making a very specific noise setting a plate down. I find out eventually. I hope to get better at asking before I spend the evening pretending not to hear it.”

Eve knew the noise he meant. “I hope to get better at using words before I get to the plate.”

“Could we put something like that in?”

“The plate?”

“Don't wait until I work it out by myself.”

A large patch of fur shifted against Eve's ankle. Dumpling had wedged himself beneath her chair and was using her foot as an obstacle to stretch around. She moved an inch. He moved an inch in the opposite direction, which satisfied whatever separate business he had been undertaking, and lowered his chin onto the floor.

Eve tested the idea by saying, “When something's wrong, I'll tell you.”

Julian wanted to know if they meant as soon as they noticed. She thought of the time it had taken her even to recognize that she'd made two entirely different decisions about how she felt on the same bus journey. Sometimes it would be kind to ask for half an hour. Sometimes they would get the necessary words on the second or third try. He turned the question over while she fed the end of the pencil through a lengthening crease in her paper.

“I can say I'll bring it up,” she said. “I can't say I'll have my head straight every time.”

“Neither can I.”

“Or that I'll tell you everything the instant it occurs to me. I think we can both be grateful for that.”

He picked up the pencil she had been threading through the paper. “What about ‘I will come back to the conversation’?”

“Will you?”

“Do you mean will I want to?”

“Sometimes.”

“Sometimes I won't. I'd like you to be able to ask for it anyway.”

She leaned closer to see him write. The short sentence took up less than half a line. He underlined nothing. When he passed the pencil back, she wrote that if she made a mistake, she would try to put right what she could. After a moment she crossed out the word immediately, which she had inserted without noticing.

The hallway light clicked off on its timer. They could continue easily enough in the kitchen, but Eve got up to turn it back on without thinking. On her way she saw Julian's newly rescued plant on the windowsill, its brown leaves arranged in a convincing argument against his earlier optimism. There were two clear places in which new growth might appear. Last night he had announced he might go away for another period of training one day if the right opportunity came up. She had asked him to tell her before he applied, not so that she could rule on it but because she wanted to hear why he was interested while the matter was still loose enough to discuss.

When she sat down, she said, “I don't want to put anything in a vow about always going in the same direction.”

“I never wrote that.”

“I nearly did. It had something to do with walking together.”

“We can walk together quite successfully.” He looked at her over the top of his page. “We can also walk past the same bakery separately and buy too much bread.”

“That is my favorite of our problems.”

Eve had not meant that they should never change direction. She had meant that Julian didn't have to want a shop, and she didn't have to become good at running a doctor's life. She wanted him to turn up at times they agreed on, and to stop her occasionally and ask whether the many things she was doing in a day added up to a life she liked. He could ask for that sort of attention from her without having to watch her rearrange his choices to fit into her diary.

She tried saying some of this and got as far as “your own pursuits” before Julian started laughing. It was easier to write underneath the mistake than to read it again.

“Ask me what I want to keep doing,” he said. “Don't make up a more convenient answer.”

“I can do that.”

“I can ask you, too.”

She wrote it down in language either of them would have used after a very long day. They let their two versions differ. There was no reason for her to say something he had already chosen to say more plainly.

A little later they came to the one sentence Eve had liked from the beginning. It concerned a moment at the noodle shop: steam, Julian's hand moving a teacup near hers, her relief at having been able to ask him to come. She read it to him as written.

“I like that,” he said.

“I don't think the room needs to know why it mattered.”

“The room can live with some gaps.”

“I don't mind them hearing about dinner.”

“Good. We're quite solid on dinner.”

They had run out of tea before they finished. Eve reheated water for another pot. Julian took the old pages and set them on the shelf instead of tearing them into confetti. When she asked what he thought he was preserving, he said the advertising version might be useful when they weren't getting along.

“How?”

“We can ask each other how much room we're providing for growth and find that quite difficult to answer without laughing.”

She carried the pot over with both hands. In due course she would recycle the pages anyway.

Julian said there was one more thing he wanted to ask. He meant the vows, not another task. On the day, if he had trouble reading his own handwriting, would she mind if he simply stopped and started a sentence again? He'd been in a wedding once where the most nervous person in the room had rushed his words because everybody was being so supportive. He didn't want to do that. He only needed a moment, not an audience coaxing him through it.

Eve looked at the little scratches and arrows on his current page. “You know you can copy it over.”

“I was thinking about a different kind of trouble.”

“Oh,” she said.

It took a second before she could speak again. She put a hand over his wrist, lightly enough that he could move it. He didn't. “Yes. Take whatever time you need. I may have to do it too.”

“All right.”

They tried the new versions out loud. Julian broke once on a word he normally used without thinking. Eve had to start a sentence again because she'd skipped the very part that made it hers. She didn't apologize to the empty chairs. The content survived a little stumbling. She made a mark at the place where she wanted to breathe.

When they finally went to put the notes away, Dumpling had moved to the other side of the kitchen and was watching a chair leg, where a faint spot from the lamp on the ceiling had caught his attention. He put a paw on it. The light remained where it was.

Eve gathered the bag that contained the papers they had already checked for the ceremony. It went by the door on her side so she would take it with her. Julian checked the ring box, then put it back in a pocket he could reach easily. They had asked Sophie if she minded holding it for the short interval before the ceremony. She'd replied that she would, if everybody understood it did not make her responsible for keeping the whole institution of marriage on schedule. Eve had promised to leave the other institutions where they were.

“Would you like to go through it again in the morning?” Julian asked.

“No. Would you?”

“I think I'll know when I've got to the end.”

She reached up to kiss him. From the way he took a second to set down the pencil first, she suspected he had been thinking about doing exactly the same thing. She wouldn't ask him to prove it.
