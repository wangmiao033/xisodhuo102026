# Chapter 141 — The Offer with a Calendar

The offer contained a calendar, which made it considerably more dangerous than an offer containing only money.

Eve opened the attachment while waiting for a client to confirm whether its event began at six or half past. Four previous messages had supplied four descriptions of the refreshments and no answer to that question.

On the new calendar, every delivery date already existed. So did the approval dates. One person would gather feedback. A minimum quarterly fee would reserve a defined amount of her time, with additional commissions priced separately.

She leaned closer.

Dumpling slept in his little bed near the window. He had turned so that his head hung over the side, achieving a position Eve would have complained about within minutes.

“Someone has discovered planning,” she told him.

He continued sleeping through the commercial development.

The message came from Good Company, a pet-supply chain Eve knew from its wide aisles and reliably unromantic shelf labels. She had bought litter there. She had also once spent twenty minutes comparing two apparently identical brushes before choosing neither.

Their partnership manager, Lydia, had seen Eve's practical information work. The chain was assembling a small group of independent creators for an upcoming quarter. They wanted regular features about everyday life with animals: household organization, preparation, navigating choices without overwhelming people.

There would be a common visual format and a consistent account identity across the group.

Eve read that part twice, then returned to the calendar.

For months, she had known roughly how much work she needed while rarely knowing which work would arrive. A late answer could leave Tuesday empty and Friday impossible. She had become skilled at moving tasks between little squares without admitting that the squares were also her days.

This offer could put some of them in place.

She saved the proposal, checked the company's public details against the sender, and asked for a meeting. Then she returned to the event client and wrote, once more, that she needed a start time before completing the invitation.

The contrast was almost rude.

Good Company's nearest branch had a small office above the stockroom. Lydia met Eve at the customer-service desk and led her past shelves of replacement clips, washable covers, and one aggressively cheerful display of dog birthday hats.

“Do those sell?” Eve asked.

“Enough that someone keeps ordering them.”

“A reliable definition of success.”

“Usually.”

A staff member stopped Lydia to ask which room contained the new training folders. Lydia pointed her toward the office, then waited while a trolley passed. Eve liked that nobody needed to pretend a meeting about content suspended the rest of the shop.

Upstairs, Lydia cleared two sample harnesses from a table. She had printed the proposal double-sided. Eve had brought hers with notes in the margin, which made her feel briefly like the sort of person who arrived prepared rather than recovered information while someone watched.

“We aren't looking for a cat performance,” Lydia said. “Your information sheets were what interested us.”

Eve looked up.

“The account has a cat in it,” Lydia continued. “Obviously. But the useful part is that people can understand what you're asking them to do.”

It was close enough to Eve's own hopes for her work that she made herself wait before answering.

“What kinds of features?”

Lydia showed her three sample briefs. One concerned arranging everyday supplies so different people could find them. Another helped customers compare the dimensions on packaging with the space they actually had. The third followed someone preparing for their first animal.

“The last one would need more discussion,” Eve said.

“Of course. We have retail staff contributing information. We aren't asking creators to give clinical advice.”

They went through the proposed work. Six scheduled pieces across the quarter. Two production days at most, with the remaining material made from approved illustrations, short interviews, and practical examples. No attendance at every branch launch. No obligation to answer individual animal-care questions in comments.

Eve asked who would handle those questions.

“The branch or service team, depending on the subject. We need a handover note so things don't sit with you.”

She wrote that down.

Lydia showed her how revisions moved through the team. An example script carried one set of comments, with contradictory requests already resolved before the creator received it. Eve opened the next page almost suspiciously.

“Who decides when a requested change means the brief has changed?”

“I do. If you disagree, we stop and discuss the schedule before you make it.”

Eve had worked with clients whose improvements arrived individually, each wearing the innocent expression of a tiny favor. The example in front of her could have saved hours on three recent jobs.

“What does the person making it think of this system?” she asked.

“I can ask whether one of our current freelancers is willing to speak with you.”

“I'd appreciate that.”

The fees were credible for the work described. The payment schedule would give her a firmer base without requiring her to abandon all other clients. Production costs had their own line. The minimum commitment would still apply if the brand delayed one of its briefs beyond the agreed window.

Eve found herself enjoying the meeting.

She had prepared to defend several things nobody was attacking. Instead Lydia wanted to know how much lead time a clear script needed, and whether Eve could flag difficult claims before the camera came out.

“Yes,” Eve said. “That's usually when they're easiest to change.”

“Please tell our launch team.”

“I assume that's a separate fee.”

Lydia smiled and turned the page.

The proposed account identity occupied two full sheets.

There was a new banner, a short introduction, three colors, and a neat grid of sample posts. Eve's existing account name would remain, accompanied by a Good Company partner label. Sponsored pieces would use a consistent cover. Ordinary posts would use a lighter version of the same frame.

Eve studied the mockup. It was handsome. Her untidy collection of useful sheets, neighborhood observations, and photographs suddenly looked like a destination someone had designed on purpose.

“The same frame for unpaid posts?” she asked.

“During the partnership. It gives the group a recognizable identity.”

“Would you approve those posts?”

“Anything connected to pet ownership would go into the shared content calendar. We aim to turn comments around within two working days.”

Eve put her pen down.

“That includes work you haven't commissioned.”

“It includes material appearing alongside the campaign.”

Lydia said it without embarrassment. Eve appreciated the clarity and disliked what it clarified.

“What happens if I want to post something outside the calendar?”

“We'd discuss it. There are guidelines rather than a script for your entire life.”

“Could I see the guidelines?”

“I'll send the full version. We also review the existing archive before confirming a partner. Sometimes older posts create a different impression from the new work.”

Eve looked again at the clean grid. In the mockup, Dumpling's photograph occupied the bottom corner. He was looking at the camera with the expression he used when somebody moved a chair he had been considering.

“I'm interested in the commissions,” she said. “I need to understand the account arrangement separately.”

“That's sensible. Most people ask about the fee first.”

“I did. Privately. At considerable length.”

Lydia laughed. They agreed that Eve would review the documents and return questions before either side reserved production dates.

On her way out, Eve paused at the customer-information shelf. The leaflets were readable, with source dates where she would expect them. A measuring guide included a drawing of the tape held in the wrong place, then the right one.

Good work already happened here. That made the opportunity more complicated than a large logo asking her to become less herself.

She took the bus home with the proposal in her bag and bought groceries before she could mistake professional excitement for lunch.

That evening, she put three versions of the next quarter beside one another. Existing confirmed work. Existing work with likely renewals. Existing work plus Good Company.

The third version allowed a reserve she had struggled to rebuild. It also made room for a paid production assistant on a busier job, rather than Eve spending an evening carrying and checking everything alone. She could commission a proper review of the trial booking process, replace a microphone that had begun crackling, and give the borrowed-room activity a regular preparation block.

None of those things required an enormous breakthrough. They required money arriving when she expected it.

She left the promising column marked proposed. No purchases followed it.

When Sophie called at the time they had arranged, Eve asked first about the newer programming vacancy. Sophie had declined to apply for the earlier coordination job after learning how many late finishes it required. This venue had supplied an actual shared rota before asking for an application.

“Second interview,” Sophie said. “They asked how I'd handle a room unavailable at short notice.”

“What did you say?”

“That depends why it's unavailable.”

“Terrifyingly specific.”

“They seemed to survive.”

Sophie told her about the shared evening rota and the question she still wanted answered before accepting anything. Eve listened without recommending her own uncertain business as an alternative career.

Only afterward did she describe the brand offer.

“That sounds like work you might like,” Sophie said.

“Some of it does. They also want the account to look consistent.”

“How consistent?”

“Apparently even my unrelated opinions would have a matching border.”

There was a pause.

“Is the border the problem?” Sophie asked.

Eve looked at the handsome sample page.

“No. I think I'd like the border.”

“Then ask about the opinions.”

After the call, Eve opened her own feed. Its inconsistency had not seemed entirely desirable that morning. It still didn't. Some captions were too long. A useful resource sat beneath an old announcement that should have been updated. There were improvements she could make without accepting somebody else's reason for making them.

She corrected the expired announcement and moved the current trial information where people could find it.

Then she looked at the post about waiting before adoption.

Its photograph was plain. The account had been much smaller when she made it. It had never become the popular piece she once hoped it might, but people still arrived through it occasionally, looking for ways to help before taking an animal home.

Below it was Mia's more recent story, complete with the untidy parts Mia had chosen to keep.

Eve could imagine both pieces looking better in the new format. She was less certain whether they would sound the same.

Julian sent a message asking whether their planned Sunday walk still suited her. She replied yes and told him she had received a potentially useful offer.

His answer was brief. He would like to hear about it when they met.

She resisted forwarding sixteen pages immediately. The proposal could survive until Sunday without acquiring an unpaid veterinary reviewer.

Lydia's full guidelines arrived as Eve was shutting down for the night.

Alongside the visual rules was a sample editorial calendar. Eve's ordinary posts had been assigned themes, tentative dates, and a column headed intended customer action. Shopping links occupied most of that column. A few entries led to checklists and store appointments.

One led nowhere. Beside her old adoption-readiness piece, the brand's reviewer had written: Discuss continued suitability within partner account.

Eve sat back down.

For the first time, the calendar looked less like an answer to where her days would go and more like a question about who would choose what belonged in them.

She marked the sentence, wrote a question beneath it, and left the rest of the offer open.
