# Chapter 116 — Who Lives Where?

The first morning, Eve liked finding two mugs beside the sink.

One held a little cold tea. The other contained a teaspoon Julian had inexplicably stood upright in a piece of toast crust. He had left early, apologizing for the alarm, and she had kissed him while still half asleep. Now sunlight reached the counter, Dumpling was making his ordinary breakfast complaint, and somebody else's cup made the room look pleasantly occupied.

She photographed the upright spoon and sent it to him.

An installation. What does it mean?

His answer arrived after she had fed the cat.

It means I missed the bin.

The night before, dinner had stretched past the last episode of a program neither of them especially liked. Eve had asked him to stay. There had been a definite question, a definite yes, and the small excitement of finding a spare toothbrush behind a packet of cotton pads. Nothing about it had felt complicated.

She washed both mugs, shook the crumbs into the bin, and started work.

Two nights later, Julian came over with ingredients for noodles. Eve had spent the afternoon correcting captions and wanted company that could pronounce a sentence without a timing marker attached. He cooked while she told him about an image that looked perfectly normal until enlarged, when a sign behind the subject became unfortunately legible.

"Could you crop it?" he asked.

"Yes. I would like you to appreciate the forty minutes before that occurred to me."

"An important stage."

"I considered reshooting."

"Less important."

After dinner she tucked her feet under his leg on the sofa. Dumpling tried to occupy the remaining space, discovered there was very little, and climbed across both of them to reach the wide armrest. His tail passed beneath Julian's nose.

"Lovely," Julian said faintly.

Eve laughed so hard that she nearly spilled her tea.

It was late when the program ended. Julian looked at the clock, then at the rain streaking the window.

"You could stay," she said.

"I've got an early start."

"You had one last time."

"You complained about my alarm."

"I complained about the idea of morning. You were incidental."

He smiled and stayed. In the bathroom, Eve moved her face cream to make room beside the basin. Later, lying close enough to feel the warmth of his shoulder, she thought the displaced cream a very reasonable price.

The next morning she could not find it.

She eventually discovered it behind the cleaning supplies, where she had put it herself. Julian had gone. His damp towel hung over the back of a chair because her usual hook already held hers. She folded it over the rail properly, warmed coffee he had made, and worked in yesterday's clothes until lunchtime.

On Friday he brought the shirt he had worn home after the first overnight, washed and folded, in a small bag. He had borrowed an old loose top of hers for breakfast when a splash of coffee made his own uncomfortable. The borrowed top was in the bag too.

"Thank you," she said, taking it.

"There's no toast inside."

"Disappointing. Your art is becoming conventional."

They had planned only a short meal. Eve wanted to finish reading Sophie's paperback before their Sunday walk. Julian needed to do laundry. At nine they were standing in the kitchen, talking about whether anyone had ever bought the smallest available bag of salad and finished it before it became soup.

At ten they were still talking.

At ten forty, he began to put on his shoes.

"You don't have to rush," she said.

He paused with one foot on the mat. "I wasn't."

"I mean, if you're tired."

"A bit."

"Well, stay."

He looked at her, then took off the shoe.

She was pleased. She also remembered that she had left the paperback open on her pillow, exactly where she had intended to return to it. She moved it to the bedside table while he washed his hands. The bus ticket she was using as a bookmark fell out. She bent to retrieve it and bumped her forehead against the table edge.

"Everything all right?" he called.

"Literature attacked me."

They slept badly. The room felt warm; opening the window made the traffic louder. Julian turned once, apologized, then turned again. Eve lay still so he would not know she was awake. After a while, staying still became the principal activity of her night.

In the morning he asked why she looked tired.

"Your elbow delivered a short lecture."

"You could have moved it."

"It seemed committed."

He touched her forehead where she had hit it. "Is that from me?"

"No. Separate enemy."

She leaned into his hand. There were enough good things about the morning to make complaining feel ungrateful. He brought her tea. She stole the warm edge of his toast. Dumpling settled beneath the table instead of crying at a closed bedroom door. Julian left to do his laundry, and the flat became hers again.

Eve took out the paperback.

Before opening it, she stripped the bed, put on a wash, collected the mugs from the sofa, and wondered what she should buy for dinner in case Julian returned.

She had not asked him to return.

Still, she bought enough for two.

Sunday's walk with Sophie lasted an hour and a little more. They discussed the paperback's improbable survivor, then Sophie's decision against applying for the late-working coordination role. Eve returned the book without a business card, a recommendation, or a suggested solution hidden between the pages. Sophie gave her a different paperback and warned her that this one contained a dog but no reliable adults.

At home Eve put it beside her drawing book.

Julian messaged while she was changing out of her shoes.

How was the walk?

She sent a short answer, then added that she had too much food for one person.

I can help with that, he replied.

It had become so easy. That was the trouble she could not yet recognize as trouble. She liked him arriving. She liked the particular way he stood close behind her to see what she was reading, careful not to rest his weight on her chair. She liked hearing him laugh before she had reached the end of a story.

She did not like realizing, halfway through putting on comfortable trousers, that she was choosing a less shabby pair because he was coming over.

The comfortable pair had a small hole near the knee. She wore them anyway, with an odd sense of making a declaration no one would notice.

He noticed the food first.

"This is enough for three."

"The vegetables were discounted. I accepted a challenge."

"From a cabbage?"

"It was persuasive."

They ate. He washed up. She felt ridiculous for being tired of hosting someone who did his share of the dishes. When he asked where a bowl belonged, she answered too quickly.

"Where it always goes."

Julian stopped with the bowl in his hands.

"Which is?"

"Sorry. The cupboard on the left."

There were three cupboards on the left. He opened the wrong one, closed it, and waited.

Eve took the bowl from him and put it away herself.

The movement was small and efficient, exactly how she behaved when something needed doing and explanation seemed slower. His hands remained empty for a second before he picked up the towel.

"I can learn where things go," he said.

"I know. I'm just tired."

"Do you want an early night?"

"Probably."

He nodded. Eve heard agreement and felt relief. A moment later he picked up his toothbrush from the cup beside the basin.

Not to leave. To use it.

She stood in the bedroom doorway, holding the new paperback, and realized they had meant different things.

There was still time to say so. There was an ordinary sentence available: I meant I wanted to be on my own tonight. She had said harder things to clients who paid her. She had told Sophie, plainly, when she could and could not go out.

But Julian had just rinsed his toothbrush. She imagined his face changing and hated herself a little for imagining it so vividly before giving him the chance to make an actual face.

She put the book down.

That night she woke when he shifted his arm away from her waist. For a moment she missed its weight. Then she stretched into the space and felt an equally immediate pleasure.

Both feelings remained true in the dark.

On Monday evening, Julian arrived later than expected. He had messaged first, and Eve had said that was fine. She reheated dinner and kept half an eye on the door while a sketch of her blue mixing bowl waited unfinished on the table.

He kissed her, took off his coat, and saw the drawing.

"That's getting better."

"It has become a bowl. Last week it was a geographical feature."

"Should I put these away?"

He gestured toward the pencils.

She wanted to say no. She wanted the table to remain a place where a drawing could stay unfinished until she returned to it. Instead she gathered the pencils herself and carried them to the shelf because the plates needed somewhere to go.

Later, while he answered a message, she found herself arranging tomorrow's breakfast in her head. Was there enough bread? Did he need a fresh towel? Could she run the washing machine in the morning without disturbing him if he started later?

"What are you thinking about?" he asked.

"Bread."

He looked delighted. "A serious subject."

She smiled because that was easier than explaining why bread suddenly felt like another responsibility.

Dumpling hopped onto the sofa and stepped heavily across her lap toward Julian. The cat paused between them, paw resting on Eve's leg. Perhaps her irritation was bright enough for him to notice; perhaps he simply disliked the way she was sitting. His ears flicked, then he abandoned both laps and settled in the chair.

Eve did not ask him what Julian was feeling. Julian was right there, rubbing a thumb along the seam of his phone case.

"Are you staying?" she asked.

He looked up.

"I thought I was. Do you need me to go?"

The question came without accusation, but her chest tightened anyway.

"I don't know," she said.

He put the phone down.

For once she did not rush to improve the answer. She looked around the room: the moved pencils, the drying towel, his bag beside the door, her own bag still on the floor because the chair had become a temporary wardrobe.

"I seem to be getting ready for you even when you're already here."

Julian glanced at the table, then back at her.

"I didn't realize."

"Neither did I. Until just now."

He stayed where he was. She was grateful for that, although she could not yet have said whether she wanted him to move closer or stand up.

In the chair, Dumpling turned one slow circle and lay down on the only seat nobody had offered him.
