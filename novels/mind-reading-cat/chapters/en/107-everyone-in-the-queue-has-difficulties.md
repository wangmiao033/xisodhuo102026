# Chapter 107 — Everyone in the Queue Has Difficulties

From her chair, Eve decided the first person waiting must think the second had jumped the queue.

Perhaps the second person thought the first was staring at her carrier.

Eve was still arranging this imagined misunderstanding when the man nearest the desk asked where he was supposed to park his bicycle.

She closed her notebook.

Tessa directed him to the permitted rack outside, then returned to the woman with the carrier. The woman had not come for an appointment at all. She was collecting something already arranged with the clinical team and wanted to know where to wait while they checked it.

Three people, three different reasons to be standing near the same counter. From Eve's chair they had looked like a single problem.

The camera remained at home.

For this observation visit, the clinic had explained her role to the people present. Eve had asked before remaining near any conversation, offered an easy refusal, and kept no names, animal details, or accounts of individual care. When somebody wanted privacy, she moved. The film needed to explain a process. It did not need footage of strangers feeling exposed.

Even so, she could feel herself collecting beginnings for stories. The anxious grip. The tired shoes. The person with something important to say but nowhere to sit. She had to remind herself that noticing a detail did not make it hers.

A visitor in a pale raincoat approached the counter.

“My appointment was ten minutes ago.”

“I know you're waiting,” Tessa said. “The team is delayed. I'll check whether there's an updated estimate, but I don't want to give you a time they haven't confirmed.”

“Then what's the appointment for?”

The raincoat was still zipped to the chin. Eve had an instinctive sympathy for Tessa, partly because she now knew how much work the desk involved. It would have been easy to make this visitor the unreasonable figure in the new version of her film.

Then the person added, more quietly, “I have someone covering my job. I can't keep saying another ten minutes.”

Tessa's expression changed a little, not into surrender but recognition.

“Would you like to discuss the options privately with the team? I can ask them to speak with you. I can't decide which option is suitable.”

“Yes.”

Eve stepped away before the conversation continued. She hadn't learned that the visitor was right about everything. She had learned that an impatient sentence could contain a life she wasn't looking at.

In the staff room she wrote only: Explain uncertainty before people build plans on an invented time.

Then she crossed out people and wrote we.

When she returned to the permitted observation chair, a small dog barked at the sound of the entrance door. Its owner gathered the lead closer and apologized to everybody in the room. Tessa offered the waiting arrangements available that morning without promising the dog would settle if the owner simply tried harder.

The owner chose to wait in the quieter place they discussed. Eve moved her own bag from the route before anyone had to ask.

She thought of the market's sign and the visitor who had not wanted to interrupt from the edge of the chairs. A useful room could still be difficult from one position inside it.

Near midday, Dr. Clark found her studying the noticeboard.

“Any conclusions?” he asked.

“Several premature ones.”

He smiled. “Those are usually plentiful.”

“The board says to speak to reception if you have a question. But it doesn't help someone know which question reception can answer.”

“Too much detail and nobody reads it.”

“I agree. That doesn't mean the only alternatives are a slogan and a wall of text.”

She showed him three brief headings she had drafted: arriving, waiting, asking about costs. Under each she had written the point at which a member of the clinical team would need to become involved. Nothing described symptoms or treatment. Nothing promised a particular outcome.

Dr. Clark asked to see the full wording later. He also reminded her that the clinic couldn't staff a separate welcome desk just because a film looked better with one.

“I need to show what you can actually provide,” she said. “Otherwise the film creates a new queue for a service that doesn't exist.”

Their conversation was interrupted by Tessa asking him to clarify an estimate for a visitor. He went. Eve put her notebook away again.

She could hear the lowered voices but did not record their words. Costs were not an abstract matter for her. She remembered the first bill for Dumpling, the terrible arithmetic of wanting to help while counting the money that had to last. Remembering did not make her qualified to explain somebody else's choice.

A poster saying they cared would not have answered her question that day. Neither would a film in which every worried owner gratefully accepted whatever came next.

At the end of the observation period, Tessa walked her to the staff-room door.

“Did you get your dramatic morning?”

“I got several mornings happening in one room.”

“That sounds more accurate.”

“I don't want to reenact those exact people.”

“Good.”

“What if we use plain questions, staff demonstrations, and empty-room routes? Not actors arguing badly so the clinic can look patient.”

Tessa considered this while unfastening the lanyard caught beneath her cardigan.

“Show where to ask before someone has to say the same difficult thing across a crowded room.”

Eve wrote it down only after asking whether that wording could be used.

Two days later, before opening, the reception area looked disappointingly small on camera. Without the busy visitors and their intersecting needs, it was a counter, a row of chairs, and a mat whose corner needed flattening.

Eve flattened the mat.

Julian, who had agreed to demonstrate a staff handover during his allocated production time, watched her move a chair out of the frame.

“Is that where it normally goes?”

“No. I was making the shot cleaner.”

She moved it back. A person following the film later would meet the actual chair, not her preferred composition.

They recorded the route from the entrance to the counter, then a demonstration with a blank appointment card. Tessa read the wording and stopped halfway through.

“This sounds as if everyone should prepare a speech.”

Eve looked at her script. In trying not to omit anything, she had produced a list long enough to intimidate someone before they entered.

“Which part matters first?”

“Tell us you've arrived. If something has changed, tell us. We'll ask what we need next.”

Eve shortened the opening. The longer preparation information could be available separately, checked by the clinic, without becoming a barrier to asking for help.

They tried again.

This time Tessa sounded like a person speaking to another person rather than somebody reading the conditions of admission to a complicated building.

Julian demonstrated receiving a handover, then insisted they retake it because his first answer implied reception could always know precisely how long the wait would be.

“You've become a difficult performer,” Eve said.

“I had a demanding director.”

“Apparently she improves.”

“Occasionally.”

Tessa held up the sample card. “Can the romance wait until we finish this?”

Eve felt her cheeks warm. Julian said, “Yes,” with enough seriousness to make all three of them laugh.

They finished within the allocated time. There was no cheering when the camera went off. Tessa needed the counter clear for opening, and Eve had two cables to put away before she became an obstruction in a film about not obstructing reception.

At home, she assembled the first sequence without music. It sounded quieter than she expected. Footsteps. A greeting. A question. The brief pause while Tessa checked something rather than guessing.

Dumpling walked between the sofa and the table, brushed her shin, and went to his water. Eve did not interpret this as a review.

She sent the rough cut through the agreed staff review process. Dr. Clark asked for one qualification on the costs section. Tessa removed a sentence that accidentally promised every question could be resolved before the appointment. Julian corrected a phrase that blurred the difference between passing on information and assessing an animal.

None of the changes made the film more exciting.

Eve took a break before resenting that.

On her walk to buy bread, she noticed a queue outside the little bakery. Someone wanted a large order. Someone was choosing one roll. Someone had returned because the wrong bag had been handed over. From the pavement they were all simply people waiting, yet a single instruction to be patient would have answered none of them.

She bought her bread when it was her turn and went home with an idea for the transition she had been struggling to write.

The film did not need to argue that the visitor or the desk had the harder day. It could show what each needed to know next.

She replaced her narration about understanding busy staff with a short question: Not sure who to ask?

Then she let Tessa explain the actual route.

Before the staff review the following afternoon, Eve printed the proposed captions in a large typeface. She asked Tessa to read them while Eve stood at the distance the screen would be from a waiting-room chair. One sentence vanished into small print. Another relied on color to distinguish two kinds of question.

They changed both.

“I'm glad you didn't use the crying music,” Tessa said.

“I hadn't chosen any.”

“You looked as though you might.”

Eve considered protesting, then remembered the first cut and its inevitable glide toward Julian.

“I might have.”

The final unresolved item was the phrase ask us privately. Tessa would not approve it until Dr. Clark confirmed what space and cover were actually available. Eve put a visible note beside it in the review copy rather than making the promise sound gentler and hoping reality would catch up.

That evening, Julian asked whether the film was nearly finished.

“Nearly accurate,” she said. “Then finished.”

They were walking along the same street where he had given her the roses. She held his hand because she liked it, not because the day's work had earned a reward. At the crossing he stopped talking to watch the traffic, and she waited for the signal beside him.

“What did you learn?” he asked on the other side.

“That I can replace one hero with another without improving the story very much.”

“Tessa would make a formidable hero.”

“She'd ask what hours the role covered.”

“She'd be right.”

Eve laughed. At the next corner they separated for their own homes. He had an early start; she had captions to finish before bed and a cat who would be unimpressed by a delayed meal.

She went upstairs without a rescued visitor's secret in her notebook. The film would have less to expose and, she hoped, something more useful to say.
