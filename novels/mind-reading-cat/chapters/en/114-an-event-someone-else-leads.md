# Chapter 114 — An Event Someone Else Leads

The confirmation email told Eve to arrive ten minutes before the bookbinding session, wear something she did not mind marking, and bring nothing except herself.

She read the final instruction twice.

Her bag already contained a pen, a notebook, two charging cables, and a small roll of tape. She removed the tape. Then she removed the notebook, since making one was apparently the point. The cables stayed because there were limits to personal transformation.

At the learning center, Sophie stood beside a display of handmade books, speaking with a woman in a staff lanyard. Her hair was clipped back with the same green clip. This time it held.

Eve lifted a hand but did not interrupt. A volunteer at the welcome table checked her name and gave her a seat number.

"Room two. The display is open afterward as well, if you don't get a chance before."

"Thank you."

There was a little confusion at the table: another visitor had booked under her partner's name, and the volunteer was checking the list. Eve recognized the moment when she usually leaned across to help locate a name, then found herself standing in the way of the next person.

She moved aside.

Sophie finished her conversation and came over.

"You brought yourself. Good."

"And two cables, but I'm seeking treatment."

"Keep them hidden. People can smell an available charger."

They smiled at each other. Eve wanted to say the display looked good. She also wanted to ask whether Sophie was nervous, whether everything had arrived, whether there was anything she needed.

Before she could choose, Sophie said, "I'm going to check the room with the facilitator. I'll see you after, but I have a debrief first."

"I'll be a normal person."

"Don't make promises at the door."

In room two, tables had been arranged so everyone could see a demonstration at the front. Eve found her numbered place beside a woman in a dark blue cardigan who was carefully moving her handbag away from the paper supplies.

"Have you done this before?" the woman asked.

"No. I have folded invoices. Badly."

"That gives you an advantage over me. My printer does that."

They introduced themselves by first name, and then the facilitator called everyone to attention. He showed them a finished notebook small enough to fit in one hand. The pages were folded into sections, the cover heavier, the binding simple enough for beginners to attempt within the session.

Eve turned the sample carefully when it reached her. She could already imagine a photograph of six hands holding different covers, the display softly blurred behind them.

She put the sample down without reaching for her phone.

Sophie stood near the door, checking the arrival of the final two visitors. The venue lead was nearby, not hovering, answering a question about another room. Nothing about the scene suggested Sophie required a secret second manager.

The first instruction was to choose a cover.

Eve selected a warm yellow sheet. The woman beside her chose blue, changed her mind, and returned to blue. Together they studied the creasing tools as if the implements might identify their preferred users.

"You want the flat edge," the facilitator said when he came past.

Eve had been holding hers backward.

"A strong beginning."

"An ordinary beginning," he said, and demonstrated again.

The paper resisted her first fold, then yielded too sharply at one corner. She tried to smooth it out. This made the corner shinier without making it square.

Across the room a participant asked whether they would finish in time. The demonstration had taken longer because several people wanted to see the hand movement again.

Eve looked automatically at the wall clock. There were eighteen minutes before the scheduled break. If they shortened that, moved the display viewing to the end, and prepared the next materials while everyone folded—

Sophie was already speaking quietly with the facilitator.

Eve pressed her crooked corner flat.

The facilitator announced that he would demonstrate the next stage at each table rather than from the front. Sophie told the group they would still have a short break, and that anyone needing to leave exactly on time should let her know so they could receive the remaining instructions clearly.

No one applauded the adjustment. A man checked his watch and nodded. Someone asked where the toilets were. The session continued.

Eve found it surprisingly difficult not to know the revised timing in full.

Her neighbor showed her a fold that had gone wrong in a different direction.

"Do you think I should start again?"

Eve opened her mouth, ready to recommend a solution, then looked at her own paper.

"I think we should ask the person whose notebook closes."

They waved the facilitator over. He showed them how much irregularity they could leave and where a new fold would help. Eve's cover would remain slightly off-center. It would still be a notebook.

At the break she went to the corridor for water. Sophie was handling a visitor's question near the display. A stack of spare chairs stood inside an adjoining room, and Eve had a sudden conviction that moving two of them would make the corridor feel less crowded.

She took one step toward the doorway before noticing the sign marking it as staff storage. The chairs were not hers to redistribute. The corridor was moving; nobody had been left without a seat. Her conviction had arrived without any knowledge of what the room would be used for next.

She went to the water station instead.

There she met a visitor admiring one of the displayed books, which had a cover made from a printed map.

"Do you know if these are for sale?" he asked.

"I'm not sure. The display information might say."

They looked together. Some pieces were marked as examples, others carried the maker's contact details. Eve pointed to the relevant label rather than inventing an answer. It felt almost comically small, doing only the thing she actually knew.

On her way back, a late arrival stopped by the doorway and asked if there was any point joining now. Eve knew there were spare materials. She had seen them. She almost said yes, then realized that supplies were not the only thing required: someone would have to teach the missed stages while the others continued.

"Sophie can tell you what's possible," she said, indicating her friend rather than summoning her with an urgent wave.

The visitor waited until Sophie finished speaking. Eve heard only the beginning of the answer before she returned to her seat. She didn't need to hear the outcome to make certain it had been handled correctly. At the table, her neighbor had placed the rolling pencil across a crease in the paper so it would stop escaping. Eve thanked her, sat down, and discovered that being an ordinary participant still left her with plenty to learn.

Back at the table, she struggled with the binding. Her fingers understood the route until they had to perform it. She passed the thread through the wrong opening and produced a loose loop outside the cover.

Her neighbor leaned over.

"Decorative?"

"Experimental."

They laughed quietly enough not to interrupt the demonstration. The facilitator came round and helped her undo the misplaced loop. When Eve finally tightened it correctly, she felt a disproportionate pleasure she had not needed an audience to justify.

A few minutes before the advertised end, the facilitator asked everyone to stop and check their work. Two people were not finished. Sophie explained where they could sit for a few extra minutes while the room was cleared, and confirmed the venue lead was available for that arrangement. Those who needed to go received the printed instructions.

Eve's notebook lay closed in her palm. One corner was shiny, the cover slightly crooked, and the thread knot less elegant than the sample. She opened it and found blank pages that were hers to spoil.

"You've made something," her neighbor said.

"I keep checking."

They exchanged a smile and parted without needing to turn an enjoyable hour into an ongoing collaboration.

In the corridor, Eve completed the short feedback form. She wrote that the table demonstration had helped, and that the first instruction about which way to hold the folding tool could have used a closer view. She did not write a full redesign of the session. When asked what she had enjoyed, she wrote Finishing something I didn't know how to start.

Sophie was collecting materials with the volunteer. Eve raised her notebook.

"It opens."

"Excellent. Does it close?"

"With encouragement."

"Then we have exceeded expectations."

A familiar impulse tugged at Eve. She could help clear the tables and be useful while Sophie attended the debrief. She could save somebody ten minutes. But the volunteer was already taking visitors' returned tools, and the facilitator was checking supplies against his own list. Additional hands were not automatically helpful hands.

"I'm going to look at the display," she said. "Would you like me to wait afterward, or shall we talk another time?"

"Another time, please. I don't know how long the debrief will take, and I'd rather not rush it."

"Okay."

Sophie glanced at the notebook again.

"I'm glad you came."

"So am I. I was terrible at it."

"You were a beginner. Try to survive the distinction."

Eve laughed and stepped aside for the volunteer carrying a tray.

She spent another fifteen minutes at the display. Some books were polished, some visibly handmade in a way that made her want to touch the paper. She read the makers' notes and did not photograph them, since she hadn't checked the display's photography arrangements. She bought a small printed card from the staffed sales table, then left through the ordinary exit.

Outside, her phone contained no request for emergency assistance. The event had happened without her directing it, and Sophie was still inside doing work Eve did not need to oversee.

She took the bus home.

At a red light, she opened her notebook and tried to write the date on the first page. The uneven binding made the page lift under her hand. She held it down and added a drawing of the folding tool, which looked unexpectedly like a fish.

On the second line she wrote: Ask Sophie how the debrief went.

Then she crossed it out. Not because she shouldn't ask, but because the new notebook did not need to become a system for managing friendship before she had even finished the first page.

Below the crossed-out sentence she drew another fish, this one with an extravagantly disappointed face.

When she reached home, she put the notebook safely away from Dumpling's interest in the dangling thread and sent Sophie one message.

I had a good time. No need to answer while you're working. I'd like to hear how it felt from your side when you're free.

Then she made tea, opened the drawing book, and gave the first fish a cup to be disappointed in.
