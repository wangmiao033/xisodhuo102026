# Chapter 086 — Another Real Job

The interview invitation arrived while Eve was trying to remove a piece of paper from Dumpling's water bowl.

He had apparently discovered that a receipt changed character when wet. She couldn't tell whether this pleased him. He sat beside the bowl, watching her lift the soft remains with two fingers.

“That was not a budget adjustment,” she told him.

Fortunately, it was an old shopping receipt she had already recorded, not the confirmation she needed to send that morning. She emptied and washed the bowl, refilled it, and put the loose papers in the drawer before opening the message properly.

The sender worked for a local adult-learning organization. Eve had submitted her portfolio several weeks earlier, when the advertised communications position first appeared. She remembered tailoring the application carefully. She had then spent enough time on immediate jobs to let the possible answer become unreal.

Now it had a time: Tuesday afternoon.

She read the attached role description twice.

The salary range was stated. So were the ordinary hours, the reporting arrangement, and the occasional weekend sessions. The organization wanted someone who could turn practical information into material people would actually understand: course pages, short films, notices, and participant interviews where permission had been obtained.

No clever cat. No veterinarian boyfriend she had not yet officially acquired. No claim that joining a passionate team would remove the need to discuss pay.

Eve sat down.

Yesterday she had declined one commercial format. That did not make every other available form of work an insult to her principles.

She checked the delivery she owed, sent the approved caption files, and accepted the interview. Then she opened her portfolio rather than Julian's messages.

The first page still gave too much space to a campaign from her old employment. She had chosen it because the organization was recognizable, not because the work best showed what she could do now. She moved the practical walking-route piece forward, followed by the small merchant project and a carefully anonymized example of a corrected information card.

The clinic material required more care. Permission to publish a particular finished film did not give her permission to use every draft as a recruitment sample. She selected only the already public version and described her own role accurately.

By lunchtime she had removed three impressive-looking slides and made the rest easier to explain.

It was uncomfortable how much better the work looked without her trying to appear indispensable to everything.

On Tuesday she left early enough to test the ordinary journey. The office was across town, above classrooms and a small reception area where a handwritten notice apologized for a broken lift. Stairs were available; anyone who needed step-free access was directed to a staff member for the ground-floor rooms.

Eve stopped to read the notice because it gave a useful alternative instead of merely announcing inconvenience.

At reception, she gave her name. The receptionist found the appointment without asking her to recount her career in front of the people waiting for classes.

“Would you like water?”

“Yes, thank you.”

A real glass arrived. Eve put it on the little table and resisted assigning moral significance to the absence of a paper cup. An organization could own glasses and still offer a job she didn't want. She was here to ask questions, not collect reassuring symbols.

The communications manager came down to meet her. In the interview room, a second staff member introduced herself as the person who coordinated course information. A timetable covered one wall. Several dates had been amended by hand.

“We'll tell you about the post first,” the manager said. “Then we'd like to discuss two pieces from your portfolio.”

Eve uncrossed her hands beneath the table.

They wanted a staff member, not an outside creator supplying the occasional campaign. Much of the work would be ordinary maintenance: finding out why two pages showed different starting times, chasing a missing course description, making sure photographs belonged to the right sessions. There would also be room to develop short explanatory films.

“How much room?” Eve asked.

The manager smiled slightly. “Less in our busiest enrollment weeks. More between them. I would rather say that now than offer you a permanent creative retreat.”

Eve laughed. “I've been to retreats with a lot of spreadsheets.”

“Then you have relevant experience.”

They discussed the walking-route project. Eve explained why the final film contained less attractive scenery than the first plan, and how she had changed the route after listening to the person using it.

“What would you do differently?” the coordinator asked.

“Check the physical route earlier. I assumed the information was ready because I had a map. It wasn't.”

She expected herself to add a bright sentence that turned the mistake into evidence of exceptional wisdom. Instead she let it remain a mistake she had corrected.

The coordinator nodded and asked about revisions.

The questions became detailed enough for Eve to enjoy answering them. How did she distinguish a late preference from a genuine factual error? What happened when the person responsible for approval was unavailable? How did she describe a small service without promising outcomes it could not guarantee?

At one point the manager showed her a public course page and asked what confused her. It was a discussion, not an unpaid request to redesign their whole site. Eve pointed to two different booking instructions and asked which was current before suggesting any wording.

The coordinator leaned forward.

“That is the question we keep forgetting to ask.”

Eve felt the familiar pleasure of noticing something useful. It didn't belong exclusively to working for herself.

Near the end, they reached the arrangements she had circled in the description.

“How often are the weekend sessions?” she asked.

The manager showed her the existing calendar. There were predictable busy periods and a few dates still to be assigned. Time off for those hours was part of the arrangement, but the sessions themselves could not simply be moved because she had another client.

“And outside projects?”

“Declared and agreed where there isn't a conflict. We couldn't promise you every weekday afternoon for them. This is a full-time position.”

“Of course.”

Eve asked whether a later start would allow her to finish current commitments. They said there was some flexibility, to be confirmed in a written offer if both sides wished to proceed. Nobody suggested she abandon people waiting for paid work tomorrow.

The interview ended on time.

Walking back down the stairs, Eve realized she was disappointed to leave the conversation. That was useful information too.

Outside, she bought a plain sandwich and sat on a bench near the stop. The journey home would not be intolerable. It would also be a journey twice a day, with less room for moving a film shoot around the clinic's needs or helping somebody check an outdoor event in daylight.

She opened her notebook and wrote the things she knew. Then, on a different page, she wrote what she was afraid of losing.

The second page began with work and acquired Julian's name halfway down.

Eve stared at it.

It was not evidence that she should refuse. It was evidence that she had more than one thing to think about.

Her phone buzzed. Julian had sent a photograph of the boat-restoration book beside a second bookmark. No people, no clinic information; only the untidy solution to a problem they had joked about.

*Progress,* he had written.

She smiled, then told him where she had been.

*How did it go?* he asked.

*Well, I think. It's an actual reasonable job. Which is inconvenient for a dramatic story about rejecting it.*

*You could have a reasonable story.*

*Less marketable.*

He asked whether she wanted to talk later. She said yes, but not while she was eating on a bench and pretending to compare an entire future with a sandwich.

At home, Dumpling met her at the door and inspected the bag. Eve put it away before taking off her shoes. There was fresh water, the room looked exactly as she had left it, and he did not supply an opinion about the salary range.

She sat on the floor for a few minutes while he rubbed his cheek against her fingers.

Going to an office would require a reliable routine for his care. It would not mean making him perform for her income or assuming he could never cope with her absence. She had lived enough ordinary days with him to begin asking practical questions instead of treating every departure as abandonment.

After feeding him, she returned to the work waiting on her laptop.

The afternoon's interview had not delivered any of it.

A client had approved a simple correction. Another had asked about dates next month. Eve answered the first and told the second when she could confirm availability, rather than accepting a booking she might have to undo tomorrow.

Then the organization called.

The manager said they would like to offer her the position, subject to the normal checks and the written arrangements they had discussed. Would she prefer to receive the details before talking further?

“Yes,” Eve said, relieved. “I'd like to read them carefully.”

“Of course. Could you let us know by Friday?”

She checked the week in front of her, though she already knew which day it was.

“Yes. Thank you.”

After the call, she stood without a purpose and moved her empty cup from one side of the table to the other.

Somebody wanted to employ her. Not to borrow her audience, not to rent Dumpling's supposed miracles, not to obtain a convenient veterinarian. They had looked at work she had done and thought she could do a job they needed.

She allowed herself to enjoy that before making it difficult.

The written offer arrived with the salary, hours, proposed start, and conditions clearly separated. She saved it beside the application and read it once without editing a refusal in her head.

For several minutes, the possibility of an ordinary monthly payment was so attractive that she almost mistook relief for a complete decision.

Then she opened the file containing the next practical post. Three reader questions waited below it. The work interested her even without a promise that it would support her forever.

Both windows remained open.

She checked one unfamiliar clause in the offer and wrote a question beside it rather than pretending she understood. The request was about permission for outside paid work, not ownership of everything she might ever make. That distinction mattered. Tomorrow she would ask for the plain meaning, and give the organization a chance to answer before supplying her own worst interpretation.

Eve took a clean page and wrote Friday at the top. She would ask the questions, look at the money, and answer by then. She did not need to make the offered job worse in order to discover whether it was right for her.

Dumpling jumped onto the spare chair and began washing one paw with grave concentration.

“You're not on the interview panel,” she said.

He continued washing.

For once, that was exactly the amount of advice she needed from him.
