# Chapter 227 — Dinner in the Budget

The room was inexpensive until somebody sat down.

Standing in the doorway, Eve had admired the plain walls, the manageable size, and the cheerful absence of a staircase. Then the manager explained that the quoted hire covered the room, not tables, chairs, linen, crockery, or anybody who might eventually carry food into it.

Eve looked at Julian.

He looked at the empty floor.

“We have chosen an affordable space for standing,” he said.

The manager laughed, which helped. She wasn't concealing the extras; they were on the information sheet Eve had been too pleased with the first figure to read properly.

They asked for the complete list and walked through the practical details before deciding anything. Access was straightforward. There was a small side room where somebody could sit quietly. The catering arrangements were less simple than the word flexible had implied.

Eve could see herself solving it.

She could borrow equipment from the venue, design the table cards, arrange deliveries, pay somebody to bring everything back, then spend the evening wondering whether the borrowed equipment had been counted correctly.

The vision was so familiar that it felt momentarily comforting.

Julian asked how long they would have for setup.

“From that morning,” the manager said. “You'd need to finish clearing by the agreed time that night.”

Eve imagined the final guest leaving and herself finding a damp cloth.

She thanked the manager and took the complete quotation home.

At the kitchen table, she put it beside the other option: a small restaurant room where dinner, ordinary table settings, and staff were included. The restaurant cost more at first glance. After adding the things missing from the empty room, the difference narrowed considerably.

“Someone has to wash the plates,” she said.

“I support paying that person.”

“So do I.”

She opened their private budget, not the venue's accounts.

They had agreed how much each could contribute without assuming the same amount suited both. The money was separate from ordinary household expenses and from the repair reserve Eve was rebuilding for You, Me & Paws. No customer payment became wedding money because it happened to arrive on a cheerful day.

Even so, the total made her wince.

“If we cut the flowers…” she began.

They cut the flowers.

It still cost more than their comfortable limit.

“If we use the venue's spare tablecloths…”

Julian put a finger beside the empty-room quotation.

“That creates a return job.”

“I know.”

“And laundry.”

“I know.”

“And whose shift is that?”

She shut her eyes for a moment.

“Mine, in the version I was just imagining.”

“Would you like that version?”

“No.”

The answer came with enough force to make both of them laugh.

They returned to the restaurant quote.

The largest optional extra was not decoration. It was the additional reception before dinner, with another menu and more service time. Eve had included it because somebody had said guests needed something to do between the formal part and the meal.

“What if there isn't that gap?” Julian asked.

“We'd have to choose timings that actually fit.”

“Yes.”

“And make sure people aren't rushed.”

“Yes.”

She read the options again. There was a simpler arrangement with a short, ordinary arrival period, followed by a meal. It did not require everyone to spend the whole day becoming hungry in different locations.

Removing the extra reception changed the figure more than arguing about individual stems of flowers had done.

Eve sat back.

“That feels like giving something up.”

“It is.”

“I wish you'd say we never wanted it.”

“I don't think that's true.”

She smiled reluctantly. She had liked the idea of a long afternoon during which nobody wanted to be anywhere else. That did not mean they could purchase it, or that paying for it would make it happen.

They kept the dinner they wanted and removed the second event they had acquired by imitation.

Later that week, Sophie met them at a cafe after her own work. Eve brought the two quotations because Sophie had offered to look at the practical differences, not because every friend who knew how events worked owed them a consultation.

Sophie read the empty-room sheet, then the restaurant sheet.

“Which one lets you eat?” she asked.

“We've become interested in that question,” Julian said.

“Good. It's often neglected at weddings by the people paying for the food.”

Eve explained the revised plan. Sophie asked about the short walk between the likely ceremony location and dinner, the places somebody could sit, and whether their guest numbers fitted the room without using every narrow corner.

“You can get a photograph of a larger room by standing against a wall,” she said. “You cannot get a larger room by doing it.”

Eve groaned.

“I know. I have given that speech.”

“Then consider this professional recycling.”

Sophie could help with one specific thing, she said: once they had a confirmed layout, she'd spend an hour checking the guest movement and the run of the meal with them. She wanted that to be her gift, if they wanted it. She would not be the coordinator on the day.

“I want to wear something without pockets full of tape,” she said.

“Granted,” Eve replied.

“And anybody doing actual setup, photography, or clearing should be booked and paid. Don't tell me everyone will be happy to help. People can be happy and still need to sit down.”

Julian nodded.

Eve closed the quotations for a moment.

“I want you at dinner as my friend.”

“I'd gathered that. I just like making it difficult for you to accidentally hire me without wages.”

They laughed, but Eve felt the kindness in the bluntness. Sophie had named an offer small enough to remain an offer. There was no resentment waiting inside a promise to do whatever was needed.

They ate their cafe food while it was still warm. Sophie told them about a production schedule that had finally survived contact with the actual delivery entrance. Eve listened without sliding the wedding papers back between them.

On the walk home, Julian asked whether she was disappointed.

“In the plan?”

“Yes.”

“A little. Less than I expected.”

“What part?”

“The picture where there's somehow time for everything.”

He considered that as they waited at a crossing.

“I'd be disappointed if I watched you work through the whole day.”

“You might not recognize it. I'd call it enjoying the details.”

“I've seen you enjoying a missing extension cable.”

She laughed and took his arm.

The following morning, Eve went to the venue for an ordinary administrative block. A supplier's final repair-related invoice needed checking against the agreed work. The amount was expected, but seeing it now made the wedding budget feel especially extravagant.

She verified the invoice, recorded it in the business accounts, and left the money allocated to it where it belonged. Their wedding choices would not be financed by quietly delaying a bill or using the reserve meant to keep agreed work running.

Robin arrived carrying the samples for the next small exchange.

“Any wedding decisions?” they asked while putting the materials down.

“Fewer activities. More actual dinner.”

“Sounds better than most programs I've hosted.”

“I've heard some of those were overcomplicated.”

“Unconfirmed reports.”

They smiled and began checking the session description. The day's work used the time it was supposed to use. Eve did not turn the paid review into a wedding-layout meeting because Robin happened to be good at organizing people.

At home that evening, she and Julian made the final revisions to their comparison.

The restaurant still needed to confirm an appropriate date and the exact arrangement. They would not pay a deposit until the written terms matched what they understood. The formal requirements and availability for the ceremony also needed checking. For now, the budget described an option they could afford, not an event already booked.

The restaurant sent a sample menu while they were sitting there. Eve opened it and immediately began choosing dishes she thought would please the greatest number of people.

“Do you like that one?” Julian asked.

“Everybody can eat it.”

“That isn't the same question.”

She looked at the description again. She didn't particularly like it. Nor did she actually know whether everybody could eat it; they would need to ask their guests about relevant dietary requirements rather than declaring a sauce universally friendly.

They marked two dishes they genuinely enjoyed and wrote down the questions for the restaurant. A shorter menu could still be a good meal. They would not make each guest choose between pretending to like something and being described as difficult.

Eve resisted building a survey that night. Nobody had even received a formal invitation yet.

“One question at the right time,” she said.

Julian looked relieved. “I was afraid you were about to issue a catering census.”

“I was considering a tasteful one.”

They closed the menu after checking that suitable alternatives could be discussed. It was another practical uncertainty left visible instead of hidden beneath an attractive total.

They left room for a few practical costs that were easy to forget: travel for themselves, ordinary alterations if needed, and paid help for the work they genuinely wanted somebody to do. They did not assign every remaining coin to decorations and hope the rest would be covered by goodwill.

Eve erased a line for elaborate printed favors.

“I wanted to draw those,” she admitted.

“You can still draw something.”

“Not forty versions with everyone's name.”

“Probably not.”

She drew a tiny dinner plate in the margin instead. Beside it she added two badly proportioned people sitting down.

Julian studied the drawing.

“My head seems concerned.”

“It's waiting for the soup.”

He smiled, then placed his hand over hers.

They talked about what would happen if a cost rose beyond their limit. The answer wasn't that Eve would take one more late assignment or Julian would accept extra shifts indefinitely. They would remove or change something before committing to it. Their existing work already supported their lives; it did not need to punish them for becoming engaged.

The budget finally balanced without borrowing from another part of their future.

Eve printed a copy and put it with the relevant papers, not on the refrigerator where it could become a daily accusation. The digital version stayed in their private folder. There was no public post about how virtuous a modest wedding made them.

A larger celebration might suit someone else beautifully. They had found a smaller one they could attend without secretly staffing it.

Dumpling appeared when Julian opened the cupboard for plates. He inspected the two humans with the patient suspicion of a cat who knew meals sometimes preceded useful discoveries.

“Nothing special tonight,” Eve told him.

He went to his own bowl anyway.

They ate at the kitchen table. The repaired chair stayed quiet. Julian asked whether the smaller dinner still felt like something she wanted, rather than something she was accepting to make the numbers behave.

Eve looked at the two plates, the ordinary light, and the person who had asked her before deciding what she could bear to lose.

“Yes,” she said. “I want it.”

Then she reached for the bread, because the best part of a dinner remained having time to eat it.
