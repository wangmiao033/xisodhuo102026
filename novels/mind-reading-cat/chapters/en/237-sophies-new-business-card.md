# Chapter 237 — Sophie's New Business Card

Sophie had given Eve two instructions for Thursday evening: wear shoes that wouldn't slip on the gallery stairs, and come through the door marked Deliveries if it rained. Eve's shoes passed inspection. Her bag was less satisfactory.

She discovered the wedding folder inside it at the bus stop.

It was the thin folder, which made it worse. Apparently she had progressed to a stage of preparation where she could distinguish between three wedding folders by looking at their edges.

There were several items she could easily ask about. The restaurant needed a final time for putting out the small place cards. Sophie had offered one hour before the wedding to walk through the room and check the guest route and meal layout. Eve had promised not to make that offer grow. Still, there would be a moment between things tonight. One question, spoken at the proper speed, might take less than a minute.

She took the folder home. The next bus left while she was on her way back, and Eve got to the gallery seven minutes later than she'd intended, carrying only her coat and a bag of oranges.

“Please say these aren't decorations,” Sophie said.

“They're for you.”

“That improves them.”

The gallery occupied a former school supply warehouse. Light from the street fell across a stretch of concrete floor where, judging from the marks, several heavy things had been moved recently. A woman in an ink-stained shirt was tightening a clamp under a plinth. Somebody on a ladder was trying to make a cable lie flat without appearing to be in conversation with it.

Eve had expected an opening with speeches. Tonight was a small preview for the people whose work had gone into the first stop of a touring exhibition. Later there would be speeches and nicer glasses. At present there were four chairs, all occupied by coats, and a plate of biscuits one of the artists had carried in on a baking tray.

Sophie wore a jacket Eve hadn't seen in daylight before. She took a card out of a pocket and put it into Eve's hand.

Below Sophie’s printed name, the card said *Lead Producer*. Underneath was the exhibition programme’s office number and an email address. The edges were cleaner than they had any right to be after an afternoon in Sophie's pocket.

“Lead,” Eve said.

“I checked that part several times.”

“For the whole tour?”

“The first three sites. They've also asked me to price the last two separately. I said I was going to wait until I could see whether the stairs exist.”

“Surely somebody knows whether there are stairs.”

“Somebody usually does. They aren't always the person who sends the attachment.”

Eve looked again at the title. Sophie had spent years being introduced at the point when something had gone wrong, often as the person people should ask about arrangements she hadn't been allowed to make. The gallery had brought her in early this time. Her name had been on the work that arrived before the equipment, the visits, licenses and the hours for getting the larger pieces safely through smaller doors. Now it was on the little table beside the artists' names too.

“You did it,” Eve said.

Sophie turned her head toward the table as if she hadn't entirely got used to seeing the words there. “Yes,” she said. “I did.”

They had time to split an orange beside a window until a young man came down the stairs carrying a clean stack of printed guides. He looked at Sophie, then at the coats.

“The floor plan says we'll have a table here,” he said.

“We will. It's still full of sandwiches.”

“I meant here.” He pointed to the only level part of the entrance, where the front doors swung inward.

Sophie wiped her fingers on a paper napkin and went to look.

Eve followed a few steps, out of the way. She recognized the floor plan in his hand. Three delivery routes had been marked in three incompatible colors. The only sheet she had seen previously had ended up folded beside Sophie's lunch, and Eve had promised herself the courtesy of not trying to fix it there.

A cork model of the building stood under a protective cover. It had been made by a retired carpenter whose daughter had offered to bring him to the preview. There was no sign yet of either of them. The young man had been told to put the guide beside the model, which he had interpreted as putting the table as close to it as possible.

Sophie stood where somebody arriving in a wheelchair would come through the front doors and held out one arm. Her hand reached right across the space he'd marked for the table without her having to bend an elbow.

“If we put the guides closer to the window,” she said, “there's a place for people to stop without immediately becoming a second set of doors.”

“Then they'll have to turn away from the model.”

“Can we try it?”

He looked as if he was still working out whether hers was an instruction. Sophie waited, still holding the orange rind. In the end he saw the little fold-up stand by the wall and said he wanted to try that first. They set it between the window and the model. Sophie had him walk in with the guides in his arms, which promptly showed him there was barely enough room to pass the stand while somebody was reading from it.

Eve knew of a second place for the stand. She had noticed it almost as soon as they came in. She waited.

The young man tried it beside the inside corner of the model's plinth. There was enough room there, and the guides were still in reach. Sophie asked him to check that no guide would slide toward the model while somebody turned a page. They put down a shallow wooden tray.

“Who's putting the coats away?” he asked.

“Whoever brought them. We can point them to the rack.”

He nodded and put the guides down. As soon as he left, Sophie turned to Eve.

“You had a suggestion.”

“You had a producer.”

“I could see you not putting your hand up.”

“Is it very visible?”

“It used to be faster than that.”

Eve took the orange rind from her so Sophie could go and greet the two people who had just come in. The older man walked with a stick and had already stopped to investigate the printed guide. He seemed to recognize the pictures before his daughter identified them. Eve stepped farther aside and got to see both of them notice the model.

Half an hour later, Sophie found her in the stairwell, where the gallery noise was less sharp but a person could still tell when a group was laughing in the room below.

“Aren't you going to ask how I got them to change the title?” Sophie said.

“I wanted to. You were showing me around.”

Sophie sat on the bottom step. Eve sat one higher and handed her another orange.

The first contract, Sophie explained, had called her the assistant producer but expected her to take responsibility across all the venues. She'd made two lists. If they wanted an assistant, they needed to name the person who would make the decisions she took to them. If they wanted her to make those decisions, the job title, fee and planning hours needed to reflect it. They'd gone quiet for a week. She'd put a completely different job in her diary for the dates she hadn't accepted. Then the programme director had called and, with a little difficulty, asked how many hours Sophie was talking about.

“I thought they'd want me to call the other job and say I'd made a mistake,” Sophie said. “They asked which dates we'd have to move.”

Eve peeled back a strip of skin. “That's lovely.”

“It was a negotiation.”

“I know. So was getting a place to sit by the door at the restaurant. I can be pleased somebody thought of asking.”

Sophie glanced up. “You brought the wedding folder, didn't you?”

“I took it home before I got on the bus.”

Sophie laughed hard enough to drop a piece of orange on her knee. Two people came through the stairwell, thought better of asking about the noise, and went down to look at the old school photographs.

“I did want to ask you when the restaurant can put things out,” Eve said. “But they have a perfectly good phone number.”

“They do. If they say you can arrive at six in the morning, don't call me.”

“I've never found you particularly susceptible to an early start.”

“I can be persuaded by money and a train ticket.”

Somebody called Sophie's name from downstairs. She held up two fingers toward the door to mean two minutes, then turned them sideways to indicate that they actually had no business extending beyond that. Eve remembered their interrupted dinners a few years back and the angry, bewildered silence after Sophie had pointed out their habit of spending most meals looking at Eve's screen. Here Sophie's phone was lighting up beside them, and Eve felt the first small sting of wondering when they'd be able to have a long dinner again.

She could ask it plainly without making it a test.

“What happens to Thursdays?” she said.

“I'll be away for some. Not every week.”

“Should we choose a different evening?”

“We could choose one when we have one.”

Eve chewed a wedge of orange. They had been meeting more or less each week, though often on a different day from the one they started with. For a long time Eve had used the next date as a small piece of evidence that the current one had gone all right. It struck her that Sophie might enjoy an evening with her without being asked to answer for the evening after that.

“Do you still want to see the terrible film?” she asked.

“Very much.”

“So when you're back, send me a picture of the listing and we'll find a day.”

“And if you're busy?”

“I'll tell you I'm busy. You can suggest another day or see the film without me. Just don't tell me whether the horse dies.”

“There isn't a horse.”

“In the reviews there is.”

“That wasn't a horse. That was somebody's description of the acting.”

Eve smiled. The director called from downstairs again, this time with a question about an artist who had been seen heading for the wrong end of the corridor. Sophie rubbed her hands clean with the napkin and stood.

“Go,” Eve said. “I want to see the model from all sides before I leave.”

Sophie went, then turned on the lowest step to say, “Keep the card. The title looks good in your hand.”

At home Eve didn't add the card to her collection of people who could solve wedding problems. She stood it on the shelf near the bowl where she kept things to show Julian. The programme logo was smaller than Sophie's name by a good margin. It was a pleasing piece of printing.

She sat at the table to send Sophie a picture of it standing upright. Then she reconsidered. From the angle she'd chosen, the photograph would show both Sophie's work email and the head of Dumpling, who was coming across the shelf with evident designs on whatever had been placed in his way. She lowered the phone and picked up the card before he could chew the corner.

She sent a message instead.

*I was glad to be there when they called for the lead producer.*

Sophie's reply arrived as Eve was setting the kettle on. *They were asking about the toilet.*

Eve could hear Sophie's voice so clearly in the line that she laughed before she had even put the kettle down. When she replied, she didn't ask what happened next. She put her phone away before she poured the water. There was half an orange left in her bag. She brought it to the table and ate it with her tea.
