# Chapter 068 — I Don't Have to Prove He's Awful

On Sunday morning, Eve wrote three reasons she did not want to see Owen again and disliked all three.

The first made him sound controlling. The second made her sound noble. The third was simply the first with better punctuation.

She crossed them out.

Dumpling sat beneath the table, occupied with a toy that had become wedged against a chair leg. Eve moved the chair half an inch. He retrieved the mouse, carried it to the other side of the table, and wedged it beneath another leg.

“At least one of us is committed to a method,” she said.

Her mother had called the previous evening. Eve hadn't answered because she was eating, then had replied by message that she would speak on Monday. She knew the likely question. What, precisely, was wrong with him?

The page in front of her looked like an attempt to provide evidence for a claim she had never made.

Owen had been courteous. He had listened. He had offered an apology without asking her to erase the conversation that prompted it. He had also described a future in which the parts of her life she was actively trying to build would become smaller and easier to put aside.

They could both mean well. The possibility felt much less useful than she wished.

She tore the page from the notebook, folded it once, and put it in the paper recycling. Then she cleaned the table because somebody was bringing her scarf back at noon and the table was sticky.

That was enough reason to clean a table. It did not mean she was reconsidering him.

At eleven forty, Owen messaged that his errand had finished early. He could wait until noon or leave the scarf with her another time.

*I'm home. Now is fine,* she replied.

She put Dumpling's water away from the entrance, checked the window latch, and took the rubbish downstairs before Owen arrived. When she returned, the cat was in his familiar chair. She did not lure him to the door or arrange him in a position from which he could deliver an opinion.

The buzzer sounded while she was washing her hands.

Owen stood outside the building with the gray scarf folded over his arm. Eve let him in and met him at her flat's door. He looked slightly windblown and was carrying a small bag from a hardware shop.

“Successful errand?” she asked.

“A replacement hinge. I bought two because the first will almost certainly be wrong.”

“A professional strategy?”

“A personal failing.”

He handed over the scarf. Her mother had folded it so precisely that it no longer resembled an object Eve owned.

“Thank you.”

“No problem.”

He glanced toward the stairs, then swallowed. It was warm in the stairwell, and the climb had brought color into his face.

“Would you like some water before you go?” Eve asked.

“Yes, please. If it isn't a nuisance.”

She invited him into the small entrance area and closed the front door before stepping farther into the flat. He left his shoes on the mat without being told and stayed where she indicated while she fetched a glass.

Dumpling lifted his head.

Eve felt herself notice the movement too sharply. Here was a stranger, nearby and mildly uncomfortable. In another version of her afternoon, she might have waited for the cat to supply a reason she could carry back to her mother.

She turned on the tap.

Behind her, Owen said, “Hello.”

Not to her.

Dumpling had climbed down from the chair. He walked into the entrance, sniffed the shopping bag, and looked up at Owen with an expression Eve had never found easy to interpret even before discovering how little she ought to trust her interpretations.

“May I?” Owen asked, lowering one hand a little.

“Let him come to you.”

Owen kept his fingers still. Dumpling sniffed them, rubbed his cheek against one knuckle, and walked away.

There was no hiss. No strategic leap between Eve and an unsuitable future. The cat went to his water as though the central question of the day had been settled by thirst.

“He's friendlier than I expected,” Owen said.

“Sometimes. He may have been checking whether you brought anything interesting.”

“Just hinges.”

“A limited audience.”

Owen laughed. Eve handed him the water and leaned against the wall, leaving him room to drink without feeling inspected.

On the table, the notebook lay closed. The discarded arguments were hidden by an envelope in the recycling box. She suddenly felt ashamed of having needed to write them.

Owen watched Dumpling step carefully around his bag.

“I thought about what you said yesterday,” he said.

Eve waited.

“I didn't mean that your work was worthless. Or that you should get rid of things you care about.”

“I believe you.”

“I like knowing what a week looks like. My last relationship involved a lot of plans changing. I think I talk about security as if everybody must want it in the same shape.”

It was more explanation than he owed her, offered without naming or criticizing the other person. Eve appreciated it.

“That makes sense,” she said.

He looked down at the glass.

“Would you be willing to have coffee once, without our mothers arranging the table?”

There it was. A reasonable request, made reasonably. She could hear her mother describing it later: one coffee, not a wedding.

Dumpling returned to the entrance and sat against the wall. He washed a paw with great care.

Eve could not pretend he was warning her. She could not pretend the absence of a warning meant anything about Owen's suitability either.

“No,” she said gently. “Thank you for asking, but I don't want to arrange another meeting.”

Owen nodded once, too quickly, then looked at the scarf in her hands.

“Is there someone else?”

The answer that would end the discomfort stood ready. Yes. A veterinarian, obviously serious, who could not be expected to tolerate this.

Eve did not use it.

“There's someone I'm interested in,” she said. “We haven't agreed to a relationship. But I wouldn't want to meet again even if there weren't.”

He looked back at her.

“I don't mean that cruelly,” she added. “I don't want to imply you should wait and see whether something else works.”

“No. That's fair.”

The disappointment in his voice made her want to offer a softer future possibility. She kept quiet until the urge passed.

Owen finished the water, thanked her, and put the glass where she indicated. Before opening the door, Eve moved Dumpling into the living room with his toy and closed the inner door. It was an ordinary precaution, not a comment on anyone's character.

Owen picked up his shopping bag.

“Your mother makes excellent rice,” he said.

“She does.”

“I hope she won't be upset with mine.”

“I'll speak to her myself.”

“Thank you.”

They said goodbye. Eve watched him reach the first turn of the stairs, then closed her door. There had been no dramatic moment that justified the whole decision to an audience. He had left with the wrong hinge, perhaps, and a disappointment she could not remove by being sufficiently kind.

She let Dumpling back into the hall.

He inspected the patch of floor where the shopping bag had stood and appeared disappointed too.

“You'd have kept him for the packaging,” she said.

The cat rubbed against her ankle. She bent to scratch his cheek, then stopped when he moved away.

For lunch, she heated some of the leftover rice and ate at the table. The gray scarf hung over a chair, creased neatly down its center. She looked at it while deciding whether to call her mother earlier than agreed.

Then her phone rang. Sophie.

“Are you busy?” her friend asked.

“Not especially.”

“Excellent. Tell me whether this sentence sounds aggressive.”

She read out a short message about a work assignment being passed to her without the corresponding hours removed from another job. Eve listened twice and suggested changing one phrase that sounded like a personal accusation into the specific scheduling problem.

“That's better,” Sophie said. “Thank you. How was the family meal?”

“An introduction in disguise.”

“Oh.”

“A nice man. Wrong for me.”

“That's unfortunate.”

Eve looked at the empty glass on the counter.

“He brought my scarf back. Dumpling liked him.”

“Did he?”

“He rubbed against his hand. There was nothing dramatic.”

“And?”

Eve pressed her lips together.

“And I spent a minute wanting the cat to be dramatic.”

Sophie was quiet.

“I didn't bring him out or test him,” Eve said. “I just noticed myself waiting.”

“What did you want permission for?”

“To disappoint everybody.”

“I'm not disappointed.”

“You haven't had to make the rice.”

Sophie laughed, then said, “You aren't required to discover a crime before saying you don't want coffee.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Eve looked at the recycling box. She could see the folded edge of her page beneath the envelope.

“More than I did this morning.”

They talked for a few minutes about Sophie's message. She had not sent it yet; she wanted to check the work schedule first. Eve didn't turn the call into a request for her friend to spend the whole afternoon rehearsing an argument with her mother.

Afterward, she washed the glass and put it away. Then she sent Owen a final thanks for returning the scarf, with no promise that they should stay in touch. He replied with a simple acknowledgment.

Her mother messaged before dinner.

*Did he get there all right?*

*Yes. He returned the scarf. I told him I don't want another meeting. I'll talk to you tomorrow as planned.*

For a while, there was no response.

Her mother was coming into the city on Monday to collect a repaired coat. During their earlier exchange, they had arranged to meet near the station afterward. Eve had offered an hour before an afternoon editing session, and her mother had accepted it as though Eve's calendar were a mildly eccentric preference.

She considered cancelling. They could argue on the phone, which would at least save the price of tea. But that would also let the whole visit shrink to the only subject on which they disagreed.

Eve opened the tailor's address her mother had sent and checked the walking route from the station. There was a café along it with seats away from the door. She sent the name and asked whether it suited her mother, rather than announcing that she had organized everything.

The reply came quickly this time: yes, and there was no need to collect her from the platform.

Eve deleted the offer she had already begun typing.

She put the phone on the shelf and began cutting vegetables. Dumpling settled on the chair beside the table, close enough to watch and too far away to improve her knife work.

When the phone finally sounded, her mother's message was short.

*I don't understand.*

Eve read it, felt the familiar need to make the next sentence solve everything, and breathed out.

*I know. I'll still come tomorrow.*

She set the phone aside again. The vegetables needed her attention now. Tomorrow's conversation would need a different kind.
