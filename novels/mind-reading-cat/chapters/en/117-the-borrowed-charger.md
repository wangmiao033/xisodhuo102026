# Chapter 117 — The Borrowed Charger

Eve's phone gave its low-battery warning while they were deciding whether to have tea.

It was an ordinary small sound. She would later resent how ordinary it had been, as though the phone might have chosen something more informative for an evening about to become unpleasant.

"I'll make it," Julian said.

"There's enough water in the kettle."

She went to her desk and reached beneath the lamp for the long white charging cable.

Her fingers found the short cable she used with her portable battery. She lifted it, stared at it, and checked the floor. Nothing. She checked behind the desk, where cables occasionally slipped between the wall and the leg. Still nothing.

"Have you seen my charger?"

"Which one?"

"The long one. From here."

"By the bed."

She went into the bedroom. The long cable was plugged into the socket on the far side, neatly coiled except for the part running to Julian's phone.

Eve stopped.

On its own, the sight was completely reasonable. A person charging a phone required a charger. She had two compatible cables. He had left one on her desk.

"Did you move it?" she asked.

Julian appeared in the doorway. "Last night. I couldn't reach the bedside table with the short one."

"I use it at my desk."

"I left the other there."

"It doesn't reach."

"I thought it did."

She took the short cable from her pocket and held it up. "The socket's behind the desk. This reaches the edge if I put the phone on the floor."

"All right. I'll change them back."

He stepped past her and unplugged his phone.

The problem was finished. The cable was free. Eve should have taken it and returned to the kettle.

Instead she said, "You could have asked."

Julian straightened slowly.

"About a charger?"

There it was: the small incredulous note that made her feel foolish before she had explained why she cared.

"About moving something I use for work."

"You said I could borrow it."

"I said you could charge your phone."

"I didn't know those were different permissions."

The kettle clicked off in the kitchen.

Eve could hear the refrigerator humming. She could hear herself preparing a sentence much more polished than the feeling behind it.

"Because you don't need to know where anything goes. You just arrive, and somehow it becomes my job to put the room back afterward."

Julian looked down at the cable in his hand.

"I put this somewhere deliberately. I didn't drop it on the floor."

"Somewhere that worked for you."

"Yes. For once."

The last two words landed hard.

Eve folded her arms. "What's that supposed to mean?"

He drew a breath through his nose and let it out before answering.

"It means I asked where a bowl went yesterday and you took it away from me. It means you tell me to make myself comfortable, then correct the way I do anything."

"I don't correct everything."

"Not everything. Enough that I spend half the evening wondering whether I'm in the wrong chair."

"You could have said."

"So could you. You've apparently been putting the room back for days."

She had. There was a towel behind his accusation, and moved pencils, and groceries bought without anybody asking her to buy them. There was also the fact that he had washed the dishes while she sat down, had brought food, had never asked for a fresh towel or a special breakfast.

Knowing this did not make her less angry. It made the anger harder to place.

"I'm allowed to want things where I leave them."

"Yes. And I'm allowed to be confused when you keep asking me to stay and then seem annoyed that I'm here."

"I didn't ask you tonight."

Julian went still.

She regretted the sentence at once, but its factual accuracy tempted her to stand behind it. He had come for dinner. He had stayed. His phone was beside the bed as if the conclusion had already happened.

"No," he said. "You didn't."

"I didn't mean you weren't welcome."

"I'm trying to work out what you did mean."

Dumpling walked into the doorway carrying one of Eve's soft slippers by its heel. He dropped it between them, looked up, and made a brief questioning sound.

Usually she would have laughed. Julian might have called it a contribution to negotiations. Tonight neither did.

Eve moved the slipper out of the walking space with her foot. Dumpling sniffed it, then withdrew beneath the bed. His tail disappeared last.

"We shouldn't do this over him," Julian said quietly.

"Then come into the kitchen."

They did. The tea had not been made. Eve poured water into both mugs, forgot which held a bag already, and poured it into an empty one. She stared at the pale hot water.

Julian reached toward the tea tin, then stopped.

She hated that small hesitation more than the charger. He was performing his own accusation in front of her, except he probably wasn't performing anything. She could not tell. She was too busy feeling watched.

"You can make tea," she said.

"I know."

He put a bag in the mug. She moved to the other side of the counter.

For a while they watched the color spread.

"I thought leaving things here made sense," he said. "Instead of carrying the same bag back and forth every time."

"Leaving a toothbrush makes sense. Reorganizing where I work doesn't."

"I moved one cable."

"And I couldn't find it."

"For thirty seconds."

The dismissiveness stung, even if he was right about the time.

"So I have to lose an entire afternoon before it counts?"

"That isn't what I said."

"It's what you sound like."

He rubbed his forehead. Eve recognized tiredness and refused, for one second, to care about it. She was tired too. She was tired of holding the words she wanted behind the words that would make an evening pleasant.

"I live here," she said.

Julian looked at her.

The sentence had finally supplied what all the smaller ones had been reaching for. It also sounded like a door being closed in his face.

"I know," he said. "You could hardly make it clearer."

He set his mug down before drinking from it.

"That was unfair," she said, although she no longer knew whether she meant his answer or her own words.

"Which part?"

She gripped the edge of the counter. "I like you here. I don't know how to do everything exactly the way I did when you weren't here."

"I'm not asking you to."

"But I haven't agreed to stop, either."

He was quiet.

From beneath the bed came a soft scraping noise, then silence. Eve nearly went to check on Dumpling, grateful for a task that would take her out of the conversation. Instead she looked through the bedroom doorway. The cat's orange flank was visible beyond the bed leg. He was lying down.

She stayed at the counter.

"I don't want a drawer," Julian said at last.

The sentence surprised her.

"I didn't say you did."

"I know. But I feel as if we're arguing about one. I haven't been trying to move in."

"Neither have I."

"Then why are we doing this every night?"

It was the question she should have asked before the battery warning.

Eve looked at his untouched tea. Because she liked him. Because ending a lovely evening felt wasteful when the evening could continue. Because the first few times she had wanted him to stay, and afterward wanting had become difficult to distinguish from not wanting to disappoint him.

None of those answers belonged entirely to a white cable.

"I don't know," she said.

He nodded once. His jaw was tight, and she wished she could say something that would loosen it without giving up what she had finally managed to say.

"I should go home tonight."

Panic arrived before agreement. It was small but sharp, a childish conviction that if he left while upset he would continue leaving forever.

She knew better. Knowing better did not stop her from wanting to say stay.

"Okay," she said.

Julian collected his phone, put his toothbrush in his bag, then hesitated over the clean shirt on the chair. Eve watched from the kitchen doorway, unable to stop herself reading meaning into every object.

"Are you taking everything?"

"I need a clean shirt tomorrow."

"Right."

He folded it once. Not angrily. She almost wanted him to do it angrily so she could be certain of something.

His bag would not close. The corner of the folded shirt had caught in the zip. He tugged once, stopped, and opened it again.

Eve nearly reached to hold the sides together. Yesterday she would have done it without thinking. Now even helping with a bag seemed to require choosing what it meant. She kept her hands at her sides, annoyed by the sudden difficulty of having them.

He tucked the shirt farther down and tried again. The zip moved smoothly.

On the shelf beside him sat the drawing book, with their two badly drawn cups inside it. Eve could see the edge of the loose paper. Nothing had happened to the drawing. It was still funny, still something they had enjoyed making together. She wanted to show it to him, as if the paper could remind him of a better room.

She left it on the shelf.

At the door he turned back.

"I shouldn't have said thirty seconds as if that settled it."

"I shouldn't have made it sound like you leave a trail of damage. You don't."

He nodded, but neither apology made the room easy.

"Can we talk tomorrow?" she asked.

"Yes. After dinner?"

"Here?"

He looked at her for a moment. "Here is fine. But I'm going home afterward."

She swallowed.

"All right."

He put on his shoes. She did not kiss him merely to force a reassuring ending, and he did not offer one as proof. They said goodnight with a space between them that felt larger than the hall.

After the door closed, Eve carried the charger back to her desk. It reached exactly where she needed it to reach. She plugged in her phone and stood looking at the small charging symbol.

There. Correctly arranged. No satisfaction at all.

Dumpling emerged from beneath the bed when she sat on the floor to retrieve the slipper. He rubbed once against her shoulder, then walked toward the kitchen. She checked his water and found it fresh. His meal had been eaten. He wanted company, perhaps, or the familiar sequence of somebody moving through the room.

She sat near his bed rather than lifting him onto her lap. After a while he settled, paws tucked beneath his chest.

Eve's phone lit up on the desk.

Home, Julian had written. Tomorrow at seven?

Yes. Seven.

She considered adding a heart. She considered adding a long explanation of how she had never meant for any of this to happen. Both felt like attempts to finish tomorrow's conversation tonight.

She sent only the answer.

Then she went back to the kitchen and emptied two cold mugs of tea.
