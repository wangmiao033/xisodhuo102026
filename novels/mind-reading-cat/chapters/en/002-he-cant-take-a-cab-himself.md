# Chapter 002 — He Can't Take a Cab Himself

The cat refused to let go of Eve's sleeve.

Every time she moved her wrist, the little hook of claw moved with it. She stood at reception with one arm inside the box and the other supporting its base, in a position unlikely to feature in a guide to responsible animal transport.

The woman behind the desk came around immediately.

“I'm Tessa. You called?”

“Yes. Sorry about the floor.”

“Floor can wait. Let's get the box supported.”

She cleared a section of counter. Eve lowered it carefully, discovering that her shoulders had been somewhere around her ears for the last twenty minutes.

A man in dark blue scrubs emerged from a side room. He took in the box, the soaked towel, and Eve's captured arm.

“Don't lift him by that.”

“I wasn't planning to carry him by my cuff.”

“Good. Keep your hand where it is.”

His tone was so flat that for a moment she couldn't tell whether they were arguing. He opened a door with his elbow and asked Tessa to help move the box. Eve followed alongside it, bent at an angle that made her feel like an unusually inconvenient piece of luggage.

“On here,” he said.

They placed the box on a low examination surface. The room smelled clean without being perfumed. A poster of a cheerful dog advised visitors to tell the team if their pet was anxious. Eve wanted one that applied to the person.

The man glanced at her.

“I'm Julian. When did you find him?”

“About half an hour ago. At the bus stop by the convenience store. Someone said he'd been around for a few days. I don't know whose he is.”

“Did you see what happened to the leg?”

“No.”

“Anything given to him? Food, medicine?”

“Nothing. I bought the towel.”

Julian nodded. He wasn't looking for an impressive answer. Apparently not knowing was usable information here.

He worked slowly around the cat, letting it withdraw beneath the towel when it needed to. Tessa stayed near the box. No one attempted to demonstrate how brave or agreeable the animal could be.

Eve kept her fingers still.

“Is he—” She stopped before asking whether it was bad. Bad wasn't an especially useful unit of measurement.

“I need to examine him before I can tell you much.” Julian looked up. “And I need your arm to stay relaxed.”

“That may be beyond my qualifications.”

“Your sleeve, then.”

Tessa's mouth twitched.

The cat's paw loosened slightly. Not enough to release Eve, but enough that the fabric stopped pulling tight. Julian adjusted the towel with a patience his voice hadn't suggested.

Eve watched his hands. They weren't theatrical hands. They did the next small thing, stopped when necessary, then continued. He spoke to the cat in the same quiet tone he used for her, except he didn't appear to expect it to answer questions about payment.

Payment.

“Before you do anything expensive,” she said.

Julian paused.

Heat rose from her collar to her face. “I mean, I'm not saying don't help. I just need to know what I'm agreeing to. I can't say yes to everything and work out how to pay afterward.”

“All right.”

She had prepared for a different response. A raised eyebrow, perhaps. A remark about taking responsibility. The words she'd been ready to defend herself with collided in her throat.

“I found him,” she said unnecessarily. “He couldn't exactly take a cab himself.”

“I understand.”

“Do you? Because you said—”

“I said not to lift him by your sleeve.” Julian's gaze flicked to the cat. “That was about the cat. Not your finances.”

Eve stared at him.

Tessa found something absorbing to do with a pen.

“Oh,” Eve said.

Rain drummed against a high window. The cat shifted, and all three of them stopped talking at once.

Julian explained what he could assess now and what might need a separate decision after that. Tessa brought over a written estimate. No one slid it beneath Eve's hand while she was distracted. They gave her space to read it.

She had spent the afternoon being told a company valued her while calculating precisely how much it cost not to keep her.

This felt painfully different. Nobody claimed that money was irrelevant. Nobody required her to pretend it was.

Eve asked which part was for the examination, what was included in the initial care, and whether they would call before any additional charge. Her voice became steadier by the third question.

“We will,” Tessa said. “I'll put that on the record.”

Eve checked her balance. This time she didn't immediately hide it from herself. There was rent, food, the final payment still to come. The number on the estimate would matter. It wouldn't mean she couldn't eat.

“I can cover this,” she said. “If something else is needed, please talk to me first.”

Julian nodded. “That's what we're agreeing to.”

Not everything. This.

There was a box for her relationship to the animal. Eve stopped there.

“Finder?” she asked.

“That's fine,” Tessa said.

Not owner. Not savior. Just the person who had found him and brought him here. Eve wrote the word with unexpected relief. She could take responsibility for this decision without having to explain how every later one would turn out. Beside her, the cat made a low, irritated sound at a movement beyond the door. Julian waited until it passed before continuing. Apparently the cat was allowed uncertainty, too.

She signed the form.

The cat chose that moment to release her cuff.

Eve slowly withdrew her arm. Three tiny pulled threads stood up near her wrist like a commentary she hadn't requested.

“Very professional,” she told it. “Waited until the paperwork.”

Julian glanced at the sleeve. “You can sit down now.”

“I thought this was the exercise portion.”

“The chair is behind you.”

She sat.

Once he had enough information to discuss the next steps, Julian showed her where the injury was without encouraging her to poke at it herself. He didn't call the cat lucky as a substitute for explaining. There was a wound needing care and a sore front leg; the assessment so far didn't suggest the catastrophe she had been constructing in her head, but he still wanted to watch how the cat settled and reassess before it left.

“He'll stay here tonight?”

“If you agree to the arrangement Tessa has explained. We'll update you before collection tomorrow. If anything changes sooner, we'll call.”

Eve nodded, then realized she was nodding at the idea of tomorrow.

Tomorrow she had planned to wake up employed. Her calendar still contained a client presentation at ten. Now it contained an orange-and-white animal and a phone call she actually needed to answer.

“Have you checked for an owner?” she asked.

“We'll check what we can here,” Tessa said. “And help you with a found notice. Not every lost animal has information we can trace. Keep the location and time.”

Eve opened her notes and typed them in. It felt good to have an ordinary fact to record.

At the doorway, a woman holding an empty carrier asked Tessa about a delayed appointment. Tessa answered, promised a realistic update, and returned without turning the visitor into a problem for Eve to dislike.

The clinic was busy in the way a kitchen was busy: not because every moment was a crisis, but because many small things needed doing in the right order.

Julian went to speak to another member of staff. Eve remained beside the cat, no longer attached.

It lay half hidden beneath the towel. Its eyes tracked her when she shifted, then followed Julian when he returned. He set something down out of reach and checked the record.

For no sensible reason, Eve felt a little offended that the cat was considering a replacement already.

“His shirt is less absorbent,” she whispered. “Think it through.”

Julian looked up.

“Nothing.”

“I wasn't asking.”

“Excellent. We're both doing very well.”

This time she was almost certain the corner of his mouth moved.

Tessa asked what name to put on the temporary record.

“Cat?” Eve suggested.

“We have several.”

“Unemployed Cat.”

“That's most of them.”

The cat had tucked its nose into the towel, leaving one round white patch above a fold of orange fur. Eve remembered the little dumplings her mother made, always one misshapen specimen in a batch, the one she gave herself because nobody had to photograph dinner.

“Dumpling,” she said. Then, quickly, “Just for the form. If someone is looking for him, he'll have his own name.”

Tessa wrote it down without making it a ceremony.

Julian addressed the cat. “Dumpling.”

No response.

“Strong start,” Eve said.

“He doesn't know that's his name.”

“Neither did I ten seconds ago.”

The sound that escaped Tessa was definitely a laugh.

Before leaving, Eve took a photograph for the found notice. No dramatic angle. No close-up of the wound. Just enough of the cat's markings to help, with the identification details held back as Tessa advised. She saved it instead of posting a story about what a terrible day she'd had.

The cat didn't need to become evidence that she was still a good person.

At reception she paid the agreed amount and saved the receipt. Tessa gave her the clinic's contact details and checked the number they should call. Eve read the digits aloud twice because she knew she'd spend all night imagining she had written one wrong.

Julian came out carrying her damp towel in a separate bag.

“He has clean bedding,” he said before she could ask. “You can take this home.”

“Right. Thank you.”

She stood there a moment too long.

“You don't need to wait,” he added.

“I know.”

She didn't move.

He looked toward the room, then back at her. “We'll call.”

That was all. No promise that nothing could go wrong. No suggestion that her leaving proved she cared less.

Eve took the bag.

Outside, the rain had thinned to a fine mist. Her hands felt strangely vacant without a box. She called Sophie, who answered over the clatter of something being dragged across a floor.

“Tell me you bought dinner.”

“I bought a towel.”

There was a pause.

“Eve.”

“And used a taxi.”

“Are you all right?”

The question nearly undid her. Not because the answer was no, exactly, but because it could be untidy.

“I got fired,” she said. “And I found a cat. Can we have something cheap?”

Sophie exhaled. “Yes. Send me where you are.”

Eve sent the location, then looked back through the clinic window.

The front door had a simple sign showing the opening hours. For tonight, someone was inside who knew what to do next.

She let herself walk away. At the corner, she checked the clinic number was saved correctly, then put the phone in her pocket instead of turning back.
