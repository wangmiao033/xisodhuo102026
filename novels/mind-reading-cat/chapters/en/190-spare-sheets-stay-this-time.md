# Chapter 190 — Spare Sheets Stay This Time

The sheets had traveled farther than anyone intended.

Eve found them on the chair after her mother left for Monday morning's meeting: a folded, faded set tied with a narrow strip of fabric, the same ones Eve had once left at her mother's house because there had been no room in her bag.

She had said yes when her mother asked whether she still wanted them. Now they occupied nearly the entire chair.

Dumpling approached, sniffed one corner, and looked at Eve.

“They're not for you.”

He put one paw on them.

“That's a strong counterargument.”

She moved the bundle to the bed before he could turn possession into a settled matter, then stripped the bedding her mother had used during the visit. The apartment felt peculiar with the suitcase zipped and standing by the door. Not empty yet, but preparing to be.

Her mother would come back for lunch, collect her things, and take an afternoon train. Julian's aunt had an earlier departure from the other station, arranged with his parents. Eve knew the times because people had told her, not because she had assembled a command center around them.

She put a wash on and opened her laptop.

For the next hour, the visit existed at the edges of work: an unfamiliar tea bag beside her mug, the folded sheets still waiting for cupboard space, a note about the soup left on the fridge. She completed a set of image adjustments and sent the client a short update before checking her phone.

A message from Julian's aunt showed the repaired lamp knob beside a train ticket. Successful mission. Thank you for dinner.

Eve replied that she had enjoyed it, then resisted adding questions about platforms. The woman had already boarded.

At lunchtime her mother returned with a folder tucked beneath one arm and the satisfied fatigue of a morning that had gone well enough. She took off her shoes and asked what there was to eat.

“Your soup,” Eve said.

“Good. I trust the cook.”

They heated two portions and ate at the table. Without visitors' conversation filling every pause, Eve could hear the washing machine making its uneven attempt to distribute a load.

Her mother glanced toward the bedroom. “You found the sheets.”

“I found their entire residential footprint.”

“They fold smaller if you do the corners properly.”

“Then they've been disappointed in me for years.”

Her mother smiled, but when she finished eating, she did not demonstrate. She washed her bowl and left Eve to decide where the bundle belonged.

Eve appreciated that more than she would have appreciated a neatly reorganized cupboard.

While her mother checked the suitcase, Eve brought out the scarf from yesterday. This time it was accepted and wound around a neck that actually wanted it.

“Next time,” Eve said, “we should decide the sleeping arrangement before you're holding a ticket.”

Her mother paused with one hand on the suitcase handle.

“Was the sofa awful?”

“Only the cushion I chose. And getting up before the cat had finished with my feet.”

“I could stay somewhere nearby.”

“You could. Or stay here if it works. I liked having you. I also had an early deadline, and I didn't think through what sharing the room would do to my morning.”

Her mother nodded. “Tell me that when we arrange it.”

“I will.”

It was such an unremarkable answer that Eve almost wished she had asked for something more difficult, to justify the rehearsal she had performed in her head. Instead, she helped carry the suitcase down the stairs because her mother accepted the offer, and they walked to the bus stop together.

At the stop, her mother noticed a thread caught on Eve's sleeve. She removed it with two fingers.

“You don't have to get on.”

“I know.”

“I'm saying it in case you're planning to buy emergency bread at the station.”

Eve laughed, then hugged her before the bus arrived.

Her mother's bag bumped her hip. The hug was warm and slightly awkward with the luggage between them, like every departure that involved more objects than hands. When the doors opened, Eve stepped back. She waited until her mother had a seat and lifted her hand through the window.

Then she went home alone.

The silence in the flat was not immediately pleasant. She had wanted space to work, and now she had it, along with three mugs to wash and nobody to make an observation about the mustard.

Dumpling met her halfway down the hall, pressed his head briefly against her shin, and continued toward the kitchen. Eve followed. Ordinary obligations were useful after a goodbye.

She cleaned his water bowl, checked his usual supplies, and put the laundry on the rack. The sheets from the chair remained folded on the bed.

Julian called during a break in the late afternoon. Behind him, people were leaving a room, their voices fading as he found a quieter place. He asked whether her mother had got away all right.

“She sent a message from the train.”

“My aunt too. My mother says the lamp fitting has been mentioned more often than the family.”

“It was a very successful fitting.”

He looked tired. Eve asked how the day had gone before describing hers, and he told her about a practice conversation in which he had answered a question nobody had asked. He had noticed halfway through and had to stop himself rather than trying to make the answer become relevant.

“Painfully familiar,” she said.

“I thought you'd appreciate it.”

They smiled at each other for a moment.

“Final week,” he said. “It feels longer from this end.”

“It feels longer from the sofa.”

“What happened to the sofa?”

She explained the decorative cushion, then showed him the spare sheets.

“Those look familiar.”

“They're mine from years ago. My mother has returned them to their irresponsible owner.”

“Where will they go?”

“That's the problem. The cupboard has decided it's full.”

She opened it to demonstrate. Towels occupied one shelf. A spare blanket filled another. A collection of bags hung from a hook and fell out as soon as she touched it.

Julian laughed.

“I have room for sheets,” he said, then stopped. “That wasn't a proposal for relocating your cupboard.”

“Good. The cupboard hasn't agreed to anything.”

She pushed the bags back and sat on the edge of the bed. There was a shirt of his folded on the small chair, left after an earlier evening and washed with her own clothes. At his flat, she had a pair of socks and a book she kept meaning to finish. None of those objects amounted to a household. They did, however, require more decisions than simply pretending they had appeared by weather.

“I think,” she said, “we should talk about where people stay. When we're both actually here.”

“Visitors?”

“Visitors. Us. Not all the same conversation, but apparently they share a cupboard.”

He leaned back against the wall behind him.

“Do you want to do that now?”

She could hear someone calling a name in the distance. Not his, but the sound reminded her that he was between course sessions, standing in a borrowed corner.

“Not the whole thing. I don't want to make a housing decision because I've run out of towel space.”

“That's probably wise.”

“And I don't want to accidentally promise that anybody can stay with either of us just because we're together.”

“Neither do I.”

He rubbed a hand over his face and continued more slowly.

“When my aunt visits, my parents usually arrange things with her. If someone asks to stay with me, I can answer for my flat. I shouldn't answer for yours.”

“Same with my mother.”

“What about us?”

The question warmed her and unsettled her in equal measure. It was not unexpected. It was simply clearer than the shirt on the chair.

“I like waking up with you,” she said. “When that's what we've planned. I also like knowing where my things are and having somewhere to work without turning every surface into a shared negotiation.”

“Both can be true.”

“That's what I'm hoping.”

He looked at her carefully, not as if there were a preferred answer hidden behind the question.

“I like my flat,” he said. “There are parts of it I wouldn't miss. There are parts I would. I haven't thought all of that through.”

“Then let's think.”

“After this week?”

“After this week. Properly. No suitcase halfway through the door.”

He agreed. They did not choose an address, assign drawer space, or turn the conversation into a promise that one person's home would absorb the other. They arranged a time to talk when he was back and had slept, leaving the exact day to confirm around the course's final arrangements.

Before he went, she held up the shirt.

“Do you need this?”

“Not urgently.”

“I'll keep it clean here until you collect it.”

“Thank you.”

She liked the small directness of that. A shirt could be a shirt without either becoming proof of a future or being hurried out of the room to avoid suggesting one.

After the call, Eve stood in front of the cupboard again.

She took out two worn pillowcases she had kept for no clear reason and set them aside to use as cleaning cloths. She folded the spare blanket differently. The sheets fitted, not beautifully, but without needing another home assigned to them.

On the fridge, the note about soup had begun to curl at one corner. Eve pressed it flat and found the notebook where she had started recording yesterday's improvised dinner. She finished the ingredients while she could still remember them, adding a warning that the amount of stock depended on the pot.

Then she sent Julian a photograph of the page.

His reply arrived a few minutes later: I see the signature dish has changed ownership.

Joint historical embarrassment, she wrote. Current recipe mine.

She made herself tea and sat at the table rather than on the sofa. The flat had room for her again, but now she noticed the spaces differently: where a second person stood while she cooked, where a visitor's bag blocked the chair, where Dumpling liked to cross without being diverted.

These were not arguments against wanting more. They were things she would need to describe when she asked for it.

Her mother sent another message, this time from home. Arrived. Did the sheets fit?

Eventually, Eve replied.

Good. Don't save them for imaginary guests.

Eve looked toward the closed cupboard and smiled. They would stay here this time. So would she, tonight, with her own work on the table and Dumpling taking possession of the sofa she no longer needed as a bed.

Julian had one more week away. There would be time for the larger conversation when he returned.
