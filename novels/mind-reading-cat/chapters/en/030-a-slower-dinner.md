# Chapter 30: A Slower Dinner

Mabel arrived at Eve's door carrying a casserole in a bag that looked capable of surviving a flood.

“Don't take the handles,” she said. “Take the bottom.”

Eve obeyed. The weight of dinner settled against her palms, warm through two folded towels.

“I thought you said something simple.”

“It is simple. It isn't weightless.”

Behind Mabel stood Julian with a loaf of bread. He held it up as if providing evidence that he hadn't ignored the instruction not to bring anything complicated.

“My contribution has already been cooked.”

“Excellent,” Mabel said. “One of you can use a knife.”

They had settled on Eve's apartment because Mabel had offered to bring supper and Eve had offered the table. Mabel's little kitchen could accommodate three people only if one stood in the sink, she claimed. Biscuit, fed and walked, was asleep at home; she intended to return before he decided to complain to the furniture.

There were no interviews to finish. No forms. The walking guide was published, with its date checked, its modest claims, and its missing stretch of distinctive street that had revealed too much about Mabel's neighborhood.

She had spent the afternoon clearing her table of work anyway.

Now she understood that removing the laptop had exposed how small the table was.

“There's an extension,” she said.

“Then extend it,” said Mabel.

“I mean, theoretically.”

Julian set the bread down. “Where does the theory begin?”

“Underneath. Please don't judge the screws.”

He crouched beside the table while Eve supported the loose leaf. For a moment they were both looking up into its underside, their shoulders almost touching, trying to determine which piece had been installed backward by someone who had followed the diagram with enormous confidence.

“You assembled this?” he asked.

“In my defense, the drawing had no emotional context.”

“The bracket is facing the wall.”

“Yes. For privacy.”

He laughed, a brief sound she could hear without worrying about whether she had remembered to switch on a microphone.

They lifted the leaf into place. Julian didn't reach for a tool kit or announce that he would come back to fix the entire table. He showed her the catch that had slipped free, and she pressed it home herself.

Mabel had already found the plates.

“Do either of you intend to wash your hands before performing surgery on the bread?”

Dumpling watched them from the doorway with the offended stillness of a landlord witnessing unauthorized alterations.

Eve had left his usual quiet corner undisturbed. His carrier remained open beneath the sideboard, blanket inside, and nobody approached him. He could join the company or continue supervising from a distance.

He chose distance until Julian sat down.

Then he walked across the room, stepped onto Eve's low storage chest, and inspected the newly expanded table. Eve moved the chest back before he could use it as a launch platform.

“Ground floor only,” she told him.

Dumpling blinked.

Mabel put the casserole on a trivet. Steam rose when she lifted the lid: potatoes, carrots, chicken, and enough herbs to make Eve's kitchen smell as if someone lived there on purpose.

“This is too much.”

“Leftovers,” said Mabel. “A concept your generation keeps trying to replace with subscription services.”

“I subscribe to rice.”

“Rice needs friends.”

Julian took the seat nearest the wall. Eve started toward the one beside him, intending to leave Mabel the easier route to the door.

Dumpling got there first.

He sprang onto the empty chair, tucked his front paws beneath him, and settled with extraordinary finality.

Eve stopped.

“There are three people.”

The cat closed his eyes.

“And three chairs.”

One ear turned toward Julian.

Eve looked at him. Julian looked back, then very deliberately at the narrow strip of chair visible around Dumpling.

“I don't think I fit.”

Mabel pulled the folding stool away from the fridge. “You can argue with him after the potatoes.”

They rearranged themselves. Mabel took the clear seat, Eve the stool at the end, and Dumpling occupied the chair between Eve and Julian, looking less like a successful host than a particularly smug obstacle.

If he had meant to bring them together, he had an astonishingly poor understanding of furniture.

Eve caught herself making that assumption and reached for the serving spoon instead. Maybe the chair was simply warm. Maybe it was the one he had wanted all along.

“Before we eat,” she said, “I wanted to ask about the bakery's opening—”

“No,” said Mabel.

Eve paused with the spoon in midair.

“The new hours for the guide.”

“Tomorrow.”

“It would only take—”

“Your chicken would only take three minutes to become less pleasant.”

Julian placed a slice of bread on Eve's plate.

“I believe the chairwoman has ruled.”

Mabel nodded toward Dumpling. “Chairman. Apparently.”

For the first few minutes Eve kept feeling the absence of something. Her phone was facedown on the sideboard. There was no useful shot of steam, no clean overhead composition, no reason to ask Mabel to repeat a line while facing the window.

She tasted the food while it was hot.

Then she took a second mouthful before deciding what to say about the first.

Mabel asked Julian whether he had always wanted to work with animals.

“No,” he said. “When I was seven, I wanted to operate a crane.”

Eve looked up. “That's very specific.”

“There was a building site across from our apartment. The operator had the highest seat in the neighborhood.”

“Status,” Mabel said.

“An uninterrupted lunch break, I imagined.”

“So what changed?” Eve asked.

“I found out they still had to climb down.”

She laughed, then waited. He tore a piece of bread before adding that he had liked biology later, and practical work, and problems where observation mattered more than sounding impressive. It wasn't a dramatic childhood rescue. He had made decisions, learned things, changed his mind about some of them.

Eve liked that he didn't improve the story for dinner.

Mabel said she had wanted to repair clocks when she was a girl.

“You sew,” Eve said, then winced at how that sounded. “I mean, I assumed—”

“I can want more than one thing across a lifetime.”

“Right.”

“The clock shop wouldn't take me on. The alterations shop did. Later I discovered I preferred people who could explain where the problem pinched.”

“Clocks are notoriously uncommunicative,” Julian said.

“Customers aren't always better.”

Mabel described a man who had insisted that his trousers had shrunk evenly in the wardrobe, every pair, while his waist remained exactly as it had been in 1986. She had let out the seams without arguing about the laws of physics.

Eve laughed hard enough to put down her fork.

The conversation wandered to clothes they had kept for unreasonable lengths of time. Julian admitted to a jacket with a broken zip. Eve admitted to a pair of boots that hurt after twenty minutes and had therefore accompanied her through years of carefully planned optimism.

“Bring the jacket next time,” Mabel said.

“I can pay for the repair.”

“You can. I'm not running a charity for men who refuse to replace zips.”

He accepted that without the embarrassed insistence that she needn't trouble herself.

Eve reached for another carrot. She had been so determined not to reduce Mabel to someone who needed help that she had almost missed how easily Mabel could offer a skill, name its worth, and still enjoy their company.

Dumpling stretched one paw toward Julian's sleeve.

Julian moved his arm away from the table edge. “No casserole for you.”

The paw stayed out.

“He might want the sleeve,” Eve said.

“He can submit a request after dinner.”

Eve felt the odd little tug she sometimes felt around the two of them: the cat reaching before anyone spoke, Julian lowering his hand to let him sniff, an arrangement that seemed to exist just outside her understanding.

Then Dumpling hooked a claw into the chair cushion and had to shake it free. Whatever secrets he possessed, upholstery still defeated him.

Halfway through the meal, Mabel's phone chimed. She checked the message and smiled.

“Biscuit is apparently snoring through the wall. My neighbor says he approves of my absence.”

“Does she want you to come back?” Eve asked.

“She wants the recipe. She smelled it in the hall.”

Mabel typed with one finger, then put the phone away.

Eve's own phone remained quiet. She had silenced notifications. For once, silence didn't feel like evidence that a project was failing somewhere she couldn't see.

When the casserole was nearly empty, Julian asked what Eve liked doing when she wasn't making something for other people to look at.

She had an answer ready for work questions. This one took longer.

“Walking around stationery shops,” she said finally.

Mabel looked pleased. “Buying?”

“Mostly imagining that the right notebook will make me a different person.”

“Has it worked?” Julian asked.

“I own nine unfinished versions of myself.”

“What else?”

“Bad mystery films. Not cleverly bad. Actually bad. The kind where you can identify the murderer because they're the only person with a second costume.”

Julian nodded solemnly. “A financially motivated clue.”

She asked him the same question. He liked repairing small things if the repair wasn't urgent, he said. Radio plays. A particular walking route with very little traffic. He didn't give her his entire life in exchange for hers, and she didn't need him to.

At the end of the table, Mabel scraped the last spoonful of carrots onto her own plate.

“Good,” she said.

“What is?” Eve asked.

“You've stopped interviewing him.”

Eve opened her mouth, closed it, and drank some water.

Julian had the decency to look at his plate. The corner of his mouth was less considerate.

They washed up together, Mabel issuing instructions from the table because she had cooked. Julian washed; Eve dried. Once, she reached for a bowl before he had rinsed it and got a splash across her wrist.

“Your production schedule is too aggressive,” he said.

“My assistant is very slow.”

“I'm unpaid.”

“You've eaten half a loaf.”

“That's catering.”

Outside, the light had faded while nobody was using it.

Mabel left first, taking her empty bag and declining an escort with a pointed reminder that she had managed the route in daylight, darkness, and several governments. Julian walked as far as the building entrance with her because he was leaving too, not because they persuaded her she needed guarding.

At the door he turned back.

“Thank you for dinner.”

“Mabel cooked.”

“You made room.”

He said it without emphasis, glancing at the table they had expanded. Eve could accept the literal meaning. It was easier, and it was enough.

After they left, she returned to the kitchen and found Dumpling asleep on the disputed chair.

Three plates drying. One container of leftovers. A table that was still slightly crooked but no longer too small.

Eve checked her phone. There were comments on the guide, two questions she would answer tomorrow, and no disaster requiring her to abandon the evening retroactively.

She put the phone down.

“Excellent hosting,” she told Dumpling. “Next time you can provide a stool.”

He didn't open his eyes.
