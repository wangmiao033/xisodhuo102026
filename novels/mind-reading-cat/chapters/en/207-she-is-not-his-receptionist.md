# Chapter 207 — She Is Not His Receptionist

The first message asked whether Julian was working late on Thursday.

Eve answered while waiting for the kettle. A woman from his training course wanted to arrange a small reunion meal, and Eve remembered hearing something about a late shift. She wrote that she would check.

The second message arrived before the water boiled.

Would Saturday be easier?

Eve looked toward the bedroom, where Julian was getting dressed. Asking him took only a moment. He said he needed to check an exchange of shifts before committing. She relayed that answer, put down the phone, and forgot her tea until it had become unusually determined.

At the venue, Robin had set three places around the temporary table.

“The front room still has clearance?” they asked.

“Yes. No change to the restrictions. The contractor isn't working during the session.”

Eve checked the entrance and the route past the chairs. The closed rear recess remained untouched. Everything they needed was on the accessible shelf.

The phone lit again.

Could Julian bring the notes from the course? Someone had lost their copy.

Eve typed, I'll ask, then stopped with her finger above send.

She sent it anyway. It was such a small thing.

The three participants arrived within ten minutes of one another. One brought a folder, another a notebook with a dog on the cover, and the third a single folded page worn soft from being carried in a pocket. They were here to make clearer household instructions for people occasionally helping with their pets. No animals were attending, and no one needed to cross into the restricted area.

Eve began with the sample card.

“Imagine you're arriving at someone else's home for the first time. What do you need to know before you start?”

“Where they keep the key,” said the woman with the folder.

“And which door,” the man with the notebook added. “My sister has three entrances and believes they're all the obvious one.”

The third participant laughed and unfolded her paper.

“I've written feed him as usual. My neighbor has never seen usual.”

They worked through it together. Eve asked them to separate practical routine information from questions that belonged with their own veterinary practice. She used a fictional address in the demonstration rather than having anyone display private household details to the group.

For twenty minutes, the small table did exactly what it was supposed to do.

Then Eve's phone vibrated against the shelf.

She didn't look immediately. She waited while the woman with the folded page finished explaining a problem with two similar food containers. But when the group began writing, Eve glanced down.

A different acquaintance of Julian's wanted to return a book. Would Eve be at home that evening? If not, could they leave it at the venue?

She started calculating her journey home.

Robin appeared beside her with the extra blank sheets.

“Could you check this example?” they asked.

“Yes. Sorry.”

The sample was perfectly simple. Eve read it twice because half her attention was arranging a handover for a book she had never borrowed.

After the session, the three participants stayed briefly to compare their revised cards. The man with the notebook crossed out a whole paragraph.

“I thought being thorough meant including everything,” he said. “It turns out I was making my sister read the history of the cupboard.”

Eve smiled. Her own phone contained a modest history of Julian's Thursday.

When everyone had left, Robin cleared the table. Eve put the remaining sheets away and called the person returning the book. They were already nearby, which apparently meant Eve should remain nearby too.

“I can leave it another time,” the man said when she explained that she was due at a client meeting.

“Oh, I can probably manage ten minutes.”

Robin looked up.

Eve heard the probably and disliked it. She was about to compress her lunch to help with something nobody had actually demanded she do.

“No,” she corrected. “Sorry. I can't take the handover today. Please arrange it directly with Julian.”

There was a pause.

“Sure. I only thought you might be easier to reach.”

“I often am,” she said.

That was the problem, expressed without malice by someone who had no reason to know it existed.

She put the phone down.

Robin dried their hands.

“Personal delivery service?”

“Apparently.”

“Paid?”

“Extremely poorly.”

They smiled, then asked whether Eve needed the room locked after they finished. It was a real work question with an agreed answer, and she appreciated the difference.

Before leaving the venue, she walked back to the sample shelf to collect her notebook. The woman with the folded page had left a pen. Eve put it in the ordinary lost-property tray and sent a short message through the booking contact. That was part of hosting the session, and she did it without resentment.

The difference annoyed her because it was so clear once she stopped rushing. She didn't dislike helping. She disliked discovering that a harmless favor had expanded into availability other people could schedule around.

Robin checked the lock-up time once more.

“I'll finish the materials count before I go. You don't need to come back.”

“Thank you.”

“And eat something.”

“That last one is not in your job description.”

“An unsolicited artistic suggestion.”

Eve bought a sandwich on the way to the client. She ate it sitting on a bench instead of answering the reunion messages while walking. A child nearby was trying to persuade a toy bus to climb the bench leg. She watched the unsuccessful engineering for a minute and felt her shoulders lower.

When her phone vibrated again, she left it in her bag until she had finished chewing.

Her client meeting went well. She left with a clear revision request and no reason to feel aggrieved. Nevertheless, by the time she reached home, Julian's course reunion had acquired two possible restaurants, a question about dietary requirements, and a request for a photograph of the notes.

Julian was sorting laundry on the bed when she came in.

“Did Daniel get his book?” he asked.

Eve stopped in the doorway.

“No.”

“Oh. I thought he was going to your place.”

“He wanted to. I had work.”

Julian put a shirt down.

“Of course.”

“Did you tell him to ask me?”

“I said you might be there this morning. I didn't know he would ask you to wait.”

“He didn't. I offered. Then I took it back.”

“Okay.”

“And the dinner people have contacted me four times.”

He frowned. “I only asked you to tell them I'd check Thursday.”

“You didn't ask that either. They asked me, and I started doing it.”

The argument from the previous evening seemed to be standing just outside the room, waiting to be invited in. Eve didn't want another round about which of them had used which exact words. She set down her bag and sat on the edge of the bed, leaving space around the sorted clothes.

“I keep becoming the available person,” she said. “Then I get annoyed with you because I'm doing your arrangements.”

“I didn't know they were still asking.”

“I know. But you also told Daniel where I might be without checking whether I wanted to receive the book.”

Julian nodded slowly.

“Yes. I did.”

She showed him the messages. He read only the conversations she opened for him, then handed back the phone.

“I can take these over.”

“I'd like that.”

“Will you forward—”

He stopped.

Eve raised an eyebrow.

“The reunion group,” he said. “I'm in it already. I can ask them directly.”

She felt an immediate, almost embarrassing relief.

He sent a message from his own phone. Eve watched him check the pending shift exchange before offering a date. He told the group to contact him about his availability, and that Eve wasn't coordinating the meal. Then he called Daniel and arranged the book return for a time they could both manage.

There was no dramatic apology from the other end. Daniel apparently agreed, because Julian thanked him and hung up.

“Done,” he said.

“The notes?”

“I'll find them after dinner.”

“You don't need me to remember?”

“I have a phone capable of making me feel guilty at a specified time.”

She laughed. It came more readily than it had the previous night.

“What about people asking whether you're on duty?” she said. “Sometimes I don't know whether it's social or they want an appointment.”

“Don't give out my rota. They can contact me for personal arrangements, and the clinic for clinical ones.”

“That sounds obvious when you say it.”

“It should have been obvious before you spent your lunch on a book.”

“I didn't, in the end.”

“Good.”

He picked up the shirt again. Eve watched him fold it, differently from how she would have done it but entirely successfully.

“This isn't because I don't want to know your friends,” she said.

“I didn't think that.”

“I want to go to dinner sometimes. I don't want to produce the dinner.”

Julian put the folded shirt on the correct pile.

“Would you like to come if the reunion happens?”

“Ask me when you have a date.”

“I will.”

It was a small, pleasant distinction. She could be invited to something instead of becoming its infrastructure.

Later, while she prepared a simple meal from the groceries she had ordered, another message arrived from the woman arranging the reunion.

Sorry, didn't realize I had made you the middle person. Julian's replied now. Hope you can come.

Eve answered that she would see whether the date worked. She did not apologize for failing to provide a service she had never agreed to provide.

She also didn't write a lecture. The woman had corrected course. That was enough.

Julian came into the kitchen carrying the empty laundry basket. He put it away, washed his hands, and asked whether she wanted company while she cooked.

“Company is available without becoming an assistant?” she asked.

“I can stand here and criticize the weather.”

“Excellent. The weather deserves it.”

He leaned against the clear counter while she cut vegetables. They talked about Wednesday's exchange, about the woman who had written as usual, and about how much knowledge people accidentally hid inside familiar words.

“Like just ask Eve,” Julian said.

She pointed the spoon at him.

“Very good. You've understood the teaching objective.”

“No certificate?”

“Budget cuts.”

Dumpling wandered in, inspected the empty basket's former position, and went back out. No one needed him to interpret the exchange. Julian had heard her without a cat inserting himself between their chairs.

After dinner, Eve checked tomorrow's work. The contractor's update needed filing. A client revision needed forty minutes. One venue session needed a staffing decision. Those tasks were hers, and there were enough of them.

Julian's reunion no longer occupied a line among them.

From the other side of the table, his phone sounded. He looked at the reminder, stood up, and went to find his course notes.

Eve returned to her own page.
