# Chapter 171 — The Sign on Opening Eve

The sign looked expensive until Eve turned it the right way up.

Then it looked expensive and wrong.

She stood outside the venue with the packaging against her knees, reading the name she had typed, checked, approved, and apparently failed to understand. You, Me & Paws. Beneath it, in smaller letters, the welcoming sentence ended with a question mark.

Come on in?

It sounded as though the building had doubts.

Sophie leaned closer. “Perhaps the building would like to know your intentions.”

“I sent a period.”

“Did you?”

Eve found the approved proof on her phone. She enlarged it with two fingers, then three, as if additional fingers might change the evidence. The question mark remained. It had been there when she approved the final file, small and perfectly legible.

“Oh.”

Sophie waited.

“I sent a period in the first version.”

“But you approved this one.”

“Yes.”

Sophie took the empty packaging before it slid onto the pavement. “Then we know which person not to shout at.”

Eve hadn't intended to shout. She had intended to write a controlled, extremely professional message explaining that the entire emotional meaning of the entrance had been compromised. Discovering her own approval made that message difficult to compose.

They carried the sign inside. The room smelled faintly of the clean cloths drying in the preparation area. Chairs stood in their tested places. The opening schedule was on the wall, and the little display beside it contained exactly the stock Eve had bought rather than the stock she wished she could afford. Nothing waited for a dramatic last-minute delivery.

Until now, this had felt suspiciously calm.

Sophie laid the sign flat on the table. Her booked preparation time ended in forty minutes. She had a morning commitment elsewhere, so she would not be here for the opening itself. Eve had known that for weeks and had not written S? on any part of tomorrow's rota.

“I could remake the lower strip,” Eve said.

“How?”

“Print it somewhere.”

“Where?”

Eve opened a search window, then shut it. The nearby print shop had closed. The other one was across the city. She could reach it before its late closing if she left immediately, if the file behaved, if someone could produce the right finish, and if she didn't mind returning after midnight.

She minded every one of those things. She was nonetheless beginning to calculate the journey.

Sophie pulled out a chair and sat down.

“What does the question mark stop people doing?”

“Taking us seriously.”

“People will read the rest.”

“First impressions matter.”

“Yes. You being able to speak tomorrow is also a first impression.”

Eve touched her throat. She had been talking too much all week: confirming suppliers, explaining session formats, calling people back when a written answer became more confusing than a short conversation. Her voice had acquired a dry edge she could hear when she laughed.

She disliked having evidence introduced against her plan.

“It will be in every photograph.”

“Will it?”

“If anyone takes one.”

There it was. The fear underneath the expensive punctuation was not that people would photograph the sign. It was that they wouldn't photograph anything. That the first formal day of You, Me & Paws would pass with a few polite visitors, a nearly empty till, and a question in the doorway that looked like a review written in advance.

Sophie didn't immediately rescue the silence. Eve appreciated and resented that in equal measure.

“I want it to look as if I know what I'm doing,” she said at last.

“You do know what you're doing. That isn't the same as never missing a mark.”

Sophie turned the sign toward herself. “May I try something on the scrap first?”

“What sort of something?”

“Very technical. Scissors.”

The letters were printed on a firm backing, with a removable decorative strip beneath the name. Sophie used the spare sample offcut, not the finished sign, to test how it cut. She made a small curved shape, then another, and arranged them beside a dot on plain paper.

Eve looked down.

“A cat?”

“An economical one.”

“It only has half a face.”

“We're keeping the overheads low.”

Eve laughed, winced at her throat, and went to fetch water.

Sophie drew a small patch on cream card. She covered the stray question mark without touching the business name, then used the cut pieces as whiskers extending from the edge. The repaired sentence became a slightly irregular Come on in. Beside it, an unfinished cat face appeared to be peering around the corner.

It was unmistakably a patch. It was also less stiff than the original.

Eve turned it toward the window. The handwritten period was a little rounder than the printed letters, and one whisker pointed in an entirely different direction from its companions.

“That one is wrong.”

Sophie held out the scissors. “Correct it if you want.”

Eve took them, considered the whisker, and put them down.

“If I start, I'll still be here at midnight.”

“I had formed a similar suspicion.”

They fixed the patch properly, checked that no edge lifted, and set the sign in its agreed indoor position where it could be read through the glass without narrowing the entrance. Sophie stepped outside while Eve adjusted the angle.

“Can you read the small words?” Eve called through the door.

Sophie nodded, then mimed opening it rather than continuing to shout through glass. Eve opened it and felt ridiculous.

“It reads,” Sophie said. “But the session information behind it doesn't.”

They shifted the smaller notice to the other side. Now a visitor could see the name, the purpose of the space, and where to find booking information without leaning around a cat face. The practical change mattered more than the original mistake. Eve hated how often that turned out to be true.

A passerby slowed, looked at the repaired sign, and asked whether they were open now.

“Tomorrow,” Eve said. “We're finishing the setup tonight.”

“Is it somewhere you can buy coffee?”

“No coffee service. We run small owners' activities and experience exchanges.”

The woman read the second notice. “Oh. My sister might like that.”

She took a photograph of the information after Eve pointed it out, thanked them, and continued down the street. She didn't mention the question mark or the whiskers.

Sophie raised an eyebrow.

“Please don't,” Eve said.

“I haven't spoken.”

“You're thinking very loudly.”

“Fortunately your cat isn't here.”

Eve smiled. Dumpling was at home, where the only sign he needed was the familiar sound of his dinner being prepared. Tomorrow he would spend the opening day in the quiet nonpublic room with his usual things, away from visitors, because Eve's day would be long and she had arranged his ordinary care around it. His presence wasn't on the timetable or the advertising. She had checked the room herself, including the latch, the water, and the place where he could hide without being disturbed.

She still needed to pack his familiar blanket. That task had nearly been displaced by a midnight trip to a printer.

Inside, Sophie completed the work they had actually booked: checking the visible schedule, testing the labels on the supplies, and helping Eve move one display so a bag couldn't catch its edge. Eve wrote the small changes on her copy. None required a new design language or another purchase.

Before they finished, Eve tried the entrance from the pavement with her tote over one shoulder. The bag brushed neither notice. She could reach the handle without stepping into the neighboring doorway. From that height, the patched sentence looked friendly rather than uncertain.

Inside again, she found Sophie watching her face.

“What?”

“You stopped inspecting the photograph.”

“I was checking whether someone could actually arrive.”

“Very advanced. They cover that in the second year of owning a door.”

Eve bowed gravely, and nearly dropped her bag.

At the end of the agreed time, Sophie put her scissors away.

“I won't be able to reply in the morning,” she said. “Phone off during the setup.”

“I know.”

“If the sign falls over, ask someone who is there.”

“It isn't going to fall over.”

“Then I've saved you a call.”

Eve walked her to the door. For a second she wanted to ask her to stay for dinner, not because they had planned dinner but because goodbye made tomorrow feel more real. Sophie had already described her early start and the journey home.

“Thank you,” Eve said instead. “For the actual work. And the cat surgery.”

“Send me a photograph when you have time. Not while somebody is waiting to pay.”

They hugged, carefully around Sophie's bag. Eve watched her leave and went back inside to finish the short closing list.

Halfway through, she found the original proof still open on her phone. Her fingers hovered over the editing controls. She could correct it now, order another strip for next week, and replace the imperfect one before many people noticed.

She saved a note about the supplier file, then closed it. There was no need to decide tonight whether a harmless patch deserved replacing. It could remain while she learned what the room actually needed.

Julian called as she switched off the main light.

“Have you left yet?”

“I'm locking up.”

“A statement of intention or an observed event?”

She turned the key. “Observed.”

“Good. What happened with the sign?”

“I've sent you a photograph.”

There was a pause while he opened it.

“The cat looks skeptical.”

“The cat is correct.”

“I like it.”

“You don't have to.”

“I know. I could have said the lettering was legible.”

She laughed, then coughed. He heard it and asked whether she had eaten. This time she could give him a useful answer: she had food at home, and she was going there to heat it, not to revise the opening description until dawn.

He had his own early start. They agreed he would come after work if his finish allowed, not appear magically at the exact moment she became overwhelmed. She wished him goodnight before reaching her street, wanting the rest of the walk without checking a screen.

At home, Dumpling met her with a complaint about a door she had only just opened. She fed him, prepared her own dinner, and packed the familiar blanket after he finished inspecting it. He climbed into his bed while she laid out tomorrow's clothes.

For once, she chose shoes before choosing a jacket.

Then she opened the photograph Sophie had taken from outside. The room beyond the glass was still small. A patch covered an error. Half a cat peered around an invitation, and the quiet corner remained stubbornly empty.

Eve sent the picture to Sophie with one line: Keeping it.

She put the phone down before an answer could arrive.

Tomorrow she would have to open the door. Tonight, for the first time in several days, she let herself go to bed before every possible thing had been improved.
