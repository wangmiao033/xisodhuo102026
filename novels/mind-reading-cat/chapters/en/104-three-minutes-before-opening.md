# Chapter 104 — Three Minutes Before Opening

At nine fifty-seven, the first queue formed in exactly the wrong place.

Eve had allowed fifteen minutes for something ordinary to go wrong. The tables had arrived on time. The tunnel seller's stuffed dog had survived being packed beneath a box of leaflets. A missing roll of tape had been found inside the tote bag clearly labeled Tape.

She had begun to suspect the fifteen minutes were a sign of unnecessary pessimism.

Then she looked toward the street.

Six people stood beside the decorative gate that remained locked, reading a notice meant to direct them around to the lane entrance. One was pointing at the arrow. From where he stood, Eve could see how it appeared to point through the gate rather than along the pavement.

A woman with a carrier tried to pass the queue and stopped when someone stepped sideways. Behind her, a delivery cyclist rang a bell.

Eve put down the programme sheets.

“Sophie. The street sign is sending people to the locked gate.”

Sophie looked up from the check-in desk.

“I'll keep this entrance clear. Take the first volunteer with you.”

Eve went, calling the volunteer by the name on her badge rather than shouting for somebody to help. The woman followed with the spare direction card they had prepared.

Outside, Eve approached from the pavement side without trying to push through the carrier and its owner.

“Good morning. I'm the planner. This gate isn't the entrance. I'm sorry—the sign is unclear. The entrance is around the corner, along the lane.”

The man pointing at the arrow frowned.

“It says here.”

“Yes. I can see why you read it that way. We're correcting it.”

She asked the volunteer to stand at the corner and show the route, then moved the misleading sign out of view. The caretaker, reached by a short call, confirmed the locked gate was not to be used for entry. Eve did not ask him to improvise a new access arrangement because it would spare her embarrassment.

The cyclist passed once the group moved. The carrier's owner remained where she was.

“Is it far?” she asked.

“Just around that corner. Would you like me to walk with you?”

“Yes, please.”

They went slowly enough that the woman didn't have to adjust her grip every other step. Eve gave no advice about the animal inside; she simply held the pedestrian door open and pointed out the quieter place where the owner could pause.

Back inside, the organizer was looking at her watch.

“Are we opening?”

“We're ready in the courtyard. We need five minutes to correct the approach. Let's tell the waiting visitors that, rather than open into a blockage.”

The organizer agreed. Eve made the short announcement herself, explaining that stalls would begin at five past ten. No dramatic apology, no story about somebody else's mistake.

It was her sign.

Sophie brought out the spare board. They changed the arrow and added the street name in lettering large enough to read without approaching the gate. The volunteer tested it from the direction visitors were arriving. This time she could understand it before reaching the obstruction.

“Stay at the corner for the first fifteen minutes,” Eve said. “Then tell Sophie whether people still need help finding it.”

“What about the check-in desk?”

“I'll cover until the first arrival rush ends. We can delay my opening photographs.”

The photographs lost. The market opened.

For ten minutes Eve answered the same four questions in different combinations. Yes, people could come without pets. No, there was no veterinary consultation. The toilet route was indoors. The owners' exchange would start after people had time to browse, and participation was optional.

The repetition was not elegant, but it worked.

A little dog stopped at the threshold. Its owner apologized twice before Eve could tell her that she could wait outside, leave, or return without the dog. The woman chose to take a quiet walk first. Nobody called the animal stubborn or tried to tempt it through for the sake of attendance.

By half past ten, the approach was flowing. The first volunteer came back with the direction card tucked under her arm.

“Most people are finding it now.”

“Thank you. Please take your break before the next shift.”

Eve checked the entrance herself, then finally looked at the stalls as more than positions on her plan.

The supplies shop had set the scratched tray on a waterproof mat. Two visitors were discussing how long a washable cover actually took to dry. At the tunnel stall, the ugly stuffed dog protruded backward from the demonstration opening while its owner explained how to fold the frame.

Nobody seemed to miss the rotating stage.

That did not mean the rejected applicant had been foolish. It meant this event had found another way to be useful.

Eve took a few agreed photographs of products and empty layout details, then asked two stallholders whether they were comfortable appearing in a wider shot. One declined. She changed the angle without persuading him that the publicity would help his business.

A loud scraping noise interrupted the next photograph.

A seller was dragging a chair into the route Sophie had kept clear. He wanted somewhere to set his coat and bag, both of which were taking up selling space.

Sophie approached before Eve did.

“That route needs to stay open. We can put your bag under the table if it's contained.”

“It'll only be here for a minute.”

“Not here. I'll help you find somewhere suitable.”

He looked over at Eve, perhaps recognizing the person who had accepted his application.

She had an absurd flash of temptation to reward him with a special exception because he had looked at her. Instead she nodded toward Sophie.

“She has the layout responsibility. Please follow her direction.”

Sophie did not smile at Eve as if she'd passed a test. She helped the seller move the bag. Work continued.

At eleven, Julian arrived wearing an ordinary coat and carrying no equipment.

Eve saw him while collecting unused chairs from the exchange area. Her first reaction was pleasure. Her second was a ridiculous desire to show him that she had already fixed everything.

“Hello,” he said. “Where do you want this?”

He was holding the chair she'd nearly bumped into him.

“Against that wall, inside the marked storage area. Thank you.”

He placed it there and returned.

“Do you need another pair of hands for a little while? I have half an hour before I need to go.”

“Ordinary hands?”

“That's what I brought.”

She laughed. Then she considered the actual tasks instead of inventing something that would keep him beside her.

“Sophie has the equipment list. We could use help returning the unused chairs, if she's happy to assign you that.”

He went to ask Sophie. Eve watched for a moment, enjoying how straightforwardly he accepted being told where to put things by someone who knew more about the site than he did.

Then a participant approached her about the owners' exchange, and she turned back to her own job.

The first speaker had brought a handwritten example of household care instructions. Eve asked her to omit addresses and contact details before showing the page. Another person wanted to talk about storage in a small flat. Neither needed a microphone; with six chairs and a quiet corner, ordinary voices were enough.

The conversation was initially awkward. People looked at Eve whenever someone finished, waiting for her to certify the usefulness of the previous sentence.

She asked one concrete question.

“What did someone helping you find hardest to locate?”

“Everything I called obvious,” the first woman said.

Laughter loosened the chairs into a group. Someone described three identical containers. Someone else admitted labeling a cupboard door only after a relative searched the wrong room.

Eve didn't turn the discussion into a guide she was qualified to issue. These were personal experiences, useful because people could question and adapt them. When a visitor asked about a specific animal's symptoms, she explained that the exchange couldn't assess that and suggested contacting an appropriate veterinary service through its ordinary channels.

She did not point at Julian.

He was at the far side of the courtyard stacking chairs with a volunteer, not waiting to become a free consultation booth.

The discussion ended at the advertised time. Two people kept talking afterward, but the others could leave without appearing rude. Eve thanked them and asked separately whether anyone wanted a brief anonymous summary of the practical topics included in the event report. She would use only what they agreed to share.

Julian returned when his half-hour was nearly over.

“I need to go,” he said. “Will you still want company after closing?”

“Yes. I won't be finished at one. There's the handover and cleanup.”

“What time should I check?”

“After three. I'll tell you if it changes.”

“All right.”

She wanted a kiss. She also had a programme sheet in one hand and a request about rubbish bags arriving from her left.

“Later,” she said quietly.

His smile suggested he understood which arrangement she meant.

He left without making the departure a promise to return as rescue. Eve answered the rubbish question and discovered they had used more bags than expected. The organizer fetched the spare supply from the room. Another ordinary problem acquired an ordinary solution.

Near noon, Eve finally drank the tea she had put aside. It was cold. She ate the sandwich she had packed before leaving home, standing in the permitted staff corner rather than deciding hunger would make her look committed.

Sophie passed with the check-in sheet.

“How's the invisible department?” she asked.

“Very visible. I'm grateful.”

“Tell them before they leave.”

“I will.”

Before the first volunteer left, Eve asked her to show the replacement where visitors still hesitated. They walked the route together. The woman pointed out a second problem: the indoor toilet arrow was visible only after somebody had already asked at the check-in desk.

Eve checked with the caretaker, then moved the approved sign onto the permitted board nearer the doorway. She asked the replacement volunteer to try following it from the entrance without help.

“That works,” he said, returning.

The first volunteer collected her coat. Eve thanked her specifically for keeping the street corner clear, then let her leave at the agreed time. She didn't attach an extra errand to the thank-you.

A quarter of an hour later, the new volunteer directed someone to the toilets while Sophie handled a supplier's question. Eve watched the two separate tasks finish without either person looking for her. For the first time all morning, she had enough attention left to notice how much she liked the sound of people talking in the courtyard.

From where Eve stood, she could see the corrected sign through the lane doorway. A new visitor paused, read it, and walked in without asking anyone where to go.

She made a note to replace the misleading version in the saved design file, not merely move today's board. Then she put the phone away and went to collect the empty cups beside the conversation chairs before the next group needed them.
