# Chapter 31: A Proper Offer

The number was large enough that Eve checked which side of the decimal point she was looking at.

Then she checked the currency.

Then she opened the message again, as though the first viewing might have used up its generosity.

Pocket Path wanted a short promotional film for its new outing tote. The company sold practical accessories for people who went places with animals: washable pouches, folding water bowls, bags with pockets for everything except the one thing their owners urgently needed.

The proposed fee would cover a meaningful part of Eve's month.

Not a symbolic amount. Not exposure. Not the chance to work with an exciting young team whose enthusiasm would somehow satisfy her landlord.

Money.

Dumpling sat beside her laptop and washed the white part of his chest.

“You are witnessing a professional moment,” she told him.

He paused with his tongue out, then continued.

The contact was named Felix. He had found her through the walking guide. He liked the way she explained ordinary things without making them look dull, he wrote, which was sufficiently close to the work she wanted that Eve read the sentence twice.

She nearly replied immediately.

Instead she scrolled down to the attachment.

The reference film showed a dog choosing one product from three, a cat appearing to push away an inferior bag, and a caption announcing that pets knew the difference.

Under deliverables, Felix had written: *Dumpling's signature clever reactions. Three distinct responses. Half-day shoot.*

Eve looked at the cat.

He had stopped washing. A loose thread on the blanket now held his full attention.

“I don't remember appointing you head of product testing.”

She read the brief again. The fee remained attractive. The half day remained four hours. The reference reactions remained things she had never asked Dumpling to do, let alone guaranteed he could repeat on command.

She made herself write a question rather than an acceptance.

*Could we meet to discuss the concept, the shoot conditions, and what you mean by three distinct responses?*

Felix answered within twenty minutes. He had a small showroom near the tram stop. Tomorrow at eleven?

Eve accepted the meeting, not the job. She put both words in her calendar to make the difference difficult to forget.

The next morning she took her camera, notebook, and no cat.

Pocket Path occupied the ground floor of a narrow building shared with a print shop. A cardboard sign in the window said the card reader was temporarily unavailable. Inside, totes hung from hooks above shelves of pouches in soft greens and dusty oranges.

Felix came out from behind a stack of cartons carrying two mugs.

“I assumed tea.”

“Correctly.”

“Excellent. My first successful assumption today.”

He was younger than Eve had expected, with tired eyes and a strip of packing tape stuck to his cuff. He peeled it off while showing her the sample bag.

“I liked your map film,” he said. “Especially the bit where you showed the step before the nice seating area. Most people would have filmed the flowers.”

“The step was more useful.”

“Exactly. This is supposed to be useful.”

He put the tote on the counter. It had a removable lining, an outer pocket for a bottle, and a separate washable pouch for things people didn't want beside their keys. Eve tried the fastenings. One caught slightly at the corner.

“That sample's been handled a lot,” Felix said. “We're changing the tab.”

She appreciated that he didn't tell her she had opened it incorrectly.

“How much fits?” she asked.

He brought out a folded towel, a water bottle, a lead, a packet of bags, and a battered paperback that was apparently his personal test of whether anything counted as a useful bag.

They packed and unpacked it together.

“Not for carrying an animal,” he said, catching her reading the dimensions. “People keep asking. It's a bag for the human.”

“Good. We should say that clearly.”

He nodded and wrote it on a sheet already covered in arrows.

For ten minutes the job looked almost uncomplicated.

Eve could imagine the film: wet bottle separated from dry notebook, hand finding keys without emptying everything on a pavement, the lining removed after a muddy walk. It was a small, understandable problem. She knew how to show it.

Then Felix turned his tablet toward her.

“We start with Dumpling.”

The storyboard showed a cat between two bags.

“He rejects the messy one. Picks ours. Then there's a reaction to the pocket opening.”

“What reaction?”

“Something like that face he makes.”

“He has several.”

Felix smiled. “The knowing one.”

Eve looked at the drawing. Two cartoon eyebrows had been added to the cat.

“Most of those expressions last half a second. Sometimes he's looking at a fly.”

“That's what editing's for.”

She had said versions of that sentence herself. Usually when a client wanted an event to look busier than it had been, or a speaker to look as if they had finished a thought without taking three detours.

“Editing can connect real moments,” she said. “It can't make a genuine product preference out of unrelated footage.”

“We don't need a scientific claim. It's playful.”

“Then the joke needs to be clearly ours. Not proof that the cat understands the compartments.”

Felix leaned back against the counter.

Through the open doorway, someone at the print shop dragged a metal trolley over uneven paving. The rattling lasted long enough for Eve to imagine Dumpling flattened inside his carrier, listening to an unfamiliar sound she would be asking him to ignore for work.

“Would you film here?” she asked.

“We can clear the back area.”

“For four hours?”

“With breaks.”

“He might never come out. He might come out and sit behind the camera. I can't promise three responses.”

“Could you bring toys? Whatever usually works?”

“Familiar things might help him settle. They don't make him an actor.”

Felix turned the tablet back around. He wasn't angry. He was looking at the drawing as though she had introduced an unexpected manufacturing problem.

“The cat is why the fee's higher,” he said.

There it was. Not an insult. A fact about the money on the screen.

Eve's hand tightened around her pen.

She could hear the old version of her answer: *Of course. We'll work something out. I'm sure he'll be fine.*

That answer had carried her through difficult clients, impossible delivery dates, and evenings when agreeing first seemed easier than spending another minute being evaluated.

It had also left her awake at two in the morning, trying to turn a promise into something physically possible.

“What if I propose two versions?” she asked. “One where he appears only if he's comfortable, without a scripted reaction. One where the story works without him.”

Felix rubbed the tape mark on his cuff.

“I can look. But the opening needs a reason to stop scrolling.”

“Agreed.”

“I'm not paying that amount for someone packing a bag.”

“Noted.”

She wrote it down, although she had no risk of forgetting.

They went through timings, where the film would appear, and whether Pocket Path wanted the raw material as well as the finished edit. Felix said he'd send the standard paperwork. Eve said she'd send questions and a concept, not a finished speculative film.

When she stood to leave, he offered her the sample tote.

“Take it. See what you think.”

“On loan?”

“Yes. Bring it back if we don't proceed.”

She asked him to put that in the message too. He did, without complaint.

At the tram stop, she noticed the borrowed bag's strap slipping off her coat. She shortened it, then lengthened it again when the bottle pocket tilted against her knee. A little girl beside her watched this operation with open interest.

“Is it broken?” the girl asked.

“No. I'm finding out how it works.”

The girl's father moved their shopping aside to give her room. Eve finally found a length that sat comfortably, and wrote the adjustment range in her notebook before she forgot. It was the sort of detail a person might actually want to know before buying something. Not spectacular. Not a cat choosing the future. Just a bag that had to fit a shoulder.

On the tram home the bag sat on her lap. It was well made. She liked the bottle pocket. Liking it made the other parts more awkward, not less.

At home, she placed it on a shelf out of Dumpling's reach until she could put away the tags and loose packaging. He sniffed her shoe, ignored her expensive professional opportunity, and went to his water bowl.

Eve made lunch before opening the brief again. Leftover chicken and potatoes from Mabel, hot enough to steam against the window.

She drew a line down the center of a page.

On one side: what she could deliver.

On the other: what required a cat to agree with a storyboard.

The second side was embarrassingly crowded.

She sent Sophie a photograph of the fee with the company details cropped out.

Sophie replied: *That's actual money.*

*I know.*

*Have you checked what they buy with it?*

Eve stared at the question.

*Currently investigating.*

*Good. I'm loading a van. No voice calls unless you're on fire.*

Eve sent a thumbs-up and let her get back to work.

She had considered asking Julian whether she should do it. The impulse came quickly enough to annoy her. He could advise on an animal's welfare; he could not decide how she should price her labor or whether a client would accept a different concept.

She drafted a narrower message instead, asking whether a short, voluntary visit to a quiet unfamiliar room was something to discuss in light of Dumpling's recent behavior. She included what the proposed shoot involved, rather than only the parts that sounded harmless.

His reply came later.

*I can't assess a room by text. Don't build your plan around him staying. If he seems uncomfortable, the plan needs to allow you to leave, not persuade him.*

No yes. No no. No magical transfer of responsibility.

Eve added another question to the page: *Can the job survive us going home?*

Dumpling hopped onto the chair beside her. He looked from her face to the closed laptop, then put a paw on the edge of her notebook.

For one unreasonable second, she wanted to interpret it as reassurance.

“You don't know what a usage fee is,” she told him.

He pressed down harder, creasing the paper.

“Neither did I, once.”

She moved his paw gently and opened the document Felix had just sent.

The fee appeared again on the first page. Below it were several paragraphs in smaller type, including a sentence so long she had to follow it with her finger.

She didn't reply that night.

Instead she saved a copy, marked the parts she didn't understand, and put the sample bag beside her work folder for the morning.

The offer was still there.

For once, she was going to find out what it contained before saying she could carry it.
