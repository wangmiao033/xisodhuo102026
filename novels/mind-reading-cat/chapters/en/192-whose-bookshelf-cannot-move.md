# Chapter 192 — Whose Bookshelf Cannot Move?

The first apartment had the sort of window Eve normally charged people to stand beside.

Light fell through it in a long, clean rectangle. The floorboards looked warm. A pale wall waited for books, plants, and a version of her life in which nothing ever needed a cable.

She stepped inside and immediately imagined a photograph.

Julian stepped inside and asked where the bedroom was.

The letting agent pointed down a narrow passage. Eve followed, carrying her little notebook and trying not to resent the practical question. They had arranged three viewings over the coming days, each within a range they had agreed to investigate rather than a price either had promised to pay. Dumpling was at home. An empty carrier, brought only to check routes and storage, bumped lightly against Julian's leg.

“This one allows a cat?” Eve asked.

“The owner has said they're open to it. We'd need written approval before anything was final.”

She recorded that distinction. Open to it was not yet yes.

The bedroom looked peaceful until a bus passed outside. Its engine rose beneath the window, followed by a squeal from the stop across the road. The glass softened the sound without removing it.

Julian stood still and listened.

“Is that stop used early?”

“It's a regular route.”

Eve looked at the light on the wall. The agent had answered honestly. She wished the truth were less well timed.

They checked the kitchen, the bathroom, and the narrow passage. Julian tried carrying the empty carrier through without turning it sideways. A cupboard door prevented the maneuver while open. Eve shut it, then opened it again because she didn't want a home that worked only when they reenacted the correct sequence.

“We could store things differently,” she said.

“We could.”

He did not dismiss the place. That made her examine it more carefully rather than defend it for the pleasure of winning.

There was no separate room for work. Her editing desk would stand in the main room, within sight and sound of the bedroom door. She imagined sorting photographs late while Julian tried to sleep before an early shift. They could use headphones. Headphones would not hide light, phone conversations, or her habit of getting up repeatedly to look for a note.

The agent gave them time to talk by the beautiful window.

“I like it,” Eve said.

“I can see why.”

“You sound as though you're about to explain why I shouldn't.”

“I was about to ask where you'd put your work.”

She pointed to the wall. Then she looked at the wall and saw not a desk but cables, boxes, a chair, and the ordinary clutter she had edited out of the first imagined picture.

“Right there. Where I'm currently pretending to be a plant.”

Julian smiled.

They left without applying. Outside, Eve took one photograph of the window for their comparison notes, then wrote noise and no separate work space beneath it. The picture didn't get to make the decision by itself.

At the bus stop she checked the journey to the venue. It was manageable. Julian's route to the clinic was less convenient, requiring a change that looked small on the map and awkward at the actual crossing.

“Let's walk it,” she said.

“You don't need to prove it's bad.”

“I need to know whether I'm making it better in my head.”

They walked. A traffic light held them through two cycles because the route required crossing one road and then another. Eve stood beside him with the empty carrier and watched a bus they might have caught pull away.

“An excellent place to photograph transport,” she said.

“Less excellent to use it.”

She crossed the apartment off their list.

Their second visit was to Julian's existing home.

He had suggested looking at it properly rather than assuming a new lease was necessary. That made sense. He knew the costs and the journey. He knew which window needed a firm push and where the afternoon noise came from. His housing terms would still need checking if another adult moved in; familiarity wasn't permission.

Eve arrived with measurements of her desk and a rough idea of Dumpling's usual things. Julian had cleared the chair, but otherwise left the rooms as he used them. She appreciated that. A staged version would have hidden the very questions they were there to answer.

They tried placing her work area on paper. The most obvious wall held his bookcase.

“That could move,” Eve said.

Julian looked at it.

“Where?”

They considered the other wall. The bookcase would narrow the space beside the door. They considered the bedroom. It would leave little room for the bedside table he used every morning.

“I could keep some books in boxes,” he said.

Eve watched his hand rest on the shelf. “Do you want to?”

“Some aren't used often.”

“That isn't the same answer.”

He took a book down, then put it back in exactly the same place.

“I like them here.”

“I know.”

She did know. She liked the shelf too: the uneven row of well-used spines, the object from his parents on the upper level, the little gap where he returned whatever he was reading. She didn't want to arrive by making him dismantle every comfortable thing. She also didn't want her own work fitted into the leftover space and called compromise.

Julian drew another rectangle on the plan.

“What if your desk went here?”

“Then I'd work beside the kitchen.”

“The light is good.”

“When nobody's making dinner.”

He paused.

Eve heard the edge in her voice and softened it deliberately. “I'm not saying your place is wrong. I'm saying I can see how it works for you. I don't yet see how it would work for both of us.”

He sat down at the table.

“I keep starting with what I already have.”

“It's understandable.”

“And then trying to find somewhere for your things.”

“Yes.”

They looked at the plan. Her desk had become a small rectangle migrating around his established rooms. Dumpling's resting place had been penciled beside it as though the cat were another accessory to her work.

Eve erased both. They began again, writing what space was needed before deciding whose furniture deserved which wall.

Quiet for sleep. A place to work where a door could close. Ordinary room for meals and resting. Safe, familiar arrangements for Dumpling, not a tiny basket chosen because it matched the sofa. Reasonable journeys for both. Costs that left money for more than rent.

Julian read the list.

“Mine isn't automatically the sensible option.”

“Nor is the beautiful window.”

“That window was persuasive.”

“It had a professional portfolio.”

They laughed, and the room became his home again rather than an argument about whether she could fit into it.

On the way out, Eve touched the shelf lightly.

“You can want to keep the bookcase.”

“So can you.”

“I want to keep my desk.”

“I've met your desk. It has strong opinions.”

The third apartment was plainer than either of them expected.

They viewed it several days later, after checking the travel times separately. The building had no charming entrance. The main room looked smaller than the wide-angle photographs, and the kitchen tiles belonged to a decade Eve would not have selected voluntarily. But there was a small second room with a door, a bedroom away from the street, and a place where both desk and bookcase could stand without blocking anything important.

Eve opened the second-room door and stood inside.

Julian waited in the main room. She closed the door gently and asked him to say something.

“What sort of thing?”

“Anything ordinary.”

“I can't find the clean towel.”

She could hear him, but softly. She opened the door, smiling.

“Very convincing.”

“The sheets have taught me caution.”

The agent explained the rental terms without pressing for a deposit that afternoon. They could take the details, check costs and references, and come back with questions. The owner would need to approve two named occupants and Dumpling in writing. Eve asked about the windows and the cat arrangements rather than relying on the phrase pet friendly in the listing.

They checked the window fittings, the storage, the places where a carrier and litter supplies could go. Nothing was perfect. The second room had less natural light than Eve wanted. Julian's commute would be a little longer than from his current home. There was no spare room left for guests once work and privacy had a proper place.

“That might be useful information for our families,” Eve said quietly.

“Extremely clear information.”

She imagined her mother arriving with food, then deliberately stopped before arranging a future visit in an apartment they had not rented.

They tested the empty carrier through the rooms. No animal had been dragged around viewings to make a decision more emotional. Dumpling's real response would require time and ordinary care if they moved, not a performance in front of a letting agent.

In the main room, Julian measured the wall twice.

“The bookcase fits.”

“And the desk?”

“In the other room, with the door able to close.”

Eve pictured an evening when she could work while he read somewhere else. She pictured being annoyed by the kitchen tiles and still able to sleep. The images were less impressive than the first window. They contained more of their actual lives.

“Worth looking at the full costs?” she asked.

“Yes.”

Outside, Julian stopped to tie his shoe on a low wall. Eve looked back at the building. A woman in the upstairs window was shaking crumbs from a small cloth onto a tray, patiently catching what the wind tried to scatter.

“What?” he asked.

“Nothing important. I can imagine getting home here.”

He followed her glance, then looked toward the bus stop rather than the window.

“So can I. Though we still have to see whether imagining it is within our budget.”

She held out a hand to help him off the wall. He took it.

They took the information home without signing anything. At a nearby cafe, they compared their notes over two drinks they had already checked the prices of. Eve opened the photograph of the first apartment. Its wall still looked lovely.

She moved it beneath the third apartment's plain plan in their shared notes.

“Do you regret ruling it out?” Julian asked.

“A little. I can regret the light without wanting to live beside the bus stop.”

He nodded.

“And you?”

“I'll miss knowing where everything goes.”

“You'll still know where some things go.”

“Not if you use the cupboard.”

She smiled at him over her cup. They had not found a perfect home. They had found a possible one that required both of them to change something, neither to vanish, and several unanswered questions to be checked.

Eve wrote down the next step: actual numbers, then a trial of daily life before a commitment.

Beside it, Julian added a question about the bookcase measurements.

She let it stay. Wanting a shared home did not require either of them to stop caring about a shelf.
