# Chapter 079 — The Unexpected Call

By Wednesday evening, Eve had become sufficiently comfortable with the idea of another date to be annoyed when her bus was late.

This felt like progress, though not the sort anybody printed on a greeting card.

She had finished the day's edits, sent the correct version, and checked the attachment before closing her laptop. Dumpling had eaten. Her bag contained the things she actually needed. There was no mysterious last-minute errand requiring Julian to come to her flat instead of meeting her at the small noodle restaurant they had chosen.

At six thirty-eight, she arrived eight minutes late and found him outside, reading the menu through the window.

“Sorry. The bus developed a philosophical objection to movement.”

“Did it resolve it?”

“Eventually.”

He smiled and offered his hand. She took it for the short walk to the door, privately delighted by the fact that it was becoming possible to do that without a preliminary conference.

Inside, the tables were close together and the chairs entirely ordinary. Eve approved of both. Julian took the seat facing the room, leaving her the one beside the wall after asking which she preferred.

They ordered quickly. She was hungry and knew what she wanted. He asked for something different, then moved the sauce bottle so the waiter could put down their water.

“How was today?” Eve asked.

“Busy. Not disastrous.”

“A category I respect.”

He started to describe an argument with the clinic printer. She interrupted to ask whether this was the same model as the one that had put a stripe across Dumpling's possible old photograph. It was. They agreed that the machine had a consistent artistic practice.

Then his phone rang.

Julian looked at the screen. His expression changed, not dramatically, but enough that Eve noticed before he spoke.

“It's the clinic. I need to answer this. Is that all right?”

“Yes.”

He stepped outside rather than talking across the tables. Eve watched through the window for a moment, then looked away. Whatever the call concerned, the details were not hers to gather because she happened to be sitting nearest the glass.

The waiter brought their food.

“Shall I put this here?” he asked, indicating Julian's place.

“Yes, thank you.”

Steam rose from both bowls. Eve picked up her chopsticks, put them down, then picked them up again. Waiting until he returned seemed polite. Letting her dinner go cold because she didn't know how long a call would take seemed less sensible.

She took a bite.

The noodles were good. This made the empty chair more irritating, not less. She had imagined telling him about the client's spectacularly vague correction, then hearing the rest of his printer story. Instead she was trying to eat in a way that wouldn't look either reproachful or indifferent when he returned.

Neither option improved the noodles.

Julian came back after several minutes. He didn't sit immediately.

“I'm sorry. I need to go back.”

Eve put down her chopsticks.

“Tonight?”

“Yes. There's a problem with a handover I was involved in. The team is handling the immediate care, but I need to clarify something from earlier and help finish the transfer. I can't do all of that from here.”

He had given her a reason without turning somebody else's difficulty into a story for their dinner. She understood that. She also felt the disappointment arrive with such force that she was briefly ashamed of it.

“How long?” she asked.

“At least forty-five minutes. It may be longer. I don't want you to sit here waiting for me to guess correctly.”

She looked at his untouched bowl. A small green leaf floated near the edge, absurdly cheerful.

He pulled out his chair but didn't sit.

“I thought the handover was complete before I left. I was wrong about what was still needed. I'm sorry.”

Her first response rose automatically: don't worry, it's fine, of course you have to go.

The last part was true. The middle part wasn't.

“I understand you need to go,” she said. “I'm disappointed.”

He nodded, looking directly at her.

“I am too.”

“I don't mean you should stay instead.”

“I know.”

She wasn't certain he did, so she went on.

“I don't want to be the person who makes you choose between dinner and something that actually needs doing. But I also don't want to act as though I wasn't looking forward to dinner.”

“You don't have to act that way.”

A waiter passed with two bowls, and Julian moved his chair out of the aisle. The ordinary interruption helped. Their conversation wasn't happening in a vacuum where every sentence needed to settle their entire future.

“Could we rearrange it?” he asked. “Not tonight with you waiting indefinitely. Another evening.”

“Yes.”

“Friday?”

She checked her actual plans. Friday's delivery was due in the afternoon. Her evening was free, but she wanted time to come home first.

“Seven,” she said. “Not straight from work for either of us.”

“I can do seven. I'll check the handover arrangements properly before confirming tomorrow.”

She appreciated the qualification, even though part of her wanted a promise with no possible weather inside it.

“Please tell me tomorrow, then.”

“I will.”

He asked the waiter to pack his untouched meal and paid for it along with the share of tonight's bill they had agreed. Eve kept her own meal at the table. There was no reason for both of them to leave hungry.

“Do you want me to walk you to the bus before I go?” he asked.

“No. I want to finish this while it's hot.”

He looked briefly relieved, then apologetic about being relieved. She almost laughed.

“Go,” she said, more gently. “I'm not going to turn into a tragic figure beside the sauce bottle.”

“I didn't think you would.”

“You looked concerned.”

“I don't like leaving you.”

That was plain enough to make her throat tighten. She nodded rather than turning it into a joke.

“Tell me when you're finished, if it isn't too late. You don't have to give me the details.”

“I will.”

He left with the packed meal, walking quickly but not running. Eve watched him disappear past the window, then looked at the empty chair.

A couple at the next table were arguing affectionately over who had ordered the dumplings. Somewhere a child dropped a spoon. Nobody knew enough about her evening to decide what it meant.

She finished her noodles.

Halfway through, the waiter asked whether she wanted the extra small plate cleared. She said yes. He removed it without a sympathetic look, for which she was unreasonably grateful.

She could have gone to the clinic afterward, carrying the role of understanding companion like a useful parcel. She imagined waiting at reception, assuring Tessa she was no trouble, offering to sort something until Julian emerged.

The image made her tired before she stood up.

She had not been asked. The clinic had people working there. Her date ending early did not create a vacancy she needed to fill.

Outside, the bus display promised twelve minutes. A small shop beside the stop sold household goods, including the brand of washing powder Julian had mentioned on Sunday. Eve smiled at the shelf, then walked past. Buying it for him tonight would be a very small act, but she knew she would secretly want it to say something much larger.

She bought the soap refill she herself needed instead.

On the bus, she opened her conversation with Sophie and typed a complaint, then erased it before sending. Not because she had to protect Julian from any mention of disappointment. Because the draft made the evening sound as though he had casually abandoned her, and she knew that wasn't what had happened.

She sent a different message.

*Dinner got interrupted by a real clinic problem. I'm disappointed and going home. No advice required; I may complain over coffee another day.*

Sophie replied several stops later.

*Permitted. I am currently arguing with a curtain track. We can exchange disappointments when mine stops falling down.*

Eve laughed aloud, earning a glance from a passenger across the aisle.

The reply didn't make dinner complete. It gave her somewhere to put a feeling without recruiting a jury.

At home, Dumpling met her near the rug. She greeted him, put away the soap, and changed out of the clothes she'd chosen for the evening. His bowl was as it should be; returning early didn't mean serving another dinner.

She found the travel book and sat on the sofa.

For several pages, she read the same paragraph repeatedly. Her mind supplied an alternate evening in which Julian stayed, they finished dinner, and she told the client story with perfect comic timing. In the imagined version, nobody needed anything and neither of them was tired.

It was an excellent date. It had the small disadvantage of requiring people not to have lives.

She turned the page and began reading properly.

Dumpling climbed beside her, kneaded the blanket, and settled with his back against her leg. Eve did not ask him to interpret her disappointment. The steady weight was useful enough without producing an answer.

At nine ten, Julian sent a message.

*Finished now. I'm going home. I'm sorry about tonight. I'll confirm Friday tomorrow after checking the arrangements.*

She read it once before replying.

*Thank you for telling me. I finished the noodles. They were good enough to deserve another attempt.*

*Mine are now less convincing.*

*That's what happens when noodles wait for a veterinarian.*

A moment later, he asked whether she wanted a brief call. Eve considered her tiredness rather than agreeing because a call had been offered.

“Five minutes,” she said when she answered. “Then I'm going to stop being coherent.”

“Five minutes is good.”

His voice sounded worn. She didn't ask him to reenact the problem for her. He said the handover was now clear and that he needed to discuss the gap in the process with the team, not assume working longer every time was the only solution.

“Not tonight,” he added.

“Good.”

He asked whether she had gotten home easily. She told him about Sophie's curtain track. He laughed, and some of the stiffness left her shoulders.

“I meant what I said,” Eve told him before ending the call. “About being disappointed. Not about you doing the wrong thing by going.”

“I heard both parts.”

“All right.”

“I'd like Friday to be an evening we actually have.”

“So would I.”

They said goodnight. Eve put the phone on the table instead of keeping it in her hand for another reassuring message.

The evening had been shortened. It had not been erased. There was an unfinished printer story, a meal worth trying again, and a promise to confirm a real time rather than leave her waiting beside it.

She moved Dumpling gently enough to free her numb foot, then reached for the bookmark.

Tonight was still hers to finish.
