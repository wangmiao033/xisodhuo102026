# Chapter 178 — Dumpling Does Not Sign Consent Forms

The suitcase emerged from Eve's cupboard wearing a scarf she had been searching for since the weather turned cooler.

“Well,” she said. “One mystery solved.”

Julian caught the scarf before it hit the floor. He had come on Friday evening with a bag of clean clothes, intending to see how much the borrowed case would hold before taking it home. Eve had promised a reliable suitcase. She had forgotten its last journey had involved a jar of jam.

There was still a faint purple mark on the lining.

“That is an old stain,” she said. “Not a feature.”

“I wasn't going to ask.”

“You looked directly at it.”

“I was admiring the luggage.”

She hung her recovered scarf by the door. Julian took off his own and laid it in the empty case while he checked the wheels.

The kitchen timer rang before she could improve her defense. Eve went to turn the food. By the time she returned, Dumpling occupied the case.

He lay across its lower half, paws tucked neatly beneath him, beside Julian's scarf. His orange back filled the space with an air of administrative finality.

Julian stood over him holding a folded shirt.

“There has been a booking conflict.”

Eve put the oven gloves down.

Dumpling looked at her, then at the shirt. His tail moved once along the lining.

For a silly second, relief came before worry. Perhaps he objected. Perhaps there was a reason she ought to listen to the animal who sometimes noticed things before either of them said them aloud.

She knew better than to give that thought a second sentence. Still, she felt it rising: he knows.

“He doesn't want you to go,” she said.

Julian lowered the shirt. “Maybe he likes my scarf.”

“Both could be true.”

“They could. But he hasn't told us either.”

The words were mild. Eve heard the argument she had been preparing inside them and felt embarrassed.

“I wasn't about to cancel the train on his behalf.”

“I didn't think you were.”

Dumpling stretched one paw onto the scarf. The gesture was very persuasive if one happened to be looking for persuasion.

Eve crouched a little distance away, leaving the opening clear. “You are making a terrible case for impartiality.”

Dumpling blinked at her.

She remembered the packing boxes that had troubled him before, the scraps of his earlier life she still couldn't turn into a complete story. It was easy to connect every box and bag to that uncertainty. Easy, too, to make the unknown parts say what she wanted them to say.

The case was open. Nobody was putting his belongings into it. His food, water, litter tray, and usual beds remained where they had been that morning. Julian had set a familiar scarf on a new surface, and a cat had chosen to lie there.

That much she actually knew.

“I'll get his blanket,” she said.

“Where would you like the suitcase?”

“Leave it. I'll put the blanket beside it. He can choose.”

She fetched the small blanket he already used on the chair rather than opening a cupboard for something freshly washed. It carried the smell of home as far as her human nose could tell, though her nose was not the one doing the important investigation.

She spread it on the floor a little way from the case. Dumpling watched with interest, then closed his eyes.

“Excellent uptake,” Julian said.

“It's a soft launch.”

She went back to the kitchen. There was no reason to turn dinner cold while waiting for a cat to approve the furniture.

They ate at the table with the suitcase still open across the room. Julian had brought enough vegetables to feed two people without making a declaration about carrots. Eve tasted the roasted onions and decided that accepting help with food might be one of her better qualities.

“What have you packed so far?” she asked.

“A notebook. Two pens. Work clothes.”

“An alarming number of pens?”

“Two.”

“Then how will everyone know you're a serious student?”

“My extremely clean notebook.”

She asked whether he was nervous. He put down his fork, considering it instead of producing his usual sensible answer too quickly.

“A little. I know how our team works. I'll have to ask questions other people there may think are obvious.”

“They've invited you to learn.”

“I do understand the concept.”

“Emotionally, though.”

He looked at her over his glass.

She pointed her fork at herself. “I am a highly experienced person at understanding a concept and disliking the part where it applies to me.”

That earned a laugh.

He told her about a question he'd been writing down and rewording because he couldn't decide how much background was useful. Eve listened, then said she had no idea whether the background mattered medically, but the version he had just said aloud made sense to her.

“Perhaps ask them how much they need,” she suggested.

He nodded. “Rather than submit a small book.”

“Save the book. You might get a publication out of this.”

Across the room, Dumpling lifted his head when Julian laughed. His ears angled toward them. He looked from one face to the other, then settled again. Eve was glad she hadn't assigned him responsibility for the evening. He would have been badly overqualified for the vegetables and entirely unqualified for the train ticket.

After dinner she washed while Julian dried. He picked up a cup with a small chip and put it aside for her to inspect instead of quietly discarding it.

“Plant pot,” she decided.

“You have several.”

“A very small plant pot.”

He accepted the promotion.

When they returned to the living room, Dumpling had moved halfway onto the blanket. His back paws remained in the suitcase, preventing anyone from declaring the matter settled.

Eve sat on the sofa. “He has negotiated access to both properties.”

Julian sat beside her with the folded shirt still in his hand. He had carried it through three rooms by then.

“You can put that down,” she said.

“I've committed to keeping it folded.”

“Your first training challenge.”

He laid it on his knees.

For a while they said nothing. Eve could hear a neighbor moving a chair overhead and water running in the wall. The suitcase had a little fabric pocket inside its lid. She remembered hiding receipts there to sort after a trip and finding them months later, irrelevant except for the way they brought back a particular breakfast.

“You can leave the scarf,” she said.

“I need it.”

“Oh.”

She hadn't meant the offer to sound so small.

He turned toward her. “Would you like me to leave something else?”

She stared at the case. “I was pretending it was for him.”

“I gathered.”

“I don't need a shrine.”

“I'd have struggled to supply one tonight.”

“But perhaps the old sweater you wear here. If you won't need it.”

“It's at my flat. I'll bring it tomorrow.”

She nodded. Asking had been much less dignified than making a joke about the cat, and considerably more useful.

Dumpling stood, stepped fully onto the blanket, and began washing his chest. Julian didn't grab the suitcase immediately. He let the cat finish moving away, then reached for the scarf with slow, uninteresting care.

Eve watched Dumpling's ears. They stayed relaxed. He glanced at Julian and went back to washing.

The scarf went into a side pocket. Julian put in the shirt, then the other clothes he'd brought. The case swallowed them without effort.

“See?” Eve said. “Plenty of room.”

“I haven't packed the books.”

“Perhaps the books could travel without several changes of shirt.”

He put his notebook on top. Dumpling approached again, sniffed the outer corner, and walked around it to his water bowl.

Eve had the peculiar sensation of being left without a useful excuse. The cat wasn't protesting. He was drinking. Julian was counting socks. Her worry remained hers, stubbornly undramatic.

“I'm going to miss you,” she said.

He stopped counting.

“I know we discussed it. I'm saying it without an agenda.”

“I'll miss you too.”

She leaned against his shoulder while he zipped the side compartment. He kissed the top of her head, then asked whether six pairs of socks was foolish for three weeks if he could do laundry.

It was such a practical question that she laughed into his sweater.

“Take seven. Allow one to abandon you.”

He added another pair.

They checked every compartment and the open space around the case before closing it. Dumpling was on his familiar blanket, watching Julian test the zipper. Eve kept the blanket where it was. There was no need to remove a perfectly good place to sleep because the human experiment had ended.

The old luggage tag still held Eve's faded details. Julian brought it to her rather than filling out a second one over them.

“I'll keep this here,” she said, sliding the card free. “Put your own contact number inside the case as well.”

He found a blank scrap of paper and wrote on it at the table. She deliberately did not tidy the capitals for him. They might be awkward, but they were readable.

He checked the address of the room against his confirmation and saved the directions for the walk from the station. The route looked short enough until he noticed a flight of steps. He checked the alternative with the case in mind.

“Level route,” he said. “A few minutes longer.”

Eve felt an absurd rush of relief at this small thing he could solve himself, in advance. She gave him a rubber band for the folded directions and resisted offering to print another map.

One wheel complained exactly as advertised when Julian pulled the suitcase toward the door.

“That sound could identify you from quite a distance,” Eve said.

“I'll use it as my arrival announcement.”

He stopped at the threshold and extended the handle. It stuck halfway.

Eve's pride in the suitcase suffered a serious injury.

“Press both sides.”

He did. The handle rose with a reluctant click.

“Reliable,” he said.

“It has character.”

“It has several characters. They're arguing.”

She tried it twice more herself. The button needed to be pressed squarely rather than at an angle. She showed him, and it worked. If it failed completely, the case still had its fixed handles; he could decide whether he wanted another before traveling.

“I'd rather you told me if it's a nuisance,” she said.

“I will. It seems fine.”

She believed him enough to stop demonstrating it.

After he went, she returned to find Dumpling kneading the blanket. He turned his head toward the door at the distant rattle of the wheel, then put his chin down on his paws.

Eve sat on the floor beside him, outside the circle he'd claimed. Her hand rested on the rug. He reached his nose toward it briefly and withdrew.

“I know,” she began, then stopped herself.

She didn't know what he made of a suitcase leaving without him. She could watch, keep his evening familiar, and notice if anything changed. There was no answer hidden in the orange curve of his back that would make three weeks disappear.

Her phone vibrated. Julian had sent a photograph of the suitcase beside his door, standing upright and apparently uninjured.

Handle still works, he wrote.

Eve replied with a picture of Dumpling on the blanket. No interpretation attached.
