# Chapter 214 — The Final Old Program

Eve found the cardboard moon wedged behind the spare display board, smiling at a wall.

One point of its crescent had folded inward. The gold paint had worn away along the bottom, where people invariably held it instead of using the handle. She remembered painting it after midnight and telling herself the second coat would make the whole event look more expensive.

It had mostly made the kitchen smell.

“Keep or cut?” Robin asked.

They were holding a box labelled SMALL USEFUL PIECES. Beside it stood a considerably larger box labelled THINGS EVE WILL ARGUE ABOUT.

“Your filing system is aggressive.”

“It has already saved us time.”

Eve turned the moon over. On the back, beneath a strip of tape, she had written the running order of an early session. Three activities, a demonstration, a photograph, questions, and a departure time nobody had ever reached on schedule.

“Use the unpainted middle for small signs,” she said. “Keep the crescent until this afternoon.”

Robin put it in neither box.

The last edition of the old weekend program was due to begin at two. Eve had told every booked customer that this would be the final session in that format, offered refunds to anyone who no longer wanted to come, and stopped taking bookings beyond it. The venue would remain open for smaller activities. She had written that distinction twice in the notice and still received a message asking whether the business was closing.

She understood. The big program was what people recognized.

A week of smaller exchanges could be useful, solvent, and pleasant without producing a photograph that looked as though anything had happened. The old event filled the room with color. It made the place look established. Giving it an ending felt uncomfortably close to taking her name off something.

She looked past Robin toward the rear window.

The barrier had finally come down on Thursday, after the contractor's drying checks, finishing work, and the building manager's confirmation that the section could return to ordinary use. The basic replacement panel was a slightly different shade. Eve had accepted it after checking the function, then put the repair record away. There would be no new cabinetry.

Having the space back did not repair the event's accounts.

They set up within the agreed capacity, leaving the passage clear. Paid preparation time was in the day's cost, as were the closing hours. Eve had cut the elaborate scenery and shortened her opening remarks, leaving every advertised activity intact, rather than hiding extra work after everyone went home. The whole farewell would end at four, including the optional sorting table.

At one forty, a regular arrived early with a folded shopping bag.

“I thought I'd get a place before everybody comes,” she said.

“We have your booking. You haven't lost it.”

“I meant a good place.”

Eve showed her the available seats. The woman chose one with her back to the window and studied the little notice beside the door.

“I liked having the whole afternoon planned,” she said. “The small groups sound like more talking.”

“Some will be. The descriptions will say what each one involves.”

“I don't always want to explain myself to strangers.”

The objection stopped Eve's ready explanation about the benefits of a smaller format. She had begun to imagine everyone discovering that less was better. Here was someone for whom less would simply be less.

“You don't have to come to those,” she said. “I'm glad this version suited you.”

“Couldn't you just charge more?”

“We looked at that. At the price we'd need, with the hours properly covered, I don't think we'd sell enough places consistently. And I don't want to keep committing the team to this schedule.”

The woman folded her bag more tightly.

“It's a shame.”

“Yes,” Eve said.

She let the word stay there without attaching a promise to reconsider.

By two, the room had filled to its booked number. Nobody needed a rescue chair in the passage. Robin checked people in while Eve adjusted a sample card that kept sliding from its stand. The cardboard moon waited at the side, looking faintly embarrassed by daylight.

Eve welcomed everyone. Her prepared introduction contained a line about journeys. She abandoned it.

“This is our last afternoon in this format. Thank you for coming, including those of you who are disappointed about that. We're going to do the activities you booked, and afterward anyone who wants to can help turn some old scenery into things we'll use in the smaller sessions. You can also leave without cutting up a single moon.”

A man near the back raised a hand.

“Do we have to be good at it?”

“Look closely at the moon,” Robin said.

The laughter loosened something beneath Eve's ribs.

The first activity went slowly. A pair spent longer disagreeing over the arrangement of their sample instructions than the exercise allowed. Eve gave the next group a shorter introduction instead of rushing the pair to finish a conversation she had invited them to have. Robin brought out the spare plain cards when one participant wanted to start again.

There was no dramatic improvement in attendance, no final-minute queue outside. The people who had booked did ordinary things, occasionally well. Eve forgot a question, found it on the reverse of her card, and admitted that she had written herself a reminder where she couldn't see it.

“That seems on theme,” the early visitor said.

Eve laughed with her.

At the break, the woman asked whether she could keep her own exercise sheet.

“Of course.”

“My sister might actually understand this version.”

Eve did not ask to photograph it. The sheet went into the carefully folded bag.

Julian arrived for the final half hour as a visitor, at the time they had arranged. He wore an ordinary sweater and carried nothing that could be mistaken for equipment. Eve pointed him toward a chair rather than asking him to solve a participant's question. A customer recognized him and greeted him; he answered, then sat beside the moon.

The sorting table was already attracting interest.

They kept the cutting tools at the supervised end and offered simple pieces people could tear or fold. Robin separated anything with exposed fastenings before it reached the table. Visitors could suggest a new use without being expected to perform the venue's cleanup. The real dismantling remained paid work after the session.

Eve laid down a panel she had once used as a pretend shopfront.

“This can become the backing for the sample wall.”

“Does it have to keep the little window?” somebody asked.

“No.”

“Good. It's a very judgmental window.”

A square came out of the middle. Eve felt a brief, absurd pain as the painted frame disappeared. Then Robin held the remaining piece against a stack of smaller display cards, and it was exactly the size they needed.

The man who had asked whether skill was required made three lopsided arrows. They tested them from the doorway. Two pointed clearly. The third appeared to be apologizing.

“We can use those two,” Eve said.

He kept the other for his refrigerator.

Julian was given a broad cardboard band and a pair of enormous ears cut from a damaged backdrop. One ear had a pink center. The other retained part of a painted price label.

“Hold still,” Eve said.

“Why?”

“Structural assessment.”

She slipped the loose band over his head. It rested comfortably above his eyebrows, the ears extending far beyond any plausible animal proportions.

Robin looked up.

“One of those costs three ninety.”

“The other one is priceless,” Eve said.

Julian inspected her face. “You're enjoying this considerably more than the accounts.”

“Your accounts should wear ears.”

He left them on while helping a visitor hold two pieces of card together. Eve watched his serious concentration beneath the ridiculous silhouette and had to turn away before laughing spoiled the alignment.

At four, she thanked everyone again. She mentioned where the new small-session descriptions would appear, without making anybody sign up as proof that the farewell had worked. Some stayed briefly to talk. Others left promptly. The early visitor wished Eve well and said she probably wouldn't book the talking groups.

“I'll still look at the instructions posts,” she added.

“I'm glad.”

Eve meant it. She didn't need every person to follow her into every version of the place.

When the door closed behind the last participant, Robin checked the room against the closing plan. The little arrows went in the useful box. The moon's crescent survived, but its plain middle became four sturdy signs. Eve kept one photograph of the empty setup for the business record, without people or private exercise sheets in it.

Julian had left a few minutes earlier to collect the groceries he had already planned to buy. He hadn't been recruited to stay for dismantling. Eve and Robin finished within their booked time, carrying the reusable pieces onto the cleared shelves and flattening the rest for the proper collection.

Before they closed the register, Robin checked the final receipts against the bookings. The session had brought in the amount they expected, without enough left to reverse the quarter. Eve entered the paid preparation and closing hours beside it. The farewell had been worth holding for the people already promised a place; that did not make it evidence for reviving the format. She saved the record with the other sessions rather than giving this one a special, flattering category.

The large program board was the last thing on the table.

Eve peeled off the strip with the recurring dates. It came away in a long, satisfying ribbon, taking a little paint with it.

“There,” she said.

Robin held out the waste bag.

“Do you want a minute?”

Eve looked at the blank space. She remembered a visitor finally finding a clear phrase, a photograph ruined by her own elbow, and the nights she had brought the leftover work home because she couldn't bear to cost it properly.

“I've had a good afternoon,” she said. “I don't need to make it last until midnight.”

She dropped the ribbon into the bag.

Outside, Julian was waiting near the corner with groceries in both hands.

He still wore the ears.

Eve stopped walking.

“What?” he asked.

She pointed at his head.

His expression changed slowly. He put one grocery bag down and reached upward, finding the cardboard before his hair.

“The cashier was unusually cheerful,” he said.

Eve laughed so hard she had to lean against the wall, clear of the doorway. Julian looked at the pink ear, then the fragment of price label on the other.

“She asked whether there was an offer.”

“What did you say?”

“That I hadn't checked.”

He took off the band and gave it to her. She carried it flat on top of the groceries while they walked home. It was too large for the useful-pieces box and too funny to throw away immediately.

Tomorrow she would decide where it belonged.

Tonight, she had finished the event. Beside her, Julian described the cashier's face, and Eve missed the turn because she was laughing again.
