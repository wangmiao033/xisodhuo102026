# Chapter 32: The Terms in Brackets

The woman beside Eve at the folding table had brought her own spoon.

It emerged from a narrow case with a napkin, which made the cracked plastic stirrer in Eve's hand feel like evidence of poor professional preparation.

“Tea always tastes like the last thing they washed in these,” the woman said, inspecting the community hall's mug. “I draw the line at borrowing the cutlery too.”

Eve smiled. “I hadn't realized there were levels of survival equipment.”

“There are levels of being burned.”

She stirred once and introduced herself as Claire.

Eve recognized the name before she recognized the face. Claire made pet-related videos with clean captions, brisk demonstrations, and a talent for making a lint brush look like something a person might want to own rather than a threat to creativity.

Eve had watched one the previous night while trying not to compare it with her own unfinished ideas.

“I know your work,” she said.

“Then I hope you know the newer work.”

“Why?”

“The older work has a recurring font crime.”

The gathering was billed as a local creators' exchange, which Eve had feared meant standing in a room saying her follower count to strangers. In practice, it involved thirty people, unreliable tea, and a screen that refused to connect to anyone's laptop.

They were waiting for a demonstration about lighting small products. Eve had come because she needed to learn how to make a bag interesting without asking a cat to become a marketing department.

Claire glanced at the tote under her chair.

“Pocket Path?”

“You know them?”

“I've seen the samples. Nice lining.”

“I'm discussing a job.”

“Discussing is good.”

It wasn't said with enough enthusiasm for Eve to feel reassured.

Before she could ask, the organizer announced that the screen had been abandoned in favor of a real lamp. A man placed a white box on the table, bounced light off a piece of foam board, and asked them to notice the difference.

Eve did. The shadow softened. The buckle became visible instead of disappearing into a dark patch. It was ordinary craft, surprisingly satisfying.

She took notes until the instructor asked someone to volunteer a product.

Claire nudged the tote with her shoe.

“Let your bag earn its seat.”

Eve carried it forward. Under the first arrangement of light it looked flat and shapeless. Under the second, the pocket seams showed. When the instructor stuffed it too tightly, she asked him to take the towel out.

“It doesn't really sit like that when it's full.”

“Good catch,” he said, and adjusted it.

She returned to her seat feeling absurdly proud of having prevented a small lie about a bag.

During the break, Claire asked what Pocket Path wanted.

Eve described the film, including the fee. She lowered her voice for the number.

Claire didn't widen her eyes.

“And the use?”

“Social channels.”

“Their own?”

“I think so.”

“Paid ads?”

“Possibly.”

“For how long?”

Eve's pride in the towel adjustment drained away.

“There's paperwork.”

“Usually where they keep the answers.”

Eve opened the saved document on her phone, then hesitated. “Would you mind looking at one paragraph? You don't have to read the whole thing.”

“I can tell you what I'd ask. I'm not your legal adviser.”

“Understood.”

She showed Claire the marked passage. Claire read silently, her expression becoming less amused but not dramatic.

“This,” she said, pointing. “Perpetual. All media. Transferable. And this includes the raw footage, not just your finished film.”

Eve read it again.

The words had been there yesterday. They had simply looked less solid than the fee.

“They could keep using it?”

“That's what they're asking you to agree to. Ask them to define the use they actually need. Don't make me the person who interprets it for them.”

“What if it's their standard form?”

“Then their standard form wants a lot.”

Someone behind them laughed at a lighting mishap. Eve could hear a chair scraping, a kettle switching on, a dozen conversations in which other people seemed to know what their work was worth.

“I don't want to sound difficult before I've even made anything.”

Claire put her spoon back in its case.

“Then sound specific.”

She didn't offer a speech about confidence. Instead she asked whether Eve had priced one edit or a permanent library of every usable moment she might film, including moments she chose not to publish.

“One edit.”

“Put that in the offer.”

“And if they say no?”

“Then you know what they're saying no to.”

At the next table, the organizer asked for volunteers to share a short film. Claire showed a simple demonstration of a washable mat. There was no emotional rescue story, no breathless reveal, just muddy boots, a sensible sequence, and a dry joke that earned a laugh.

Eve watched how little the film wasted.

Afterward, she told Claire she liked it.

“The client wanted a dog to look grateful,” Claire said.

“What did you do?”

“Asked who was washing the floor.”

Before she left the hall, Eve helped stack the folding chairs. Claire took the other end of a stubborn table, and together they discovered that neither of them knew which lever released the legs.

“Professional creators,” Claire said, while the organizer demonstrated the obvious catch.

“Very strong with visual instructions,” Eve added.

They carried the folded table to the wall. Eve asked whether Claire ever took a job she regretted.

“Recently?”

“That recently?”

“A beautiful product. Three people giving incompatible feedback. I charged for two rounds of revisions and performed seven.”

“Even you?”

Claire gave her a look. “Please don't make me into an inspirational object. I'm tired enough already.”

Eve apologized, laughing. It was a relief to like her without needing her to be proof that there was a perfect way to work.

They exchanged contact details beside a bin full of broken plastic stirrers. Claire declined Eve's offer to send the whole contract; one useful conversation wasn't an unlimited retainer.

On the way home, Eve stopped in a café and bought the cheapest hot drink that justified a table. She wrote to Felix while the conversation was still clear in her mind.

She listed the deliverable: one finished short film, one agreed revision, no raw-footage transfer. She asked him to specify the platforms and duration. She separated the higher fee for wider use from the smaller fee for a limited campaign.

Then she wrote the uncomfortable part.

Dumpling's participation could not be guaranteed. Any trial would be short, quiet, and voluntary. No repeated attempts after clear reluctance. The concept would have to work without him, or they would stop without pretending usable footage existed.

She read the message aloud under her breath. The woman at the next table looked over, and Eve pretended to be checking a voice note.

Finally she pressed send.

Felix called before her tea cooled.

“I thought we were roughly agreed,” he said.

“We agreed to discuss it.”

“Right. But this is a smaller license for the same production fee.”

“The original fee was what you proposed. I hadn't accepted the rights attached to it.”

There was a pause. She could hear packing tape being pulled from a roll.

“We use that form for everything.”

“I understand. I need the agreement for this job to describe this job.”

He exhaled, not angrily, but with the fatigue of someone whose afternoon had acquired another task.

“Let me look at the numbers.”

Eve agreed. She didn't fill the silence with a discount.

When the call ended, her hands were cold around the mug.

She had imagined that saying a reasonable thing would make her feel reasonable. Mostly it made her feel as if she had pushed a good offer off the table and would now have to watch it hit the floor.

At home she worked on an unrelated captioning job while waiting. The work was dull and paid by the hour. She finished it on time. Money didn't become less real because it lacked a dramatic negotiation.

Felix's revised message arrived after six.

He could accept a limited campaign using only the finished film. The full concept still depended on whether Dumpling would appear. Rather than commit to production, he proposed a short trial in a small bookable photo room away from the shop's deliveries.

Eve read the costs carefully this time.

They would split the room's minimum preparation booking. Her share was thirty-six, payable whether or not the trial produced anything. Neither would owe the other a film or a further booking if it failed. No footage from the trial could be used in advertising without a separate agreement.

She called to ask why he needed the trial at all.

“To see whether the cat can be comfortable near the setup,” he said. “And whether what I'm imagining exists outside my head.”

That, at least, was a better question than how to obtain a guaranteed clever response.

They went over the room: a closed door, no other animals, no public visitors, no loud playback, a place for Dumpling's carrier to remain open without anyone surrounding it. Eve asked for photographs, then arranged to see it without him first.

Felix agreed to a maximum of twenty minutes of quiet settling time and no attempt to coax Dumpling onto a mark. If he stayed in the carrier or withdrew, that would be the answer.

Eve could afford thirty-six, though she would notice its absence. She wrote it into her trial budget, not her imaginary future earnings.

At the room the next morning, she checked the entrance, the window latch, and the space behind the backdrop. She asked for the spare cartons to be removed so there would be no inaccessible hiding gap. The attendant did it while Felix held the door.

“This is more planning than the bag's first catalogue shoot,” he said.

“The bag doesn't get frightened.”

“Fair.”

They signed the short trial arrangement only after the wording matched what they had discussed. Eve saved her copy and photographed the room as it actually stood, not as she hoped it would feel.

On her way out, she found a message from Claire.

*Did you get your answers?*

Eve typed, *Some. The rest are written down as questions now.*

*That counts.*

She almost sent the new fee, asking whether she had done well. Instead she thanked Claire for pointing out the usage clause and put the phone away.

At home, Dumpling was asleep in a stripe of sun, his chin resting on the edge of his blanket. She sat nearby without waking him.

The trial might fail. The larger fee might disappear. Nothing in the signed page required her to believe otherwise.

For the first time since the offer arrived, that uncertainty had a shape she could see.

It was smaller than the whole month. It had a time limit. It had a cost she had chosen, and a door they were allowed to leave through.
