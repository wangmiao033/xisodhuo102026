# Chapter 063 — It Isn't the Camera I Care About

Sophie held two curtain samples against her coat and asked Eve which one looked less like a waiting room.

“The green,” Eve said.

“Which green?”

Eve looked properly. Both samples were green. One contained tiny cream leaves; the other appeared to have been designed by a person who had recently become angry with stripes.

“The leaves.”

“You answered before looking.”

“I was thinking.”

“I noticed. You nearly walked into a lamp.”

They stood in the narrow aisle of a secondhand household shop, surrounded by objects whose former owners had all apparently preferred brown. Sophie wanted a curtain for the small window above her kitchen sink. Her plants had survived long enough to deserve a less crowded arrangement, and the current curtain caught on everything.

Eve had offered company. She had not, so far, supplied much.

“Sorry,” she said. “Show me the measurements again.”

Sophie unfolded a paper from her pocket. The window was narrower than the samples suggested. They found a plain piece of fabric on a lower rail, measured it with the tape hanging beside the till, and discovered it was short by an amount too small to be dramatic and too large to ignore.

“Can you add a strip?” Eve asked.

“I could. I could also choose something that fits.”

“A radical purchasing strategy.”

Sophie put it back.

They spent another ten minutes finding a piece with faded blue flowers and enough length. The shopkeeper let them hold it against the window to check whether it became transparent in daylight. It didn't. Sophie bought it, along with a wooden spoon she insisted had excellent balance.

Outside, Eve asked whether she wanted tea.

“Yes. And then you can explain why you've been having an argument with an invisible person all morning.”

“I haven't been arguing.”

“You said 'exactly' to a shelf.”

“It was a very persuasive shelf.”

They found a café with a back table large enough for the curtain bag. Sophie put the spoon beside it as though reserving an additional place.

Eve ordered tea and something warm to eat. She hadn't skipped lunch; it was simply late enough for a second small meal to be an honest desire rather than a failure of planning. Sophie chose soup and asked whether the bread was included before accepting the extra portion.

When the server left, Eve described the film with Claire.

She began with the sensible parts: the defined fee, Mabel's interview, the decision not to bring Biscuit to a set for the sake of a familiar animal face. Sophie listened, turning her spoon over once between her fingers.

“It sounds good,” she said.

“It is.”

“Then where is the argument?”

Eve wrapped both hands around her cup.

“Claire works well with Julian.”

“Yes?”

“Very well.”

Sophie waited.

“She knows how to get him to sound natural. She doesn't have to explain everything. He laughs.”

“Has she done something to you?”

“No.”

“Has he?”

“No.”

The answers were so short that Eve had no place to hide inside them.

Sophie placed the wooden spoon back on the bag.

“Do you think the clinic won't hire you again?”

“I don't know. They can hire whoever they want.”

“That isn't what I asked.”

Eve looked toward the counter. A server was trying to fit an enormous cake cover beneath a shelf built for smaller ambitions.

“I was worried about that at first,” she said. “Or I told myself I was.”

“And now?”

“Claire has offered me paid work. Work she actually thinks I'm good at. It would be difficult to keep calling her a threat to my career.”

“You could. People manage quite difficult things when they want to avoid a simpler answer.”

Eve gave her an unamused look. Sophie didn't soften the sentence into a joke.

“Are you afraid of losing a client,” she asked, “or of him liking somebody else?”

The soup arrived before Eve answered. She was grateful for the interruption until she realized she would still be at the same table when the server left.

Sophie thanked him, moved her bag away from the bowl, and waited.

“The second one,” Eve said.

She said it quietly enough that she could have pretended the café noise swallowed it. Sophie heard.

“All right.”

“That's all?”

“What else do you want me to say?”

“I don't know. Something that makes me less ridiculous.”

“Feeling jealous isn't especially original. Treating Claire badly because of it would be a choice.”

“I haven't.”

“Good.”

“I was cooler than I needed to be at the clinic. Mostly to Julian.”

“Did you expect him to guess why?”

Eve thought about the word *perhaps*, delivered as if he had asked something unreasonable instead of whether he would see her later.

“For a minute, yes.”

“And did that help?”

“No.”

Sophie took a spoonful of soup. It was too hot. She set the spoon down and waited without turning the small discomfort into a test of endurance.

Eve watched the steam.

She had wanted her friend to say Claire wasn't interested, or Julian obviously preferred Eve, or no one who remembered vinegar could possibly be a source of uncertainty. Those would all be comforting claims Sophie had no way to establish.

“You don't know what he thinks,” Eve said.

“No. I know what you've told me he said.”

“He wanted to keep talking. He asked me out for lunch. We had a good time.”

“Those sound like things that happened.”

“They did.”

“You can enjoy them without making them a contract he hasn't signed.”

Eve looked down at her tea. “I don't want a contract.”

“Then don't act as if someone breached one.”

The words stung. She let them.

For several minutes they ate. Sophie asked for a little more water, and Eve passed the jug. The café continued around them without requiring a revelation from either woman.

At last Eve said, “I want him to be interested in me.”

Sophie nodded.

“Not just my work.”

“Yes.”

“And I don't want to find out that I've been turning ordinary kindness into something it isn't.”

“That part sounds frightening.”

“It is.”

There was the useful response. Not a verdict about Julian, not a strategy for defeating Claire. A place to admit the risk without immediately repairing it.

Eve leaned back, letting the chair hold her weight.

“I keep thinking I should ask Dumpling something,” she admitted, lowering her voice. “Then I remember how absurd that is.”

“Which part?”

“All of it. He can't tell me what somebody elsewhere is thinking. And even when something strange seems to happen, I don't know how to interpret it. I'd still be trying to get an answer without asking the person.”

Sophie looked at her steadily. She was one of the few people Eve had involved in the harmless observations, and she did not pretend those afternoons had turned the cat into a reliable private investigator.

“What would you actually ask Julian?”

Eve opened her mouth, then closed it.

There were several large questions she wasn't ready to say. Do you like me the way I like you? Was lunch a date? Have I missed the moment when I was supposed to know?

Underneath them was a smaller question that also mattered.

“I don't even know whether he's seeing someone.”

“Then perhaps that's information to ask him for. Not information to collect from Tessa or Claire.”

“I wasn't going to ask them.”

“I'm glad.”

Eve stirred her tea though there was nothing left to dissolve.

“I don't have to ask today.”

“No. You also don't have to keep pretending the camera is what bothers you.”

They paid separately, as they usually did when neither had offered a treat. Outside, Sophie unfolded the receipt and discovered the wooden spoon had cost less than she thought.

“A successful outing,” she said.

“Because of the spoon?”

“Among other things.”

Eve walked with her to the bus stop. The curtain bag was awkward against Sophie's knee, and Eve offered to carry it while Sophie checked the route display. Her friend handed it over without interpreting help as a moral problem. They were allowed to assist each other with a bag without repeating the entire lesson of the hall event.

“How is your own work?” Eve asked.

Sophie looked at her. “You have time for the answer?”

“Yes.”

The bus was still several minutes away. Sophie described a task that had finally been assigned to the person responsible for it, and another duty her employer still hadn't settled. It wasn't a triumphant conclusion. She was tired of having to notice every ambiguity first.

Eve listened without offering a script.

“What would help this weekend?” she asked when Sophie stopped.

“Putting up this curtain and not talking about work for an afternoon.”

“Do you want company?”

“For measuring, yes. Not for redesigning my whole kitchen.”

“I can manage one curtain.”

“You said that about a plant tray.”

“I have developed.”

Sophie smiled. They arranged Sunday afternoon, with a clear understanding that buying hooks might prove more useful than giving each other speeches.

After Sophie's bus left, Eve went back to the café to collect the shop's borrowed tape measure. They had taken it along to check the curtain width against Sophie's measurements and then forgotten to return it. When Sophie checked her purchases at the bus stop, Eve had remembered the bright label lying beside the café till. Fortunately, the staff had kept it safely.

The return journey took ten minutes. Eve carried the tape curled in her palm, feeling less like a romantic heroine than a person conducting a small, avoidable errand.

At the shop, the assistant thanked her and hung it up again.

“People bring things back more often than you'd think,” he said.

“Even tape measures?”

“Especially those. They get home and realize it isn't a particularly good theft.”

Eve laughed all the way to the corner.

On the walk back, she passed a display of scarves. One was almost the same blue as Julian's. She noticed it, felt the familiar warm pull, and kept going without buying a gift to turn uncertainty into an event. She could like somebody and still walk past a shop without buying an answer.

At home, Eve found Dumpling batting a loose paper label across the floor. She collected it before he could chew it and replaced it with his fabric mouse. He sniffed the substitute, then walked away as if she had misunderstood the entire appeal.

“I don't know either,” she told him.

She sat at the desk and opened Claire's outline. Two questions about the filming position needed answers. Eve supplied them plainly, without the extra sharpness she had been tempted to disguise as professional rigor.

Then she opened Julian's conversation and looked at the last exchange: a small joke about the clinic's impossible drawer of clips.

She didn't type a test. She didn't manufacture an urgent reason to see him.

Instead she left the phone beside her notebook and wrote one private sentence at the top of a blank page.

*It isn't the camera I care about.*

For once, she did not improve the wording.
