# Chapter 139 — There May Be No Villain

By morning, Eve was cross with the cupboard door.

It had never closed properly when the pan handles faced outward. She knew this. She also knew that pushing it harder would not make the handles shorter. Nevertheless, she pushed again, heard the small wooden knock, and felt tears rise with an immediacy entirely disproportionate to kitchen storage.

Dumpling sat behind her, waiting for breakfast.

“Yes,” she told him. “One of us has priorities.”

She turned the pan, closed the cupboard, and fed him.

While he ate, she found a loose seam at the corner of his bed cover. A small pale tuft had worked its way through. She removed the exposed filling, checked the opening, and took the damaged cover off for mending. His familiar blanket and the intact cushion stayed in their usual place.

He sniffed the cushion when he returned. Then he walked away to investigate the refrigerator, which had apparently become more promising since breakfast.

Eve held the cover against her chest.

Yesterday she had stood in an emptied room trying to picture a cat's bed. Today she had a bed in her hands and no idea why a broken seam felt like something she ought to apologize for.

The message from the center arrived before lunch.

They had checked the readily available material. There was no individual animal record matching her inquiry among it. Older event photographs and papers had not all been sorted, and the current staff could not identify everyone who had helped at the temporary rooms. They would keep her contact details with the inquiry if anything relevant emerged.

Eve read the message twice.

No new thing had happened to Dumpling. Nobody had become less careful with him since yesterday. Yet the few polite sentences made his earlier life feel farther away.

She thanked the person who had checked. Then she opened her notes, looking for another question.

Daniel had supplied what he knew. Ada had shown the records she was entitled to share. The former coordinator had corrected dates and passed on the inquiry. Eve could ask each of them again, using slightly different words, and consume another afternoon without obtaining another fact.

She closed the notes.

The bed cover waited on the table beside a small tin of sewing things. She found a needle, threaded it on the third attempt, and discovered that her first knot passed straight through the fabric.

“This is an unreasonable entrance requirement,” she said.

Dumpling lifted his head from the windowsill.

“Not for you.”

She started again.

Julian arrived at three, at the time they had chosen. Eve had asked him to bring onions for soup because she was making it and had forgotten the onions. He brought two, rather than the ingredients for an entirely different dinner.

She kissed him in the doorway and took the bag.

“Is this the appropriate number?” he asked.

“I'm not qualified to judge emotionally. Practically, yes.”

He looked at her more closely, then stepped inside when she moved back.

Dumpling came down to inspect his shoes. Julian greeted him without picking him up. He hung his coat and followed Eve to the kitchen, where she put an onion on the board and immediately reached for a second knife she didn't need.

“Would you like me to do those?” he asked.

“Yes. Please.”

She handed him the correct knife and sat at the table.

He noticed the bed cover. “Repair?”

“Attempted. I think the stitches have become ornamental.”

“Does it hold?”

“Not yet.”

“Then very ornamental.”

She almost smiled. He sliced the onion while she studied the crooked line she had sewn. The useful thing about sewing was that a mistake remained visible. Her thoughts about Dumpling rearranged themselves every time she looked away.

“The center couldn't find anything else,” she said.

Julian waited while she explained exactly what they had checked. He did not turn limited files into a final answer, and she was grateful until she discovered that gratitude wasn't the feeling she wanted.

“I think I wanted someone to have done something obviously wrong.”

The knife stopped.

Eve looked down. “That sounds horrible.”

“Tell me what you mean.”

“Someone I could be angry with. Someone who wouldn't get to say it was complicated.”

Julian set the knife down before answering.

“You can be angry about what you found him in.”

“The rain. The injury. I am.”

She tightened the thread until the seam puckered. Then she loosened it with her fingernail.

“But I keep trying to send that anger somewhere. A person who shut a door. Somebody who knew and didn't care. If they were there, at least I'd know who to hate.”

“Were they?”

“I don't know.”

He resumed cutting, more slowly. Eve watched the pieces gather on the board. It helped that he had something to do besides look gently at her.

She told him about the changing room bookings. About the coordinator asking which time she meant. About the way the room had smelled faintly of cardboard and paste, nothing like the home she had prepared to recognize.

“What if everybody cared some of the time?” she asked. “And it still wasn't enough?”

Julian moved the onion into the pan.

“That can happen.”

The answer hurt more than an argument would have. Eve had expected him to remind her that good systems existed, that people did their best, that she had no right to imagine every handover as a failure. He said none of those things.

She put the needle down.

“I don't want you to make it reasonable.”

“I wasn't going to.”

“Sorry.”

“You can tell me what you need.”

She pressed her palms against the table. The room was warm, and the onions had begun to soften. Dumpling stood in the doorway, watching the work with the detached interest of someone whose own meal had been completed.

“I want to be sad about it for a while,” she said. “Without turning sadness into a job.”

Julian lowered the heat. Then he came to the chair beside hers.

“Come here?” he asked.

She leaned into him.

His hand settled between her shoulders. He didn't say that everything had brought the cat to her, as though someone else's difficult months could be justified by the comfort she had gained. She was glad. She would have hated that, and then hated herself for being ungrateful.

For a little while they sat with the pan quietly cooking behind them.

“I was also afraid,” she said into his shirt. “When Marion thought he was hers. I still am, a bit. What if the person we find is someone he wants?”

Julian's hand paused, then continued its slow movement.

“We don't know who we'll find.”

“That isn't the part I like.”

“I know.”

She drew back enough to see him. “You could at least be more irritatingly certain about my moral character.”

“There's a needle beside your hand. I thought I'd choose my moment.”

Eve looked down at the needle, safely on the table where she had left it. He followed her gaze.

“I may have misjudged the risk.”

She laughed for real, a small ugly sound that ended in a sniff.

He offered her a clean tissue from the box. She accepted it and went to stir the onions before they became part of another regret.

They finished the soup together. Eve added too much water, tasted it, and changed the heat rather than declaring dinner ruined. Julian washed the board and found the lid she wanted on the second attempt.

When they returned to the table, she showed him the private photographs from the room. He looked at the records first because she handed him those first, not because he was performing an investigation for her.

“This confirms the use,” he said.

“Yes. And the dates fit. I still don't know where he slept.”

Julian looked across at the window. Dumpling had climbed back onto the sill.

“Would you have believed him comfortable there if the room looked nicer?”

Eve considered the question. “Probably.”

“Would that have told us more?”

“No.”

She turned the phone face down. The cardboard and the empty sink were affecting her more than the words on the agreement. Another room, clean and full of cushions, could have left exactly the same gaps.

“I'll keep the inquiry open,” she said. “If somebody finds something, I want to know. I don't think I need to spend tonight asking the same people again.”

“All right.”

“What did you want to do tonight?”

He looked mildly surprised. “Eat the soup. See you.”

“Very limited ambition.”

“I also need to leave in time to do some washing.”

She smiled. His separate flat, with its ordinary demands, felt reassuring rather than like a departure she had to prevent.

While the soup finished, Eve repaired the seam properly. She took out her first stitches, held the edges together, and worked more slowly. Julian read beside her, occasionally shifting his chair away from the patch of light reflected off the phone.

Dumpling came to look. Eve kept the needle and thread out of reach, finished the last knot, and put every sewing thing back into the closed tin before offering the cover for inspection.

The cat sniffed the mended corner and stepped over it.

“Extensive praise,” she said.

“A demanding client.”

After she replaced the cover around the cushion, he kneaded the blanket twice. Then he walked away again.

She let the disappointment pass without carrying him back to demonstrate the comfort she had restored.

They ate soup with bread. Halfway through her bowl, Eve realized she had scarcely asked about his week.

“Have I used the whole afternoon?” she asked.

“For what?”

“My feelings.”

He considered the question while breaking a piece of bread. “You asked me to come because you were sad. I said yes.”

“I'd still like to hear about you.”

“Then let me finish this mouthful. It has become unexpectedly important.”

She waited, smiling. When he spoke, she left her own spoon in the bowl and listened without preparing another question about the cat.

Julian told her about a book whose ending had irritated him enough to improve his commitment to finishing it. Eve described the instructional photographs she'd rearranged. Neither story was important in the way the blue door had seemed important. She liked having room for them anyway.

When he left, she kissed him at the door and said she had wanted his company very much.

“I wanted to come,” he replied.

Later, while she washed the bowls, Dumpling finally settled in the repaired bed. His paws were tucked under his chest, the imperfect seam hidden beneath the cushion where no audience could admire it.

Eve dried her hands and sat nearby with her own book.

The missing parts remained. She felt them when she looked at him, but she also noticed the faint whistle from his nose and the ridiculous angle of one ear.

She could keep asking about the past when there was someone with something to tell.

Tonight she read three pages, lost her place, and read them again while he slept.
