# Chapter 199 — A Date Without Work

Eve was fastening an earring when the venue phone lit up on the dresser.

She looked at the screen in the mirror. A customer wanted to know whether Saturday's session included printed instructions. Beneath that was a question about a delivery, followed by a photograph of a chair from Robin.

The chair had four legs and no visible emergency.

Eve put the earring down.

From the hallway, Julian said, “Ready?”

“Nearly.”

She typed an answer about the instructions. The delivery question required opening an email. The chair, it turned out, was one of two possible replacements Robin had found for a damaged seat. They were asking for a decision before tomorrow afternoon, not before Eve left the bedroom.

Julian appeared in the mirror.

“Do we need to go later?”

“No.”

She was still holding the phone.

He waited. His coat was on, his hair was damp from the shower, and for once nothing about him suggested a man leaving halfway through another obligation. They had kept their weekly free evening on the calendar through the move, but twice it had turned into dinner surrounded by cardboard. Last week they had eaten while Eve dealt with a booking correction that took forty minutes.

Tonight she had chosen a small restaurant she wanted to try. She had made a reservation. She had even found both earrings.

The phone vibrated again.

“I need five minutes,” she said.

Julian took his coat off.

That simple movement made her stop typing.

“Actual five minutes,” she added. “To stop doing this. Not to answer everything.”

She went to the kitchen and called Robin.

“I'm about to go out. The printed instructions are included. I've answered that one. The chair can wait until tomorrow.”

“I know,” Robin said. “That's why I wrote tomorrow.”

“I read the words. Apparently I didn't believe them.”

“Your evening may survive a chair.”

Eve smiled and opened the delivery email. The supplier wanted confirmation of a receiving time for next week. She gave it, forwarded the confirmed slot to the shared booking record, and closed the thread.

Robin was finishing the last paid hour of preparation at the venue. No public session was running tonight. They confirmed what remained, including locking up, and when they would next be working. Eve changed the message on the business phone to say when ordinary inquiries would be answered. The building's urgent contact information was already available where it belonged; she checked that it was current rather than making herself the only possible route for every problem.

“Anything you actually need before you leave?” she asked.

“No. Enjoy dinner.”

“You too.”

“I intend to. Somewhere with no chairs I have to purchase.”

Eve ended the call and put the business phone on its charger. Her personal phone would come with her. There was no need for a dramatic disappearance from the world.

Julian stood by the table, examining one of Dumpling's toy mice.

“Have you done whatever you need to do?” she asked.

“Yes. I'm off duty, the handover is done, and the clinic knows who is covering. I told my mother I'd call tomorrow rather than hoping she'd guess.”

“Very advanced.”

“Years of professional education.”

She returned to the bedroom, found the abandoned earring beneath a tissue, and fastened it.

At the door, Dumpling sat beside his box and watched Julian tie his shoes. Eve had fed him, checked his water, and left the rooms arranged as usual. He neither occupied a coat nor attempted to relocate an essential sock.

“We're going out,” she told him.

He began washing a paw.

“A moving farewell,” Julian said.

Eve took his hand outside and did not ask whether he thought she had handled the phone situation well.

The restaurant was a narrow place with steamed windows and three handwritten specials. Their table stood against a wall beneath a crooked picture of a harbor. The waiter apologized because one of the specials had sold out.

“Which one?” Eve asked.

“The one you're pointing at.”

She lowered her finger.

Julian looked at the remaining choices. “A strong recommendation, then.”

They ordered two different dishes so Eve could try the sauce she had been curious about. Nobody asked Julian what he did for work. Nobody recognized her from an account photograph. The waiter was primarily interested in whether they wanted still water.

Eve found this luxurious.

While they waited, she told Julian about seeing a man carry an enormous lampshade onto the bus. The shade had occupied the seat beside him, upright and belted in with his arm.

“I respected it,” she said. “That lamp had somewhere to be.”

“More organized than we were when we moved.”

“Much better dressed.”

Her phone buzzed inside her bag. Her fingers moved toward it before she remembered that she didn't have to abandon the table for a sound.

She checked the screen once. Sophie had sent a photograph from her own event setup: a stack of decorative oranges and the caption, They have requested less orange.

Eve laughed and showed Julian.

“Can she achieve that?”

“She can achieve anything if the client accepts the additional cost.”

She sent back one sympathetic sentence, told Sophie she was at dinner, and put the phone away. Her friend's evening continued elsewhere. It did not need to enter this one in full.

Julian reached for the water jug.

“That reminds me of the walking tour,” he said.

“What walking tour?”

“The one during training. I started telling you on the phone.”

Eve remembered a missed connection, a short message, and something about a woman with an umbrella. She had supplied a laugh without ever learning what had happened.

“You said you'd accidentally attended something.”

“Yes.”

“Start again. Properly.”

He filled her glass, buying himself a few seconds.

“The training group had arranged a short walk after lunch. I was late leaving the building because I couldn't find my room key. There was a group by the entrance. Someone asked whether I was joining them.”

“And you said yes.”

“I was attempting to be sociable.”

“A dangerous impulse.”

“They walked toward the river. That was where we were supposed to go. A woman with a yellow umbrella started talking about the town, and I thought she was particularly well informed.”

“How long before you noticed?”

“Longer than I'd like.”

The waiter arrived with their food. Julian stopped while the plates were set down. Eve looked at her sauce, smelled garlic and something sharply sweet, and resisted the old reflex to photograph it before steam could blur the lens.

“Continue,” she said.

“She asked whether anyone knew when the old bridge was built. I thought this was an unusual team exercise, but training had included worse.”

“Did you answer?”

“I guessed.”

“Of course you did.”

“Incorrectly. She was very kind about it. Then we stopped at a building and she said the walking tour tickets included access to the courtyard.”

Eve put down her fork.

“Oh, Julian.”

“I looked around and realized I didn't recognize anyone.”

“You hadn't noticed that before?”

“I had spent the week meeting people I didn't recognize. It seemed consistent.”

She laughed hard enough that the woman at the next table smiled without knowing why.

“What did you do?”

“I told the guide. Quietly. She said I could leave at the next corner or buy a ticket and stay.”

“Which did you choose?”

“I bought a ticket. The actual training group had already gone back, and I wanted to see the courtyard.”

This made Eve laugh again, for a different reason. She could picture him, earnest and mildly mortified, deciding that embarrassment did not require wasting a perfectly good afternoon.

“Was it worth it?”

“There was a stone dog above the doorway. The guide said people thought it was a lion for about a century.”

“Professional misidentification.”

“I wasn't consulted.”

“How did the others find out?”

“The guide took a group photograph. I asked a colleague if he wanted to see the dog.”

Eve covered her eyes.

“I created the evidence myself,” Julian admitted.

She reached across the table and squeezed his hand. He was smiling now, a little rueful, waiting for her to recover enough to eat.

She loved that he had stayed for the courtyard. She loved that the story contained a choice he had made for no useful professional reason, and that tonight she had heard the whole thing, including the stone dog.

“Show me later,” she said.

“I will.”

They tasted each other's food. Her sauce was better; his potatoes were better. They negotiated exchanges with more enthusiasm than fairness, then ordered one dessert and two spoons.

The dessert came in a shallow dish with a brittle sugar crust. Eve tapped it with her spoon and sent a small shard onto the table.

“Delicate work,” Julian said.

“You try.”

He struck more gently. Nothing happened. He tried again, then handed the dish back to her.

“I withdraw the criticism.”

She broke the crust and gave him the larger piece. A song she half remembered played above the counter. Neither knew its name, and neither reached for a phone to find out. They hummed different versions until they both lost the tune.

Halfway through it, Julian asked what she wanted to do after dinner.

“Walk a little. No guide required.”

“A pity. I've developed expertise.”

“Which century was this pavement built?”

“I'll need to guess.”

They paid and went outside into cool air. A little rain had fallen while they ate, leaving the pavement dark. It had stopped now. Eve tucked her hand into his coat pocket with his, and they walked past closed shops without evaluating any of them as potential venues.

At a secondhand bookshop that stayed open late, they paused over a box of inexpensive paperbacks. Julian found a collection of local walks. Eve found a novel whose cover promised a secret and an inheritance, then decided she had already spent enough time with fictional property disputes.

She chose a short book about a failed expedition instead.

“Holiday planning?” Julian asked.

“Managing expectations.”

They browsed separately for a few minutes and met again by the counter. He bought the walking book. She bought hers. Outside, they compared the opening pages under the shop light until the owner came out to bring in the display.

“Sorry,” Eve said, moving aside.

“No hurry. Mind the step.”

They headed home by the longer route because neither needed to get somewhere else immediately. Julian showed her the stone dog on his phone when they stopped beneath an awning. It looked nothing like a lion and only approximately like a dog.

Eve studied his face at the edge of the group photograph. He looked pleased to be there.

“I like this one,” she said.

“So do I.”

At home, Dumpling was asleep in his box. Eve took off her earrings and left the business phone on its charger without checking the accumulated messages. Tomorrow had working hours in it.

Julian put the walking book beside his chair, then caught her hand as she passed.

“Good dinner?”

“Very good.”

“Despite the missing special?”

“I may recover.”

He kissed her. There was no ringing phone between the beginning and the end, and for once she stayed long enough to notice.
