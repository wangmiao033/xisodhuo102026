# Chapter 050 — Not a Work Invitation

The jigsaw box claimed to contain three hundred pieces and a pleasant afternoon.

Someone had crossed out *three hundred* and written *probably*.

Eve found this more trustworthy than most advertising.

She had picked it up from the hall's exchange table after packing away on Saturday. The picture showed a crowded seaside town: striped umbrellas, a lighthouse, several boats, and a man apparently carrying a wardrobe onto the beach. The box cost nothing. Finishing it would produce nothing she could invoice.

On Sunday, she tipped the pieces onto the kitchen table and discovered that the previous owner had also donated a shopping receipt and the corner of a crossword.

Dumpling reached for the receipt.

“No. That isn't the recreational part.”

She put both scraps in the recycling and moved the pieces farther from the edge. He watched from a chair, offended by the poor placement of everything interesting.

Julian was coming at three. They had arranged it after his Tuesday conversation with Dr. Clark, when she had asked whether he wanted tea on Sunday and he had said yes without consulting a list of unfinished projects between them.

Now she had a cleared table, two cups, and an activity whose attendance requirements she was trying not to formalize.

She nearly put the jigsaw away. Perhaps it was an odd thing to offer someone who spent the week concentrating on small details. Perhaps he would rather talk. Perhaps she should have asked first.

The doorbell interrupted her before she could create a questionnaire.

Julian stood outside with a paper bag from the bakery.

“I brought biscuits,” he said. “The woman asked if I wanted six or twelve.”

“And?”

“I said six. She told me twelve was better value.”

“So we have twelve.”

“We have six. I'm telling you because it felt like an achievement.”

Eve laughed and let him in.

Dumpling came to greet him, rubbing along one trouser leg before investigating the bakery bag. Julian put it safely on the counter and let the cat sniff his hand instead.

“Did you get your afternoon off yesterday?” Eve asked.

“Yes.”

“What did you do?”

“Bought groceries. Read. Fell asleep on the sofa.”

“Was that planned?”

“The reading was.”

She waited, half expecting a story about an interruption. He unbuttoned his jacket and hung it on the empty hook by the door.

“That was all,” he said.

“Sounds good.”

“It was.”

In the kitchen, he stopped beside the table.

“Is that man carrying a wardrobe?”

“I thought so. You may be looking at the central mystery.”

“Why is he taking it to the sea?”

“Perhaps the drawers wanted a holiday.”

Julian leaned closer to the picture. “It's a changing booth.”

Eve looked again. The box was worn enough to have removed one side of the object and most of the man's second arm. He might have been pushing rather than carrying it.

“That's less interesting,” she said.

“Still fairly inconvenient.”

“Would you mind a puzzle? I can put it away.”

“I don't mind.”

“You don't have to be good at it.”

“I appreciate the early reduction in expectations.”

She made tea while he turned pieces faceup. By the time she returned, he had separated several edges and placed a small cluster of red shapes near the box.

“Roofs?” she asked.

“Possibly the umbrellas. I'm preserving uncertainty.”

They sat at adjacent sides of the table, with Dumpling occupying the third chair. Eve placed the biscuit plate away from the puzzle, then promptly put a piece on it by mistake.

Julian lifted it off before adding a biscuit.

“Very dry texture.”

“Locally sourced cardboard.”

For a while they worked without speaking. Eve fitted three pieces of lighthouse together and discovered that her shoulders had lowered. She wasn't holding a question ready for the next pause. Julian wasn't looking toward the door.

Outside, someone was practicing a short passage on a wind instrument. The same few notes emerged, stopped, and began again with determined differences.

“Do you play anything?” Eve asked.

“No.”

“Nothing?”

“I had recorder lessons at school.”

“Everyone has a difficult past.”

“My family may have had the more difficult part.”

She smiled and told him about the time she had volunteered to operate music for a school performance because she thought it would keep her out of sight. She had played the entrance music twice, once while the performers were leaving, and the teacher had made a gesture she still couldn't interpret.

“Perhaps it meant stop,” Julian said.

“It involved both elbows. It could have meant anything.”

“Had anyone shown you the controls?”

“Yes. Unfortunately, I remembered them confidently and incorrectly.”

He laughed, then held two pieces together without forcing them. The colors matched; the edges didn't.

Eve took one and turned it.

“Different umbrella.”

“There are a lot of umbrellas.”

“Apparently the weather at this resort is a matter of trust.”

She found the missing piece and handed it across. Their fingers touched briefly. It was ordinary, accidental contact, and she still noticed it enough to become annoyed with herself.

She reached for her tea instead of examining the feeling.

Julian asked whether she had always liked puzzles.

“Not particularly. I used to like knowing where all the pieces were supposed to go.”

“That seems useful.”

“Until someone donates half a crossword.”

She showed him the crossed-out promise on the lid. He studied it, then looked at the spread on the table.

“We may have two puzzles.”

“What?”

He pointed to a dark green piece with a sliver of gold. There was no green of that shade anywhere in the seaside picture.

Eve picked it up. “A tree?”

“Or somebody's other afternoon.”

They searched without much system. Five more dark pieces appeared, including what looked unmistakably like the ear of a horse.

Eve began to laugh.

“It's an inland complication.”

“We can put those aside.”

“But what if the horse belongs on the beach?”

Julian turned the lid toward her. “Is there a horse?”

“There could be one behind the wardrobe.”

He looked at her steadily until she gave in and moved the doubtful pieces into a saucer.

Dumpling rose on his chair and stretched toward it. Eve moved the saucer to the counter, along with the loose pieces nearest his paw. She gave him the empty shallow box lid on the floor, away from their feet. He stepped into it, turned once, and sat with his sides extending beyond its inadequate walls.

“He doesn't seem concerned about completeness,” Julian said.

“He's supplied the missing section.”

They returned to the table. With the green pieces excluded, a section of harbor wall began to make sense.

Eve's phone vibrated on the counter.

She glanced at the screen. The hall coordinator had sent a question about whether the summary could include a larger photograph for a noticeboard. It wasn't urgent; the agreed summary wasn't due until Tuesday.

Her hand stayed near the phone for a moment.

Then she set it facedown.

Julian didn't praise her for leaving it. She was unexpectedly grateful. She wanted an afternoon with him, not a demonstration of improved working habits.

“What about films?” she asked. “Do you have a terrible childhood experience with those too?”

“I once watched the second half of a film before the first.”

“How?”

“Two recordings. Wrong labels.”

“Did you notice?”

“I thought it was unusually demanding.”

She laughed hard enough to dislodge a piece from the edge she'd just completed. He caught it before it fell and put it beside her hand.

“What sort was it?”

“A crime film. Everyone seemed to know what had happened except me.”

“That's most crime films.”

“This was more pronounced.”

She told him she liked old comedies, including the ridiculous ones where a person could prevent the entire plot by opening the correct door. She disliked films that spent their final twenty minutes explaining why the audience should feel foolish for believing the first ninety.

“Even if the explanation works?” he asked.

“Then I'm annoyed more respectfully.”

“And frightening ones?”

“I watch through my fingers and pretend I'm checking the composition.”

“Useful professional vocabulary.”

“Years of training.”

He fitted a piece into the harbor wall and sat back. “I don't like the ones where the animal dies.”

“Even if everything else is good?”

“I can appreciate the rest without choosing to watch it again.”

Eve nodded. She didn't turn the answer into a story about his work. It could simply be something he preferred not to see on a Sunday evening.

The instrument outside finally made it through the passage. They both paused, listening. A second attempt faltered at the beginning.

“Oh, no,” Eve whispered.

“Progress is under review,” Julian said.

She rested her forehead against her hand, laughing.

At half past four, the puzzle had a complete lighthouse, most of a harbor, and no reliable boundary on one side. The man with the changing booth remained headless. Julian collected their empty cups while Eve found a flat board that would hold the unfinished sections on top of the high cupboard, out of the cat's reach.

“You don't have to finish it today,” he said.

“I wasn't going to.”

He looked at the loose piece in her hand.

“This one was almost in.”

“Of course.”

She tried it once, found the fit was wrong, and put it back in the box.

They carried the board together. It flexed slightly under the weight, and Eve directed him around the chair with more concern than the half-built lighthouse deserved. Once it was safely stored, they stood looking at the newly empty table.

“That was nice,” she said.

“Yes.”

He didn't add that it had been useful to take a break. She didn't ask whether he had enjoyed himself enough to justify coming.

While he washed his hands, Eve fetched her large canvas carrying bag from beside the cupboard. It still held the empty cloth sleeves from yesterday's event. She shook them out and set the open bag on the chair so she could fold it.

Julian came back and paused in the doorway.

“There's an outdoor film in the neighborhood next Saturday,” he said.

Eve looked up. “At the park?”

“Yes. The old comedy on the noticeboard. Would you like to go with me?”

Her mind offered three practical responses before it admitted what he had asked.

“I could take the camera if they wanted—”

“No camera.” He said it gently, then continued before she could mistake his meaning. “I'm not asking you to cover it. I'd like to watch the film with you. If you'd enjoy it.”

Eve left the cloth sleeve unfolded in her hands.

She had spent the afternoon noticing the sound of his laugh and learning that he disliked an unnecessary tragedy involving an animal. She wanted more of that. She didn't need to rename it, or shrink it into a task she knew how to perform.

“Yes,” she said. “I'd like to go with you.”

“Good.”

“Without the camera.”

“That's the invitation.”

A rustle came from the chair. Dumpling had stepped into the canvas bag and was pressing its base into the shape he required. Eve freed the handles from beside his paws and folded them outward so he couldn't tangle himself.

Julian looked down at the orange-and-white head above the rim.

“He appears to have booked transport.”

“He's booked a seat in my kitchen. The screening can manage without him.”

Dumpling lowered himself farther into the bag.

Eve left him there while she walked Julian to the door. They would confirm the meeting time during the week. There was no work file to send, no equipment to collect, and no excuse she needed to make more convincing.

“Saturday,” she said.

“Saturday.”

After he left, she returned to the kitchen and put the remaining biscuits away. Dumpling's ears were just visible above the bag's edge.

Eve entered the film in her calendar without opening the work grid.
