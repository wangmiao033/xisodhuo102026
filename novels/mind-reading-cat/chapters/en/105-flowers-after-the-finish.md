# Chapter 105 — Flowers After the Finish

At one o'clock, the market did not end so much as change occupations.

Customers became people looking for the bags they had put down. Stallholders became packers. The ugly stuffed dog vanished into a carton before Eve could say goodbye to it, leaving only a backward-pointing ear visible above the tape.

Sophie checked the courtyard against the closing list. Eve collected the last agreed photographs and asked the organizer which supplier receipts were still outstanding. Nobody needed her to announce that the day had been a success while the bin bags remained beside the door.

The supplies-shop owner handed back his stall card.

“Useful morning,” he said.

“Did the tray survive?”

“Better than my back. But that's packing, not your event.”

He had sold several mats and received questions he wanted to turn into clearer product labels. Eve asked whether she could include that in the private event report. He agreed, then corrected the number she had almost written down.

She corrected it without embarrassment. A smaller true number was more useful than a larger one he hadn't supplied.

The organizer stood beside the table stack, counting under her breath. Sophie counted separately, and they compared results before signing the handover. One borrowed chair was missing.

For a moment Eve felt the unpleasant surge of being almost finished and therefore particularly unwilling to encounter anything else.

Then the caretaker pointed through the open room door.

“Someone took it in to sit down.”

The chair stood beside the noticeboard. Eve brought it back. Nobody had stolen community furniture; a person had used a chair as a chair.

Sophie checked the count again.

“There. All yours.”

The organizer signed. Sophie packed her notebook and tape measure, accepted the drink Eve offered, and checked the time.

“I've got to go.”

“Yes. Thank you.”

Eve gave her the payment confirmation for the balance due that day, rather than promising to sort it out once everything was quiet. The amount matched their agreement. The late supplier collection was no longer Sophie's responsibility just because she was good at counting tables.

At the gate, Eve hugged her briefly after asking whether her hands were free.

“Go and do the other part of your day,” she said.

“I intend to.”

Sophie left with her bag. Eve watched her turn the corner, then went back to the room, where the organizer had spread receipts across the folding table with the short leg.

“We should do another one,” the woman said.

“Let's finish this one first.”

“I mean, people asked.”

“Good. Write down what they asked for.”

They did. One stallholder wanted another date immediately. Several residents had asked whether the owners' conversation could happen again without a whole market around it. Someone wanted a longer event; someone else had been pleased by the short hours. The answers didn't combine neatly into a triumphant sentence.

Eve separated inquiries from bookings. No future income existed merely because a pleased person had said they might come back.

The table supplier arrived at two-thirty. The organizer handled the collection count while Eve cleared the final pieces of temporary signage with the caretaker's permission. She left the venue's permanent notices untouched. When the van departed, the courtyard looked unexpectedly large.

A scrap of removable tape clung to her sleeve. She peeled it off and put it in the rubbish instead of flicking it toward the paving.

By three, the known costs sat beside the confirmed income. They had not become rich. The market had made a modest surplus after Eve's planning fee, Sophie's agreed work, rentals, supplies, and the other recorded expenses. The organizer still needed to reconcile the final paperwork before calling the figures closed.

Eve took a photograph of the working totals for the authorized project record, making sure no private payment details were in the frame. She would check the final report from the receipts, not from the pleasantly colored columns in her head.

“We didn't need the arch,” the organizer said.

“No.”

“I thought it would look empty.”

“So did I.”

They stood at the doorway looking at the ordinary courtyard. The open space had permitted people to move, pause, and leave. It had not needed to earn its place in a photograph.

The organizer asked whether Eve would consider planning something in the next season. Eve said she would be happy to discuss a brief once the committee had reviewed the report. She did not offer to preserve the same fee or hold every weekend until they decided.

They agreed on a follow-up call. Then the organizer locked the room.

Eve checked her phone. Julian had messaged once, just after three.

*How far through are you? No hurry. I'm nearby.*

She replied that she was finished and named the corner where she would meet him. Finished meant the site work, not the invoices or the report. It was still a real ending she could leave behind for the afternoon.

He stood outside the small grocery shop, holding a bunch of flowers wrapped in brown paper.

Eve stopped walking.

They were not an enormous arrangement. A few bright roses, without filler greenery, and one stem leaning at an angle that made the whole bunch seem slightly surprised by itself.

“For you,” he said.

“You've brought evidence of an occasion.”

“I thought there was one.”

She took them carefully. Her hands smelled faintly of adhesive and cold tea. The flowers did not appear offended.

“Thank you.”

“I didn't want to turn up while you were trying to finish.”

“I would have had to put them somewhere beside a receipt.”

“I suspected.”

She looked at him over the paper. At another time she might have resented the morning he hadn't spent beside her, quietly counting his absence while pretending to be independent. Today she remembered him asking where to put a chair, then going where Sophie directed him.

“You were useful,” she said.

“A cherished romantic assessment.”

“And now you're very welcome.”

“Better.”

She stepped close and kissed him, briefly, because she wanted to and because he leaned toward her with the same unhurried answer. A shopper passed carrying potatoes and gave them precisely the amount of attention strangers usually deserved.

“Food?” Julian asked.

“Yes. Something that requires sitting.”

They found a small place with a table by the window. Eve set the flowers beside her chair where nobody would step on them. She had eaten her packed sandwich, but a day spent answering questions seemed to have used another meal's worth of energy.

Julian ordered, then asked what had gone wrong.

“The sign. Three minutes before opening.”

She told him about the queue, the locked gate, and the delivery cyclist. He listened without explaining how he would have managed it.

“I delayed opening by five minutes,” she said.

“Did that help?”

“Yes. I hated announcing it.”

“I can imagine.”

“I kept wanting it to have been somebody else's sign.”

His mouth curved.

“A reasonable wish. Not a useful correction.”

“No.”

Their food arrived. For several minutes Eve paid it the attention it deserved. Across the table, Julian removed a slice of something he hadn't expected from his sandwich and examined it before deciding to eat it anyway.

“What?” she asked.

“Pickled. I thought it was fresh.”

“An ordinary betrayal.”

“A manageable one.”

She laughed. The day began to loosen inside her, no longer a sequence of jobs she might have forgotten.

After eating, she asked about his morning. He told her about an unremarkable errand and a book he had returned late. She listened without waiting for a story grand enough to balance her event. His day did not need to have been difficult for his time to belong to him.

They walked back to her building slowly. Eve carried the flowers upright while Julian carried the bag containing her folder, with her permission, because it was heavy and she wanted a free hand for his.

At home, Dumpling came to the door, sniffed the paper, and attempted no meaningful commentary on professional achievement.

Eve kept the roses away from his curious nose. Julian told her he had asked the florist for roses alone, and the little label confirmed the bunch, but she still had no wish to leave stems, thorns, or vase water for Dumpling to investigate. The paper went away too, rather than becoming a crinkling invitation.

Julian helped her find a stable vase. It had spent months holding spare pens. The pens moved to a cup, and the cup immediately looked overqualified for its new job.

They set the flowers in the closed-off room where Dumpling couldn't reach them. Eve could enjoy them when she worked there; she didn't need a centerpiece at the cost of supervising every curious paw.

Then she changed into comfortable clothes and returned to the sofa.

“I'm not opening the report tonight,” she said.

“Was that a statement or a request for enforcement?”

“A statement.”

“Good.”

She rested her head against his shoulder. Dumpling took the other end of the sofa and spread himself over an amount of fabric that suggested an independent lease.

For a while nobody asked anything useful.

On Monday, the final supplier receipt arrived. Eve checked it, reconciled the expenses with the organizer, and confirmed the modest surplus without rounding it into a miracle. Her own earned fee remained separate from the event's balance. She sent the final report with the corrected entrance diagram, attendance limitations, and specific feedback, then filed the approval.

The committee's next-season inquiry remained an inquiry. Two residents had separately asked about another practical exchange. Eve recorded those requests without promising a programme she hadn't priced or planned.

One comment in the organizer's forwarded notes stopped her. A visitor had enjoyed the market but found the owners' exchange difficult to hear from the outermost chair. Eve remembered the easy conversation and realized she had judged it from the middle, where she had sat.

She called the organizer rather than writing a defensive explanation.

“Did anyone else mention that?”

“Two people. One moved closer. The other didn't want to interrupt.”

Eve added the comment to the report. A future version might need fewer participants or a different arrangement, not automatically a microphone and a larger audience. She asked whether the organizer could pass on her thanks for the feedback.

Then she changed the final sentence she had written about the conversation. It had been too pleased with itself. The smaller sentence acknowledged that several people had found it useful and that hearing from the edge needed improvement.

She sent the corrected version before making lunch. The event had still gone well. It simply had not gone identically well from every chair.

Before closing her work for lunch, she looked at the flowers. The leaning stem had not straightened. It seemed happier at its angle.

She took one private photograph for herself, not a promotional announcement about a boyfriend rewarding her success. Then she put the phone down and went to feed a cat whose life had continued comfortably while she learned how to run a morning without him.
