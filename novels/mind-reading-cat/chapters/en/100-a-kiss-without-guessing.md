# Chapter 100 — A Kiss Without Guessing

Eve's scarf had acquired a knot she couldn't remember tying.

She stood in front of the mirror on Saturday afternoon, trying to undo it without pulling her hair into the operation. The more carefully she worked, the smaller and more determined the knot became. Behind her, Dumpling occupied the chair containing her other scarf.

“You're not helping,” she told him.

He opened one eye.

She left the spare beneath his tail and sat on the stool instead. With the knotted scarf resting in her lap, she could finally loosen it. Eve put it on properly and selected a coat that needed no further negotiation.

Her phone lit with Julian's message. His morning arrangement had finished; he was leaving now and expected to be on time.

She answered that she was leaving too. Then she put the phone in her bag, checked the door, and went downstairs before the mirror could suggest another problem.

They met near the small park where they had agreed to begin. Julian stood beside a noticeboard, reading an announcement for a gardening group with the solemn attention of someone considering a second attempt at plant ownership.

“Too soon?” Eve asked.

He looked up. “There are supervised sessions.”

“A safer model.”

“I thought so.”

She took his offered hand and they went through the gate. The afternoon had the pale brightness of a cool day that might still reward people for going outside. A few children were making a game out of stepping only on particular paving stones. Eve had to resist applying their rule to her own route.

“Which ones?” Julian asked.

She looked at him.

“You were watching your feet.”

“The pale ones. Apparently the dark ones are impossible to survive.”

“That complicates this section.”

They both looked ahead. The path changed almost entirely to dark stone beside a bench.

“Structural failure,” Eve said.

“Perhaps we should tell the gardening group.”

She laughed and stepped onto the forbidden paving. Julian followed her into certain imaginary disaster.

They had no ambitious route. Eve liked knowing they could turn left because a path looked pleasant rather than because she had promised herself an afternoon would include six separate proofs of enjoyment. There was a small pond, a low bridge, and a stand selling hot drinks by the wider entrance.

At the stand, Julian ordered a drink without first consulting the menu. Then he discovered it was no longer offered.

“I've been looking forward to that,” he said.

The server suggested two alternatives. Julian chose one, and Eve chose the other because she liked its description. They carried the cups to a bench where the backs of their knees wouldn't block the path.

Julian tasted his and looked disappointed.

“Not the same?” she asked.

“Not remotely.”

“Mine's good. Want to try it?”

They exchanged cups for a sip. His was sweeter than Eve wanted, but not unpleasant. She said so. He liked hers better.

“We can swap if you want,” she offered.

“You chose the good one.”

“Yes, and I also don't mind yours. I'm not offering a tragic sacrifice.”

“All right. Thank you.”

They swapped. Eve drank the sweeter one and found she enjoyed it more now that she had decided to have it. Julian watched a boy trying to sail a leaf through a puddle without touching the water.

“I would have used a stick,” he said.

“That may violate the rules.”

“The rules seem poorly designed.”

“You're having a difficult afternoon with this park.”

“Several systems need attention.”

He sounded so serious that she nearly spilled the drink laughing. He reached toward the cup, then saw she had it and let his hand fall back.

They finished before moving on. The drinks had warmed Eve's fingers, and she kept the sensation by putting one hand in her pocket and the other in his. At the pond, she stopped to look at the reflected branches breaking whenever a bird disturbed the surface.

“I used to come to places like this when I needed ideas,” she said.

“Does that spoil them?”

“Sometimes. I start looking at everything as something I should take away.”

“What are you taking away today?”

“A terrible opinion of the paving. And a drink I didn't order.”

“A productive visit.”

She smiled. On the opposite path, a woman was trying to photograph a friend who kept looking away at the exact moment she pressed the button. Eve understood the photographer's frustration, but she didn't offer to solve it. Soon both women were laughing too hard to continue, and their phone went back into a pocket.

Julian asked whether Eve wanted to walk farther or find something to eat. She was hungry. So was he. They left by the smaller gate and found a place where the menu was short enough to read without making a strategic plan.

The table rocked.

Julian put a hand on one corner; the opposite corner rose in reply.

“It has opinions,” Eve said.

“A familiar theme.”

The server brought a folded support and adjusted the leg. Eve thanked her. Then she and Julian spent an unreasonable amount of time deciding whether they wanted the same dish or different ones they could share.

“Two different,” Eve said finally. “Unless you object.”

“I don't.”

“Excellent. A successful negotiation.”

They ate slowly. Julian told her about a shop assistant who had asked him to describe the exact sort of pen he wanted and then found it immediately, making him feel both grateful and faintly predictable. Eve described the repair-business client's satisfaction at finally having an address visible long enough for a viewer to read.

“That sounds useful,” he said.

“It was. They said somebody had already used the new instructions without calling twice.”

She found herself pleased all over again. The result was small enough to fit inside a conversation, and still hers.

After the meal they returned toward the park, taking the narrower path along its outside edge. The sun had lowered, leaving the building windows bright while the pavement was mostly in shade. Eve tucked her scarf closer.

Julian asked whether she was cold.

“A little. Not enough to go home yet.”

“Neither am I.”

He meant the second part, she thought, and then realized he had said it clearly enough that she didn't have to repair his sentence in her head.

They reached a quiet stretch beside the railings. There was room to step aside without blocking anyone. Eve stopped to fix the loose end of her scarf, which had become caught beneath her bag strap.

Julian waited.

She looked up at him and forgot, briefly, what she had been trying to free.

His face was familiar now in a hundred small ways: the crease beside his mouth when he was trying not to smile, the attention that could seem stern until she discovered what had actually interested him, the little pause before he chose a word he wanted to mean.

She wanted to kiss him.

The wanting was clear enough to make her nervous. She had no clever line prepared. For once, that seemed a useful absence.

“I'd like to kiss you,” she said.

Julian's expression changed, the stillness becoming attention directed entirely at her.

“I'd like that too.”

“Now?”

“Yes.”

She stepped closer. He lifted his hand to the side of her face, stopping lightly enough that she could turn toward it. She did. His thumb rested beside her cheek, warm despite the cool air.

The first kiss was gentle and a little awkward because Eve smiled just before their mouths met.

She drew back a fraction, embarrassed.

“Sorry.”

“Don't apologize for that.”

“I was aiming for composed.”

“I prefer this.”

The words made her smile again. This time she let herself. Julian leaned closer, and she met him willingly, her hand resting against the front of his coat. The second kiss lasted longer. She could feel the small movement of his breath and the way his fingers settled, neither hurried nor holding her in place.

When they parted, Eve remained close. Her heart was beating hard enough to make the silence feel full.

“Was that—” she began, then laughed at herself.

“Yes,” he said. “I liked it.”

“I liked it too.”

A person with a shopping bag came along the path. They stepped farther toward the railings to leave room, suddenly ordinary people making space for someone on the way home. The interruption did nothing to diminish the warmth in Eve's face.

Julian brushed a loose strand of hair away from her scarf.

“Would you like to keep walking?”

“In a minute.”

“All right.”

She took his hand again. For that minute they stood together while the light changed on the windows across the road.

Then Eve kissed him once more, briefly, because she wanted to and because he smiled and came toward her when she did.

By the time they reached her building, the afternoon had become evening. Julian needed to go home. Eve wanted more time, but she also wanted to keep the evening she had rather than stretch it until both were tired and hungry again.

They arranged to speak the next day. At the entrance, their goodbye contained another kiss, less hesitant now, and her delighted inability to find anything sophisticated to say afterward.

“Get home safely,” she managed.

“I will.”

She watched him leave, then went upstairs with her scarf still badly arranged.

Dumpling was on the chair where she had left him, though the spare scarf now lay on the floor. She picked it up, checked his water, and sat beside him long enough for him to lean against her hand. Then she prepared his usual meal.

Her own phone waited in her bag. There were a few ordinary notifications and an email addressed to her business account from a community organizer.

Eve opened it at the desk.

The sender had seen her work on practical pet-owner stories and neighborhood communication. They were preparing a pet-friendly market and wanted to discuss paid independent event planning: a format, participant arrangements, and publicity that would suit the community rather than merely produce a busy photograph. Would she be available to visit and provide a quotation?

She read the message twice, this time because the opportunity contained actual questions she needed to answer.

No date had been agreed. No fee, either. She would need to see the space and ask what they expected before deciding whether the work fit her capacity.

Eve opened her calendar, found two possible meeting times, and wrote a short reply offering them. She asked for the proposed scale and budget range so the first conversation could be useful. Then she sent it under her own name.

Julian's message arrived while she was putting the calendar away. He was home. He had enjoyed the afternoon.

*So did I,* she wrote. *Very much.*

She added that a new work inquiry had arrived and she would tell him about it once she knew more. Then she set down the phone, smiling at a memory that needed no caption.

Tomorrow she would have questions about a market. Tonight she could still feel the warmth of his hand against her cheek.
