# Chapter 006 — Not a Free Sample

When Sophie arrived, Eve was revising the third biography of a dog who had never needed a job.

“He was looking for a home,” Sophie said. “Now he seems to be applying for management.”

Four cards covered the table. On the first, the same white-chested dog was adorable. On the second, brave. On the third, he appeared to have survived a global financial crisis. The fourth had no story at all: a photograph, a name, and a few useful facts.

Eve moved her barely touched coffee to the window. “Different directions. So they can choose.”

“How many did they buy?”

“One.”

“Who are the other three for?”

Steam from the breakfast shop downstairs drifted past the glass. Eve nearly said they were an investment in future collaboration. It sounded like something she would write for a company awards dinner.

“I couldn't sleep.”

Sophie set down a bag of steamed buns without accepting this convenient explanation. She wasn't here to work. She had an event to dismantle that afternoon and had stopped by to return Eve's charging cable and eat breakfast.

Dumpling lifted his head from the low bed at the smell. Sophie folded the bag shut and showed him her empty hands.

“No staff meal for you.”

The cat did not abandon her for this refusal. He actually closed his eyes again.

Eve considered the example unnecessarily pointed.

Last night, Tessa had sent the confirmed brief. One set of information cards, one event poster, one consolidated round of revisions. The clinic would arrange printing separately. The payment date was clear, as was the person responsible for providing the text.

It felt more like an actual job than several novel-length briefs she had received while employed.

The trouble had started afterward.

First she wanted a livelier heading. Then the typeface didn't feel warm enough. Version two was too cute; it might make adoption look frivolous. Version three told stories, which put animals without good stories at a disadvantage. At one in the morning, she had been drawing icons while repeatedly describing the work to herself as a little extra.

“They're finished now,” she said. “Not sending them would be a waste.”

“If you ordered three extra meals, would the client have to eat them?”

“Design isn't a steamed bun.”

“Obviously. You can tell when a bun has gone cold.”

Eve laughed, but not for long. The four filenames reminded her of her final presentation at the company. Her manager had asked everyone to show more commitment. She had produced another complete proposal. They'd used it. The promised time off had never appeared.

Not wanting to revisit that meeting room, she concentrated on peeling the paper from her breakfast.

“I don't want them thinking they hired someone who got fired and still need to teach her how to work.”

Sophie didn't immediately tell her she was brilliant. Nor did she condemn the company. She picked up the fourth card and pointed to the bottom.

“Can anyone read this from a distance?”

They propped it against the bookshelf and backed toward the door. The dog remained appealing. The consultation hours became a faint gray suggestion.

“No,” Eve admitted.

“Fix that first. Not whatever review you imagine happening inside someone else's head.”

Sophie could spare ten minutes. Then she had to leave; a removal truck didn't run on friendship. Eve put the cards up one by one and asked what her friend noticed first.

On the first: the dog.

On the second: a large heart.

On the third: FINALLY, YOU, which sounded like a television rerun.

On the fourth: the dog's name, followed by MEET FIRST. DECIDE LATER.

“That one gives me something to do,” Sophie said. “The others want me to feel something first.”

It wasn't perfect. Eve changed the hierarchy, kept practical care information on the front, and put the consultation details on the back. Important facts would no longer be squeezed into something resembling a disclaimer. Beside the design, she wrote why she'd chosen it: visitors needed a name, useful information, and a next step. They shouldn't have to finish a tragic biography first.

Sophie stood when her ten minutes ended.

“Could you just help me—” Eve began.

Her friend already had her work bag over one shoulder. A tape measure stuck from its pocket. Someone on her phone was asking where the truck was.

“Never mind. Go. I'll tell you how it went. No proofreading.”

“An update is fine. A zip file isn't.”

After the door closed, Eve moved the other three versions into an unused folder. She didn't delete them. She didn't turn them into gifts, either.

She sent Tessa the fourth design and three sentences explaining it. At the end, she had written, If you don't like it, I have other options available at no extra charge.

She read that twice, deleted it, and pressed send. The selected file contained the actual design, not a photograph of her crowded desk or evidence of how late she had worked. They needed the result, not a record of her exhaustion.

Then she washed her cup.

Her phone didn't ring immediately. She washed the cup again before realizing she was waiting by the sink for permission to do a job she had already been hired to do.

Dumpling brushed her ankle and looked up at the running tap. She turned it off, refreshed his water, and finally ate the cold half of her bun.

Tessa replied while Eve was enlarging the poster's lettering.

This version is clear. Consultations need to start half an hour earlier. I'll collect the other comments and send them together.

Eve contemplated thanking an entire newly discovered civilization for the final sentence.

Then another message arrived.

Dr. Clark asks whether you could also do a short video for our event post. Just a simple record of the weekend. Nothing complicated.

Her fingers had already typed, Of course. No problem.

The words sat in the box, as familiar as her old employee badge.

How long would she be filming? Who would appear? Who would approve the footage? How long was the finished video? There were more hours concealed inside simple record than in all three of last night's extra designs.

She deleted her reply and opened the agreed fee. It wasn't much: enough to help with this month's expenses, not enough to make refusing anything feel easy.

What if they decided she was difficult? What if they gave the cards to someone else, too?

The unused folder sat quietly on her screen. Its contents had not become worthless simply because nobody was being pleased by them.

A video would be an additional deliverable, she wrote. Could I confirm the purpose and filming time, then quote for it separately?

She sent the message and turned her phone facedown.

It vibrated against the table a few seconds later.

Yes. Can you come and look at the space this afternoon? Dr. Clark will be here. No need to bring filming equipment. Let's agree on the work first.

Eve looked at the camera batteries she had already charged. Her entire apartment appeared to be testifying against her.

That afternoon, she took only a notebook and her printed design. Dumpling stayed in his familiar room with his food, water, and resting place arranged as usual. This was a work meeting. He didn't need to prove anything for her.

Several folded tables leaned against the wall of the clinic's activity area. Tessa led Eve to a small table, where Dr. Clark looked over the sample.

“Can all the dogs' details go on the back?” he asked.

Eve resisted nodding automatically. She turned the card over and indicated the available space.

“We can include more animals, but not all their information on this card. The text would be too small. Either they each get one, or we add an index directing people to the individual cards.”

Dr. Clark chose the individual cards already planned. Then he asked whether the video could show everyone finding a home.

“If that doesn't happen, I can't edit it so it does.”

The sentence sounded blunter aloud than it had in her head.

“But we can show people how to start asking questions,” she added. “Even if they aren't ready to decide.”

Julian approached with a black pen in his hand. He didn't explain her meaning for her. He moved a packet of brochure holders away from her sample and sat to one side.

They agreed on a short film, with limited time for preparation footage and the event itself. Visitors would not automatically become subjects; the organizer would confirm permission. Eve would plan and edit. The people caring for the animals would decide whether filming suited them. No animal would be required to perform. Comments would arrive in one round, and any new use would need a separate discussion.

When they reached the fee, Eve pressed her tongue briefly against her teeth. She managed not to add, But of course I can charge less if money is tight.

Dr. Clark considered the figure.

“This month's budget won't cover all of that filming time.”

Heat rose in her face. She nearly offered half a day for free.

Instead, she put her pen between two items and crossed out the unnecessary second setup.

“We can shorten the shoot. Keep the introductions and the consultation process, without a separate set of interviews. That would bring it down to this.”

He calculated again, then agreed.

Nobody applauded. Nobody congratulated her for learning to value herself. Tessa began checking which materials would be ready tomorrow. Work moved on, as though Eve had simply put a chair where it belonged rather than jumped off a cliff.

Before she left, Julian handed her his black pen so she could circle the revised consultation time on her own copy.

“For changes like this, I ask Tessa to collect everything?”

“Yes. Otherwise you'll receive one message from each of us, each apparently the most important.”

“Including you?”

“Especially me.”

For once, her laugh didn't feel tight.

On her way home, she sent Sophie a screenshot of the agreed scope, carefully excluding the client's details.

We agreed on a fee for the extra work. They weren't angry.

Her friend answered a little later.

They're hiring a planner. Not adopting one.

Eve laughed aloud outside the convenience store.

She bought dinner, but not the unnecessarily expensive coffee she briefly considered as a reward. Today's money was still an agreement, not a payment. At home, she needed to prepare her filming questions.

This time, though, she knew which parts were her work. She knew where they ended.

When she opened the door, Dumpling raised his head from his low bed. He gave a small stretch before coming toward her at a leisurely pace.

“You weren't there today,” she told him. “And I still got a job.”

The cat sniffed her dinner bag.

Apparently, he represented a different department.
