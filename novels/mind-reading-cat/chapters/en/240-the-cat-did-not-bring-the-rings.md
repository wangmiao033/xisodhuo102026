# Chapter 240 — The Cat Did Not Bring the Rings

By the time Eve was ready to leave the flat, Dumpling had tucked himself into the wash of Saturday morning light beneath the window. She had been looking for him to say goodbye. She found instead that she had said it twice to a lump in his blanket, with no apparent loss to either of them.

Julian stood in the hall looking down at his coat.

“What have you forgotten?”

“Nothing. I'm trying to remember whether I ever wore this without a pen.”

He took one out of the inside pocket and laid it on the table. He patted the other pocket for the ring box. She heard the little click of his fingernail against it.

Tessa was coming by in the afternoon as agreed. She knew where the food and the scoop were, and that Dumpling might stay behind the chair the whole time. The necessary things were in the usual places. Her key was on its own ring so she wouldn't have to wrestle with their front lock. Eve had begun a note explaining the key to the front lock, then crossed it out, having watched Tessa use it without comment a week earlier.

She found a reasonably upright part of the blanket and said, “We'll be back after dinner.”

Dumpling twitched the ear that was in the light.

Downstairs she realized she was still holding a felt mouse. She had picked it up from the edge of her shoe and carried it out of the room. Going back for the sake of one small object felt absurd. She dropped it into the side of her bag with the fastening that actually worked and followed Julian into the street.

The room they had booked for the ceremony was reached by a short ramp. Her mother was at the top of it, crouching with some difficulty to pick a leaf off the mat. She straightened when she saw them. Nothing had ever required her to pretend so earnestly that she was just passing through.

“That's a good coat,” she told Julian. “Eve, is this where you want me?”

Eve could hardly see the coat. She could see her mother's pin, which she had worn years ago in a favorite photograph with all the faces half out of frame. She went up the ramp and hugged her. Her mother held her a little tighter when she started to let go.

“You're exactly where I want you.”

Inside, one of Julian's relatives was trying to find the correct side of the room from a set of directions nobody had given. A friend moved a chair so that two people who wanted to sit together could do so. Beth, their photographer, checked with both of them whether there were still people who'd prefer not to appear in the pictures. Eve spoke to the one person who hadn't answered rather than asking for an answer on their behalf. Beth moved her position a short way, out of the line between the first and second rows.

The registrar collected the papers and drew Eve aside to confirm where she would wait and when she could start. The formal words would be prompted. There was a plain clock on the wall, and no music to pitch her steps against. It was a room with a job, and when the door opened there would be people she liked in it. Her legs did not appear to find this argument persuasive.

Sophie came down the little passage as Julian left to take his place. She was carrying the ring box in one hand with her thumb curved firmly over the join.

“He has already told me where it opens,” she said. “Should I have taken notes?”

“No. It closes again in the same place.”

“You see why I've refused to be responsible for a schedule.”

She was smiling. Eve could see the effort it was costing her to stand there without asking four practical questions. That particular effort was more touching than a surprise she might have organized.

“I don't know what I'm meant to do with my hands,” Eve said.

“Whatever you like. Most people will be looking at your face.” Sophie considered her. “You could give me the felt mouse.”

Eve looked down. She had opened the wrong pocket in search of her folded page, and two ears and a red tail were visible between her fingers.

“I stole that from Dumpling.”

“He seems unlikely to file a report in time.”

Eve put the mouse back, found her page, and closed the bag. When Sophie left to sit near the front, Eve handed the bag to Beth, who had agreed to put it beside the others. Then nobody stood beside her. From behind the partly open door came a sound she recognized from work, the shuffling of people completing a small arrangement before they became quiet. She heard her mother's voice telling somebody they were perfectly comfortable where they were. The registrar gave Eve the signal they had agreed on.

She stepped into the room.

There was no reason not to look at people on the way. She saw a sleeve she recognized, the pale pin, somebody with a loose shoelace. If it had been somebody else's wedding Eve might have seen these things in photographs later. She was glad to see them now. But there was a point about halfway down when Julian came fully into view, and she stopped collecting details for later.

He kept his eyes on her. Nothing in the way he stood suggested he was waiting to take her from anyone else. Eve walked the rest of the way to him and put out her hand.

His hand was cold.

“Good,” she whispered. “I thought it was just me.”

He let out the little breath she'd seen him holding. The registrar, who had managed to hear this, gave them both a moment.

They said the words they needed to say. Eve was startled when her voice came out at much the same volume she used to ask for a loaf of bread. Julian spoke clearly until he reached the words he'd written himself. Then he stopped for a few seconds in the middle of a sentence. Eve knew she could have supplied the next word. She left it with him.

“I'll come back to the conversation,” he said when he was ready.

Eve looked down at her folded sheet. She had told him most of what she needed to tell him at home. From her side of the table it had been possible to look at him, get the wrong word out, and try again. Here she looked up and found that it was possible, too. She spoke of the noodle shop, and the pleasure of asking him to dinner and finding him walking toward her. She said she would take care to ask when the answer mattered and try to put right her own mistakes. She had practiced a sentence about making room for the work he wanted to do. What actually came out was: “I want to hear about the things that matter to you, even when they aren't about me.” Julian smiled before she had finished saying it.

Sophie brought the rings over when the registrar asked. The lid stuck for a second. Sophie pressed the hinge with her thumb and got it open without involving anybody's teeth. They put the rings on each other, signed where indicated, and stood when the registrar invited them to stand. A kiss, Eve discovered, was possible even with the radiator clicking furiously behind her. It was a very good kiss. She was still laughing against Julian's cheek when she heard Sophie's voice from the front row.

“I have finished my one job.”

“You may now disorder the rest of the day as you please,” Eve told her.

At the restaurant, a member of staff showed her mother where to leave coats. Everybody who wanted a seat had one. They had not bought enough speeches to cool the food, so someone managed to make Julian laugh while he still had a mouthful of bread. Eve saw her own dinner arrive and ate it at the temperature the cook had intended. Halfway through she put her fork down to listen to the end of a story from someone she hadn't seen in months. She had time to ask how it finished.

One of Julian's relatives asked, gently, whether the cat had been entrusted with the rings. Eve explained how many people would have had to wait in silence for him to come out from behind an unfamiliar curtain before that could have happened. By the time she had finished, they were talking about cats who knew all about wearing a collar until the collar came within a foot of them.

Tessa's message arrived while they were saying goodnight. Food and water had been checked, the visit was over, and the shaded part of the sitting room belonged to Dumpling again. Eve wrote back to thank her. No one tried to persuade Tessa to return with stories about what he did while they were gone.

When they arrived home, Dumpling came to the door at the sound of the lock. His greeting consisted of inspecting the hem of Eve's coat and finding it unremarkable. She sat down on the floor to take off her shoes. Julian sat beside her a little awkwardly because the coat he still had on didn't bend in the ways he was expecting. Dumpling took the narrow gap behind his back. With his front feet on the loose fold of Julian's coat, he looked as though he had finally managed to find a household object exactly as expensive as it needed to be.

Eve asked Julian whether he wanted one more picture. He held up his phone on the arm furthest from the cat, and Eve fitted into the small space between his shoulder and the edge of the frame. In the image they were slightly blurry in the lamplight, and Dumpling was looking away. They kept it in their private album. Then Julian got up to put the kettle on. The cat pulled his feet off the coat and went to see where Eve's shoes had ended up.

Putting the felt mouse back near the chair, Eve set the bag of dry cat food on the kitchen counter so she could refill its storage tub. A folded section of the bag snapped outward under her hand. A stream of little brown pieces ran over the bench, down a cupboard door, and onto the floor. Julian put the lid on the half-filled tub and slid it safely out of the way. Eve shut the kitchen door for a minute, leaving Dumpling on the other side with free run of the rest of the flat.

A single piece rolled out from beneath the cupboard long after she thought it had all stopped.

“Oh, that's ambitious,” Julian said.

He got the dustpan. Eve fetched a damp cloth and went round the skirting board with the side of her hand, taking care not to push anything underneath the cabinet. He dropped his left hand flat on the floor and blocked the last escaping piece before she could reach it.

“Good,” she said. “That wasn't your only job after all.”

He bent to kiss her on the forehead, shifted to avoid the dustpan between them, and sat down hard. She laughed so much she had to put the cloth aside. Dumpling, outside, washed his face where she could see him through the glass. She opened the door when the floor had dried. He walked past both of them and found his own bowl in the place where he had left it.
