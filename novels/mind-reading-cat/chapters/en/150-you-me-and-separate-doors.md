# Chapter 150 — You, Me, and Separate Doors

The first name Eve wrote down sounded like an insurance product.

The second sounded like a place that would charge extra for oat milk.

By the fourth, she had begun to suspect that she should name the project after the materials box and be done with it.

Dumpling lay beneath the desk with one white paw over his nose. He had slept through Community Companion Studio and showed no greater enthusiasm for Everyday Tails.

“Fine,” Eve said. “You have never had to fit a business into a booking-page heading.”

She crossed out another line.

The evaluation arrangement did not need a polished identity to function. The landlord knew who was paying, the insurance description used actual activities, and the first access time was confirmed. But Eve needed a name for the small service she was testing, one that could survive whether it lived in this unit, a borrowed room, or a downloadable page.

She did not want to call it the shop before it was one.

She also wanted, inconveniently and honestly, to like it.

Sophie arrived after lunch for their planned hour reviewing the trial description. She brought her own laptop and a pastry, which she divided before opening either document.

“Names?” she asked, looking at the page.

“An outbreak.”

Sophie read the list. “This one sounds as though I need a referral.”

“I have already killed it.”

“Not thoroughly. I can still read it.”

Eve drew a darker line through the words and handed over the description instead.

The practical part was easier. They checked what visitors would buy: a small facilitated session to make ordinary pet-household information easier to use, or to exchange relevant experiences with other owners. No one would receive individual veterinary advice. Nobody would be buying animal handling or guaranteed time with Dumpling.

Sophie stopped at a sentence promising a friendly place for pets and their people.

“Which pets?”

“Only when the activity allows it and we've confirmed arrangements.”

“Then say that before somebody brings a rabbit to a crowded discussion because friendly sounded universal.”

Eve changed the sentence. They made the distinction between people attending alone and approved sessions where an owner could bring an animal visible without forcing anyone to search a long page.

Then they reached the photograph Eve had selected for the project introduction.

Dumpling occupied one side of the frame, asleep on his blanket while her materials box stood nearby. It was an ordinary image from home, not a performance. Eve liked the warmth of it after so much text about dates and formats.

“That's very much his face,” Sophie said.

“People know him from the account.”

“They do.”

Eve added a caption saying that photographs illustrated her existing work and did not promise an animal would attend a session. It seemed clear enough to her. She left the image in the draft, then moved on to the booking questions.

By the time their paid hour ended, the description was shorter, the activity was clearer, and none of the names had improved.

Sophie shut her laptop.

“You can ask people what they hear in a name. You can't ask them to make you feel successful when they say it.”

“I was going to offer pastry.”

“Already consumed. Terrible negotiating position.”

They spent the next ten minutes talking about Sophie's new rota. The Thursday event had survived the rearrangement of chairs, although one guest had tried to take a seat from a row that was still occupied. Sophie demonstrated the man's apologetic expression so accurately that Eve nearly dropped her plate.

After Sophie left, Eve returned to the page with a lighter mind.

The project was about the untidy space between knowing that one loved an animal and making daily life work. It involved people speaking to other people. Sometimes the useful thing was a place to sit; sometimes it was a clear instruction or another person asking where the supplies actually were.

She wrote You, Me & Paws.

It was a little sentimental. She read it again and found that she did not mind.

Then she wrote it without the ampersand and preferred the shorter version. The mark looked like two things meeting without becoming one long word.

She almost explained that to the cat, reconsidered, and made tea instead.

Julian met her at the unit later for an arranged look at the draft activity description and the space it referred to. The landlord opened up for the booked visit and reminded Eve when she needed the room cleared. No ribbon appeared. Eve had brought a notebook, a tape measure, and two paper cups of tea from the cafe.

“I have a name,” she said, handing Julian a cup.

“For the tape measure?”

“For the project. Although he has earned several.”

She showed him the page.

“You, Me & Paws,” he read.

“Too much?”

He looked toward the empty room, then back at the words.

“It sounds like people are included.”

“They are supposed to be.”

“I like it.”

Eve felt the disproportionate pleasure of hearing him say a name she had made. She let herself have it before asking the next question.

“Does it sound as if we're running a clinic?”

“No. The rest of the description will matter.”

They went through that part together. She had a list of examples that fitted the work: organizing information, making household instructions usable, sharing experiences without turning them into universal advice. Julian suggested a clearer distinction between recording a vet's existing instructions and deciding what those instructions ought to be.

Eve changed the line.

“If somebody asks about a symptom during a session, the answer needs to be simple enough for whoever is hosting,” she said.

“Yes. Direct them to appropriate veterinary care. Don't turn it into a group discussion while everyone offers a story.”

“Even if you're sitting there.”

“Especially if my presence has made the description confusing.”

He did not offer to be permanently available at the end of a phone. Eve did not add a quiet promise of access beneath the printed one.

She showed him the place on the draft page where she had almost written in partnership with the clinic. The words had been removed before she printed it.

“I couldn't justify them,” she said. “This isn't their service.”

“No.”

“Would you ever want to do a booked educational session?”

“Possibly, if the subject, time, and arrangements work. We'd discuss that specific job. It doesn't need to be part of starting this.”

She nodded and wrote possible future collaboration in her notebook without putting a date beside it.

The room made every pencil movement echo. Somewhere beyond the wall a door closed, followed by ordinary footsteps. Eve paused, becoming aware of people living and working around the attractive rectangle she had spent so much time measuring. The building had a life before her ideas arrived.

She looked toward the front.

“The sign would go there eventually,” she said.

“If you take the place.”

“If I take the place.”

She held the paper up to the window, well inside the glass, to imagine the size. The letters were too small even from where Julian stood.

“Very discreet,” he said.

“Exclusive. Only customers with exceptional eyesight.”

She lowered the page. There would be no sign installed under the evaluation arrangement. For now the name could fit a folder and a temporary activity description. It did not need to claim the building.

They walked to the rear room. Eve imagined a quiet corner, then caught herself placing furniture before she had tested routes. Julian remained near the doorway rather than filling in the floor plan for her.

“What?” she asked.

“Nothing. I'm waiting to see where you put the tea.”

She had set it on the floor beside her bag. Moving the bag would certainly have knocked it over.

Eve rescued the cup.

“A strong first operational result.”

They finished the drinks and took their rubbish out. When the landlord returned, Eve confirmed the next access request rather than asking to keep a key. There were layout trials to arrange, details to check, and no reason to pretend the room belonged to her merely because she had stood in it with her boyfriend.

Outside, Julian asked whether she wanted dinner.

“Yes. Somewhere with chairs already chosen by other people.”

They went to a small restaurant where the table rocked until Eve folded a paper sleeve beneath one leg. Julian watched her do it.

“Off duty?” he asked.

“I refuse to spill soup recreationally.”

Over dinner, she told him which names Sophie had rejected. He contributed one so solemnly dreadful that she needed a moment to realize he was joking.

“You cannot use your serious face for that.”

“It's the one I brought.”

When the food arrived, they talked about his week. The training application remained pending. He had received confirmation that the materials were complete, nothing more. Eve asked what work he had been doing while waiting, and he told her about a communication problem the clinic team had finally made less cumbersome.

She heard Tessa's part in it and asked about that too. His life did not become an interval between visits to her possible venue.

Afterward they walked toward the point where he would go home and she would take the bus. Eve reached for his hand.

“I'm glad you liked the name.”

“I did.”

“I wanted you to help choose that part.”

“I can help choose a name without signing for the room.”

“I know.”

This time the words felt like something she knew, not something she was promising to learn quickly.

At home, she found Dumpling stretched across the blanket, one hind foot protruding at a ridiculous angle. He blinked at her, accepted a brief scratch beside his cheek, and declined further participation.

Eve changed the project heading to You, Me & Paws. Under it she put the plain description of the actual service. She saved the small introductory draft, including the photograph she still thought the caption explained, for the approved trial announcements. There was no public opening date to invent.

She sent Sophie the final name and the next confirmed planning time. Then she wrote to the landlord with the layout-visit request, attaching only the information needed for access.

On a fresh sheet, she drew the outline of the room.

A long sofa would look wonderful along one wall. She penciled it in lightly, then added a table, the rear doorway, and the entrance. There was plenty of space on paper. That was what she would need to test.

Before bed, she set the drawing aside and washed her cup. Julian was at his own home; the unit was locked by the person who owned it; Dumpling was asleep where he had chosen to sleep.

Eve turned out the desk lamp. The name could wait until morning without anybody having to stay awake and prove it was real.
