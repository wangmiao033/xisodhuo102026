# Chapter 217 — It Is All Right to Miss Something

The woman liked the table by the window until Eve offered it to her.

Then she looked toward the door.

“We can sit somewhere else,” Eve said.

“No, this is fine.”

But she didn't put down her bag.

Tuesday's consultation had been booked as a short, free discussion about a possible private content session. The description was deliberately modest: twenty minutes to see whether Eve's service matched what someone needed. No obligation to buy. Eve had written those words herself, and now she found herself wanting the visitor to behave as though they meant something slightly different.

The month's figures had been on her screen before the door opened. The quieter schedule was helping people go home. It had not yet filled the gap left by the old program.

A private booking would help.

Eve closed the laptop and moved her notebook onto the window table.

The visitor sat at last, keeping the bag against her knees. She had given her name as Ruth when booking. Her coat was still buttoned, although the room was comfortably warm.

“Would you like some water?”

“No, thank you.”

“All right. You mentioned photographs in your message.”

“Yes.”

Eve waited.

Ruth looked at the sample wall, where a very cross drawing of a calendar faced a much calmer diagram of a bag.

“Do people have to show you their own things?” she asked.

“Only what they want help with. We can use practice objects first.”

“And does anything get posted?”

“No. Private work stays private unless we separately agree otherwise. A booking isn't permission to use your pictures.”

Ruth nodded. Her grip on the bag loosened, but she did not take anything out.

Eve began to describe the session. They could work on framing and simple instructions, using the visitor's own phone if she preferred. She could explain how to make an object recognizable without making the picture elaborate. She couldn't promise to repair every old image or teach an entire editing program in an hour.

Ruth listened with the expression of someone trying to locate a particular word in a long list.

Halfway through, Eve stopped.

“I'm giving you a lot of possibilities. What would you like to be easier afterwards?”

Ruth looked at her.

For one foolish second Eve wanted Dumpling to move. A glance toward a bag, a paw on a certain picture, some tiny interruption might have supplied a direction without making her ask again.

The cat wasn't even at the venue. He was at home, following his usual quiet day, and had never signed up to resolve her difficult sales conversations.

Eve kept her hands still on the notebook.

“I'm not sure,” Ruth said.

“That's all right.”

“Is it?”

“Yes. We can work out whether I can help. Or you can decide you don't want this.”

Ruth gave a short smile, then looked down again.

A delivery van stopped outside. Its driver carried a parcel past the window, checking the numbers on the doors. Ruth watched him until he disappeared.

Eve could invent five explanations for that glance. She had once considered that inventiveness a professional strength. Today none of the explanations belonged to her unless Ruth chose to share one.

“Would you prefer not to talk about the reason?” Eve asked.

“I would, actually.”

The answer came quickly.

Eve nodded.

“Then we won't.”

She turned the notebook slightly so Ruth could see its blank page. She had written only the date and the appointment time, not a speculative story about the person sitting opposite her.

“We could discuss what the work involves without the reason. But we don't have to keep going.”

Ruth considered this.

“Could I see the sort of thing you mean?”

Eve fetched two ordinary sample photographs. One showed a storage box from above, its depth concealed. The other showed the same box beside a familiar mug, with the lid open. They contained no customer's property or personal information.

Ruth studied them.

“The second one tells you more.”

“Yes. Even though it isn't especially pretty.”

“Would you come to somebody's home?”

“Sometimes, for a separately agreed assignment. I'd need to know the kind of work and what access was involved before quoting. I don't need private details that don't affect the job.”

“What if you got there and it wasn't what you expected?”

“We'd stop and discuss it. I wouldn't quietly turn a small booking into a bigger bill.”

Ruth put one photograph over the other, aligning the corners carefully.

Eve heard herself approaching another explanation and resisted. More words would not necessarily make this woman feel safer. They might only make her feel she owed Eve an answer for the effort.

The wall clock clicked.

“I don't think I want to book,” Ruth said.

Eve's disappointment arrived so plainly that she was grateful the notebook hid her hands.

“All right.”

“Not today.”

“You don't have to decide another day either.”

Ruth looked up, and this time her smile lasted.

“Thank you.”

They had used seventeen minutes. Eve did not fill the remaining three with a smaller package, a discount, or a warning that the calendar might become busy. She offered the one-page service description only after asking whether Ruth wanted it.

“Yes, please. On paper.”

Eve printed a copy and checked that the ordinary contact information was clear. She did not add Ruth to a mailing list or write herself a reminder to pursue the sale. The visitor had asked for paper, not continued attention.

At the door, Ruth finally unbuttoned her coat.

“I'm sorry I wasn't more useful.”

The apology startled Eve more than the refusal had.

“You came to see whether this was useful to you,” she said. “You haven't failed an interview.”

Ruth laughed softly.

“Habit.”

“Yes,” Eve said. “I know that one.”

When the door closed, she stood beside it for a moment. No revelation had followed. Ruth hadn't turned back to explain her hesitation. There was no late booking to reward Eve for respecting the refusal.

She returned to the table and entered the appointment as completed, no booking made.

Under outcome, she nearly wrote uncertain.

Then she deleted it. Ruth had been clear enough about the decision Eve actually needed.

Robin arrived for their scheduled preparation block while Eve was putting the sample photographs away.

“How did it go?”

“No booking.”

“Oh.”

“She didn't want to explain much.”

Robin set down their bag. “Did she need to?”

“No.”

Eve heard the answer settle into the room. She had still been arranging a defense for a sale nobody had made.

They got on with work.

A corner of the sample wall had begun lifting away from its backing. Eve pressed it flat and watched it spring up again. The adhesive she'd chosen was too weak for repeated handling. She could fix that problem without learning a stranger's private history.

They took the board down before opening, laid it across the table, and separated the reusable pieces from the ones that needed replacing. Robin found a simpler fastener in the supplies and checked that it left no sharp edge exposed. Eve trimmed the curled paper and reattached it beneath the clear cover.

The potato-dog photograph stared up at them through the operation.

“He looks judgmental from this angle,” she said.

“He's seen your first attempt.”

“That was a prototype.”

“Very diplomatic word for falling off.”

Eve laughed, and the tight feeling beneath her ribs eased.

A man came in during open hours asking whether the smaller exchange could help him make a holiday packing card. Eve described the session and its price. He said he would have to check his schedule. She showed him where the dates were listed and returned to the board without treating his interest as money in the account.

Later, another visitor bought a place for the following week. Eve recorded that payment normally too. It didn't convert Ruth's appointment into a delayed success or prove that refusing to push always paid off. Two people had made different decisions.

During a quiet interval, she reviewed the consultation description on the website. The words no obligation to book appeared at the bottom, below a bright photograph. She moved them nearer the time limit, where somebody deciding whether to come could see them before scrolling.

She didn't change the twenty minutes into half an hour. Generosity was not the same as making her own time uncountable.

Before closing, Robin asked whether they should contact Ruth to check if the leaflet had answered her questions.

“No. She has the details. She didn't ask us to follow up.”

“All right.”

The decision took one sentence. Eve wished more of her internal conversations could learn from it.

At home, Dumpling greeted her by inspecting the bottom of her bag. She had brought nothing edible, and he moved away without disguising his disappointment.

“Fair,” she told him.

Julian looked up from the kitchen counter. “Bad review?”

“Accurate review.”

He was measuring rice. Eve washed her hands and took out vegetables, then told him about the consultation while they prepared dinner. She kept Ruth's name and possible reasons out of it, because she didn't know the reasons and Julian didn't need the name.

“I kept wanting to understand,” she said.

“Did you need to understand everything to answer her?”

“No. I needed to explain what I offered and let her choose.”

“Sounds as though you did.”

“It didn't feel particularly accomplished.”

Julian checked the pan. “Most of the necessary things don't.”

She leaned against the counter, watching him put the lid on.

“For a moment I wished he was there.”

Dumpling had settled in the doorway, clear of their feet but ideally placed to notice any developments involving food.

“To interrupt?” Julian asked.

“To point me toward something. Which is absurd. He might have pointed me toward a moth.”

“A strong possibility.”

“And even if I'd guessed right, it wouldn't have made the information mine.”

Julian nodded. He didn't congratulate her for learning a lesson she had understood long before today. Knowing something and remembering to live by it were not always the same task.

Eve turned back to the vegetables. One carrot had rolled beneath a folded cloth. She found it by lifting the cloth, then cut it into pieces that were uneven but perfectly capable of becoming dinner.

After they ate, she took her leaflet from the bag and read it once more. It described real work, a real price, and an honest opportunity to say no.

Nothing in it promised she would understand a person before they were ready to speak.

She put the leaflet away.

On the sofa, Dumpling shifted to make more room for his own body, not for hers. Eve moved a cushion and sat beside him. Across the room Julian chose a book, asked whether she wanted the lamp left on, and waited for her answer.

“Yes, please,” she said.

It was a small question. He hadn't needed to guess.
