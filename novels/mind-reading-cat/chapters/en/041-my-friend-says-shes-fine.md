# Chapter 041 — My Friend Says She's Fine

Sophie brought two bags of groceries and no explanation for either of them.

One contained bread, tomatoes, and a packet of cheese. The other contained bread, tomatoes, and a different packet of cheese.

“I remembered buying lunch,” she said, putting them on Eve's counter. “Apparently I didn't believe myself.”

“You've provided for a remarkably consistent party.”

“Everyone has to like tomatoes.”

She smiled, unwound her scarf, and stood holding it after there was no practical reason to keep standing. Eve took the groceries rather than the scarf. Yesterday's message had asked whether she could come over. It hadn't said she needed rescuing, entertaining, or an assessment of her ability to shop.

The afternoon light reached across the kitchen floor. Eve had finished her captioning assignment before lunch and cleared the table, leaving the work folder on a shelf where its colored tabs couldn't keep reminding her of things. There was nothing arranged for Sophie's visit except two plates.

Sophie eventually hung up the scarf. A little rectangle of silver tape clung to her sleeve.

“Your clothing is still affiliated with event production,” Eve said.

Sophie peeled it off. Beneath it, the fabric was darker and slightly flattened.

“That's been there since yesterday.”

“Good tape.”

“Excellent tape. Terrible employer.”

Eve looked up.

“The tape,” Sophie said. “I'm reviewing the tape.”

They made toasted sandwiches with the smaller packet of cheese. Sophie cut the tomatoes into slices so thin that Eve could see the chopping board through them, then stopped halfway through one and stared toward the window. A bus groaned past below. Somewhere in the building, somebody was moving furniture an inch at a time.

Eve took the knife when Sophie set it down and finished the tomato without commenting.

They had known each other long enough for quiet to take several forms. There was companionable quiet, offended quiet, the quiet of Sophie calculating whether a piece of equipment would fit through a doorway. This one kept starting as a pause and failing to become a sentence.

“How was this morning?” Eve asked.

“Fine. We got everything in.”

The same answer as the noodle shop, in different clothes. They had got it done then too.

Dumpling emerged from under the sideboard. He sniffed Sophie's shoes, passed the table, and doubled back to press his cheek against her ankle.

“Hello, management,” she said.

He followed when she moved to the sink. When she returned to her chair, he settled against the heel of her shoe, his white paws tucked under the loose edge of her trouser leg.

Sophie glanced down. “I've been adopted by a floor cushion.”

“He doesn't usually stay there.”

“Maybe I smell expensive.”

“You smell like tomatoes.”

“Imported, potentially.”

Eve sat opposite her with lunch. Sophie ate the first half of her sandwich quickly, then seemed to remember there was no need to. She placed the other half on the plate and brushed a few crumbs together with one finger.

Her phone lit up. She looked at it, locked it, and turned it over. A moment later her foot moved as if she were about to stand, but Dumpling lifted his head and Sophie remained in the chair.

A small, dangerous certainty arrived in Eve.

He knew.

She had no words from him, no picture she could inspect. Only the cat pressed against a friend who kept smiling as if the smile were a door she had been told to hold open. Yet Eve felt the pieces connect with the satisfying click of a well-cut sequence. Sophie wanted to leave and dreaded leaving. Dumpling had caught something, and now Eve could help.

“You don't have to pretend with me,” she said.

Sophie stopped gathering crumbs.

“I'm eating a sandwich.”

“I mean about work. You're obviously—”

“Please don't finish that sentence for me.”

The words weren't loud. They landed more cleanly because they weren't.

Eve looked at Dumpling. He had lowered his head again.

“He's been following you since you got here,” she said, and heard how foolish the evidence sounded only after offering it.

“So have you, a little.”

Heat rose along Eve's neck. She had invited Sophie into a quiet room and immediately filled it with an interpretation of her face.

“I haven't worked out how to say it yet,” Sophie added.

Eve kept her hands on the edge of her plate. There were several answers she could make, all of them longer than useful.

“All right,” she said. “I won't make you do it now.”

Sophie nodded once and picked up the remaining sandwich.

Eve wanted to apologize again. She wanted to explain that concern had been responsible, that she hadn't intended to corner anyone, that she really did notice when something was wrong. The explanation would require Sophie to put down her lunch and reassure her.

Instead she asked whether the tomatoes needed more salt.

“They need a different month,” Sophie said.

Eve passed the salt anyway.

After lunch they carried their plates to the sink. Dumpling followed Sophie for three steps, then abandoned her in favor of a rectangle of sunlight. There was no momentous change in the room that Eve could identify. He simply wanted another place to sit.

She rinsed the crumbs from a plate, watching the water carry them toward the strainer. Perhaps there had been something unusually strong about Sophie's worry. Perhaps he liked the rough fabric of her trousers. Both possibilities fitted the afternoon well enough to trouble Eve.

Sophie leaned against the counter and inspected the handle of her work bag. A seam had opened where the padded grip met the webbing.

“That'll come off in the middle of a crossing,” she said.

“Do you want my sewing tin?”

“Do you own a sewing tin?”

“A tin. We can investigate its professional qualifications.”

It contained three needles, several tangled lengths of thread, a button with no known garment, and a tiny screwdriver Eve had been looking for since moving in.

“Your sewing tin has diversified,” Sophie said.

They sat by the window. Sophie chose a needle and pulled the separated edges together while Eve found the strongest thread. It wasn't an emergency repair; the strap itself was sound. Sophie could have wrapped the grip in tape and gone on carrying it. Instead she spent several minutes examining how the old stitches had run.

Eve remembered her doing the same thing years ago with a borrowed coat, working out which side of the lining would hide a repair. Back then Sophie had still talked about the pleasure of making something hold together. Lately Eve mostly heard about the things she had prevented from falling apart.

She didn't say so.

Outside, a child was attempting to ride a scooter in a straight line, with ambitious results. Each successful stretch ended at the same drain cover. Sophie watched the third attempt and winced in sympathetic anticipation before the wheel even struck it.

“Take the other side,” she murmured.

The child went over the drain again.

“Strong commitment to the original concept,” Eve said.

Sophie laughed. It loosened something in her shoulders, though Eve didn't want to make too much of that either.

The needle resisted the thick webbing. Sophie tried a different angle, then asked Eve to hold the handle flat against the table while she pushed. They worked in short, awkward movements, abandoning the idea of invisible stitches almost immediately.

“This will look terrible,” Eve said.

“It'll be under my hand.”

“A private aesthetic failure.”

“My favorite kind.”

They finished with a line of uneven black stitches. Sophie tugged the padding, examined the join, and put the needle back in the tin. The handle held. The repair hadn't solved anything beyond the handle, which seemed a respectable amount for an afternoon's sewing.

When Eve went to make tea, her phone buzzed on the shelf. A client had sent a revision question. She checked the first line, saw that it concerned next week's delivery, and put the phone down without answering.

Sophie was now sitting on the rug with her back against the sofa. Dumpling had approached her again, this time investigating the bag she had placed beside her. He pushed his nose against its outer pocket.

“There's nothing in there for you.”

Sophie unzipped it and brought out a folded pair of clean work gloves, a pen, and a sealed packet of crackers.

“Oh. My lunch from yesterday.”

Eve held the cups carefully as she came back.

Dumpling sniffed the place where the crackers had been, then rubbed his face along the corner of the bag.

It was a wonderfully ordinary explanation for at least part of his interest. It explained neither the moment he had settled against Sophie's foot nor all the other strange moments Eve could remember. Still, she couldn't discard it because it made her earlier certainty look bad.

She placed the tea on the low table and moved the packet beyond his reach.

“Very perceptive,” she told him quietly.

Sophie looked up. “Me or him?”

“Undetermined.”

They spent the next hour watching a mystery film Eve had found on a free channel. The wealthy victim had three cousins, an implausible gardener, and a solicitor whose dramatic spectacles practically announced a second identity. Sophie chose the gardener. Eve chose the solicitor's spectacles as the independent criminal.

Neither asked for the film to be paused when a message arrived.

At the first advertisement, Sophie said, “I thought coming here might help me stop making lists for a bit.”

“Has it?”

“I've only made one.”

“That's nearly a holiday.”

“It was a list of the murder suspects. Before you get excited.”

Eve stretched her legs and let the advertisement sell them a household gadget neither of them wanted. She had been glad when Sophie asked to come over. Somehow, between that gladness and opening the door, she had decided the visit ought to produce a revelation. It could simply have produced a sandwich and a mended bag.

When Sophie finally stood to leave, the sky beyond the window had turned pale and flat. Dumpling stayed on the rug, washing a hind paw with absorbing concentration.

Sophie tested the handle once more.

“Much better.”

“Bring it back if the stitches go. I have an extensive screwdriver-based warranty.”

She smiled and reached for her scarf. Eve held the door while she put on her shoes, resisting the question that rose as Sophie checked her phone.

“I've got a free morning in a couple of days,” Sophie said. “A real one, unless something changes.”

“Would you like to come for breakfast?”

“Yes. Nothing with an agenda.”

“Toast has very limited ambitions.”

At the threshold Sophie touched Eve's sleeve, a brief press rather than an embrace.

“Thank you for stopping.”

Eve nodded. “See you for toast.”

After she left, Eve folded the spare grocery bag and put the second loaf in the freezer. Dumpling came into the kitchen, apparently certain it was now time to discuss his evening meal.

She looked at his upturned face and felt the old question open again. There were things he did that she couldn't explain. That remained true.

But Sophie had given her one clear piece of information today, in her own voice.

She wasn't ready to say it yet.

Eve didn't need a clever cat to understand that sentence.
