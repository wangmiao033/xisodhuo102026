# Chapter 118 — Going Home Is Not Breaking Up

At six forty, Eve put the spare towel back in the cupboard.

She took it out again almost immediately. Putting it away looked decisive in a way she did not feel. Leaving it on the chair looked hopeful in a way she did not want to announce. She stood with the towel in both hands until Dumpling came to investigate the open cupboard.

"This is an object," she told him. "Apparently I need reminding."

She put it on the shelf and closed the door before he could climb inside.

Julian had said he would eat before coming. Eve had eaten too, badly, standing at the counter while a bowl of rice cooled. Now there was no cooking to hide behind. She washed the bowl, dried her hands, and moved her phone from the table to the desk. The long cable lay exactly where she had left it.

He knocked at seven.

For a moment they performed the uncertain choreography of people who had recently known how to greet each other. Eve stepped back to let him in. He leaned slightly toward her, stopped, and looked at her face.

"Hello," she said, and touched his arm.

"Hello."

She kissed him, briefly. The relief was immediate and incomplete.

He had brought no overnight bag.

"Tea?"

"Yes, please."

They made it together without either pretending that this was evidence of anything. Eve put out the mugs. Julian filled the kettle. Dumpling sniffed his shoes, then returned to the rug.

At the table, Eve sat in her usual chair and waited while he lowered himself into the other. She had rehearsed the beginning of this conversation in the shower, on the walk to the shops, and while failing to eat rice. Every version sounded like a speech delivered by somebody with much clearer feelings.

"I didn't sleep very well," she said.

"Neither did I."

She looked up quickly.

"Last night," he added. "Though I haven't slept brilliantly here either."

Eve stared at him.

"You said you were fine."

"I was fine. I was also awake because I thought moving would wake you."

"I thought moving would wake you."

They looked at each other over the table.

A laugh escaped her before she could make it appropriate. Julian's mouth shifted, not quite a smile.

"We've developed a very still sleeping arrangement," he said.

"Extremely considerate. Nobody benefits."

The joke loosened something without removing the need to talk. Eve wrapped her hands around her mug.

"I like waking up with you," she said. "I don't like setting an alarm in my head before your alarm, because I don't know whether you need the bathroom first. Or whether I should get up and have breakfast with you."

"You don't have to get up."

"I know you haven't asked. I do it anyway. Then I get annoyed about something I invented."

"I'd prefer you told me."

"I would also prefer to be that sensible person."

He leaned back, listening. She could speak more easily when he stopped looking as if he were bracing for the next accusation.

"And when I say I'm having an early night, sometimes I mean I want to put on terrible trousers and read until I'm too tired to follow a sentence. I don't want to discuss the book. I don't want to explain why I'm quiet."

Julian looked down into his tea.

"I know that feeling."

She paused. "Here?"

"Here. At home. Especially after a full day of talking. Sometimes I don't want to decide what to eat with anybody. I want something uninteresting and no further questions."

The admission hurt in a small place she hadn't expected.

"Including mine."

"Including yours."

He said it gently, which somehow made it more convincing.

Eve watched a line of steam disappear above his mug. She had believed his quietness was ease. Sometimes it probably was. Sometimes she had asked him three different questions because she wanted to give him an opening, when what he wanted was to finish chewing.

"Why did you keep coming?"

"Because I wanted to see you. Those feelings can happen on the same day."

"And staying?"

He rubbed one hand over the other.

"At first because you asked and I wanted to. Then because it seemed to be what we were doing. Leaving started to feel like making a statement."

"Me too. Asking you to leave, I mean."

"I noticed you were tired. I thought staying was the easier thing. No journey, no goodbye while you were already half asleep."

"I thought offering was the kind thing."

They sat with the results of their kindness.

Outside, somebody dragged a bin over an uneven patch of pavement. Eve followed the noise until it stopped, grateful for a pause that belonged to neither of them.

"I don't want to live together yet," she said.

The words came out plainly. No joke, no imaginary drawer.

Julian nodded.

"Neither do I."

She had wanted him to agree. Hearing it still produced a foolish flicker of disappointment, as though he ought to want something she did not and graciously refrain from having it.

She looked at that feeling, disliked it, and let it pass without handing it to him.

"I want my own evenings," he continued. "And I want coming here to feel like seeing you, not finishing the next part of a route."

Eve smiled slightly. "Work, groceries, girlfriend."

"You've left out laundry. A serious omission."

"I almost bought you breakfast cereal yesterday."

"I don't usually eat cereal."

"Exactly. I was provisioning a fictional man."

This time his smile reached his eyes.

Dumpling rose from the rug and came to sniff the lower edge of Julian's trousers. He rubbed against one leg, moved to Eve's, and then climbed into the unoccupied chair beside the wall. Eve watched him settle, but found nothing in his behavior that needed translating.

"Could we stop the automatic staying?" she asked. "For now. Dinner can just be dinner."

"Yes."

"Not as a punishment."

"I didn't think you were punishing me."

"I might think I'm punishing you. Even when I'm the person asking."

He considered that, thumb resting on the handle of his mug.

"Then I can say whether I'm upset. You don't need to decide in advance."

Eve nodded. She did not feel cured. She felt given something she could try the next time her imagination outran the man at the table.

They discussed the charger again, now that it no longer had to carry the whole relationship.

Julian apologized for moving it without telling her. She apologized for treating his use of it as evidence that he didn't care about her work. He explained that at his place he kept charging cables wherever there was a reachable socket; she explained that her desk was arranged around several inconvenient ones. Neither system became the correct system for all homes.

"If you need something moved, ask me," she said. "And I need to answer instead of taking it out of your hands."

"Including bowls."

"Including bowls."

He pointed toward the cupboards. "Which one was it, actually?"

She stood and showed him. He followed her into the kitchen, and she opened the right door without picking up a bowl to demonstrate competence.

"Left of the stove," he said.

"Yes. My original directions were technically an insult."

"They covered half the room."

He took a clean bowl from the draining rack and put it away. The cupboard door closed with a small, unremarkable click.

Eve hadn't expected that to be the moment she nearly cried.

She turned toward the sink and drank some water until the feeling settled.

When she looked back, Julian was gathering the mugs. He did not ask her to explain her face. She was grateful enough to want to hug him, but her face still felt unreliable. She stayed beside him instead, drying a teaspoon he handed her without looking.

While he dried the mugs, Eve opened the refrigerator to find somewhere for the leftover rice. A large bowl occupied the shelf where it would usually fit. Inside were enough cooked vegetables for another two meals.

"Would you take some of these?" she asked.

Julian looked over. "Do you want them?"

"I want about half. I bought them because you might be here, and now I'm angry with a quantity of cabbage."

He came to look. "I can eat cabbage."

She found a container and spooned half into it. He held the lid while she wiped the rim, then pressed it closed. When she handed it over, the exchange felt ordinary again. She wasn't supplying the breakfast of an imaginary resident. He was taking food to his own refrigerator, where it would either be eaten or become his problem.

"Return the container eventually," she said.

"I'll try not to reorganize it."

At eight, he said he should go.

Her body reacted before her thoughts: a tightening behind her ribs, the urge to offer another drink. They had spent an hour agreeing that going home was ordinary. Apparently her body had missed the meeting.

"Okay," she said.

He looked toward the bedroom. "I left a pair of socks here somewhere. Clean ones, I think."

"A comforting uncertainty."

They found them beneath the chair, one tucked inside the other. Eve shook them out and checked that Dumpling had not adopted any other piece of clothing. Julian put them in his coat pocket, then looked at the shoes by the door.

"Tomorrow?" he asked.

She knew he meant whether they should see each other. Her first impulse was yes, offered quickly enough to prove they were fine.

Tomorrow evening contained nothing scheduled. A client delivery would be finished by five. She could go to the shops, cook, draw, read. No one would know whether she did any of those things well.

"I'd like tomorrow on my own," she said.

He nodded. "All right."

"What about you?"

"I have several very quiet plans involving a washing machine."

She laughed and stepped closer. This kiss was longer than the one at the door when he arrived. His hand rested at the back of her neck, warm and familiar. Wanting him did not become less real because she could also imagine closing the door after him.

"Message me when you're home?" she asked.

"Yes."

He left.

Eve stood in the hall and listened to his steps fade. Then she went into the bedroom, put on the trousers with the hole at the knee, and returned to the kitchen.

The mugs were washed. The bowl was in the right cupboard. The spare towel remained on its shelf. There was no satisfying transformation in the furniture, no visible evidence that they had managed something difficult.

She took her drawing book to the table and opened it at the page holding their two cups.

For a moment she touched the edge of Julian's drawing. Then she moved the paper safely aside and turned to an exercise she had not tried yet.

The first line went wrong.

Nobody was there to reassure her, and nobody needed to be. She drew the next one.
