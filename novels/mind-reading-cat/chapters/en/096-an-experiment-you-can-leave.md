# Chapter 096 — An Experiment You Can Leave

Julian had brought nothing except himself.

Eve noticed because she had spent Sunday morning imagining objects that might tell her how the afternoon would go. A bag of groceries would mean dinner. A book would mean he intended to stay. Instead he stood on the landing in his ordinary coat, with his hands empty, and she had to welcome a person rather than decode a parcel.

“Hello.”

“Hello. May I come in?”

“Yes.”

She moved aside. He put his shoes where there was room for them and hung up his coat. Dumpling watched from the chair, one white paw dangling over the edge.

“Tea?” she asked.

“In a little while.”

They sat down. Eve had left the table clear. Its bare surface suddenly looked like another decision she had made too conspicuously.

“How were your two days?” Julian asked.

“Work, mostly. I finished the repair-business edit. Sophie borrowed a screwdriver that didn't solve anything.”

“An honest screwdriver.”

She smiled. “And you?”

“Work yesterday. Laundry this morning. I thought about what you told me.”

The smile left her face without becoming a performance of solemnity. She waited.

“I don't have a new explanation for the cat,” he said. “I do have a clearer question for you. What would you want from me if I never became certain?”

Eve looked at the empty table.

Once, she might have said she wanted nothing, grateful for the chance to appear undemanding. That would not have been true. She wanted him not to laugh at her. She wanted somewhere to put an experience she couldn't explain without first making it entertaining.

“To be able to tell you when something unsettles me,” she said. “Without pretending I know what happened. Not to have you agree every time.”

“I can listen. I may still disagree.”

“I know.”

“And I don't want this to become a project I'm responsible for finishing.”

“It won't.”

She nearly added a longer promise. Instead she asked the question she had kept in her private note.

“What do you need me to understand about the effect on you?”

Julian rested his forearms on his knees.

“I need to be able to have an unfinished thought near you. Even a thought about us. Not every uncertainty is an emergency requiring another source of information.”

Eve nodded, then stopped herself from filling the pause with agreement.

“I've been remembering conversations,” he continued. “Not because I think all of them were false. Because I didn't know when you were listening to me and when you were checking.”

“That's fair.”

“It isn't pleasant.”

“No.”

She could feel an explanation rising: how frightened she had been, how often her own fear had supplied the supposed signal. He already knew that. Repeating it now would move the conversation back onto ground she preferred.

She let him finish.

After a while Julian asked whether she still had the cups.

“In the kitchen. I haven't set them out.”

“I wondered if we could look at what you mean. Only if we both want to. Not because a result will settle the other conversation.”

Eve looked directly at him. “Do you want to?”

“Yes. Briefly. Something deliberately unimportant. No personal questions.”

She thought of the three cheap plastic cups, their bottoms scratched from ordinary use. In her imagination they had become the apparatus of a strange revelation. In the cupboard they held spare clips.

“I'd like that,” she said. “But if you change your mind, we put them away. Even if we haven't started.”

“And if he leaves, he leaves.”

Dumpling chose that moment to yawn so widely that Eve could see the pink roof of his mouth.

“He's negotiating a limited engagement.”

“Very limited.”

The joke loosened something. It did not make them experts.

Eve brought out the cups, emptied the clips into a small bowl, and washed the plastic. Julian dried the table where she dripped water. They moved to the rug because neither wanted to encourage Dumpling onto the dining surface.

Nothing went beneath the cups. There would be no food to find, no withheld meal, no reward for touching the thing they preferred. Eve checked that the route to his chair and the hallway remained open. The cat could ignore the entire undertaking without stepping over either human.

“Do we need to call it an experiment?” she asked.

“Not if that makes it sound better than it is.”

“An afternoon with poor equipment.”

“That seems accurate.”

She fetched one clean sheet rather than the old notebook. Julian asked what she had counted with Sophie. Eve explained the problem: looking at a cup wasn't necessarily choosing it, and touching two cups made a pleasing story if she remembered only one.

They agreed to write what actually happened. First contact, later contacts, no contact. No grand score at the top of the page.

“Before we know the choice,” Julian said.

“Yes.”

Eve found a few small blank cards left from a project. They marked the cup positions left, middle, and right from the sofa. Julian could write one position, fold the card, and hold it without showing her. Then he would spend a short moment thinking about that position while sitting still.

“That won't make me stop looking at it,” he said.

“Or stop me noticing where you're looking.”

“Exactly.”

They were not eliminating all ordinary cues. Naming that limitation did not erase it, but it stopped Eve from quietly promising herself a proof.

“Only a few minutes,” she said.

Julian nodded. “And no putting him back in place.”

They sat on opposite ends of the sofa, leaving plenty of room between their knees and the cups. Dumpling remained on the chair.

For a moment nothing happened.

Eve felt the impulse to make a small inviting noise. She swallowed it. If all they learned was that the cat preferred his chair on Sunday afternoon, the afternoon could survive.

Julian folded a card and placed it beneath his hand.

Dumpling lifted his head.

Eve became aware of every little movement in the room: Julian's thumb against paper, the quiet settle of the building, her own breath becoming careful. She looked at the cups, then away, worried that looking itself had become interference.

The cat stepped down. He stretched first, back legs reaching behind him in turn. Then he approached the rug and sniffed the cup nearest the window.

The right-hand one.

He touched its rim with his paw, moved toward the middle cup, and sniffed that too before rubbing his cheek against the sofa.

Eve wrote the sequence. She read it aloud before Julian opened the card.

Right.

For one bright instant, satisfaction arrived in her with the force of a correct answer on an exam she hadn't admitted taking.

She looked up.

Julian was looking at the cat, not applauding. “I did glance toward the window.”

“Yes,” Eve said. “I saw.”

She wrote that too. It made the line less beautiful and more useful.

They reset nothing except the slightly shifted cup. Dumpling stayed beside the sofa, washing between his toes. Julian selected again while Eve watched the ceiling, which had a faint mark she couldn't remember seeing before.

This time the cat stood, crossed in front of the cups, and knocked the left one with his shoulder. It rolled a short distance. He turned and tapped it twice as it settled.

“Shoulder first,” Eve said, writing. “Then paw. Not an approach straight to it.”

Julian opened the second card.

Left.

Eve held the pen still. Two correspondences were enough for the old hunger to begin constructing a staircase. She could almost feel the next step waiting beneath her foot.

Julian picked up the cup. There was a little line in its plastic where the light caught.

“This one's different,” he said.

“They're all scratched.”

“Differently.”

She nodded. Of course. Same kind of cup was not the same as indistinguishable to a cat. Neither of them knew what the freshly washed plastic smelled like to him, or what about the room had drawn his attention.

They exchanged the positions of the cups. Eve wrote that down rather than silently improving the procedure and treating the first results as though the improvement had applied all along.

When she put down the pen, Julian said, “I want a pause.”

Her heart gave an absurd little protest. They had only just reached the part that seemed promising.

“All right.”

She set the paper on the table and got up.

“Tea now?”

“Yes, please.”

The kettle's ordinary demands were a relief. Fill it, switch it on, find the cups intended for people. She took the clean mugs from the shelf and left the plastic ones where they stood, no longer a stage requiring performers.

Julian came to the kitchen doorway.

“I could feel myself trying to keep everything still,” he said. “It stopped feeling like sitting in a room.”

“I was doing that too.”

“I don't want to make myself anxious to produce a stronger thought.”

“Then we don't.”

She realized only after saying it that this was the first useful result they had obtained. A person had asked to stop, and she hadn't asked for just one more attempt before honoring it.

She took a biscuit from the tin for herself and offered him the tin. He chose the broken one on top. Eve had been about to insist there were whole ones underneath, then saw the absurdity of supervising even his biscuit.

“There's something very ordinary about this,” she said.

“Biscuits?”

“Not getting the afternoon I imagined.”

“Is that bad?”

“No. I think the imagined version had you convinced by half past three and me entirely forgiven by four.”

Julian looked at her, then down at the broken biscuit.

“That was an efficient schedule.”

“Unworkable, though.”

“Yes.”

They waited for the water. Julian noticed a loose button beside the kettle and asked where it belonged. Eve put it in the sewing tin, laughing at her own claim to efficient storage.

When the tea was ready, they returned to the sofa. Dumpling had settled between the plastic cups and the chair, not enclosed by either. He looked up at the mugs, then lowered his head.

“We can finish now,” Eve said.

“I know. I'd be willing to watch once more after tea, if you still want to. Then stop.”

She checked her own answer. She did want to, but not because he owed her a third matching card. That distinction would be harder to keep once the card existed.

“One more after tea,” she agreed. “No matter what happens.”

She took a sip too soon and burned the very tip of her tongue. Julian slid the water glass toward her without comment. Eve drank, then laughed.

“Some observations are repeatable.”

“Unfortunately.”

The sheet lay on the table with its unremarkable descriptions and two words that still made her curious. She did not cross either part out. The strange possibility remained strange, surrounded by ordinary things they hadn't controlled.

For now, the tea needed to cool. Julian was beside her because he had chosen to return. The cat was free to go somewhere else.
