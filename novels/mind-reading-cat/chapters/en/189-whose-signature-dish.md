# Chapter 189 — Whose Signature Dish?

Julian's face appeared between the salt jar and a wooden spoon, at an angle that gave him a commanding view of the ceiling.

“I can see a light fitting,” he said.

“It's helping.” Eve adjusted the phone against a heavy mug. “You're a guest in this kitchen. Be polite.”

“I thought I was the instructor.”

“That depends on the result.”

Behind her, her mother was washing tomatoes. Julian's aunt had come back with the successful lamp fitting in her bag and a paper parcel of green beans. Both visitors would leave the next day, though Eve's mother still had her work commitment in the morning. Tonight's dinner had begun as a practical way to use food and become an event the moment Julian claimed he could explain his favorite quick chicken dish over video.

Eve had accepted the challenge before asking what ingredients it required.

This was the first mistake.

The second was believing that the phrase quick chicken dish meant the same thing to two different people.

“Show me what you've got,” he said.

She turned the phone toward the counter.

There was chicken, still refrigerated until she needed it. There were tomatoes, beans, onions, a lemon, and the quarter cabbage which had survived several attempts to evict it from the fridge. There was a carton of stock and a collection of spices whose labels did not all face forward.

“Where's the cream?”

Eve looked at the counter, then at the screen.

“You said lemon chicken.”

“With a pan sauce.”

“Lemons are there. Pan is there.”

His aunt laughed into the towel she was using to dry her hands.

Julian had returned to his training accommodation less than an hour earlier. His bag lay open behind him, a dark spill of clothes visible on the bed. He had bought his own dinner on the way back and was eating a roll Eve had given him while the rest waited. There was something deeply unfair about receiving cooking advice from a man currently doing nothing more demanding than chewing.

“We can adapt it,” he said.

“I admire your faith.”

“Do you have mustard?”

Eve opened the fridge and lifted the long-lived jar.

“My tenant has finally found employment.”

Her mother passed her the tomatoes. “What do you want me to do with these?”

“Cut them, please.”

“How small?”

Eve looked at Julian.

“Tomatoes aren't in my version.”

“They are in ours,” her mother said, without hostility. “They're already washed.”

The aunt drew up a chair at the table and began trimming beans. The kitchen was too narrow for three people to stand shoulder to shoulder, and Eve was grateful when everyone settled into a space that actually existed instead of pretending enthusiasm created more counter.

Dumpling watched from the sitting room doorway. When the aunt dropped a bean end into the scrap bowl, he lifted his head. Eve moved the bowl farther onto the table and gave him access to his usual quiet corner. He settled there after investigating a chair leg, leaving the humans to their inexplicable meal.

Julian explained the order of his recipe. Eve listened, then repeated it back while taking out the chicken and setting up a separate board. Her mother started the onions at Eve's request. The aunt asked whether the beans were going into the pan too.

“Not in my version,” Julian said again.

“You'll need a different phrase soon,” his aunt told him.

He smiled. “I'm beginning to realize that.”

Eve had intended to brown the chicken, make the sauce, and serve the vegetables alongside. What actually happened was that she chose a pan too small, then decided against cooking in several batches because the onions were already ready and everyone was hungry.

She stopped before committing to the arrangement, put down the tongs, and took out the larger pot.

“Change of plan.”

Julian tilted his head. “How large a change?”

“The sort with a lid.”

“That isn't really the same dish.”

“No. But I think it'll be dinner.”

Her mother looked into the pot. “Good. I know where to put the tomatoes now.”

They rearranged rather than rescuing something burned. Eve browned what needed browning with enough space, transferred it, and let the onions soften properly. The tomatoes went in next. The lemon stayed on the counter while she considered what it would do to everything else.

Julian waited while she checked the consistency. When she asked whether the stock level looked sensible, he said he could not judge it accurately from the camera angle. She lowered the heat and looked herself.

It was not as flattering as having a confident voice guide every move. It was considerably more useful.

His aunt leaned toward the screen. “Do you remember your first soup?”

“No,” Julian said immediately.

“You remember it.”

“I remember several successful soups.”

“Your mother said she could have used it to hang wallpaper.”

Eve turned off the extractor for a moment. “I'm listening.”

He sighed with the resignation of a man whose relatives possessed evidence.

“I was young.”

“You were old enough to read the packet.”

The aunt explained that Julian had tried to thicken a soup by adding dry grains with no allowance for the liquid they would absorb. He had defended the result as substantial. His mother had loosened it into something edible, and his father had requested a fork.

Julian looked at Eve. “This story has been improved over time.”

“Unlike the soup,” his aunt said.

Eve laughed so hard she had to set down the spoon.

Her mother, who had been very quiet, began arranging the chopped cabbage with suspicious concentration.

Eve noticed too late.

“No.”

“I haven't said anything.”

“You have a face.”

“I usually do.”

Julian leaned closer to his camera.

Eve's mother told the story of a childhood cake made with a recipe Eve had copied from a friend. The original had called for a small amount of salt. Eve's handwriting had transformed the instruction into a quantity so heroic that the cake became inedible. She had decorated it beautifully and insisted everyone take a generous slice before tasting her own.

“I was proud of the icing,” Eve said.

“It was excellent icing.”

“The rest is slander.”

Her mother looked at Julian. “She tried to solve it by adding jam.”

Julian covered his mouth with his hand.

Eve pointed the wooden spoon at him. “Your soup needed structural assistance.”

“I'm saying nothing.”

“Your shoulders are saying quite a lot.”

The food cooked while the stories became smaller and less polished. The aunt recalled a dinner she had prepared for friends when she discovered, after everyone arrived, that her oven had stopped heating. They ate sandwiches with the expensive salad she had intended as a side. Eve's mother admitted that one of Eve's favorite childhood meals had begun as an attempt to use the wrong vegetables before they spoiled.

No one asked whether Eve would cook this way for Julian forever. No one ranked the women by how well they fed him. They were four people with histories of being hungry and getting things wrong, and the warmth of that was more satisfying than praise she would have had to keep earning.

Eve checked the chicken with her kitchen thermometer rather than trusting the color on a screen. She adjusted the seasoning, tasted a spoonful of sauce after letting it cool, and decided the lemon could contribute a little zest instead of taking command.

The beans went into a separate pan. Her mother cooked the cabbage. The aunt laid three places at the table, then paused with a fourth plate in her hand and grinned.

“He'll have to supply his own.”

“I have,” Julian said, lifting his dinner container.

They moved the phone to the end of the table, safely away from steam. Eve poured water while her mother brought the vegetables over. The aunt tried the sauce first.

“Well?” Eve asked.

“Very good.”

“Compared with a salt cake, or independently?”

“Independently.”

Julian demanded a description. Eve described the softened tomatoes, the little sharpness of mustard, and the lemon that had finally found a useful supporting role. His expression grew increasingly mournful.

“Mine has rice,” he said.

“So does ours.”

“That isn't helping.”

His aunt offered to hold a forkful up to the camera. He declined on the grounds that cruelty did not become hospitality merely because she used a clean fork.

For a while, the conversation moved around him rather than always toward him. Her mother and the aunt compared tomorrow's train times. Eve asked about the old friend from breakfast and learned that they had once shared an office with a heater that made everyone on one side of the room unbearably warm. Julian contributed a story about his course classroom, where the person nearest the window had become the unofficial custodian of everybody's temperature preferences.

Sometimes the connection caught on a word. Sometimes somebody asked him to repeat a sentence. They waited when necessary without treating the interruption as a verdict on the evening.

After dinner, Eve's mother packed her papers for the morning. The aunt called for her ride back to Julian's parents' house. Eve put leftovers into suitable containers for prompt refrigeration, then wrote a label before her mother could ask whether the contents were an old rice thing.

“What are you calling it?” Julian asked from the screen.

She looked at the marker.

“Dinner.”

“Very distinctive.”

She wrote the date, then added Chicken, tomatoes, mustard. It was enough information for the fridge, if not for a restaurant menu.

When the aunt had left and her mother was checking a document in the bedroom, Eve brought the phone to the sofa.

Julian was putting his own things away now. She watched him fold a shirt with more care than it deserved.

“Was that all right?” he asked.

“The cooking?”

“The evening.”

“Yes. Even with your terrible recipe.”

“You made a completely different recipe.”

“Then it was excellent supervision.”

He smiled at her, the familiar uneven movement that made the distance briefly less important.

“I liked hearing you laugh,” he said.

Eve shifted the cushion behind her neck. “I liked having you here.”

Not entirely here. Not within reach. The difference remained, but it had not prevented dinner.

Dumpling climbed onto the far end of the sofa and began washing one paw, indifferent to the small face on the screen. Eve left him to it.

“Send me what you actually put in that pot,” Julian said.

“Why?”

“I want to make it when I get back.”

Eve looked toward the kitchen, where the labeled leftovers waited behind a door that finally closed.

She reached for the notebook beside her chair, finding a clean page between shopping reminders. For once, she wanted to record an accident rather than correct it.

“All right,” she said. “But the salt is negotiable.”
