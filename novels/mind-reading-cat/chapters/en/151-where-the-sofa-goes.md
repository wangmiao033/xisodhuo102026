# Chapter 151 — Where the Sofa Goes

On Eve's drawing, everyone had exceptionally narrow shoulders.

She discovered this when she tried to carry an empty pet carrier between the long sofa and the table. The carrier struck the table leg. She turned sideways, knocked the sofa with her hip, and arrived at the proposed welcome point facing the wall.

Julian stood by the door holding her second carrier.

“Welcome,” she said to the wall.

“A distinctive approach.”

“Very private.”

The sofa was beautiful. That was becoming the main thing she could say for it.

It had come from an event-furniture supplier on a short trial hire, delivered that morning during their booked access period. Eve had seen the pale green upholstery in a photograph and immediately imagined two owners talking without a table dividing them. A washable cover was available if she eventually ordered something similar. The broad arms looked reassuring. The long back would hide the less attractive lower half of the wall.

In her sketch, a person entered, turned gently, put down a carrier, and found a chair. In the room, the gentle turn required the person to pass through an armrest.

The delivery team had placed the sofa exactly where she asked. Before leaving, one of them had turned the little welcome table sideways to get back out.

She had turned it back.

Now she set the carrier down and looked at the marks on her plan.

“I measured everything.”

Julian looked at the sofa, then at the carrier he held. “Including this?”

“The furniture.”

He nodded without adding anything.

The landlord had unlocked the unit at nine and would return before the end of their slot. They could test removable furniture, take measurements, and leave the approved pieces against the inner wall until collection. They could not move into the place by becoming enthusiastic enough.

Eve checked the time. Nearly forty minutes had gone on positioning the sofa for a photograph she had not taken.

“Let me try from the other direction.”

She moved the table toward the window. This freed the first turn and narrowed the route to the rear room. Julian walked it with the larger carrier. He stopped beside the table rather than squeeze through.

“If someone is sitting here?” he asked.

“They can pull their chair in.”

“How far?”

Eve sat down.

Her knees hit the underside of the tabletop. Julian remained where he was. The carrier remained where it was. Nobody had become smaller through cooperation.

“All right,” she said.

She stood and tried moving the chair instead. That put its back into the approach from the entrance.

On paper, she had drawn the chairs as circles. Circles did not have knees, coats, walking sticks, or a bag they kept forgetting on the floor. They were very easy customers.

Julian placed the carrier safely beneath the window and took the chair she offered him. He was here for an hour before meeting a friend for lunch, a plan Eve had nearly forgotten while asking whether he could help move things.

“Can you see the notice from there?” she asked.

He looked toward the sheet she had propped on the welcome table.

“The upper half.”

The name, You, Me & Paws, was legible. The information about where to wait was hidden behind a sample leaflet holder.

Eve took the holder away.

“What if you try the route from wheelchair height?” Julian asked. “You keep looking down at everything.”

There was no wheelchair available, and she did not want to mistake sitting on an ordinary chair for understanding another person's experience. Still, she lowered herself onto the seat beside the entrance and looked across the room.

The sofa's long back cut the room into two unrelated halves. She could see a strip of table, Julian's hands, and a plant stand she had marked for later. She could not see whether anyone at the proposed welcome desk had noticed her.

“Oh,” she said.

Julian waited.

She shifted the chair to the first turn. At that height the underside of the table and the solid sofa arm filled most of her view. Even if a route could be squeezed between them, the person arriving would have to trust that someone knew where they were meant to go.

She had just finished a paid project explaining an awkward entrance. Apparently expertise did not vaccinate a person against wanting a green sofa.

“I need someone who actually uses the route to test it,” she said. “And the measurements checked properly. This isn't that.”

“No.”

“But it tells me I shouldn't buy this.”

Julian looked at the sofa. “That seems a useful result.”

She had wanted him to say it could fit somewhere else. She was annoyed with him for several seconds before recognizing that she had supplied both sides of the argument herself.

Together they moved the small table first, then lifted the sofa back to the clear delivery position. They took their time. Its reassuring broad arms proved less reassuring when one had to carry them.

At the wall, Eve stepped back.

The center of the room opened. She could see from the entrance to the far window. With the table pulled away, Julian could walk the route carrying the larger box without turning his shoulders.

“It looks empty,” she said.

“It is less full.”

“I know those words are related.”

He smiled and checked his phone for the time.

“You have twelve minutes,” she said.

“Enough to try again?”

She picked up the smaller carrier.

This time they included arriving at the same moment. Eve came through the doorway while Julian stood inside holding the larger carrier and a tote bag. Neither box contained an animal, which made their repeated collisions less consequential than the real thing would be.

The first attempt failed because Eve had made the welcome point the nearest available flat surface. A visitor stopping to ask a question would block whoever followed.

The second failed because she put the waiting chair in the one place the door needed.

The third worked until Julian asked where he would put his wet coat.

“Outside, on the body it arrived on.”

“An unusually firm coat policy.”

She laughed and marked another question on the plan.

When he left, he kissed her beside the unhelpful sofa.

“Lunch tomorrow?” he asked.

“I have a client call until one.”

“Half past?”

“Yes. Somewhere whose furniture is none of my business.”

He went out with his own bag. Eve watched him through the window for a moment, then returned to her plan.

By afternoon she had three versions. In one, two modest chairs replaced the sofa. In another, a small movable bench sat near the window. In the third, she left the choice blank and drew the route first.

She prepared that version for the access adviser whose short assessment she had included in her evaluation costs. The message asked for a visit and a quote for checking the actual space, not approval of a photograph. She attached measured openings and noted the threshold she wanted assessed. No furniture order needed to precede the answer.

She also changed the storage drawing. In the first version, spare chairs lived in a vague rectangle behind the welcome table. When she counted them, that rectangle had to become nearly twice as deep. The table would then move forward into the route she had just cleared.

She laid the spare chairs against the wall and measured the stack rather than the outline she wished it made. Two could remain; the others would need to be brought for particular bookings or stored somewhere she had actually arranged. She reduced the everyday seating count before sending the plan.

This changed the number of people the room could welcome at once. Eve opened the income forecast, adjusted the possible session sizes, and saved it beside the drawing. A wider route could not pay for itself by quietly admitting the same crowd through a narrower estimate.

Then she called the hire supplier.

The woman who answered asked whether there was anything wrong with the sofa.

“No. It does everything a sofa should. It's the room that won't cooperate.”

“Collection as arranged, then?”

“Yes. And please don't prepare the purchase option.”

The delivery and hire charge would still be due. Eve wrote it into the evaluation costs while she could still remember the actual amount rather than the smaller amount her disappointment preferred.

A trial had cost money and prevented a larger purchase. She did not feel triumphant. She felt like someone who had hired a very expensive obstacle for a morning.

Before the landlord returned, she took a photograph from the entrance for her own records. The sofa stood against the wall, safely away from the routes, looking much better than anything else in the image.

She nearly cropped the picture to include only that part.

Instead she kept the whole room.

At home, the plan covered half her desk. Dumpling climbed onto the other half and put one paw on the corner of the paper. Eve moved the pencil before he could knock it down.

“Your household sofa remains safe,” she told him.

He settled with his back toward her.

She opened the image again. On the small screen, the green sofa looked even more convincing. A square crop would produce a charming announcement. She could write about warm conversation and a place to belong, and nobody viewing it would need to carry a box around the corner.

Her phone rang before she could become too pleased with the imaginary caption.

It was Mabel, returning the message Eve had left about visiting the unit.

“Are you asking me to admire it or find things wrong?”

“The second. You can do the first if anything survives.”

“Will Biscuit be allowed?”

“For a short arranged visit, yes. Only you and me. If that suits him.”

Mabel asked about the route there, the time of day, and whether she would have somewhere to sit. Eve answered the first two questions and paused at the third.

“I've just rejected a sofa.”

“That doesn't answer me.”

“There will be a chair with a back.”

“Inside?”

“At the moment.”

“Then let's not make too many arrangements before we arrive.”

They chose a short slot for later in the week. Eve confirmed it with the landlord, then looked again at the floor plan.

She had drawn a person inside every zone. Someone talking, someone listening, someone putting down a bag. She had not drawn anyone deciding to remain outside.

On the edge of the sheet she added the entrance apron and the covered strip along the front. The proportions were uncertain. She would have to measure them.

Dumpling rose, stepped over the paper, and went to his bowl.

Eve followed him to refresh his water. When she came back, her phone held a reply from the adviser offering a time within her next access window. She accepted, sent the landlord the details, and put the appointment into her calendar beside the client work that still paid for all this.

The sofa's place on the drawing remained blank.

For the first time that day, the blank looked like room to turn around.
