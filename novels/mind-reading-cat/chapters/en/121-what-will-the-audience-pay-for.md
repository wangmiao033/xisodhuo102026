# Chapter 121 — What Will the Audience Pay For?

The line on Eve's screen had stopped climbing, although the cat responsible for most of it was currently climbing the curtains.

Not very high. Dumpling had secured one claw in the hem and appeared unwilling to abandon either his dignity or the fabric. Eve saved the report, crouched beside him, and freed his paw.

"Your contribution to household expenses has been noted."

He walked away without looking at the small puncture.

For three months, the account's audience had grown quickly enough that Eve could describe any quiet week as an exception. Now four quiet weeks sat beside one another. People still liked the photographs. A picture of Dumpling looking offended by an empty laundry basket had received more attention than a carefully checked guide to preparing questions before a veterinary appointment.

Neither had paid the electricity bill. Her client commissions had done that.

She wasn't in immediate trouble. Two invoices had cleared, the next assignment was booked, and she had money set aside for ordinary expenses. But she had been promising herself that the account would eventually become something sturdier than a place to display work for other people's businesses. Eventually was becoming a suspiciously comfortable word.

She opened a blank document and wrote: What do people need?

Below it, almost automatically, she typed three possible video series.

Then she deleted them.

The following afternoon she met Mabel outside a bakery. Biscuit lay beside the chair with his chin on his paws, declining the existence of passing bicycles. Eve had asked for twenty minutes to discuss what Mabel actually used, and brought no camera.

"You're interviewing me," Mabel said, inspecting the little notebook.

"If you don't mind. You don't have to be encouraging."

"Good. That would take longer."

Eve asked which of her recent posts Mabel remembered.

"The one about writing down the clinic's instructions. And the cat in the basket."

"Why the basket?"

"He looked like my brother when someone mentions parking."

Eve wrote that down before deciding she probably didn't need to.

When she asked what Mabel had found difficult recently, Mabel pointed at the bakery door.

"Yesterday I wanted to know whether there was somewhere to sit outside after the owners' talk. The announcement said refreshments. That tells me very little."

"You wanted somewhere Biscuit could come?"

"No. He was staying home. I wanted a chair with a back, and to know whether I could leave before the end without disturbing a lecture."

Eve looked at the question she had written. Pet owners' needs. She had immediately supplied the pet and nearly missed the owner.

"Did you go?"

"I couldn't establish what time it finished. So I didn't."

Biscuit shifted one paw, then settled again. Mabel reached down to check that his lead wasn't caught beneath the chair leg.

"Would you pay for something like that if the information were clearer?"

"I already pay for things. The question is whether I want the particular thing."

It was the sort of answer Eve would have edited out of an inspirational interview. She underlined it instead.

Two days later, she had spoken to six people. One followed her account for the cat and wanted nothing else from it. One wanted affordable photography and was already saving for a session. Another had stopped attending neighborhood activities because every discussion became advice from the loudest person present.

The sixth, a man called Ben who had answered her invitation to talk, arrived with a sandwich he had bought on his way from work.

"I don't want a membership," he said before sitting down.

"I haven't offered one."

"Good. I keep accidentally having them."

They sat at a café table near the window. Eve bought her own drink; Ben had declined one. She explained that she was exploring a paid activity, not collecting testimonials, and he could skip anything he preferred not to discuss.

He owned an older cat. He liked hearing how other people managed ordinary household problems, but disliked open comment threads where every question acquired a frightening answer.

"Last month I asked how people kept track of appointments," he said. "Someone told me I needed to change all his food."

"That wasn't the question."

"Apparently I was asking the wrong question without knowing it."

Eve smiled and asked what a useful hour would look like.

He thought while peeling the sandwich wrapper back.

"A few people. Somebody keeping us on the subject. Actual information about where to find things. And it has to finish when it says."

"Would you bring your cat?"

Ben stared at her.

"He would resign from the household."

The answer relieved her more than it should have. She had been imagining a room filled with animals because that was the picture people might expect from her account. She could already see the transport worries, incompatible needs, and work involved in making such a room appropriate.

An owners-only exchange could be useful without turning anybody's pet into an admission ticket.

"Would you pay for that?" she asked.

"Depends how much, and whether you mean a conversation or someone selling me something halfway through."

She showed him a possible fee, written beside a proposed length of seventy-five minutes.

"I could try that once," he said. "I wouldn't book every week on the strength of this conversation."

She wrote once in larger letters than the rest.

At home, her interview notes covered the kitchen table. Dumpling sat on the only sheet she had not weighted with a mug.

"You are not a representative sample."

He began washing his shoulder.

Eve moved the papers to the desk and grouped the answers. She left the disagreements visible. Mabel wanted an afternoon. Ben could manage an early evening. Someone else wanted Saturdays and childcare, which Eve could not provide. There was no single perfect event hiding inside the six conversations.

There was a possible small one.

She contacted the neighborhood center about a meeting room for one early evening. The coordinator sent the public hire information, including the fee, clearing time, access arrangements, and the fact that another group needed the room afterward. Eve visited before booking.

The room had six ordinary chairs around a square table, a whiteboard with an unconvincing eraser, and a window facing a brick wall. She sat in one chair long enough to discover that its front edge pressed beneath her knees. Another was better. She asked whether she could use the comfortable set stacked beside the cupboard and received permission to move them, provided she put them back.

No fairy lights. No attractive rescue photograph above the door. A room where six people could hear one another.

She booked a single slot.

Her first announcement was excellent advertising for an event she had no intention of running.

A warmer life with your pet begins here, it said.

She read it aloud and felt her shoulders rise.

"Does it?" she asked the empty kitchen.

Dumpling came in because he had heard her voice. She pointed at the screen as if he might provide editorial criticism, then remembered he had recently attempted to eat a label.

She rewrote the opening.

A small, hosted exchange for pet owners about the everyday arrangements that make caring for an animal easier. Bring one ordinary story or question about your routine. Listening is welcome too.

She added the length, location, access details, fee, and a maximum of six. Animals would stay at home. There would be no filming, no product pitch, and no veterinary consultation. Participants would leave with their own practical next step and links to clearly identified sources, where useful. She could organize discussion and help people sort information. She could not promise to solve every question they brought.

The refund terms took longer than the description. She wanted something she could actually honor, not comforting language that evaporated when money arrived. For this first trial, anyone who found the advertised format unsuitable could request a refund by the end of the following day. No explanation beyond poor fit was required. She included that cost in the amount she could afford to lose.

Then she calculated what six tickets would return after room hire, materials, and payment charges.

The number looked modest even before she counted her time.

She counted it anyway.

At full attendance, this was a paid test, not a new livelihood. At three, it would cost her some of the money she had deliberately set aside for trying it. She wrote both figures down and circled the maximum trial loss. If the experiment taught her nothing, at least it would not be able to teach her an unlimited amount at once.

Julian came for dinner that evening because she had invited him. He arrived without a work bag and kissed her before asking whether the pan on the stove was supposed to be making that noise.

"Probably not."

He moved while she rescued the onions.

Over dinner she told him about Ben's sandwich, Mabel's chair, and the woman who wanted photographs rather than conversation.

"Which one are you making?" he asked.

"The conversation. For now. The photographs may already be a different service someone else does better."

"And the cat?"

"Continues to offer unsolicited upholstery work."

Dumpling was under the table. At the sound of his name, his tail touched Julian's ankle.

Eve showed Julian the announcement after they had eaten, asking him to flag any wording that could imply medical advice. He pointed to one sentence about leaving more confident in pet care.

"That could mean more than you're offering."

She changed it to knowing the next practical step for the issue discussed, then looked again and shortened it further. Participants would make a note of a useful next step. No guaranteed change in their emotional state.

"Do you want my name on anything?" he asked.

She shook her head.

"You're not running it."

"Good. I have no expertise in chair selection."

"Your lid purchase suggests a broader problem with dimensions."

He looked wounded for precisely as long as it took her to laugh.

After he left, Eve checked the booking page on her phone. The address displayed properly. The end time was visible without opening another section. The payment button worked in the service's test mode, and the confirmation explained what to expect. She removed a photograph of Dumpling from the top because it made the event look more like a chance to meet him than a room for humans.

She replaced it with a plain photograph of the table, taken with the center's permission while the room was empty.

Then she published the invitation.

There was no immediate flurry of bookings. One person asked whether pets could attend despite the sentence saying they could not. Eve answered politely and made the sentence easier to find.

At ten, Ben booked one place.

The notification sat beside her client email, unremarkable in its little rectangle. A real person had read what she intended to offer, seen the price, and decided to try it.

Eve entered the payment in her trial accounts rather than counting it as proof of anything larger.

Then she closed the laptop. Dumpling was waiting beside the repaired curtain, apparently prepared to reopen negotiations.
