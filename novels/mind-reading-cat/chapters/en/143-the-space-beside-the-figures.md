# Chapter 143 — The Space Beside the Figures

Julian's shopping bag contained a repaired shoe.

Only one. The other, he explained, was at home, having done nothing wrong.

Eve looked into the bag as they left the repair shop.

“Will they still match?”

“I'm hoping people won't inspect both soles.”

“You can't control your audience.”

“I rarely try.”

Their Sunday walk had acquired two errands by mutual agreement. Eve had collected printer paper; Julian had collected the shoe. Now they followed the long path beside the recreation ground, where a man was attempting to teach three children a game none of them seemed interested in playing.

One child sat down inside a hoop.

“Strong tactical choice,” Eve said.

Julian watched a ball roll past the seated player. “Conserving energy.”

She would have liked to keep walking and criticizing other people's sports. The figures in her bag made a small, stiff presence against her hip, waiting for the conversation she had asked to have.

At his flat, Julian put the repaired shoe beside its partner and gave Eve the better of two mugs. She recognized the chipped one he kept for himself, though she had stopped interpreting every ordinary preference as a sign of devotion.

“Tea first?” he asked.

“Please.”

He made it while she put her coat away. A folded clothes rack stood beside the window. Two socks hung from a rail with the defeated posture of people who had waited a very long time for a bus.

“I forgot those yesterday,” he said, following her glance.

“They seem patient.”

“They have limited alternatives.”

Eve sat at the kitchen table. It was his table, in his home, with a book he was reading beneath the fruit bowl and a shopping list written on the back of an envelope. She liked being here. Lately she liked it more because arriving was an invitation rather than the default direction of every evening.

Julian brought the tea and sat opposite her.

“Where do you want to start?”

She took out two pages.

The first showed the quarter she could cover with confirmed work and cautious estimates. The second showed what the brand package would have allowed. She had highlighted the difference in yellow, which made it look cheerier than she intended.

“Here,” she said. “This is the version I got attached to before anybody signed it.”

She explained the minimum fee, the account-wide approval, the request to remove the adoption-readiness post. Julian listened without reaching for the pages.

When she described Mia's marked-up story, he asked whether the existing post had changed.

“No. Nothing was removed or reused. They sent examples. I told them those examples weren't available.”

He nodded.

Then she explained the possible smaller work. Lydia had acknowledged her proposal and would discuss it with the content team. There might be projects outside the partnership budget. There might not.

“What stays possible if they say no?” Julian asked.

Eve showed him the first sheet.

“Existing commissions. The booked room session. A slower attempt at the next one. More time looking for ordinary work.”

“And these?”

He pointed at the highlighted rows without pulling the paper toward himself.

“Things I wanted to bring forward.”

She described them. Paid assistance on larger shoots. Enough advance time to improve the owners' exchange rather than rebuilding it around whatever deadline arrived. A reserve that made late payments unpleasant instead of immediately important.

Saying it aloud made the lost version sound reasonable. That was the difficulty. She had not filled it with velvet chairs and unnecessary launch parties. She had wanted a less precarious way to do fairly sensible work.

“Could you reach it more slowly?” he asked.

“Probably.”

The word landed with an irritation she knew he had not put into it.

He let her drink her tea.

“I know slowly is an answer,” she said. “I've become very knowledgeable about slowly.”

“I wasn't trying to make it sound pleasant.”

“I know.”

She looked at the pages. Beneath all the calculations was another thought, one she had not written down because writing it would make it look like an expectation.

Julian had steady work. He cared about her. He had seen the room and said he would like to see what she made of it. There had been moments that week when Eve imagined the uncomfortable difference between her two forecasts simply becoming less frightening after they talked.

She had not asked herself how that would happen.

Now she could hear the answer hiding in the silence.

“If I couldn't cover the difference,” she said, “would you help me keep the larger plan going for a few months?”

Julian looked at the second sheet.

“Do you mean pay the shortfall?”

“Yes.”

“All of it?”

Her face warmed.

“Until the work caught up.”

He was quiet for a moment. She could see him taking the question seriously, which should have made it easier to hear the answer.

“I wouldn't agree to cover that,” he said.

Eve put her mug down carefully.

He did not soften the refusal into a possible yes she could spend later. He also did not push the pages back at her.

“I can listen,” he continued. “I can help you think through something you want to discuss. But I haven't chosen to fund this plan, and I can't become responsible for whatever the work doesn't bring in.”

“I wasn't asking for whatever.”

“The difference hasn't got an end date beyond the work catching up.”

She looked down.

He was right about the sentence she had used. She resented how visible that was.

“I could pay it back.”

“That would need a different conversation, with an actual amount and a way to repay it. I don't want to say yes to a loan because we're upset about something else.”

For a few seconds, Eve wanted to say that he did not have to make it sound so businesslike. The papers between them prevented the complaint from coming out. She had brought him a business problem and hoped he might answer it as a boyfriend.

He waited.

From the street came the brief scrape of a delivery trolley. Somewhere in the building a tap ran. His flat continued having an ordinary Sunday around the very large disappointment inside her.

“I thought you'd want to help,” she said.

“I do.”

The quietness of the answer made her look up.

“I want the work to go well,” he said. “I want you to have the room and the time if you can make them work. I also have plans for my own money. Being glad about yours hasn't committed mine.”

Eve rubbed a small tea mark with her thumb.

She knew those things. She had told him, in an empty borrowed room, that showing him a possible future was not a request to make it happen. At the time she had meant it completely. Apparently meaning something did not prevent a more frightened version of herself from trying a side entrance.

“I liked imagining there was something underneath it,” she said at last.

“Underneath what?”

“The risk. Something I didn't have to put in the accounts because it was you.”

Julian's expression changed, though she could not have named the change precisely.

“I can be here when you're disappointed.”

“I know.” She looked at the yellow marks. “I nearly counted you as a reserve without asking.”

“You've asked now.”

“And I don't like the answer.”

“I noticed.”

That almost made her smile.

He reached for the teapot and topped up both mugs. The gesture was so unaffected that it hurt a little. The refusal had not emptied the room of care. She had briefly behaved as though it might.

“I'm sorry,” she said. “For making wanting to help mean one particular thing.”

“Thank you.”

He did not tell her she had done nothing wrong. He also did not give her a lecture about independence she could have written herself, with unattractive confidence, a week earlier.

They sat quietly until Eve could look at the first sheet without feeling it accused her.

“Could you ask me the question again?” she said.

“Which one?”

“What stays possible.”

He did.

This time she answered without describing the smaller plan as an emergency version of the real one. The booked session would run. Existing customers would still receive the work they had commissioned. She could return to two former clients with specific availability rather than offering to do anything. The brand might commission a smaller project. She would not count that until it existed.

Julian asked what she needed to decide first.

“Whether I still want the extra trial dates before I know the result of the booked one.”

“Do you?”

“I want to be the sort of person who already has the next dates.”

He waited.

“That isn't the same answer,” she admitted.

“No.”

She folded the second sheet.

“Thank you for listening. And for not agreeing because I looked awful.”

“You don't look awful.”

“Financially persuasive, then.”

“Your highlighter did most of the work.”

She laughed, finally, and the sound loosened something beneath her ribs.

Julian glanced at the clock.

“I need to do half an hour of reading before dinner. Would you rather stay or take the walk back now?”

“Stay, if that's still all right. I can read too.”

“It is.”

He moved to the chair by the window with his book. Eve put the figures away and took the novel from her bag. For a while she read the same paragraph without following it. Then a character made a foolish decision unrelated to money, and she found herself properly interested.

At six, they made omelettes. Julian's broke when he folded it; Eve's stayed whole and then stuck to the pan.

“Which of us has won?” she asked.

“The mushrooms.”

They ate from plates that were not arranged around her documents. Julian told her about a confusing supplier label at work, then about a radio program he had wanted to hear and missed. Eve told him which fictional decision was currently offending her.

When she left, there was no transfer waiting on her phone, no secret purchase meant to make his refusal sweeter. There was a kiss at the door and an invitation to call the next evening if she wanted to tell him how the reply went.

At her own flat, Dumpling lifted his head from the familiar bed. He watched while she put away her bag, then stretched and came to inspect her shoes.

Eve fed him before opening the forecast.

She kept the supported version. She moved the larger one into a folder of possibilities, where it could remain without demanding that someone pay for its existence.

Beside tomorrow's first task, she wrote: Ask which projects they actually need.

Then she sent Julian a message about the novel. The foolish character had somehow made matters worse on the bus home.

His reply arrived while she was washing Dumpling's bowl.

He sounded surprised that she expected anything else.

Eve smiled. The conversation was still there, available for something besides saving her.
