# Chapter 181 — The Missed Video Call

Eve had arranged the phone against a mug so that Julian would see the finished part of the room.

This required the mug to stand on a stack of sample cards, which required the cards to stay still, which they refused to do. At six minutes past eight, the whole construction slid sideways and presented her boyfriend with an excellent view of the ceiling.

Except that her boyfriend wasn't there.

His message said the practical session had run late. Could they try in twenty minutes?

She sent back a yes and rescued the mug before it fell. On the dark screen, her own face looked faintly offended by its lighting.

Tuesday had been their first proper attempt at a video call since he left on Sunday. On Monday they had exchanged ordinary updates between his induction and her work. Tonight they had chosen a half hour that appeared empty in both calendars. Eve had cleared the table, put away the accounts, and told herself she wasn't preparing a performance.

Then she had moved the untidy stack out of shot.

Robin came through from the storage area carrying two folded cloths.

“Am I in the way?”

“No. He's delayed.”

“Right. These are dry. The other one needs another night.”

“Thank you.”

Robin finished their booked clearing time and left. Eve checked the door after them. Dumpling was at home, with her return planned around his familiar evening routine. She could wait twenty minutes here without making him wait indefinitely for everything else.

She sat down again.

At twelve minutes past, a customer called about a payment receipt. Eve answered because she was still there and because the question sounded simple. The receipt was correct, but the customer had used a different email address for the booking. Eve located the booking, confirmed where the receipt should go, and resent it.

Then the customer asked about the next session.

At twenty-three minutes past, Eve heard the little vibration of Julian calling. She looked at his name while the customer explained why a particular afternoon wouldn't suit her sister.

Eve let the call pass. A moment later she said, “The available dates are on the page I've sent. Have a look together and let me know through the booking form.”

The conversation ended with no disaster.

She stared at her phone.

She could have said that four minutes earlier.

She called Julian back. No answer. She sent a message saying she was free now, then gathered the papers she had hidden behind the mug. He replied while she was switching off the lights: he'd had to go down for the accommodation briefing about tomorrow's early start. He would message when he was back.

Eve put on her coat.

There was nothing suspicious in the exchange. Nobody had vanished into a mysterious life. They had simply taken turns becoming available after the other person had stopped being so.

It was astonishing how lonely something so uninteresting could feel.

At home, Dumpling walked across the hall with his tail upright and led her toward the kitchen. She attended to him, washed her hands, and heated her own dinner. The phone lay faceup beside her plate. Every time the screen brightened, she stopped chewing.

One notification advertised a discount on storage boxes.

“Unhelpful,” she told it.

Dumpling, who had finished eating and begun inspecting an entirely innocent patch of floor, offered no interpretation. Julian was several train rides away. Even if the cat had been interested in her phone, there would have been nothing of Julian's thoughts for him to hear through it.

By the time Julian was free, Eve had brushed her teeth and put on the old shirt with the stretched collar. His message asked whether it was too late.

She wanted to say no.

She also wanted to go to bed without placing her own exhaustion behind a more flattering face.

“Ten minutes?” she typed.

He called. This time she answered at once.

His image appeared sideways, corrected itself, and became mostly forehead.

“Your ceiling is better than mine,” she said.

“I haven't seen yours.”

“You missed a remarkable presentation earlier.”

He adjusted the phone. Behind him stood a plain wardrobe and a curtain whose fabric looked heavy enough to stop a small argument. He was wearing a shirt she knew, with one cuff turned up and the other hanging loose.

The sight of that unevenness made her suddenly miss him more than the empty chair had.

“Hello,” she said, softer.

“Hello.”

For a few seconds they smiled without finding an efficient use for the connection.

Then they both spoke.

“You go,” Eve said.

“No, you were—”

“The thrilling story is that somebody used the wrong email address.”

“I think mine may be about finding the right building.”

“Strong competition.”

He laughed, and she heard another sound beneath it: somebody closing a door in the corridor. The noise reminded her that this room had a whole building attached, a place she couldn't picture. She wanted to ask about every part of it. Ten minutes would not contain that appetite.

Julian told her he had sat at the wrong table during the first group exercise and contributed for several minutes before discovering the group had been assigned a different question.

“What did they do?”

“Let me finish.”

“Kind people.”

“Then asked whether I could move.”

She laughed into her sleeve. His smile warmed at the sound, and she wished she could put a hand against his cheek rather than touch the edge of a glass rectangle.

At nine minutes, he glanced away.

“Early morning?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Then sleep.”

“What about your day?”

“I'll tell you tomorrow. Nothing caught fire.”

“That's a high standard for every day.”

“We're ambitious here.”

They said goodnight before the little call became a test of which of them could remain awake longer. Eve put the phone down. She had enjoyed seeing him. She had also spent nearly two hours waiting for a ten-minute conversation, including time neither of them had asked her to reserve.

On Wednesday, the problem reversed.

The afternoon exchange was supposed to end with guests pairing labels to empty containers. One participant discovered she had brought a jar with a lid so firmly stuck that three adults became personally invested in opening it. Robin brought the activity to a close on time, but departures took longer than planned. Eve stayed to help a guest find a bus route after a service change, then dealt with a small spill beneath the table.

Julian's message arrived while she held a damp cloth in each hand.

Free for half an hour before the evening discussion, if you are.

She could see it. She could not reasonably pick up the phone and leave Robin to finish the work they had agreed to share.

Twenty minutes later she replied. His half hour had nearly ended.

Eve leaned against the counter and felt irritation gather, searching for somebody to belong to.

The guest hadn't deliberately missed a bus. Robin hadn't engineered the spill. Julian hadn't designed the timetable to make her feel irrelevant. All of this was obvious, which did very little to improve her mood.

Robin glanced over. “Bad news?”

“No. Bad timing.”

“That can be quite bad enough.”

She liked them for not asking her to be grateful it wasn't worse.

On the way home, Eve began composing a complaint. They needed to make more effort. They needed a better time. They needed to remember the evening they had promised each other.

Then she imagined receiving that message while trying to learn in an unfamiliar place, already disappointed after waiting beside her own silent screen.

She stopped at the crossing and let the lights change.

At home, she took care of Dumpling, ate before the food went cold, and opened the voice-message button.

“Hello. This is not a summons,” she said.

She stopped recording and listened back. She sounded like somebody announcing terms at a railway station.

She deleted it and tried again.

“I missed you today. I also got annoyed at a cloth, which suggests my approach isn't working. Could we leave each other short messages when we can't find the same gap? I'd rather hear one thing from your day than spend the whole evening preparing to hear everything.”

She paused, then added, “Also, nobody opened the jar. We respected its decision.”

She sent the message before she could polish the warmth out of it.

His reply came after she had put the washing away.

“I'd like that. I was disappointed too. I don't want you waiting with your dinner. And I have questions about the jar.”

His voice sounded tired and amused. There was no visual proof that he was sitting attentively in the right room. Eve found she did not require it.

Before answering, she moved to the kitchen. The cupboard door was hanging open, and a teaspoon had somehow traveled into the basket of clean towels. She carried it back, listening to his message a second time. On the first evening she had tidied the slice of room visible behind her shoulder, making a little stage on which to be agreeably missed. Now she was barefoot on a cold patch of floor, looking for the lid of her tea tin.

She recorded the reply there.

Halfway through it, a spoon fell off the draining board. The clatter was appalling.

“That's my evening assistant,” she said into the phone. “We are reviewing his contract.”

She almost started again, then left the noise in. When Julian answered, he was laughing before he managed a word. She had wanted him to see a pleasant room. Apparently what she had really missed was having him inside an untidy minute.

They exchanged three short messages. She told him about the guest who had labeled an empty container THINGS I WILL REMEMBER, then admitted that she no longer remembered what those things were. He told her about a tutor who had taken a paragraph of his written exercise and asked him to say it aloud as though speaking to a worried owner. Apparently his first attempt had contained four separate uses of the word regarding.

“You'd have enjoyed that,” he said.

“I would have charged admission,” Eve replied.

They chose another longer call for a day when both schedules genuinely allowed it, with a message if it needed changing. The short recordings could carry the ordinary days between. They weren't replacing every conversation with efficient little parcels. They were stopping themselves from sitting miserably beside a phone out of loyalty to an empty half hour.

Eve left her last message while preparing for bed.

“I'm buying a smaller packet of those crackers. They taste less special without the catastrophic opening around them.”

Julian's answer arrived while she was rinsing her cup.

“I suspected the lighting was doing some of the work.”

She laughed aloud.

Dumpling lifted his head from his bed, decided she had no food, and went back to sleep. Eve set the phone to one side without checking whether Julian was still online.

She could hear him again tomorrow. Tonight, at last, she had heard him without giving the entire evening away.
