# Chapter 097 — When He Gets It Wrong

The third card said middle.

Eve would learn that several minutes later. At the time, the card was folded beneath Julian's hand, the tea was finally drinkable, and Dumpling was intensely interested in something neither of them had chosen.

A narrow shadow moved across the rug.

The tree outside was too far from the window to touch the glass. Its fine branches nevertheless supplied a shifting pattern whenever the breeze moved them. Eve had lived with that pattern long enough not to notice it. Dumpling apparently considered Sunday an excellent day to reopen the investigation.

He crouched behind the right-hand cup.

Eve held the pen ready. Beside her, Julian sat very still.

The cat's hindquarters gave a small, concentrated wriggle. He sprang past the plastic, landed on the edge of a dark shape, and looked faintly offended when the shape moved underneath his paw.

Julian made a sound he tried to turn into a cough.

Eve lowered the pen.

“Don't encourage the research subject.”

“I wasn't aware we still had one.”

She laughed first, which freed him to laugh properly. Dumpling looked over his shoulder at the noise, then returned to the much more consequential matter of the rug.

Eve wrote: crossed between cups, no contact, pounced on moving shadow.

“Was there anything else?” she asked.

“He ignored my entire contribution.”

“That isn't the observational language we're aiming for.”

Julian unfolded the card. Middle, written with a firm downward stroke that suggested an entirely reasonable expectation of being understood by stationery.

The middle cup had remained untouched.

Eve looked from the word to Dumpling. There was a neat little escape available. The shadow had distracted him. Perhaps the thought hadn't been strong enough. Perhaps he had known the answer but chosen not to cooperate. Any of those possibilities could keep the first two contacts shining while putting this one in a separate drawer.

She wrote the chosen position beside the description.

Then she put down the pen.

“That's the last one.”

“Yes.”

Julian leaned back. His shoulders looked less carefully arranged now that he wasn't trying to provide an unambiguous thought to an animal.

“Do you feel disappointed?” he asked.

“A little.”

“Because he got it wrong?”

“Because I was already imagining what three in a row would feel like. Which is not the same thing.”

Eve picked up the nearest cup. Dumpling's head turned at the movement, and she stopped until he had returned his attention to the window. There was no need to turn packing away into another invitation.

“I could explain this one out of the record very easily,” she said. “There are so many explanations available when I don't like what happened.”

“There were explanations for the first two as well.”

“I know.”

He wasn't scoring a point. That made it easier to hear.

She gathered all three cups and carried them to the kitchen. For the first time since she had retrieved them, they looked like objects she might actually use for something other than wondering whether the world had a hidden door.

Julian followed with the empty biscuit tin.

“That was ambitious,” he said, looking inside.

“I forgot it was nearly empty.”

“There's one corner left.”

“An exceptionally modest second course.”

He offered it to her. She took the broken corner and ate it over the sink, catching the crumbs with her hand. The result required no interpretation: she needed to put biscuits on the grocery list.

The plastic cups still smelled faintly of washing-up liquid. Eve rinsed them again, not to improve a future test, but because clips didn't need to smell of lemon either. She set them on the rack and returned to the sitting room for the paper.

Dumpling had abandoned the shadow. He now sat beneath the chair with one forepaw laid across his toy.

“You've retired?” Eve asked.

He blinked slowly. She didn't pretend this answered her.

On the sheet, her first description ran into the second because she had left too little room. The failed third attempt occupied the largest space. Looking at it, Eve remembered how tempted she had been to start with a clean version afterward, each event reduced to a tidy line.

A tidy line would lose the shoulder bump. It might lose the glance toward the window. It would certainly lose how badly she had wanted a particular kind of afternoon.

She added one final sentence: both humans knew the arrangement and could have given cues; cups differed; too few observations to establish an explanation.

Then she dated the paper and folded it once.

“Where will that go?” Julian asked.

“In the notebook, with the other observations. Not on the account.”

“Would you like to keep it?”

“Yes. I don't want to throw away the part that didn't please me.”

He nodded. “That makes sense.”

She put it in the drawer. For a moment her fingers rested on the old notebook's cover. There were pages inside she still needed to review for careless claims about other people. That work belonged to her; it didn't require Julian to sit beside her and approve each sentence.

She closed the drawer without beginning it now.

“I'm going to put the clips back when the cups are dry,” she said.

“I support their return to employment.”

“The clips or the cups?”

“Whichever is currently underused.”

Eve sat beside him again, this time closer than before. Their knees were not touching. She could feel the space and choose whether to do something about it rather than regard it as a verdict.

“Can I sit against you?”

“Yes.”

She moved until her shoulder met his arm. His arm was warm through his sleeve. Eve settled comfortably against it, letting her own shoulders drop.

After a while Julian said, “I was thinking about the middle one so hard I stopped noticing the cat.”

“That might be the first time anyone has made him the less demanding participant.”

“I was trying not to move my eyes.”

“Did it work?”

“I have no idea.”

She laughed against his sleeve. The laughter felt different from the sharp relief of a successful result. It didn't need the cat to become impressive again to remain available.

Outside, somebody called up to a neighboring window. A reply came down, muffled by glass. Dumpling's ears turned toward the ordinary sound. He did not carry either human a message.

Eve looked at the empty patch of rug.

“I used to think that if I understood what he was doing, I'd stop making mistakes about people.”

Julian was quiet for a moment.

“Would that have been an improvement?”

“Making fewer mistakes?”

“Never having to ask somebody?”

She considered it. The imagined skill had always arrived in her mind as safety: the warning she wouldn't miss, the chance to avoid making a fool of herself, the certainty that someone wanted her present before she risked staying.

It had contained very little room for another person changing their mind.

“I think I'd have become very difficult to talk to,” she said.

“You can already be determined.”

She lifted her head. “That was delicately phrased.”

“I have had tea.”

She smiled, but she could take the observation seriously too. She had been determined when she insisted a client needed a more moving ending than the one requested. Determined when she offered Sophie help without asking which part was actually needed. Cleverness wasn't always the thing missing from her mistakes.

Dumpling left his toy and approached the sofa. Julian moved his foot out of the way. The cat sniffed his trouser cuff, stepped over Eve's slipper, and pressed his head briefly against her shin.

Eve lowered her hand. He leaned into a stroke, then walked past her toward the kitchen.

For half a second she wanted to announce what the movement meant: dinner, reassurance, approval of the experiment's end. The possibilities were all hers.

“He's gone to look at a cupboard,” she said instead.

“A popular choice.”

She got up to check the time and the water. Dinner was not due yet, but the bowl had a small floating hair in it. She refreshed the water and returned without bringing food to reward the visit.

Julian was examining the small tear at the edge of her sofa cushion.

“That's old,” she said. “He didn't do it.”

“I was wondering whether the cover came off.”

“It does. Putting it back on is less certain.”

“Then perhaps don't begin while you have company.”

She laughed. “A wise distinction.”

He didn't offer to mend it or diagnose the fabric. Eve liked that he could notice an imperfection without making it his afternoon's new responsibility.

They began talking about the bookshop he had mentioned on their walk. He had finally found the displaced section upstairs, beside a shelf of wildly specific gardening manuals. Eve asked what he had bought. A small book about the history of ordinary tools, he said, and then looked slightly defensive when she smiled.

“I'm not mocking you.”

“You have the expression.”

“I'm imagining a passionate chapter about hinges.”

“There is one.”

“That makes me happier than it should.”

He described an illustration he liked. Eve listened, learning something she would never have put on a list of urgent questions about him. She hadn't known he cared how commonplace objects acquired their shapes. It made the way he looked at a badly designed card seem less like professional severity and more like a person who genuinely wanted things to work.

When he paused, she asked another question. No hidden method was required.

He tried to demonstrate the movement with his fingers, gave up, and asked for a pencil. Eve handed him one and the back of an old envelope. His drawing looked like a frightened insect until he added an arrow.

“Now I understand,” she said.

“The mechanism?”

“Why they hired an illustrator.”

He looked at the envelope, then laughed so unexpectedly that she nearly dropped her water glass. Eve set it down and drew her own version beside his. It was not better. They turned the envelope around, trying to agree which part either of them had meant to be the handle, until the practical explanation had become enjoyable nonsense.

The afternoon light shifted until the shadow left the rug altogether. Dumpling returned to his chair and began the elaborate business of fitting himself into the smallest possible circle.

Eve noticed, then continued listening to Julian's account of a remarkably argumentative can opener.

In the kitchen the cups dried upside down. Soon they would hold clips again. The afternoon had not resolved the strange thing she believed might happen around her cat. It had contained two striking correspondences, an ignored choice, a pause someone asked for, and a conversation she would not have predicted.

When Julian offered to make the next tea, Eve said she would rather have water.

“All right.”

He brought two glasses, asking before setting hers on the low table.

The glass was cool against her palms. Eve drank, then moved aside so he could sit close again.
