# Chapter 202 — It Is Not Canned Food That Is Missing

On Friday morning, Eve received a photograph of twelve tins of cat food and one enormous bag of litter.

The message beneath it said, I have supplies. That isn't the problem.

She looked at the answer she had sent a minute earlier and winced.

A regular customer called Nina had asked whether anyone nearby might be able to help with something while the sessions were cancelled. Eve, thinking about the delayed delivery at the venue, had immediately asked what food she was missing.

She called instead of guessing again.

“Tell me what you actually need,” she said.

Nina laughed once, without much amusement. “I live on my own above three flights of stairs. My friend was going to help me move two boxes down to my storage cupboard. She can't get here today. The plumber's coming tomorrow, and the boxes are in his way.”

“What's in them?”

“Cleaning supplies and the litter. All sealed. I bought too much when it was on offer. I've arranged a very expensive obstacle course.”

Eve could picture it. Nina had attended an instructions exchange and complained that every useful cupboard in her flat was occupied by something she rarely used.

“Are the stairs dry and usable?”

“Yes. It's all inside. I can carry ordinary things. I just don't want to wrestle those boxes down by myself.”

“Would it work to ask someone already in your building?”

“I know the woman next door a little. I don't want her to think I'm asking her to spend the day sorting my flat.”

“What if you asked for those two boxes and said how long you thought it would take?”

Nina was quiet. Then paper rustled near the phone.

“I could put the smaller things away first. It's probably fifteen minutes.”

“Would you like me to help you word the message, or would you rather call her?”

“I'll call. She'd be startled to get a formal application to lift a box.”

“Fair.”

Eve ended the conversation without acquiring Nina's address, cupboard key, or entire Saturday.

Dumpling sat beneath her chair and stared at the open space where her foot usually moved. She had been tapping it throughout the call. She stopped, and he stepped forward to rub against her ankle before continuing to the kitchen.

The apartment was warm and dry. Julian had left for the clinic using the route he had checked before setting out. Eve had two hours set aside for cancellation follow-up, then an unrelated editing job that still needed doing. The weather had interrupted her venue. It had not turned the rest of her working life into empty time.

She put a note beside the laptop: ask first.

Then Mia called.

“Are you doing any kind of drop-in today?”

“No. The venue's closed.”

“I thought maybe, if other people were stuck too...”

Eve waited rather than immediately suggesting a gathering.

Mia explained. Her usual dog walker could not reach their neighborhood because of the transport disruption. The morning visit had been cancelled, and the later one was uncertain. Mia had a remote work presentation she couldn't simply abandon. Her first thought had been that a room full of helpful pet owners might solve the problem.

“How long is the presentation?” Eve asked.

“An hour, then questions.”

“And what parts of the day can you cover?”

“Before it. After it. It's the middle that I've made impossible in my head.”

Eve heard a dog collar jingle near the phone. Mia said something softly away from the receiver, then returned.

“My walker offered tomorrow if the route's running, but I don't know whether to wait.”

“You can change today's arrangement without deciding the whole weekend. Is there someone nearby your dog already knows?”

“There is the neighbor who helped before. But she said she couldn't be the regular backup.”

“Then ask whether this particular visit works. She can still say no.”

Mia exhaled. “I keep wanting one person who can always fix the gap.”

Eve looked at the time reserved for her editing work.

“I understand the appeal.”

She didn't offer herself instead.

They talked through the actual gap. Mia could shift a nonessential meeting, take the dog out before the presentation, and ask the familiar neighbor about one short visit afterward. If the neighbor couldn't do it, Mia would need to use a properly arranged paid alternative or change the work commitment further. The venue had no staffing or facilities to become an unplanned daycare.

“I'll call her,” Mia said. “And the walker, so she isn't still trying to solve today for me.”

“That sounds useful.”

Afterward, Eve opened a blank message to the customers who had asked about the cancellation. She almost wrote that they were coordinating neighborhood assistance.

The phrase looked larger than anything she could actually provide.

She deleted it.

Instead, she replied individually to the two people whose messages contained unfinished practical questions. She could help clarify a task or connect someone with a willing contact they already knew. She couldn't collect animals, promise transport, or keep a room open indefinitely.

One customer only wanted confirmation that the refund would return to the original payment method. Eve checked and answered. No neighborhood network was required.

The other had assumed the cancelled event meant a scheduled home-care visit arranged with a different provider was also cancelled. Eve told them to contact that provider directly and gave them no prediction about its answer.

By eleven, the rain eased to a fine, persistent mist.

Sophie called while Eve was putting the kettle on.

“I saw your notice. Rough weekend?”

“Financially unattractive. How's your event?”

“Moved indoors yesterday. We lost a scenic backdrop and gained an argument about floor tape.”

“Did you win?”

“I achieved tape.”

Eve smiled and reached for the blue cup.

Sophie had a short break before a production call. She asked whether Eve needed anything specific, and Eve found herself ready with a vast answer involving deliveries, refunds, visitors, and the general unfairness of water.

“Could you tell me whether this message sounds as though I'm offering a service I can't run?” she asked instead.

She read the two sentences she had drafted for direct replies.

“The first is fine,” Sophie said. “The second says you'll help people find cover. Will you?”

“I can help them think of someone to ask. I can't guarantee anyone.”

“Then say that.”

Eve changed the sentence.

“Thank you. That's all.”

“Beautiful words. I have to go and defend the tape.”

“Good luck.”

Eve sat down to her editing job. The client needed captions for a set of storage photographs. Two images had been numbered in the wrong order, making a drawer seem to contain things that appeared in a different cupboard. Eve noticed because a red handle changed sides between frames. She checked the reference sheet, corrected the sequence, and sent a short question about one remaining label.

The client replied with the missing detail. No one mentioned the rain. Eve found the ordinary exchange unexpectedly steadying. She still knew how to do this work, and somebody still needed it done accurately, even while the venue's weekend income was disappearing.

She worked for forty minutes before Nina replied with a photograph of an empty stretch of hallway.

The woman next door had not been able to lift the boxes. She had suggested asking the building caretaker, who was already on site and could help later within his normal paid work. Nina had confirmed the charge herself. The supplies were now downstairs in her own locked storage cupboard, and she had cleared the remaining space for the plumber.

I was embarrassed about needing a whole rescue, she wrote. Apparently I needed one man with fifteen minutes.

Eve replied that she was glad it was sorted. She did not ask for a second photograph with the helper in it.

At lunchtime, Mabel called to check whether the porch at the venue was closed too.

“Yes,” Eve said. “I'm not there, and I don't want anyone expecting seating or staff.”

“Good. I was telling someone that. They thought you'd be handing out soup.”

“Why?”

“Because people like the idea of soup.”

Eve looked at her own uneaten lunch and could hardly dispute it.

“Biscuit all right?”

“Annoyed by the weather. Perfectly capable of expressing it.”

Mabel explained that a friend who usually visited on Fridays had changed to Sunday rather than attempt the bus journey. She had arranged a short check-in with a neighbor that evening because she liked company, not because she needed Eve to turn up carrying groceries.

“I've got food,” she added, as though the whole neighborhood shared Nina's grievance.

“I'm beginning to notice a pattern.”

“Ask people before buying tins.”

“I've written that down.”

“Good. Now eat something.”

Eve ate after the call, not because Mabel had diagnosed her through a telephone but because the sandwich was beside her elbow and lunchtime had arrived.

Mia messaged in the early afternoon. The familiar neighbor could manage one short visit after the presentation, but not the evening. Mia had accepted that specific offer, rearranged her later work call, and confirmed tomorrow separately with the paid walker. She would handle the evening herself.

The solution contained three pieces instead of one endlessly available person. Eve found that reassuring in a way she wouldn't have a year earlier.

She marked both questions complete and returned to the client captions.

A new message arrived asking whether people could donate spare pet food at the venue. Eve thanked the sender and explained that they were not collecting supplies. She had no storage plan, no confirmed recipients, and no reason to make somebody carry a bag through rain so she could feel useful beside it.

After sending the reply, she put the phone face down for the rest of the editing hour.

The work went slowly. She still checked the window too often. But by late afternoon she had delivered the revised captions, completed the remaining refund follow-up, and not left the apartment to invent an errand.

When Julian returned, he found her kneeling beside the cupboard with a tin in each hand.

“Should I ask?”

“I'm checking our own supplies. Apparently that's where I have authority.”

He hung up his coat and came to see. She was moving Dumpling's food into a single visible row rather than leaving half behind the spare mugs.

“Do we need more?”

“No. We need to stop hiding it from ourselves.”

Dumpling approached, examined the unopened tins, and sat down with the patient intensity of someone who disliked administrative delays around dinner.

Eve fed him at his usual time. Julian began the human meal. She told him about Nina's cleared hallway and Mia's rearranged visits while she washed the peppers.

“So nobody came to the venue?” he asked.

“Nobody needed to.”

Outside, the rain strengthened again. Eve dried her hands and checked that the closure notice still showed the right dates.

Then she put the phone away. Somewhere nearby, two boxes stood in a better cupboard, a dog had received a familiar visitor, and people were carrying on in separate homes. It was smaller than the busy room she had first imagined.

It had also answered the questions they actually asked.
