# Chapter 161 — Friends Can Recommend Someone Else

Robin arrived carrying a canvas bag, a folding umbrella, and a small rubber chicken.

Eve looked at the chicken for long enough that Robin tucked it behind the umbrella.

“Emergency equipment,” they said.

“For which emergency?”

“The kind where a microphone stops working and a hundred people are looking at me.”

“We don't have a microphone.”

“Then I've already solved one problem.”

She laughed. Relief made her laugh a little harder than the joke deserved. In her head, Sophie's recommendation had become a person who could walk through the door and understand everything Sophie understood, without requiring any of the years in between.

Robin had a pleasant, carrying voice and shoes sensible enough for a long shift. They had read the brief. They knew this was a small, invited rehearsal, that nobody was bringing an animal, and that the premises were still being prepared. They asked where to leave wet things before their umbrella could drip on the floor.

All this ought to have settled Eve.

Instead she watched them arrange the chairs in two straight rows and felt a small, unreasonable loyalty to Sophie's untidy semicircles.

“We normally do them a little more like this.” She moved one chair sideways.

“Of course. How many today?”

“Six invited. Four confirmed.”

“Cozy.”

Robin said it confidently. Eve heard the word from the other side of an invoice.

They had agreed a paid preparation meeting and a short rehearsal, with setup and clearing included. It was a trial for both of them, not a promise of every future booking. Eve had written that plainly. She still found herself wanting Robin to love the place before they had taken off their coat.

Sophie had sent the introduction four days earlier, after Eve asked whether she could host. Her reply had included her own work dates and Robin's contact information, followed by: Ask them what they need. I am not a warranty.

Eve had replied with a laughing face. This morning she had almost sent a photograph of the chairs for Sophie to judge.

She put her phone in her pocket.

The rehearsal was meant to test a short owners' experience session. No expert talk, no filming, no pets to distract everyone from the people who had actually booked. Guests would try an opening activity, share one small household frustration, and tell Eve which parts felt useful or awkward. Nobody had paid to attend; their invitation explained the feedback purpose.

Robin took out a stack of colored cards.

“I usually start with something lively. Favorite pet disaster, thirty seconds each. Breaks the ice.”

“Could we say inconvenience?” Eve asked.

“Less dramatic. Absolutely.”

She should have asked them to demonstrate. Instead she checked the entrance for the third time and became absorbed in a stubborn corner of tape.

The first guest was a woman with a bicycle helmet clipped to her bag. The second came directly from work and asked whether he could keep his coat on. Two neighbors arrived together, apologizing for having almost walked into the wrong doorway. Robin welcomed them all with impressive ease.

Then they clapped their hands once.

“Welcome to the secret society of people whose animals have more followers than they do.”

The woman with the helmet said she didn't use social media.

“An exclusive society,” Robin amended. “Even better.”

The man in the coat gave a courteous smile. Eve had used that smile in meetings where somebody had announced mandatory fun.

“Let's discover who really runs your household,” Robin continued. “Spoiler: probably the one who doesn't pay rent.”

One of the neighbors began, “Actually, we're still deciding whether—”

“Then you're getting in early. Excellent planning!”

The neighbor stopped.

Eve felt herself leaning forward, ready to finish the sentence for her, and forced her shoulders against the wall instead. Robin hadn't been cruel. They had simply reached for a practiced rhythm, one joke carrying them over the tiny gap before the next. In a noisy hall it might have worked beautifully.

Here she could hear somebody's coat lining crackle.

The cards went around. Robin asked everyone to describe their animal in three words. The undecided neighbor passed hers to her friend. The man in the coat said, “Old. Brown. Asleep,” and then looked worried that he hadn't done it properly.

Robin smiled. “We may have the same housemate.”

Nobody laughed this time.

Eve picked up the pitcher of drinking water they had brought for themselves and realized she was inventing a job. She set it down without pouring.

“Could we pause for a minute?” she asked. “This is exactly what the rehearsal is for. I think I gave Robin a brief that describes the room better than the people.”

Robin lowered their cards. Their face remained open, but their fingers squared the stack twice.

She asked the guests whether a short break suited them. One wanted to stretch; another welcomed the chance to check a message. Nobody objected. Eve pointed out the clear route and the chairs they could use, then joined Robin at the preparation table.

“I can change the opener,” Robin said quietly.

“I should have said some people won't have animals. And some won't enjoy performing an affection test in front of strangers.”

“That wasn't the intention.”

“I know.”

Her phone vibrated. For one hopeful second she imagined Sophie offering exactly the right phrase. It was an invoice reminder.

Robin glanced toward the four guests. “I'm used to rooms where someone has paid me to lift the energy. Here I think I've been lifting people out of their chairs against their will.”

Eve laughed, this time because she recognized the room they were both standing in.

“Could we let them talk about something on the table instead of themselves first?”

“What have you got?”

Three blunt pencils, a roll of removable tape, blank paper, and the absurd chicken peering from Robin's bag.

“Not him,” Eve said.

“He's very disappointed.”

They settled on an object that required no loyalty: an ordinary bag clip from Eve's lunch. Robin placed it in the middle of the table and invited guesses about what tiny household annoyance it might solve. Guests could answer, write, or pass. There was no round to complete and nobody would be called upon in turn.

When they returned, Robin did not pretend the first opening had succeeded.

“I brought my large-room voice,” they said. “It turns out this room has perfectly good ears. We're trying something else, if you're willing.”

The man in the coat took his hands out of his pockets.

The bicycle woman said the clip could close the packet that always fell behind her washing machine. Her dog had nothing to do with it. The neighbor who had stopped speaking earlier said she used something similar to mark which shopping bag contained frozen food.

“Otherwise my partner puts the peas in the cupboard.”

“Only once,” her friend said.

The room laughed, including Robin, who didn't add a punch line.

From the clip they moved to objects that made an ordinary routine easier. A hook at the right height. A towel by the door. A bowl stored where its owner could reach it without shifting six other things. The man in the coat described the drawer that stuck every morning, and how he kept blaming the dog for standing in front of it.

“The dog moves,” he said. “The drawer remains terrible.”

This time he looked pleased with his own joke.

Eve fetched blank paper when someone wanted to sketch a cupboard. She didn't turn the conversation into a lesson. The undecided neighbor eventually explained that she and her friend were considering sharing responsibility for a pet and had realized they disagreed about almost every daily arrangement. Robin asked what they hoped to learn before making the decision, rather than congratulating them on a future adoption.

By the end, there were three drawings and no completed colored cards.

They finished at the time they had promised. Robin gave the guests a simple feedback sheet and explained that they could leave it blank. Eve received two written responses. One said the beginning felt like a party she hadn't dressed for. The other said: More time to look at things before talking, please.

Neither asked for the chicken.

After the guests left, Eve and Robin returned the chairs and checked the floor. Robin retrieved a pen from beneath the table and held it up like a recovered artifact.

“First casualty.”

“That one's mine.”

“Then a rescue.”

Their clearing time ended in twelve minutes. Eve wanted another hour of planning immediately, while the failed jokes and the successful silence were still sharp in her mind. She also had client work waiting at home and enough experience now to recognize how easily enthusiasm could become unpaid labor for the person standing nearest.

“Do you have room for another preparation session this week?” she asked. “Paid, separate from today. We can redesign it properly.”

Robin took out their diary instead of saying yes from politeness. They had a school booking and a private event. The first slot they offered was inconvenient for Eve.

She looked at her own calendar and moved a task that did not involve another person's time.

“That works.”

“Before you confirm,” Robin said, “I'd like a clearer sense of what decisions you want me to make in the room. You kept waiting for me, and I kept waiting for you.”

Eve could see herself against the wall, pretending the water pitcher required supervision.

“I thought giving you the room meant staying out of it.”

“Sometimes it means telling me what you can see that I can't.”

They agreed that either could call a rehearsal pause. For the next session, Robin would prepare two openings and try them with Eve first. They would keep the objects, lose the compulsory round, and leave enough time for a quiet person to finish a sentence.

Robin packed the cards. The chicken emerged last.

“Should I put him down as unsuitable equipment?”

“Keep him for the hundred people.”

“He'll be unbearable when he hears that.”

After Robin left, Eve finally sent Sophie a message. She wrote that the first attempt had been awkward, they had found a better format together, and another paid session was booked.

She did not add: Would you have done it differently?

Sophie's reply came while Eve was locking up.

Good. How are they with the clearing time?

Excellent, Eve wrote. Recovered a missing pen.

Promote them immediately.

Eve smiled at the screen, then slipped it away. On her own copy of the next brief, she crossed out one sentence: Host to create a warm atmosphere.

It sounded like asking someone to install sunshine.

Underneath, she wrote three things Robin could actually use: Give people time to look. Let them pass. Ask before rescuing a silence.

Then she took the key out of the lock and went home to finish the work that would pay for their next meeting.
