# Chapter 010 — Pay the Planner, Not the Cat

The first number Eve wrote at the top of her results page was zero.

Zero dogs had gone home with new families at the event.

She looked at it long enough for Dumpling to walk across the bottom of the paper and sit on the corner she wasn't using.

“Thank you,” she told him. “A sensitive editorial contribution.”

He began washing a shoulder.

On her laptop, Button's wet nose filled the preview window. Beyond it were all the quieter things she'd filmed: cards turned over, a volunteer pulling out a chair, people listening instead of posing with animals in their arms.

There was enough material to make a short, useful film.

There wasn't an ending in which everybody waved goodbye.

Eve selected the zero and deleted it.

Then she put it back.

The problem wasn't the number. It was the heading she'd given it: Successes.

She changed that to Same-Day Placements, considered whether the category belonged in her report at all, and opened the event brief. It had never promised that people would take animals home immediately. The agreed purpose was to introduce the animals, explain the process, and help suitable prospective carers begin asking informed questions.

She had invented a finish line because it looked better on a screen.

Dumpling leaned into her wrist when she moved the mouse. She stroked his head once, then shifted the report out from under him before his damp paw could add another result.

Tessa's update arrived that morning. Two households had agreed to continue to the volunteer group's next assessment steps. Several other people had taken information away to consider. Mr. Bennett wanted more time to think about his routine. Nothing had been finalized.

Eve read the message twice, checking that she hadn't turned two households into two adoptions merely by wanting a cleaner sentence.

She asked which summary could be included publicly. Tessa checked with the organizer before confirming the wording. No names, personal circumstances, or implied guarantees.

She also separated the practical notes from the public summary. The sign-in table needed a better place. The chair by the entrance needed to stay. The card photographs could show scale more clearly, and future filming required fewer people reaching for the same strip of tape.

These weren't impressive results for a promotional post. They were useful to the people running the next event. Eve sent them privately to Tessa, without attaching anyone's personal story, and received a grateful request to keep the chair in the reference photograph.

Eve returned to the film.

The first cut ended on a cheerful shot of Button looking up. It was charming. It also made the final caption feel as though he had just found his person.

She moved him earlier.

The ending became an information card resting beside the empty chair, with a clear way to contact the volunteer group. It wasn't an ending that told people to cry. It told them what to do next.

She watched it without music, then with a restrained track she had permission to use. The words were readable. The people who had declined filming were absent. No private forms could be seen in the background.

One frame caught a visitor's phone screen reflecting in a brochure holder. Eve cut around it. She was briefly annoyed at the lost second, then more annoyed with herself for wanting to keep it.

By late afternoon, the approved corrections were complete. She exported the final files, checked them from beginning to end rather than trusting the progress bar, and sent the agreed versions with their filenames plainly labeled.

There was no bonus montage, no surprise second film, no attachment called Just Another Idea.

She made tea while she waited.

This time she only washed the cup once.

The reply arrived before she'd finished drinking. Tessa confirmed the delivery was accepted and that the clinic would arrange payment as agreed. The volunteer organizer had approved the public wording.

Eve leaned back.

She had expected relief to be more dramatic. Instead, she noticed how stiff her shoulders were and how long she had been sitting on one folded leg.

Her phone showed a message from Sophie asking whether the weekend had swallowed her.

Still here, Eve replied. Delivered. Two households going forward for assessment. No instant happy endings.

Sophie sent back: Did you deliver what they hired you for?

Yes.

Then stop trying to add a wedding to the invoice.

Eve laughed, waking Dumpling, who opened his eyes with the offended expression of a colleague interrupted during an important meeting.

The next afternoon, she went to the clinic to return the sample holders and collect her small folding stand. She took her notebook, a printed delivery summary, and the inexpensive ledger she'd started using for freelance income.

Dumpling stayed home. He had no outstanding role in the transaction, despite his apparent belief that every paper surface belonged to him.

Tessa checked the returned items against her list. One sample holder had a cracked corner. Eve pointed it out before Tessa had to ask.

“I noticed that when I packed it. I can't tell whether it happened during the event.”

“That one's been cracked for weeks.” Tessa turned it over to show a small piece of old tape. “I keep meaning to replace it.”

Eve had already been preparing to offer to buy a new set. She shut that particular shop in her head. If she'd broken something, they could discuss it. She didn't need to pay for every imperfect object in reach simply because someone had hired her. The activity area was back to its ordinary arrangement. Without the signs and cards, it looked almost too plain to have occupied so much of Eve's week.

Dr. Clark came through to thank her for the work.

“The video makes the process clear,” he said. “We've had a few questions from people who didn't attend.”

“Were they looking for information, or expecting to collect a dog?”

“A bit of both. The team is answering them.”

Not a perfect result, then. Not a failed one either.

He pointed out a small correction to the contact hours for future use. Eve noted it on her own copy and checked that he wasn't asking for a different version of the delivered event film. He meant the next information sheet, if there was one.

She didn't rush to volunteer to make it tonight.

When her phone vibrated, she assumed it was another message about a file.

It was the payment notification.

The amount matched the agreed fee, including the additional video work.

For several seconds, she simply looked at it.

Not a fortune. Not a replacement for a salary. It covered a real piece of her month's expenses, and it had arrived because she had done a defined job, not because someone felt sorry for her or owed her a rescue.

“Received,” she said, a little too loudly.

Tessa looked up. “Good.”

“I'll send the confirmation.”

“You can do it when you're home.”

Eve nodded, then opened her ledger anyway. She wanted to write down the amount while it still felt real.

Her pen made one pale line and died.

“Excellent timing.”

Julian, passing with a folder, held out his black pen. “Try this.”

“You have more faith in office supplies than I do.”

“Only this one.”

She wrote the date, the client, and the amount. Under it, she listed the proof-printing expense she had already kept separate from her fee. The difference mattered. Money moving through her hands wasn't all money she'd earned.

Julian glanced at the event summary, not at the figures in her ledger.

“Wendy said the quiet introductions worked better than crowding everyone around the animals.”

“That was her suggestion. I just stopped putting the chair in the doorway.”

“You moved it when it didn't work.”

Eve almost credited the cat. The impulse was absurd; Dumpling had spent most of the event asleep at home. Still, a joke would let her dodge the pleasure of being noticed for something she'd done.

Instead, she said, “I did.”

He nodded as if that were a complete answer.

From the hallway, someone called his name. He turned, answered that he was coming, and laid the folder on the shelf before leaving. Eve set the pen across her ledger while she finished checking the returned equipment with Tessa.

A few minutes later, she closed the book, gathered her stand and bag, and went home.

It was only when Dumpling interfered with the accounting that she discovered the theft.

She had opened the ledger on the table to send the receipt confirmation. The black pen rolled out from between its pages. Dumpling, sitting on the chair beside hers, put a white paw firmly over it.

Eve stared.

“That isn't mine.”

He looked at her.

“It isn't yours either.”

She reached for it. He lowered his chest over the pen, trapping it against the ledger, with the calm determination of someone protecting a very small inheritance.

Eve could see the end sticking out beneath him. There was a little pale scratch near the clip. The same pen Julian had handed her at the meeting, she thought. Apparently his trust in it was based on an actual working relationship.

She took her hand away rather than making the pen a tug-of-war toy. A familiar toy on the floor eventually persuaded Dumpling to investigate elsewhere. She retrieved the pen and put it inside a zipped pocket.

“You've earned none of this money,” she informed him. “For the record.”

He rolled onto his side with the toy between his paws.

She wrote a message to Julian.

I've accidentally brought your pen home. I can return it when I drop off the remaining printed copies on Friday. Will leaving it with Tessa be all right?

Then she waited, aware of how easy it would be to add a question she didn't need answered.

His reply came later.

Yes. I'll be there after three, but Tessa can take it if I'm busy. Thank you for rescuing it.

Eve considered telling him that Dumpling had briefly claimed ownership. She could make it sound like the cat was demanding another visit, an amusing little excuse that meant nobody had to take responsibility for anything.

Instead, she wrote: Friday after three. I'll leave it at the desk if you're occupied.

A perfectly ordinary arrangement.

She put it in her calendar beside the delivery reminder. No heart, no cat face, no invented emergency.

Then she sent the payment confirmation and photographed the completed ledger page for her own records, careful not to include any client details in the update she forwarded to Sophie.

First job paid.

This time, she allowed the sentence to stand without making it smaller.

Across the room, Dumpling had abandoned the toy and was staring at the zipped pocket containing the pen.

“You'll have to make an appointment,” Eve told him.

He blinked slowly.

She couldn't tell whether he approved of the arrangement.

For once, the appointment remained in her calendar anyway.
