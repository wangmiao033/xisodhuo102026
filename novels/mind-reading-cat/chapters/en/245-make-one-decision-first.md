# Chapter 245 — Make One Decision First

Several months passed. By the time the market was selling strawberries again, the bottom drawer had begun to stick.

Eve could still open it by putting her foot against the cabinet and pulling. She had done it discreetly for weeks, as if the cabinet might tell Julian she was responsible for the loose runner. On Thursday she pulled too hard. The drawer came completely out, scattering walking maps, spare keys and a folded piece of paper across the floor.

Dumpling, asleep in his box beside the bookcase, opened one eye at the noise. Eve collected all the small things before he could reconsider its significance.

The folded paper was theirs. She recognized the crease, more elaborate on one side because she had first folded it while Julian was still reading what she'd written. It was from the evening of the puppet show. She found the relevant sentence halfway down. Both want to keep the possibility open. Not trying now.

Eve sat on the heel of one foot.

She remembered the fox's head coming off. She remembered asking Julian what frightened him and finding that some of his fears resembled hers without quite matching. She could still put the paper back. What they had written had been true, and there was no requirement for a thought in a drawer to develop into anything.

She laid it on the table instead, where the keys wouldn't fall on it again.

Julian came home carrying strawberries and an armful of papers from the clinic. He put both on the kitchen counter, considered the leaking strawberry punnet, and moved the papers.

“I have bad news about the drawer,” Eve said.

“I can see all of it from here.”

“And something else. Do you want to talk about what we wrote after the puppet show?”

He stopped with a strawberry in his hand. It had a great deal of dirt on one side.

“Yes,” he said. “What about it?”

“Whether we still want the same thing. I think I might have a different answer about the now part.”

For a second his expression opened so warmly that she wanted to answer on the strength of it alone. He put the dirty berry down.

“Do you want to talk tonight?”

Eve looked at the table. She had a client call in eleven minutes. There was food to cook, and the drawer needed putting somewhere nobody would walk into it. She would have postponed a less important conversation on those grounds quite easily.

“On Saturday?” she suggested. “After the market, if we don't make any other plans.”

“Let's do that.”

Julian put the folded paper where they would both see it. Then he set about making supper. By the time Eve joined him again, he'd washed the berries and left her a small bowl. He hadn't dug out the household accounts or begun measuring a room.

He did ask if she could pass him the little blue strainer. Eve passed him the large red one because that was what she could reach. He looked at it and used it. She suspected he would be quite good at some of the little things he worried about, though she kept that thought to herself. It wasn't evidence for the other things.

They bought carrots on Saturday. A child with a sun visor put a strawberry down on the edge of a stall, announced that it was too cold and told the fruit seller she could have it back when it was warm. The woman waited for the child's father to retrieve it before she laughed.

Eve and Julian brought their coffee home instead of sitting in the crowded market. They left the carrots by the sink and carried their cups into the sitting room. On the table Julian had put the folded paper and a page from a plain pad. The date in the top corner was the only thing he'd written.

“I didn't want to put things down that you hadn't said,” he explained.

“I've probably got a few things to say that won't look good written down.”

“We can leave those out of the minutes.”

She put her cup close to his, then moved it again so there was room for the paper between them.

“I want to try to have children with you,” she said. “I was going to start with six other sentences so this one wouldn't have to walk into the room alone.”

“Were they good sentences?”

“No. One of them was about the size of the dishwasher.”

He reached for her hand. She gave it to him.

“I want to try too,” he said.

For a little while that was all they needed to hear. Eve looked at the line of his wrist above his sleeve and felt a sudden, almost violent wish to keep this Saturday as it was. It surprised her that she could want change and feel this equally powerful desire not to lose anything. She pressed his knuckles with her thumb.

Then Julian drew a breath.

“I was going to say if you wanted to work less—”

She took her hand away.

He noticed. “I'd better start that again.”

“I do want to hear the end of the sentence.”

“I was going to say I could cover the difference with more hours. Which doesn't work very well, does it?”

“It might make the numbers work. It would put you somewhere else while the rest happened.”

Julian picked up his cup, then put it right back down. “I can see I've started in the wrong column.”

She let herself smile a little at that.

They'd spent a long time getting the current hours to hold. Julian had stopped being the first person called for questions somebody else at the clinic had been assigned to handle. Eve's team had the materials, keys and authority for the sessions they ran. There were still inconvenient days, but they'd got through several of them without one of them abandoning whatever they were doing at the first hint of trouble. The thought of making herself the person automatically available at home brought back a feeling she had once had at work: every doorbell, even when she was in another room, sounded for her.

“Some weeks I might want to do less,” Eve said. “I'd like that to be a choice I can make more than once. I don't want to choose it on a Saturday and discover there's no route back.”

Julian uncapped his pen.

“What if I ask about keeping my hours predictable instead of expanding them? There are parts of my work I can't move. The clinic may have options for the parts I can. I'd need to ask, not put one of them down as a promise.”

Eve nodded. “We could both find out what leave and changes are actually available. And price care assuming we have to pay for it. If someone offers hours they really want to give us, that makes those hours easier. It doesn't have to hold up the whole arrangement.”

“I can find out about care too.”

She turned toward him.

“I mean the questions and calls as well,” he said. “I didn't mean you'd find three places and send me the one you liked best.”

She moved the pad closer and wrote two headings across the page. One was time; the other was money. They had learned enough about their ordinary expenses to know which months depended on one good commission. Julian suggested using a low month for Eve's income instead of a generous average. She suggested leaving the money set aside for the venue alone. They didn't have figures for everything. Several numbers simply remained blank.

Then Eve began to list the people who might help, and stopped halfway through the second name.

“I think I'm writing a rota for people we haven't asked.”

“You've also given your mother Wednesday, which she would immediately trade for a day we hadn't heard of.”

“I'm trying to make it look more possible.”

He put his pen down. “It's possible without making Wednesday her problem.”

They crossed both names out. Julian wrote down who he would need to ask about his hours and when he could make the first calls. Eve went to bring the strawberries in. One had tipped onto its side and released a spot of juice through the paper lining. She rinsed the berry and ate it standing at the counter. It was already warm.

When she returned, Julian was looking at their old note.

“Does saying we want to try mean we have to agree now about how long we'd try?” he asked.

“No,” she said. “I don't know what we'll be asked to decide later. I think I'd like us to be able to say we've had enough of a particular thing without having to explain why we ever said yes.”

He looked down at the pad. “Even if one of us would rather continue.”

“Yes. That might be a hard conversation. We still have to have it.”

She waited. He hadn't moved his face away as he sometimes did when he needed to think, but his eyes had gone to the fold in the old note. Eve put one foot against the leg of the table. From the floor there came a soft bump as Dumpling stretched in his box, found one side closer than he'd expected and settled back against it. For once Eve was grateful the room could contain a sound neither of them was required to interpret.

“All right,” Julian said at last. “We can decide the part we know we're choosing.”

Eve tipped her head toward him. “Can I ask one more thing first?”

He nodded.

“If all we'd got from the last year was the life we have now, would you think we'd done it wrong?”

He picked up a loose strand of thread from the seam of his sleeve, rolled it between two fingers, then tucked it into his pocket to throw away. His hands did that kind of thing now without Eve reminding him that the cat would investigate.

“No,” he said. “I wouldn't want to stop walking with you while I waited for something else to make it a proper life.”

“You do stop quite often to look at bus timetables.”

“And you've never walked off without me.”

“Once.”

“The bus stop had been moved. There were four people looking for it.”

Eve laughed and leaned against him. After a moment he leaned back.

They read the old sentence again. Eve didn't cross it out. She had meant it when she wrote it and could still remember how relieved she'd been that Julian hadn't tried to make the answer larger. On the new page she wrote what they'd said to each other, simply enough that neither would have to explain the wording back to the other person. They wanted to try to have children. They were choosing to begin; anything further they hadn't decided. The questions about work, leave and care stayed on the pad, with a name next to each first step.

“Do we put a date on it?” Julian asked.

“You already have.”

“So I have.”

They looked at the weekend's small pile of loose drawer fittings and then went to get the screwdriver. Eve held the runner while Julian replaced a screw. The first time, they'd put it back a quarter inch too far forward and the drawer stood out an inch from the cabinet. They took it out again. Once it closed properly, Eve returned the maps and the two pieces of paper. She left the notebook on the shelf where it had been. There wasn't a new job for it.

That evening they made carrots with too much garlic. Dumpling emerged when his own dinner was ready and went away when he'd decided the people had nothing better. Julian washed up and Eve put the remaining strawberries somewhere they wouldn't forget them. At the sink, Julian turned around and opened his arms without saying anything. Eve crossed the few steps to him because she wanted to, the way she still did sometimes when there was absolutely nothing to mark.

When they went out the following morning, the early bus was empty enough for them to choose seats. Eve took the window. Above the houses, the sky had cleared so completely that a pale half moon remained visible. She nudged Julian when she saw it. He had already started to look the other way. Then he turned back.
