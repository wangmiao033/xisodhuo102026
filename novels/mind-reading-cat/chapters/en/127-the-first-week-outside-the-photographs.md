# Chapter 127 — The First Week Outside the Photographs

On Toby's third morning, Mia sent a photograph of one folded ear and the corner of a rug.

The caption said, Finally asleep.

Eve was eating toast beside an invoice. She smiled, typed a small heart, and nearly added, You must be so happy.

A second message arrived.

Please don't ring. If anything makes a noise I'm moving to the moon.

Eve deleted her sentence and asked whether Mia wanted anything collected from the shops.

Milk, Mia replied. Mine. Rachel's has the blue dot. This is now legally significant.

They arranged a brief visit that afternoon. Eve had a client call at four and told Mia she would leave by half past three. It felt ungenerous to give friendship an end time until she remembered how often an unspoken end time made her resent the wrong person.

At the flat, Mia opened the door in a sweatshirt with a damp sleeve. Toby stood several feet behind her, watching the gap. Eve waited outside while Mia followed the arrival arrangements they had discussed with Denise. Only then did she step in and put the milk on the counter.

“Do I get a dot?”

“Green. No, orange. Green is Leah.”

“You've created a difficult country.”

Mia laughed, but it stopped almost immediately. There was a folded blanket on the sofa, a bowl in the sink, and a phone charging beside a list of times. The flat was clean in interrupted patches. Someone had begun wiping the table and then abandoned the cloth exactly halfway across.

Toby sniffed Eve's trouser leg. She let him approach without reaching down, and he returned to Mia.

“He's lovely,” Mia said.

“He is.”

“He really is.”

Eve looked at her more carefully.

“Did you sleep?”

“Yes. In installments. Like a terrible sofa purchase.”

The first night, Toby had paced and whined. The second had been better until a delivery in the stairwell. Mia had contacted the group's support number and spoken to Denise. They had reviewed what she was seeing and agreed what to record and when to call again. Nothing about the tiredness, however, could be completed by filling in a box.

Mia took two mugs from the cupboard, put one back, and took it out again.

“You don't need to make tea,” Eve said.

“I want tea. I just can't remember which stage I'm at.”

“Water, apparently.”

The kettle was empty. Mia stared at it, then laughed so abruptly that Toby lifted his head.

“Sorry,” she told him at once.

Eve filled the kettle. It was a small job with a visible ending. She was grateful for it.

While they waited, Mia described the arrival afternoon. After everyone left, Toby had stood by the front door. He had eaten later, rested on the rug, and eventually followed her into the kitchen. She had taken twenty-seven photographs of him sleeping and deleted twenty-six because they were almost identical.

“What was wrong with the last one?” Eve asked.

“Nothing. I'm saving that for when I'm a normal person.”

Toby moved toward the corridor. Mia stood up immediately.

“He may just be moving,” Eve said, then wished she hadn't. She did not know what Mia had been advised to watch for.

“I know. I keep thinking I'm missing something.”

Mia followed at a distance, checked, and came back. Her tea was still too hot to drink. She sat on the edge of the chair as though permission to stay there might expire.

Rachel emerged from her bedroom in slippers, her hair flattened on one side. She greeted Eve quietly and took her blue-dotted milk from the fridge.

“I didn't wake you?” Mia asked.

“The van outside did.”

“Because he barked at it?”

“Because it reversed outside my window for six years.”

Mia attempted a smile. Rachel glanced at the chair and the untouched tea.

“Sit down properly. You're making my knees tired.”

It was almost the old household voice. Eve watched Mia lean back one careful inch.

Rachel had agreed to share the flat with Toby. That had not erased night work, sleep, or the occasional irritation of finding a lead where a shopping bag used to hang. She moved the lead to its new hook and pointed at it.

“Here, please. I caught my sleeve.”

“Sorry.”

“I don't need a confession. I need the hook.”

After Rachel left, Mia looked down at her hands.

“She's been good about everything.”

“She can also ask you to move a lead.”

“I know.”

Eve drank her tea. Toby lay down near Mia's chair with his head turned toward the corridor. Mia looked at him, then at the clock.

“My leave ends on Monday.”

“You had the shorter shifts arranged.”

“Yes. It looks fine on paper.”

“What does it look like today?”

Mia picked at a loose thread on her cuff. “Like I need to be asleep during all of them.”

She had checked in with Nora that morning. The walker was due for another introduction, and Mia was not supposed to begin stretching absences simply because work had a date attached. The plan depended on how Toby actually managed, with further support if necessary.

Eve could hear the gaps between the practical facts. Mia had done the preparation. The preparation had not protected her from being exhausted by the life she wanted.

At three twenty, Eve washed the mugs. Mia started to apologize for wasting the visit.

“I brought milk,” Eve said. “It's in the fridge. A measurable success.”

“Orange dot.”

“Internationally recognized.”

She left on time and made her client call. For the first ten minutes she kept imagining Mia in the chair, listening for the next sound. Then the client explained a genuine problem with an instruction card, and Eve returned to work.

That evening, a cheerful picture appeared on Mia's own account. Toby lay with his chin over her ankle. The caption was only his name and a yellow heart. Eve liked it, then looked again at the clean rectangle of floor inside the frame.

She knew where the damp cloth had been.

Knowing did not entitle her to supply the missing information beneath the photograph.

On Friday, Mia came to Eve's flat for forty minutes while Denise stayed with Toby during an agreed support visit. Mia had requested the break herself, she said, and then repeated the sentence as if testing whether it remained true.

Dumpling approached her bag, sniffed, and withdrew to the other end of the sofa.

“He disapproves,” Mia said.

“He's discovered a smell he didn't order.”

Eve set a plate of toast on the table. Mia sat down without removing her coat. She ate half a slice, stared at the other half, and said, “I miss Tuesday.”

“Last Tuesday?”

“The Tuesday before he came. When I could get up late and be annoyed about nothing.”

Eve kept her hands around her own mug.

“I wanted him so much.”

“You can tell me.”

“That makes it sound like there's something terrible to tell.”

“All right. You can finish your toast and tell me something moderately terrible.”

Mia's mouth twisted. She took off her coat.

She had made a mistake in a work message, she said. Nothing disastrous, but something she normally caught. She had cried because the laundry detergent had run out. She had stood in the kitchen the previous evening and felt angry when Toby followed her, then guilty for being angry, then angry about the guilt.

“He wasn't doing anything wrong.”

“No.”

“I know that. I know he's in a new place. Everyone says the beginning can be hard. It's different when the beginning is in every room.”

Dumpling stepped down from the sofa and went into Eve's bedroom. The doorway remained open. Eve let him go. Whatever strong feeling he might sense, whatever ordinary dog scent offended him, he was not going to settle this conversation for them.

Mia glanced after him. “Even your cat has had enough.”

“He leaves conversations about vacuum cleaners too.”

Mia laughed, then covered her eyes with her hands. Eve pushed the tissue box closer without touching her.

For a moment, an idea arrived with embarrassing clarity: the glossy photograph beside the unslept night, the unspoken truth of adoption, a piece people would actually share. Eve knew how to frame it. She knew the opening line.

She also knew Mia had come to eat toast.

“Do you want me to listen,” she asked, “or help you work out what to say to Nora?”

“Listen first.”

So she listened to the full account of a morning spent searching for a missing work badge. To Mia's embarrassment over a message she had nearly sent Denise at two in the morning. To the fear that everyone who had supported the application would feel cheated if they knew she was not enjoying every minute.

Eve did not tell her that every owner felt the same. She did not know every owner. She could remember crying over money after Dumpling arrived and loving him while wanting him to stop needing things for an hour, but Mia's circumstances were her own.

“I once ate cereal from a measuring jug because washing one bowl felt impossible,” she said instead.

“That's normal for you.”

“Cruel, but fair.”

Mia took another piece of toast.

Before leaving, she asked Eve to sit beside her while she wrote a message to Nora. Eve did not dictate it. Mia typed that the week had been harder than she had admitted, that she needed to discuss the work plan before Monday, and that she was frightened to say she sometimes wished she had waited.

She read the last line aloud.

“Is that awful?”

“It's clear.”

Mia sent it. Her hand remained over the phone until the screen went dark.

Outside, someone closed a car door. Dumpling returned from the bedroom and settled on the warm place Mia had left when she stood to find her coat. She looked at him, then at Eve.

“Efficient.”

“He's very experienced at other people's absences.”

At the door, Mia asked whether Eve had thought about making anything from this.

The honest answer was uncomfortable.

“For a second,” Eve said. “I haven't written it down or told anyone. This visit is yours.”

Mia nodded slowly. “Thank you for saying the first bit.”

After she left, Eve cleared the plate. There was no recording to delete, no brilliant draft to sacrifice. There was simply a choice not to make something, which would leave no visible mark in her work folder.

Julian called at their agreed time. Eve told him she was tired and asked about his day rather than handing him somebody else's difficulty to resolve. They talked for fifteen minutes. He made her laugh about a cupboard door that had defeated three competent adults, and she told him about the community center's missing pen.

When they said goodnight, her phone showed a message from Mia.

Nora's calling tomorrow morning. I told Denise the full version too.

Eve replied that she was glad they had a conversation arranged. Then she put the phone on charge, moved Dumpling's water away from the charging cable, and washed the toast plate while the crumbs still came off easily.
