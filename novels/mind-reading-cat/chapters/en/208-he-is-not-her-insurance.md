# Chapter 208 — He Is Not Her Insurance

On Thursday, the folding display refused to fit through the door in the direction Eve had confidently drawn on paper.

Robin held one end. Eve held the other. Neither of them was carrying its full weight, because they had stopped before lifting it off the low trolley, but both were regarding it with increasing dislike.

“It used to be smaller,” Eve said.

“No. The drawing was more optimistic.”

The large display belonged in the dry front storage area until the rear repairs were finished. They had moved only the light sections themselves. This final frame needed a properly planned move, and the narrow route left no room for improvising around a hand trapped against the wall.

“Back down,” Robin said.

They settled it securely on the trolley and stepped away.

Their booked venue assistant had called that morning to say they were ill. The shift included moving the display and checking the large-event kit, work Eve had hoped to complete before the following week's decisions. Robin's paid hours covered the small sessions and their preparation. Eve had already used part of her own editing block trying to make the room accommodate a piece of furniture that did not care about either schedule.

“I'll find another pair of hands,” she said.

“Is Julian free after work?”

Eve answered automatically.

“He might be.”

Then she heard herself.

Robin was reaching for their phone, presumably to check the shift record or message Eve later. She raised a hand.

“Wait. No. I don't know whether he is free, and that isn't our cover.”

Robin stopped.

“You said he helped move the shelves before.”

“He did. Once. When we'd arranged it.”

“And you mentioned yesterday he wasn't working this evening.”

Eve remembered saying it. She had been pleased about the prospect of eating at a reasonable time, and had spoken as though a gap in his clinic shift were a public resource.

“Yes,” she said. “I made it sound available. That was my mistake.”

Robin put the phone down.

“I wasn't trying to volunteer him.”

“I know. I just nearly did.”

The display remained on the trolley, no less awkward for their improved understanding.

They moved it only far enough to leave the passage clear, using the safe space they had already established. Eve checked that it was stable and couldn't roll, then put a notice on it so nobody would attempt the move alone.

The rear recess was still closed. The building manager had confirmed that morning's check required more drying time before the final surface work. It was not an emergency, and it did not create permission to put things wherever they happened to fit.

Eve sat at the temporary table and called the ordinary moving service recommended by the building manager. They could send two people the following morning for a short paid job, provided she supplied dimensions and photographs so they could assess the move first.

The minimum charge made her wince.

“Even if it only takes twenty minutes?”

“Travel, two staff, and the booking window,” the woman explained. “We can confirm once we've seen the details. If access doesn't work, we won't force it.”

Eve thanked her and asked for the quote in writing.

She measured the frame, the door, and the clear route. Robin held the tape at the far end and checked the figures before she sent them. The photographs showed the actual obstruction rather than a flattering angle.

While waiting for the reply, Eve opened the event calendar.

A practical setup rehearsal for their larger weekend format was scheduled for Friday afternoon. With the repair still incomplete and the move uncertain, it would be foolish to preserve that time simply because people had already written it down.

She called the other assistant first, then the two customers who had agreed to test the revised layout. Neither had paid for the rehearsal; both had arranged their afternoon around it.

One was easy about the change.

The other was not.

“I swapped a shift,” the woman said. “I know you can't control a leak, but this is the second change.”

Eve looked at the calendar. She wanted to defend each previous decision separately until the customer could see how reasonable they had all been.

Instead, she let the woman finish.

“You're right,” Eve said. “I should have kept this provisional until the room work was clearer. I won't give you another date today.”

“So what happens?”

“I'll contact you when the space is confirmed ready. You can decide then whether you still want to take part. You don't owe us another afternoon.”

The woman exhaled.

“All right. But please don't describe me as flexible in the update.”

“I won't.”

After the call, Eve crossed out the rehearsal. The blank square felt expensive.

Robin watched her enter the moving quote when it arrived.

“That comes out of the repair reserve?”

“The part attributable to moving our equipment, yes. I need to keep the contractor's building work separate.”

“And my extra half hour today?”

“Paid. Put it on the record.”

They nodded, then looked at the frame again.

“I suppose Julian really would have been the cheapest option.”

“Only on our page.”

Robin's expression changed.

“Fair.”

Eve didn't expand it into a speech. She had made the same calculation seconds earlier. The useful correction was the confirmed booking, the moved rehearsal, and Robin leaving at the end of their actual shift rather than waiting for someone else's boyfriend to rescue the furniture.

The service replied with a workable plan after reviewing the measurements. The frame would need to be turned and one removable section detached using its ordinary fittings. Their staff would handle the move within the cleared front area, with the rear barrier untouched. Eve confirmed the price and time, then sent the access information.

The cost was real now.

Before accepting the booking, she checked the cancellation terms and the scope of the quote. Moving the frame did not include reassembling the entire event set, hauling damaged materials, or any work in the closed recess. She wrote those limits beside the appointment so tomorrow's version of herself could not turn two paid movers into an unlimited solution.

The woman from the service asked where they could park briefly while unloading their equipment. Eve checked with the building manager rather than assuming the delivery entrance would be clear. One more small call, one more fact established before it became somebody else's inconvenience.

She rearranged her editing block rather than letting the client deadline absorb it silently. There was enough time if she finished the rough cut that evening and reserved a clean hour for the final pass the next morning. She did not offer Julian's evening as compensation for losing her own.

At four, Robin put on their coat.

“Tomorrow's small exchange is still fine?”

“Yes. The moving slot ends well before it. If that changes, I call the participants. We don't run around the movers.”

“Good.”

They left with the empty lunch container they had been searching for all week. Eve watched them check their bag, then lock their phone. It was a modest pleasure to see someone actually finish.

Her own finish took longer.

She had to send a revised rehearsal notice, confirm the replacement assistant's paid time, and explain to a supplier that the large kit check would happen later. None of the messages was dramatic. Together they used the hour she had planned to spend choosing a pleasant photograph for the account.

She left the photograph unchosen.

At home, Julian was reading at the table. Dumpling lay on the nearby chair, one paw stretched over its edge.

“Did the display move?” Julian asked.

“No. People with suitable equipment are coming tomorrow.”

He looked up.

“Did you get hurt?”

“No. We stopped before trying anything stupid.”

“Good.”

She put her bag down.

“I nearly volunteered you.”

He closed the book around a finger.

“Nearly?”

“Robin asked whether you were free. I said maybe, then caught it. I had mentioned your evening off yesterday. It sounded like a staffing option.”

“It's an evening off.”

“Yes.”

He waited, and she resisted making the next sentence a request for credit.

“I booked cover and postponed the rehearsal. It costs more.”

“I imagine it does.”

“I wanted to complain about that without making you feel you ought to offer.”

“You can complain.”

“The minimum charge is painful.”

He nodded solemnly.

“And the frame is badly designed.”

“You liked it when you bought it.”

“It has changed.”

“The frame?”

“My relationship with the frame.”

He smiled. Eve sat opposite him and let herself be annoyed about an ordinary business expense without converting the annoyance into his responsibility.

“I have editing to finish tonight,” she said. “The meal plan is leftovers. Are you okay eating before me?”

“Yes.”

“I'll be about an hour. If it takes longer, I'll tell you.”

“Thank you.”

The exchange was less tender than a spontaneous rescue would have looked in a film. It also left Julian's evening recognizably his.

Eve worked with headphones while he read. Halfway through, she saw him stand and carry the laundry basket toward the bedroom. She did not ask whether that meant he had spare time after all.

The client film concerned an ordinary shop preparing orders. Eve trimmed a sequence of hands folding paper, then watched it without sound. It worked better when she removed a decorative shot she had spent too long capturing. The cut made the process clear. She sent the rough version with the agreed notes and closed the program.

Fifty-eight minutes.

She took her plate from the refrigerator and warmed the food. Julian joined her with tea, having already eaten.

“Tomorrow morning?” he asked.

“Movers, check, editing, small session. In that order. If the first part fails, the later parts get changed rather than piled on top.”

“That sounds plausible.”

“I was hoping for magnificent.”

“Plausible is rarer.”

The next morning, two movers arrived within their booking window. Eve showed them the clear route and the closed area. One checked the frame's fittings while the other measured the doorway again. They confirmed the approach before touching it.

Robin arrived near the end of the work, in time for their own shift. Together they watched the display turn, briefly resemble a defeated insect, and settle into the intended dry storage position.

It fitted.

Of course it fitted. People had measured, planned, and brought what the job required.

Eve paid the agreed amount, received the record, and checked that the passage was still clear. The empty trolley rolled away with a surprisingly small noise.

Robin looked at the calendar.

“Do you want to put the rehearsal back?”

Eve considered the newly available floor. She could almost feel the temptation to retrieve every cancelled thing at once.

“No. We keep the time we already gave back. The room still has work outstanding.”

She opened the client file instead.

Behind her, Robin laid out the small session's materials without needing a rescue, a favor, or a veterinarian.
