# Chapter 185 — Only One Supermarket

Julian had bought the same disappointing sandwich three times because the shop was on the way back from class.

Eve discovered this in front of a refrigerated display, where he was considering buying it for a fourth.

“You described the bread as damp cardboard.”

“I know how long it takes.”

“So does a banana. It has more ambition.”

He put the sandwich down.

The supermarket was neither charming nor conveniently empty. Someone was stacking crates of oranges beside the entrance. A child in a yellow coat was explaining, with great seriousness, why a family needed six lemons. Eve stood beside Julian with a basket hooked over her wrist and felt the odd happiness of having nowhere more impressive to be.

They had a few hours before her train. Her equipment was safely in the basket for the moment, taking up space where a large romantic picnic might otherwise have gone. Julian's temporary room had a kettle and access to a shared kitchen. His culinary plans had apparently stopped at those facts.

“Do you have a shelf in the fridge?” she asked.

“A section.”

“Large enough for food, or theoretical?”

“Some food.”

They bought enough for the next couple of days, not a supply intended to prove she could organize his whole stay. Bread, eggs, tomatoes, a little cheese, fruit. He chose soup he could heat in the shared kitchen. Eve selected a packet of biscuits and put it back when he said he genuinely disliked that kind.

“Useful information,” she said.

He looked at her, then grinned.

“No decorative curves either.”

“The shelf has taught us a great deal.”

They chose different biscuits, the cheap ones they both liked. At the end of the aisle Julian reached for a large bottle of laundry liquid, hesitated, and read the label. Eve checked the small bottle beside it.

“How many weeks are you planning to stay?”

“Three.”

“This one could see you through a change of profession.”

He chose the smaller bottle. They spent longer comparing laundry instructions than selecting lunch. It was an excellent use of their limited time, Eve decided, in the sense that it was exactly what they were doing and neither of them wanted to stop.

At the checkout, she began placing her own things separately. Julian paid for his groceries and the laundry liquid. She paid for her drink and the biscuits she had offered to buy. The division was so unremarkable that they managed most of a conversation about the lemon child while doing it.

Outside, the handles of the reusable bag dug into his fingers. Eve took the lighter one. He still carried her camera equipment, which made the arrangement look unequal until she remembered what the lenses weighed.

His accommodation was a short walk away. At the entrance he checked the visitor procedure, signed her in where required, and took her upstairs. Eve had seen a carefully chosen portion of the room on video: pale wall, desk lamp, the corner of a curtain.

The rest contained a drying towel, two mugs, and a suitcase that had not completed its migration into the wardrobe.

“It's larger than I thought,” she said.

“That's the chair I've been hiding.”

The chair was covered in clean clothes.

“Very professional framing.”

“Thank you. I've watched an expert.”

He moved the clothes so she could sit and put her equipment on the desk, away from the edge. She backed up one final set of files while he took the groceries downstairs to the shared kitchen. A few minutes later he returned with the empty bag and reported that another resident had helped him find a container for the tomatoes.

“This is a very exciting visit,” he said.

“I've seen a new fridge.”

“Not everyone gets access.”

She closed the laptop and let him kiss her properly this time. His hand warmed the back of her neck. For a minute she did not need a route, a timetable, or a better description of what they were doing. The unfamiliar room became simply the room they were in.

Then a timer sounded on his phone.

They both looked at it.

“Laundry slot,” he said.

Eve rested her forehead against his shoulder and laughed.

“Your timing remains extraordinary.”

The laundry room was downstairs. Two machines stood beneath a notice asking residents to remove their washing promptly. A narrow folding table occupied one wall, and a single abandoned sock lay on the windowsill as though waiting to be claimed by an embassy.

Julian carried his laundry bag. Eve carried the small bottle he had nearly replaced with a lifetime commitment. They checked pockets together before he loaded the machine. She found a receipt, he found a pen, and both of them stared at a folded piece of paper that had survived in a shirt pocket.

“Course notes?” she asked.

“Directions to the supermarket.”

“Keep them. It's a difficult emotional journey.”

He flattened the paper and put it safely away. She read the machine settings while he measured the liquid. The lid on the bottle was slippery and took both of them to close without spilling.

Once the machine started, they went back upstairs and made lunch.

There was a little space at the shared kitchen table. Julian checked that they weren't taking a place someone needed, then sliced tomatoes while Eve cut the bread. He passed her the end piece because he remembered that she liked it toasted. They ate without toasting anything, too hungry to improve the arrangement.

She told him about Robin's dry reply to her final check of the session materials. He told her about getting off at the wrong stop on the first morning and arriving early only because he had been excessively cautious about the journey.

“I considered leaving that out of the safe-arrival report,” he said.

“You did leave it out.”

“I arrived safely at both stops.”

She nudged his foot beneath the table.

Her phone showed the carer's update from the second visit window, which had begun while they were shopping. Dumpling had eaten and was resting near his usual blanket. The attached photograph showed most of the blanket and one indignant ear.

Julian leaned closer when she offered him the screen.

“Excellent ear.”

“He's charging separately for the face.”

She thanked the carer and confirmed that her return was still on schedule. Then she put the phone away. Dumpling was not performing happiness for her, but the person looking after him had done what they agreed, and his ordinary needs were being met.

Robin's closing message arrived a little later. The session had finished, one guest had left an umbrella, and the till and materials had been checked. Eve replied with thanks and asked them to leave the umbrella in the usual place. She did not request a photograph of every chair.

“Everything all right?” Julian asked.

“Yes. Apparently the venue can survive my being in another supermarket.”

“Only one.”

“Let's not get ambitious.”

They washed their dishes and returned for the laundry before the cycle finished. Waiting downstairs, Eve sat on the narrow bench while Julian leaned against the opposite wall. The machine turned with a slow, damp rhythm.

She had imagined so many versions of seeing him: a restaurant where nobody hurried them, a walk with perfect light, a room she could afford until morning. The actual visit included a discarded receipt and the smell of detergent.

She wanted another hour of it.

“When do you hear about next weekend?” she asked.

“The rota should be settled early in the week. I may be able to come back after the Saturday session.”

“For the night?”

“If the times work. I'd need to be here again on Sunday evening.”

He took out his phone and showed her the train he had been considering. The fare was manageable with the option he had found, but he had not booked it. The course still had another week after that; a visit home would not be the end of it.

“I don't want you saving an entire weekend,” he said.

“I have work anyway. Tell me when you know.”

He took a screenshot of the possible journey and sent it to her. She saved it with the words *if the rota allows*, exactly as he had said.

The picture was small and unromantic: stations, times, an ordinary price. She liked having something concrete to consider without pretending that a search result was a promise.

The machine clicked open. They transferred what could be dried to the dryer and laid the remaining things where Julian could hang them later. Eve untangled a sleeve while he read a care label that had faded almost beyond usefulness.

“This shirt has been through a lot,” she said.

“Mostly my interpretation of the symbols.”

The dryer left one towel damp at the corners. Julian shook it out, checked it, and decided to hang it rather than buy another whole cycle. Eve folded a T-shirt into a square much smaller than his usual method produced.

“That won't fit with the others,” he said.

“Your shirts have agreed dimensions?”

“Only by habit.”

She unfolded it and watched him demonstrate. His version was less neat but easier to lift from the shallow drawer without disturbing everything else. She copied it for the next shirt, then handed him a pair of socks whose partnership she could not establish.

“Those aren't a pair,” he said.

“They both seem committed.”

He found their actual partners in the bottom of the bag. She kept one of the folded shirts on her lap for a moment, warm from the machine, while he finished sorting. The clock on the wall continued moving. Neither of them argued with it.

By the time the dry clothes were folded, they had to leave for the station. Eve packed her equipment, checked beneath the desk, and made one last visit to the shared kitchen to retrieve her water bottle. Julian put the clean clothes away instead of rebuilding the chair. It took less than a minute.

At the door she looked back. The room seemed more familiar, though almost nothing in it belonged to her.

She did not leave anything deliberately as a claim.

They walked to the station with enough time to avoid rushing. Near the entrance he stopped and adjusted the twisted strap on her bag. She could have done it herself. She let him, then kissed him with a hand curled in the front of his coat.

“I'm glad I came,” she said.

“So am I.”

“Even without the spectacular lunch.”

“The tomatoes were quite good.”

They stood together until the platform number appeared. At the barrier she turned back once. He was still watching, one hand lifted, his other hand empty now that he had returned her bag.

On the train, Eve found her seat and sent the promised message that she was aboard. She checked the screenshot again, then closed it. Next weekend would become definite when the missing information arrived.

For now she had work safely backed up, a cat waiting at home, and the taste of a perfectly ordinary lunch still with her.

She leaned her head against the seat, tired enough not to improve the afternoon in her imagination.
