# Chapter 172 — The Earliest Complaint

The first person to complain arrived before the first person to pay.

Eve had just turned the sign when a woman in a bright scarf came through the door with a friend. Both stopped beside the table, looked around the room, and then looked behind Eve.

“Where are they?” the woman asked.

“The activities start in twenty minutes. You're welcome to look at the information while—”

“The cats.”

Eve felt the welcome she had rehearsed leave her face.

“There aren't cats for visitors to meet. This is an owners' community space, not a cat cafe.”

The friend lowered her phone. She had not begun recording, but she had been holding it in the expectant way people did when an attractive animal might appear.

“Oh,” she said.

The woman in the scarf frowned at the sign through the glass. “It says paws.”

“It does.”

“And there's a cat.”

“A drawing. I'm sorry the name gave you a different impression.”

Behind Eve, Robin placed the remaining pencils on the activity table without joining the conversation. They had already agreed who would welcome visitors and who would handle the first session. Eve appreciated not being rescued, although she would have appreciated rescue rather more at that precise moment.

The woman took out her phone and found a shared image. Eve recognized an older account photograph of Dumpling beside a box, followed by someone else's caption about the new place. It was not the current opening notice, which described the activities and stated that there were no resident animals available for petting.

She could explain that. She could also hear how it might sound: You looked at the wrong thing, so your disappointment is your own fault.

Instead she asked whether they had booked a session.

“No. We thought we'd look in and get something to drink.”

“We don't serve drinks either.”

The woman gave a short laugh with no amusement in it. “What do you do?”

The question hurt more because Eve had spent months learning to answer it.

“Small activities for people who live with animals or are preparing to. Today there's an exchange about making everyday care instructions clearer. You don't need to bring a pet. There's also a small supplies display.”

“That sounds like work.”

“It may not be what you want today.”

The friend glanced toward the door. “We can go somewhere else.”

“Is the cat here at all?” the woman asked.

Eve's eyes almost moved toward the nonpublic room.

Dumpling had arrived with her before opening. She had settled him with his own blanket, water, and familiar hiding place, checked that he could rest comfortably, and closed the door. The room was outside the guest route. Robin knew not to admit anyone. Eve's care checks were on her own schedule, not announced as a chance for visitors to see him.

“He isn't part of the opening,” Eve said. “I won't bring him out for visitors.”

“Even for a minute?”

“No.”

The word was harder without a long explanation behind it. Eve could feel several possible speeches crowding her mouth: his history, the noise, the difference between loving animals and expecting access to them. None would give these women the outing they had come for.

The woman put her phone away.

“Well, you should make it clearer. We came out of our way.”

“I'll review what people are seeing. I'm sorry you had a wasted journey.”

They left. The friend nodded on the way out; the woman didn't. Eve watched them cross the pavement and turn away from the window.

Robin waited until the door had closed.

“Do you need a minute?”

“No.”

They looked at her.

“Yes. A small one.”

Eve went to the preparation table and drank water. Her hands were steady, which seemed unfair. Something inside her ought to have been allowed to rattle visibly after that conversation.

The room had not even filled once, and already someone thought the idea was disappointing.

She opened the account on her phone. The pinned opening notice was correct. The booking page was correct. The older photograph was still there, because Dumpling had been part of the account long before the venue existed. She wasn't going to erase him from the account's ordinary life to prevent every possible misunderstanding.

But the short profile line beneath the account name still said a place for people and paws. Warm, compact, and sufficiently vague to mean almost anything.

Eve changed it to describe small owners' activities and nonmedical experience exchanges. She added that the space was not a cat cafe. The sentence looked less charming. It did a job the charming sentence had avoided.

Then she put the phone down.

Robin was arranging the demonstration supplies: empty containers, blank labels, and a fictional household card with no real address or access information. They had learned enough during the trials not to ask people to expose private routines merely to participate in a game.

“Can I look at that one?” Eve asked.

Robin held up the card.

“The cupboard is called the cupboard,” Eve read. “Very daring.”

“I was tempted by the third kingdom beyond the washing machine, but we're aiming for beginners.”

She laughed. The laugh brought her back into the room more effectively than a reassurance about how well the day would go.

The next visitor had booked. He gave his name, checked that he was in the right session, and asked where to leave his wet umbrella. Eve showed him the place they had prepared. He thanked her and sat down.

No performance was required from either of them.

More people arrived in twos and ones. A returning participant from the trial recognized Robin and asked whether the rubber chicken would be attending. Robin said it had accepted a larger engagement. Eve could hear the first cautious laughter of the morning as she checked the booking list.

The opening was modest enough that every arrival seemed to change the entire room. Three guests made it look sparse. Five made it feel promising. One person standing with a coat and bag made it look too full until Eve showed them where to put both.

A young man came in midway through arrivals and asked whether he could bring his cat the following week.

“Only if a particular activity says animals are included and the arrangements suit them,” Eve said. “Most of these sessions are for people only.”

He read the display, then said, “Good. Mine hates the carrier. I thought I'd be failing the assignment.”

Eve smiled so suddenly that he looked surprised.

“No animal homework,” she assured him.

When Robin began, Eve stayed near the entrance with the remaining information sheets. She could hear the exercise moving around the table: where would a stranger look first, which words needed a picture, what was obvious only because somebody had lived with it for ten years? The laughter had recognizable pauses now. Robin let people finish before deciding whether a joke belonged there.

Eve felt a small, fierce pride that was not entirely her own accomplishment. She had paid someone, trusted them, disagreed with them, and watched them develop a way of working that fitted the room. She didn't have to interrupt to show that she had helped.

Her phone vibrated against the table.

For a moment she expected a review from the woman in the scarf. She imagined one star, a photograph of the patched sign, and a caption announcing that the place contained neither cats nor coffee.

It was a delivery update for next week's supplies.

Eve turned the phone facedown.

She checked on Dumpling during the planned break, closing the outer door to the guest route before opening his room. He was curled on the familiar blanket with one paw laid over his nose. When she crouched, he lifted his head, blinked, and put it down again.

The ordinary sight made her throat tighten.

She could have carried him out. He might have tolerated a minute. He might have been photographed, and the photograph might have pleased someone who otherwise disliked the room. There was no way to know. His peace would still have become something she spent to soften a business problem.

Eve refreshed his water quietly and left him to sleep.

Outside, a guest was asking Robin where they could buy the large labels used in the exercise. Another wanted to know whether a friend without a pet could attend next time. The questions were small and answerable. Eve wrote down the first request for later and answered the second without adding a pitch.

Near the end of the session, the woman in the scarf appeared again at the door.

Eve's stomach tightened before she saw the folded umbrella in the woman's hand.

“Is this ours?” the woman asked, pointing toward the stand. “My friend left hers.”

Eve checked the description with her and handed over the plain black umbrella they had noticed after the women left.

“Thank you.”

“You're welcome.”

The woman glanced into the room. Somebody was holding up a drawing of a cupboard while three people debated which side was the front.

“They seem to be enjoying it,” she said.

“I hope so.”

“It still isn't my thing.”

“That's all right.”

This time Eve meant it without needing the woman to add that it was a wonderful idea for somebody else. She could dislike it, collect an umbrella, and go on with her day. Neither of them had to win.

After she left, Robin brought the session to its close. There was no group photograph to hold people in the doorway. Guests could take away the blank template if they wanted one; completed private notes stayed with their owners. Two people asked about another date. One said the activity had been useful but she probably wouldn't return soon.

Eve thanked all three with the same voice.

A man lingered over the template, folding it carefully rather than pushing it loose into his bag.

“Can I show this to my brother?”

“The blank one, certainly.”

“He thinks all my instructions are overcomplicated.”

“Perhaps ask him to try following them before you settle that.”

The man smiled. “That sounds dangerous for my pride.”

“Mine has survived the morning. Just.”

He looked up, then laughed with her without asking for the story. Eve found she liked not having to supply one. She gave him a second blank sheet and let him leave.

The room began to empty. A chair needed wiping, somebody had left a pencil under a table, and the sign remained patched. The first complaint had not vanished or turned into a glowing testimonial. Eve had made the profile clearer and kept the offer the same.

During the next care check, Dumpling was still asleep.

His ear moved when she opened the door. He did not get up to discover whether his absence had damaged the opening. Eve looked at him for a moment, then closed the door gently and went back to the guests who had come for what she actually offered.
