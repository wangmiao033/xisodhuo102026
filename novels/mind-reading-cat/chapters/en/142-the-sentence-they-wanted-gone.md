# Chapter 142 — The Sentence They Wanted Gone

On the shared screen, the sentence had turned gray.

Sometimes the useful next step is to wait before adopting.

Eve remembered writing it for her later resource post linking to the volunteer group’s Not Adopting Yet film. The original film remained on the group’s page; Mia’s private decision had not appeared in it. At the time, Eve had worried that the sentence sounded disappointing. Now somebody had found a practical use for that concern.

“We recommend archiving this piece for the partnership period,” Lydia said.

The video call showed her in the same upstairs office. A man from the campaign team sat beside her, partly obscured by a plant that looked less healthy than anything sold downstairs.

Eve moved her notebook closer.

“Because the information is out of date?”

“No,” the man said. “The issue is the journey through the account. We bring people in through positive first-pet content, and this sends them back to questioning the decision.”

His name was Martin. He had introduced himself as responsible for campaign performance, and Eve had already written it down so she would not spend the meeting silently calling him the man behind the plant.

“What action are you measuring?” she asked.

“Click-through to the preparation range, then purchase. We're also looking at checklist downloads.”

“So somebody deciding they're not ready would look like a failed visit.”

“Within this campaign, yes.”

Lydia shifted the document slightly. “Nobody's asking you to encourage an unsuitable adoption. We want a clear focus. People who've already decided need help getting started.”

“I can make work for those people.”

“Which is why we're talking,” Lydia said.

Eve looked at the gray sentence. The requested change went beyond the work they were buying. It reached backward into the things that explained why she might be worth hiring.

“Would the post stay available through its existing link?”

“Archived posts wouldn't be public,” Martin said. “We'd also remove the link from your preparation resources.”

“For the whole quarter?”

“Yes. We could review it afterward.”

Eve wrote three months beside the sentence, though she did not need the arithmetic.

The next example was Mia's story.

They had marked the passages about considering a return and the ongoing cost of paid help. A suggested introduction said that planning and support had transformed a difficult start into a happy home.

Eve felt the familiar hot urge to answer too quickly.

She asked Lydia to scroll down instead.

Mia's approved sentence about feeling steadier had survived. Nearly everything that made steadier different from fixed had been removed.

“That story can't be used in the campaign,” Eve said.

“We meant it as an example of the tone,” Lydia replied. “We'd get releases for any reuse.”

“Good. The existing post also stays as Mia approved it. I won't rewrite her account to make the ending more certain.”

Martin glanced toward Lydia. Eve could not know what the glance meant, and the plant provided no useful interpretation.

Lydia asked whether a more general positive framing might work around the original piece.

“What would it say?” Eve asked.

“That preparation pays off. That support is available.”

“Sometimes support changes the decision. Sometimes people can't arrange what Mia arranged. Her story says that because she asked us to keep it.”

“We do understand responsible ownership,” Lydia said, a little more firmly. “Our adoption partners assess applicants. We don't place animals ourselves.”

“I know. I'm asking to keep information that may help someone decide not to apply yet.”

The silence was short. It still made Eve want to offer something easier.

She could move the old post out of the main view. Leave a vague sentence somewhere else. Tell herself three months was temporary, that she would have more resources afterward, that nobody could reasonably expect a commercial account to carry every possible answer.

But a person following the existing link would find nothing.

The removal would do precisely the thing Martin had described. It would narrow the route until a purchase became the clearest destination.

“I can agree to a consistent format for commissioned pieces,” she said. “I can agree to a schedule for those pieces. I can't give the partnership control over whether these independent posts remain available.”

Martin leaned forward far enough to escape the leaves.

“Then the partner-account package may not be the right product.”

It was such an ordinary business sentence that Eve almost resented it. She had prepared an enormous internal refusal, and he was describing a mismatch in the thing they had offered.

“That may be true,” she said.

Lydia proposed pausing the review while they considered whether the content work could be separated from the account partnership. Eve agreed. She would send a concise description of the part she could do; Lydia would check which budget could cover it.

No production dates were held. No fee had been promised. Before ending the call, Eve repeated both points so that her hopeful notes could not later pretend otherwise.

Then the screen returned to her own face.

She looked remarkably unchanged for someone who had just watched a quarter become smaller.

At eleven, she had a client revision to finish. She opened it and spent eight minutes moving the same caption up and down.

Dumpling entered the room, inspected the chair beside her, and returned to his bed. He did not bring her an alternative revenue stream.

Eve got up to make tea.

The mug she wanted was beneath two plates in the sink. She washed all three with unnecessary force, then stopped because splashing her own sleeve was not a useful objection to a marketing strategy.

She had liked the opportunity. She still liked much of it. She wanted the organized calendar and the minimum fee, the possibility of buying reliable help without first holding her breath over three invoices.

Refusing part of the offer had not made those desires disappear.

When the kettle boiled, she poured the water and returned to the client work. The revision took forty minutes once she stopped asking it to provide emotional compensation. She sent it on time.

After lunch, she opened her quarter forecast.

The Good Company proposal had remained in a separate column. This had seemed admirably restrained when she made the sheet. Now she discovered she had nevertheless built a second life beside it.

A new microphone. Paid production help. Two more trial dates, with preparation properly allowed for. A small reserve that would survive one delayed invoice.

There was also a line called flexible project time.

Eve stared at it. She had not booked anything against that line. It represented the ability to say no to an ill-fitting commission without immediately searching for something else. She had assigned money to the feeling of not being cornered.

Without the package, that line disappeared first.

She moved it into a section headed postponed, then adjusted the room trials. The confirmed booking stayed. The two additional dates were only possibilities, so there was nothing to cancel, no participant to disappoint. Still, deleting the preparation blocks felt like losing something she had already touched.

The microphone could be repaired or borrowed for a particular shoot if necessary. The assistant would need to be included in each job's quote rather than supported by predictable monthly income. A slower client renewal could no longer be treated as harmless.

Her ordinary expenses were covered for the current period. This was not an eviction notice disguised as a disagreement. It was a narrower next quarter, with less room to make mistakes and less time to develop work she cared about.

She wrote that plainly under the figures.

At the neighborhood center the following afternoon, Eve tested the printout for her already booked session. The coordinator had allowed her ten minutes in the room between activities.

Someone had left a paper flower on the windowsill. Its folded petals cast an unexpectedly elaborate shadow on the brick-facing glass.

Eve placed her sample information sheet beneath it and sat in the far chair. The type was readable. The headings held. She turned the page and tried writing in the space provided, which was still too small for anyone who wrote with normal enthusiasm.

She made a note to remove one question.

The coordinator came in with a stack of notices.

“Still six at most?”

“Yes.”

“Four bookings so far, according to your update.”

“That's right.”

“And the next date?”

Eve had meant to ask about that. For days she had pictured booking ahead with the calm authority of a person backed by a quarter's work.

“I need to finish the accounts for this one before deciding,” she said.

The coordinator nodded. “Let me know. I can't hold the room without a booking.”

“I understand.”

There was no punishment in it. Another group might take the slot. Eve would then have to find a different date or wait. She had turned down one arrangement in order to keep choosing, and choosing included this inconvenient little space on the schedule.

She folded her sheet and handed the room back.

Outside, she received a message from Mia. It was a photograph of a torn shopping list, with a question about whether Eve could still read the bottom item. Toby had not been accused; Mia said she had shut the paper in her own coat zipper.

Eve enlarged the image.

“Washing liquid,” she replied. “Unless you've begun buying washing squid.”

Mia sent back a laughing face and an assurance that she wasn't ready for another dependent creature.

Eve smiled. For a moment she wanted to tell her about the marked-up story, to have Mia agree that she had done the right thing.

She didn't. Mia's post remained unchanged and her permission had not been expanded. There was nothing Mia needed to fix, approve, or comfort Eve through. If a new use were genuinely proposed later, they could discuss it then.

At home, Eve drafted the reply Lydia had requested.

She removed the paragraph defending the value of her entire account. Lydia had seen the value; the two sides wanted different authority over it.

She described practical commissioned work for the brand's own channels, with the subjects and claims agreed in advance. Her independent archive, future unpaid work, and participants' existing permissions would remain outside that arrangement. Any suitable project would need its own budget and schedule.

She read the message once more, then sent it.

Julian called that evening to arrange the Sunday walk. Eve told him the offer had changed shape.

“Less useful?” he asked.

“Potentially much smaller. I said no to making the account fit their campaign.”

“Would you like to talk about it on Sunday?”

“Yes. Could I bring the figures? I don't need you to check the arithmetic. I need to say it to a person.”

“You can bring them. We can sit at mine afterward.”

She agreed, grateful for somewhere the disappointment could be ordinary conversation instead of another document.

After the call, she looked at the old post on her phone. The sentence about waiting was still there. Below it, the link opened as it always had.

Keeping it had cost her an offer she could almost imagine accepting.

Eve put the phone down beside the smaller forecast. Both facts belonged on the table.
