# Chapter 241 — The Title Changed, Not the Alarm

On Monday the alarm went off at exactly the same time it had on Friday, which struck Eve as a failure of imagination.

She reached across the nightstand and knocked something to the floor.

“What was that?” Julian asked.

“My watch.”

“Was it going off?”

“No. I've made conditions worse for an innocent party.”

He pulled the blanket over his head. Eve got up before his approach began to seem reasonable.

In the kitchen, one of the two mugs from last night stood upside down on the rack. The other waited beside a heap of envelopes they had brought home from the restaurant. Someone had written *For the bride and groom* in handwriting so fine Eve could hardly make it out before turning on the light. The envelope underneath had a small grease spot from the restaurant bread she had wrapped to take home.

She slid the letters away from the sink. They could open them properly after work. This morning she had a proofs deadline, a shorter bus route than Julian, and eighteen minutes in which to iron something that did not come out of its hanger shaped like a question.

Dumpling emerged at the sound of the cupboard. He walked across the clean patch where the dry food had spilled on their wedding night and sniffed the skirting board. The two of them had swept, moved the mat and swept again. As far as Eve could tell, he was conducting a professional assessment of their integrity.

She fed him his breakfast. He ate a little, went to drink, and returned.

Julian appeared by the time the kettle boiled. He was in his clinic trousers, with a clean shirt tucked under one arm and a sock in his hand.

“Have you seen the other one?”

“No.”

He looked at Dumpling, who had left his bowl to wash between his front toes.

“Have you interviewed him?” Eve asked.

“I don't want to prejudice the investigation.”

They found it halfway underneath the rack, empty and deeply ordinary. Julian put it on while Eve checked a message from her print supplier. The message looked like a standard collection alert until she noticed the amended collection window. Their package would now be held until noon, exactly when she was supposed to meet another client across town.

She opened the booking page on her phone to move the pickup. There were no afternoon times left.

“Would you wake up again,” she asked Julian, “if I went back to bed?”

“I'd try once. Then I'd feed myself.”

“A punitive marriage.”

“I'd save you a piece.”

She put the bread in the toaster. Her client should not have to move a meeting because the printer had changed a pickup, and Robin had a paid shift already allocated to the venue. Eve found herself starting a message asking if Robin could go across town, then stopped with only their name in the box. The collection and back would consume much of the shift for something that fit in an envelope.

She phoned the supplier when the line opened and asked about the post instead. The assistant apologized for the change and quoted the ordinary delivery charge. The package would reach the venue on Tuesday, which would leave enough time for her to check the proofs before the client needed them. Eve asked for delivery, paid the small charge, and corrected the scheduling note she had sent Robin. Nobody needed to run.

While she was listening to the assistant repeat the amount, the toast popped. Julian put two pieces on a plate and cut one into halves with the attention he usually reserved for bad kitchen knives.

Eve ended the call. On her screen the minutes had slipped away without making a sound.

“Bus.”

“Ten minutes.”

“Seven if I miss the first set of lights.”

Julian set one piece of toast near her hand. “Eat that part before you calculate the rest.”

She took a bite and burned the roof of her mouth. He brought her water. There did not seem to be any married way of drinking it.

“Dr. Patel has the morning list,” he said while she took another sip. “I'm going in for my own appointments. I'll have time to sit down at lunch if nobody puts something unexpected in my diary.”

“Can you ask them not to?”

“I can continue declining things that aren't mine.”

They put the toast they hadn't finished into separate boxes. Eve slid the larger one toward him.

“That's yours,” he said.

She had been about to say she wasn't especially hungry. Instead she put the box in her bag. He put the other in his. Leaving a portion for the other person was a pleasure; making sure each of them claimed one took approximately thirty extra seconds.

At the door, they reached for the same hook. Eve's bag knocked against his elbow. His keys landed on the mat.

For a minute on Saturday she had been able to see him across the room with nobody between them. Now there was a pair of wet walking shoes and the sort of standing space one and a half people could use comfortably.

“You go first,” he said.

“That sounds generous until you see which of us has to pick up the keys.”

He bent and picked them up himself. She kissed the top of his head on her way out.

By the time Eve reached the venue she had finished half her toast, taking modest bites at crossings. She let herself in, hung up her coat, and found the new printing appointment reflected correctly on the board. Robin had written *by post* underneath in smaller letters.

“Look at that,” they said from the front table. “Civilization.”

“If they ever offer us a parcel chute, we may get an entire hour back.”

The work waiting for her occupied three seats: a stack of older handouts that needed to be separated from the current ones, a laptop, and a damp cloth Robin had put down after cleaning a spill. Eve moved the handouts before the cloth touched them. Then she connected to her early call.

The client was setting up a useful guide to spaces that allowed quiet bookings. They had commissioned Eve to review the wording and propose short, clear booking instructions. The current sample displayed six large photographs before a reader reached the actual information. Eve had asked for a screen-reader check from the client's own developer and prepared edits to the text that she could stand behind.

She was midway through opening the shared draft when her client said, “Congratulations, by the way. My sister calls everyone Mrs. as soon as she sees a ring. Do I have a new name to put on the contributor page?”

There was a gap of two beats while Eve looked at the page. Her first impulse was to answer with an overly formal explanation of how they had decided to manage their lives, though they had not had to hold any particularly memorable meeting about her name.

On the contributor page, the current placeholder read *Guest contributor to be confirmed*. She pointed to it with the cursor.

“Just Eve, as before. Same professional name and same invoice contact.”

“Of course. Old habit,” the client said. “I wanted to get it right.”

“Thanks for asking. I can't yet get used to anyone calling me a married woman. It sounds like someone who knows where the spare scissors are.”

“Do you?”

“No. But I can show you where your cancellation details went.”

The client laughed and enlarged the relevant page. The subject was over without Eve having to smooth anything else down. She hardly noticed the ring for the next forty minutes except when it clicked once against her laptop stand.

The larger problem emerged when they opened a sample booking from a customer's perspective. The words *quiet booking* described a lower-capacity appointment, but the draft never said which route clients would use or that the place remained noisy near delivery times. The pictures had all been taken before the first delivery. Eve could see the attraction of leading with them. She could also see a visitor arriving at the wrong half hour and feeling foolish for expecting what the page appeared to have promised.

The client offered to remove the word *quiet*.

“They can still book a smaller session,” Eve said. “Let's say when and what the staff can arrange. Then someone can decide whether that's enough.”

They tried a shorter line and found the route remained unclear. Eve asked the client to test it with the venue employee who actually met visitors at the door. That person might know of a recurring obstruction neither of them could see from the draft. They agreed to hold the page for that quick check. Eve would deliver the text that day as agreed; the client would decide when to upload it once their staff confirmed the facts.

When she closed the call, Robin had set the current handouts in a shallow tray and moved the retired version to the cabinet.

“What about a cup of tea?” they asked.

“I'm very married. I think I can have tea unaided.”

“Are the congratulations still available, or did you spend them all?”

“Still available. Tea also available. Thank you.”

Robin brought two mugs over, then went to check the shelf by the door before the first booked visitor arrived. Eve ate the other half of the toast. The crisp edge was gone. There was a narrow strip Julian had left plain because she'd complain if he spread all the jam to the edges.

She sent Julian a quick account of the naming question, then got halfway through the last bite before he replied with a photograph of his lunch bag. Inside it the little container had tipped to one side, jam entirely on the lid.

*Has a professional packing person seen your work?* she wrote.

*She has declined to change her name, and may deny knowing me.*

*I know you. Put it on the toast with a spoon.*

He sent back *Already done* and a second photograph. The piece was balanced on a paper towel beside an unopened apple. He had eaten it before their usual lunch break, when the appointment gaps finally allowed him time, rather than waiting hungry until he'd managed to turn lunch into the kind of lunch he had meant to take.

Eve set the phone down and went to meet the visitor. There were forms to collect and a table to straighten. The first pen they handed over was out of ink. The second worked. It was a little humiliating how much of the future still came down to checking the pens.

In the evening she got home before Julian and set the envelopes on the table. Dumpling investigated the corner of the top one, then began to slide his shoulder against the cardboard edge of his own box. Eve moved the envelopes out of reach and left the box alone.

Julian got in while she was looking up the correct place to write back to a relative who had given them a card without a return address. He washed his hands and sat beside her.

“How was your first day with your new title?” he asked.

“Busy. You?”

“I was Mr. Someone's Left the Blood-Pressure Cuff in the Other Room.”

“Was it you?”

“It was. The title was awarded on merit.”

She moved his cup closer. He picked up the first card, looked at the front of it, and waited until she could read it beside him. They didn't finish all the envelopes. Two were left for Tuesday because dinner had to come out of the oven, and neither of them wanted another Monday meal going cold while they performed affection in the wrong order.

When Eve walked into the kitchen, she found he'd set the table with the ordinary plates. There was a little piece of bread beside each one, from the same loaf they'd started that morning.
