# Chapter 036: The Cold Face in the Clip

The first time Eve saw the video, her toast was still warm.

By the fourth viewing, the butter had become a pale, unappealing skin, and she was holding the same corner of the bread so tightly that it had begun to fold.

Julian stood behind the clinic counter. The camera had caught him at an angle that made the light above reception shine across his forehead. Someone out of frame was speaking. Before the question finished, he shook his head.

“No. You can't leave him here. I've already explained that.”

The video stopped on his face.

Underneath, a caption asked whether compassion cost extra.

Eve knew that counter. She knew the scratch near the card reader and the basket of clean pens Tessa moved whenever someone set a carrier on the wrong end. Familiar objects made the accusation feel as if it had entered a room she had left unlocked.

She played it again.

Twelve seconds. Not enough to know what had been requested, why it had been refused, or who had decided that the exchange should become everyone's breakfast.

Quite enough to make Julian look cold.

The local page that had shared it was not enormous, but people she recognized had commented. One asked whether it was the vet from the walking guide. Another said this was why she distrusted clinics. A third pointed out that the question had been cut off.

That comment had become its own argument.

Eve put the toast down and opened the reply box.

*This does not show what happened. I have worked with—*

Her fingers stopped.

She had worked with him. She had not been there.

Behind her, Dumpling attempted to pull the corner of his blanket out from beneath a chair leg. The fabric stretched; the chair remained unmoved. He sat back and looked at the obstruction with the grave disappointment of someone discovering poor public infrastructure.

Eve got up and lifted the chair.

“There. Evidence-based intervention.”

He dragged the blanket six inches, turned around twice, and abandoned it.

She returned to the table and deleted the unfinished comment.

Instead she sent Tessa the link with a short question: *Have you seen this? I won't respond from my account. Let me know if you want help identifying what's circulating.*

Then she put the bread in the pan to warm it again. There was no useful reason to make herself hungry as well as angry.

Tessa answered while Eve was washing the pan.

*Seen it. Dr. Clark is handling contact. Please don't send people here to explain.*

Eve stared at the last line. She hadn't been planning to send anyone, but she could immediately imagine the kind of help Tessa meant. Loyal clients calling to express outrage. Visitors arriving with advice. Ten people insisting that reception hear how strongly they supported reception.

*Understood. Are you asking for any practical help?*

A few minutes passed.

*Dr. Clark can speak at 3:40 for ten minutes if you'd like to discuss a small communications job. No filming.*

Eve accepted the ten minutes. She left Dumpling home, where the most controversial decision facing him involved a rectangle of afternoon sun.

On the tram she searched only far enough to identify the source of the shared video. She found two reposts and one longer upload linked beneath an accusation that the clinic was hiding the truth. She did not open it in the crowded carriage. Even through headphones, she disliked the idea of turning someone else's distressed voice into the soundtrack of strangers' journey.

She copied the public link into a note.

By the time she reached the clinic, a woman was holding the door for a man carrying a cat basket with both hands. Nothing about the ordinary exchange matched the feverish little room inside Eve's phone.

Inside, the floor smelled faintly of disinfectant and wet pavement. A dog nosed its owner's shoe. Tessa was explaining where to leave a completed form, her finger resting beside the correct box instead of tapping it impatiently.

Eve waited until the counter was clear.

“I'm early.”

“Three minutes. A manageable offense.”

Tessa indicated a chair away from the waiting animals. Her hair was coming loose at one temple, and a cup of tea stood beyond her keyboard with its label still hanging down the side.

The telephone rang.

“Yes, we're aware of the video,” she said. “I can't discuss another person's visit. If you need an appointment—”

She listened, eyes on the blank wall above the noticeboard.

“No. I haven't given you a different answer because you asked more loudly.”

Eve looked down at her own shoes, suddenly embarrassed by how close she had come to adding herself to that queue.

Tessa ended the call and wrote down something brief. She did not give Eve a meaningful look or perform exhaustion for her benefit. The next caller needed to change an appointment. Tessa helped them do it.

At 3:40, Dr. Clark brought Eve into the little staff room. Someone had left a packet of crackers open beside the kettle. He folded its top before sitting, a practical gesture that made him look less like the head of an embattled institution than a man trying not to inherit stale biscuits along with everything else.

“We're not asking you to make this disappear,” he said.

“I can't.”

“Good. We can start there.”

He had contacted the person who uploaded the recording and the visitor involved. Those conversations were not Eve's work. He wanted help making any public explanation clear, provided there was something appropriate to explain.

Eve opened her notebook.

“The short version doesn't even establish what he refused.”

“No.”

“If there's a fuller recording, we could show where the—”

She stopped, remembering the link.

“There may be a longer version already public. I found it, but I haven't watched it.”

Dr. Clark asked her to open the page with the sound low. Not an internal file, not someone's clinical record. Just the public material the clinic needed to know was circulating.

The longer recording began with the camera pointing at the floor. There were shoes, the corner of a carrier, and an adult voice saying that arrangements had changed. Eve could hear a child somewhere outside the frame.

Then the camera lifted.

The visitor's face appeared only briefly, but a distinctive striped coat remained visible. On the counter lay an unfolded hospital letter. The print was small. Eve did not enlarge it.

She didn't have to.

The speaker began explaining an illness in the family, a journey, someone who could no longer help. Details came quickly, in the breathless order of a person trying to make their situation understood before the answer closed against them.

Eve paused the video.

The room had become very quiet.

“There are names in the audio,” she said.

Dr. Clark nodded.

“And even if we remove those, the coat, the day, the circumstances—”

“Yes.”

She looked at the frozen image. A hand had come down on the counter with its fingers spread. Her first professional instinct had been to find the missing sentence. Now she could see how much she would have to carry out of this person's life to get it.

The fuller recording might explain Julian's refusal. It would also tell a neighborhood things the visitor had said while asking for help, not while auditioning for public sympathy.

“Who posted it?” Eve asked.

“Someone who was with the visitor. We're discussing it directly. Please don't contact them on our behalf.”

“I won't.”

She closed the page.

For a moment she hated doing it. The answer appeared to exist. It was right there, longer and more complicated and inconveniently persuasive. Her hands wanted to work: trim, caption, arrange, restore. She could already hear the brisk opening of a corrective video.

*Here is what you weren't shown.*

It would sound fair. It would travel well. It would make other people's private circumstances the admission price for believing that Julian wasn't cruel.

Eve crossed out the word *footage* in her notebook.

Dr. Clark pushed the cracker packet toward her. She shook her head, then took one after all. She hadn't eaten lunch properly, despite her sensible position on toast.

“What can you do instead?” he asked.

“First, distinguish things the clinic can say about its services from things it only knows because somebody needed those services. Then decide whether a statement is actually useful.”

“That sounds like a smaller job.”

“It is.”

He asked for a quote covering a short written draft and one revision, with his approval before anything was posted. She would not moderate the comments, handle complaints, or become the contact for individual visitors. They agreed she would send the scope that evening and, if accepted, begin on Monday.

The conversation had taken eleven minutes.

Outside the staff room, Julian came through the corridor carrying a folded towel. He stopped when he saw her.

“You've seen it.”

“Yes.”

A door opened behind him, and a colleague asked where the spare liners had been put. He answered, set the towel on the appropriate shelf, and returned his attention to Eve. The small interruption was almost comforting. His work still contained towels that had to go somewhere.

“I was going to suggest the full version,” she said. “I won't.”

“Thank you.”

“I hate that the short one is what people are seeing.”

His gaze dropped briefly to the notebook against her chest.

“The short one is still something I said.”

Before Eve could answer, Tessa called his name for an actual clinic matter. He excused himself and went, leaving the sentence with her rather than explaining it away.

At home, the flat smelled of the apple she had cut and forgotten to finish. Dumpling emerged from under the table, stretched with elaborate care, and bumped his cheek against her trouser leg.

Eve lowered a hand. He allowed two strokes before moving toward his bowl.

She fed him, opened the window a little, and wrote the quote. The task description looked almost offensively modest beside the noise online: review of public-facing wording; one short statement; one revision; no case commentary.

She did not add a free defense of Julian's entire character.

After sending it, she reopened her own account. Someone had tagged her beneath the clip with a question about whether she knew him.

She wrote a private draft, not a public reply.

*I know him. I don't know everything that happened. Knowing him doesn't give me permission to tell you about the person standing opposite him.*

She read it once and closed it.

The sentence was useful, but not every useful sentence needed an audience.

Across the room, Dumpling had found the blanket again. This time he settled without rearranging it, his white paws disappearing beneath his chest.

Eve put her phone in the drawer and made dinner.

The rice began to boil over while she was cutting a carrot. She turned the heat down, wiped the hob, and left the phone where it was.
