# Chapter 007 — The Dog Outside the Frame

Button understood the camera.

For the first two seconds after Eve pressed record, at least, he stood against the bright background with his head tilted and his tail providing all the cheerful music the shot required.

In the third second, he licked the lens.

A vast, damp nose filled her screen.

“Approachable,” she said, reaching for her cleaning cloth. “Very engaged with the process.”

The volunteer holding his lead laughed.

The event was tomorrow. Today was the short preparation shoot they'd agreed on. The clinic had lent the volunteer group its side activity area; the volunteers would handle the animals and subsequent adoption assessments. There were no visitors yet, and the furniture wasn't fully arranged.

Eve had thought this would make filming particularly easy.

Button was a small dog with a white chest who greeted every person as if they were a colleague capable of fixing all his problems. She had footage of him waiting for his water bowl, tilting his head at a sound, and reluctantly abandoning the completely inedible lens cloth.

Enough for a lively opening.

She checked the time and prepared to film his name card. A short-haired volunteer approached carrying a folded old towel rather than a dog.

“Could you have a look at Pepper today, too?”

“Of course. Where is he?”

Instead of fetching him to the brightest part of the room, the woman pointed behind a folding screen.

A gray-brown adult dog lay on a mat in the corner. His body was longer than Button's, with one ear that folded naturally. He glanced at Eve, then turned his face toward the wall.

“I'm Wendy,” the volunteer said quietly. “He doesn't like people pointing things at him straightaway. Don't film yet. Just sit for a minute.”

Eve lowered her camera and crouched. Then she wondered whether crouching was necessarily less alarming than standing. At Wendy's suggestion, she moved to a chair farther to the side. Its legs squealed against the tiles. Pepper's ear twitched.

“Sorry,” Eve said quickly.

Wendy didn't scold her. She laid the towel beside the mat without trying to pull him into view.

Eve checked his details. Adult. Slow to warm up. Needed time to become familiar with new surroundings. The words were perfectly legible on the card she had designed. They were harder to turn into appealing footage.

“Do you have videos of him playing?”

“A few. We'd need to check with the people who took them. You might not get that today.”

“That's fine. Some everyday footage would—”

She stopped.

Already, she was looking for a version of this quiet animal that deserved to appear.

Wendy set down a water bowl and sat on the other side.

“Pepper isn't good at attracting people at events. They stop, see he isn't coming out, and move on. Button's easy to remember.”

Someone laughed beyond the screen. Button had probably introduced his nose to another available hand. Eve didn't need to look. If she wanted the smoothest possible opening, he was the obvious star.

She had already arranged it in her head: happy paws, smiling faces, cards held up, a warm invitation at the end.

Pepper would slow it down.

But they weren't trying to find a home for the editing rhythm.

“What does he like normally?” She opened her notebook to a clean page. “It doesn't have to be adorable. Ordinary is fine.”

Wendy considered. He liked someone nearby doing their own thing. They didn't need to stroke him or keep calling. Once he knew them, he might drag a toy closer. If nobody responded, sometimes he left it by their feet and settled elsewhere.

“He waits?”

“Sometimes. Sometimes he takes it away again. Don't make him sound saintly. He goes through bags.”

Eve laughed and crossed out quiet companionship. It sounded too much like a guarantee. She replaced it with give him time to know you, then added a reminder to have the wording checked.

They sat without making a performance of waiting. Wendy sorted supplies she would take home later. Eve checked the names on her cards. Pepper moved his nose slightly out from between his paws. He didn't approach.

A small impatience stirred in Eve. Every filming session was now included in a quote. She couldn't wait indefinitely. But quietly raising the camera wouldn't become appropriate simply because she needed footage.

She folded the camera's display closed.

“I'd like to change the opening. We don't need every dog looking at the lens. Could I start with an empty chair?”

Wendy looked puzzled, so Eve indicated a chair on the other side, away from Pepper, and framed it with her hands.

“Someone coming here can sit down and ask questions. They don't have to choose immediately. We move from the chair to the information, then the animals who are comfortable being filmed. If Pepper isn't, his details and a sentence you've checked are enough.”

“Will it look as good?”

“It'll be slower,” Eve admitted. “But not everybody knows they're allowed to be.”

First she found Tessa to check the actual process: where visitors would register, who would introduce the animals, whether a quieter consultation was possible, and which areas must remain out of frame.

Tessa stood at the entrance holding a stack of blank forms in place of prospective visitors.

“If the chair goes there, nobody behind it can get in.”

“Excellent. Our most authentic footage: the planner blocking the door.”

They moved it against the wall. Eve filmed the empty table, the cards, and a volunteer's hand turning a card over after checking that the person agreed to appear. No radiant crowd filled the frame. For the first time, however, it looked like a place someone could actually find their way through.

When Julian arrived to check the wording, she was frowning at suitable for every family. It had survived from an older event handout, and she had originally intended to reuse it.

“Not that sentence,” he said.

“I was about to delete it.”

He glanced at her without arguing about who'd noticed first. “Good.”

She read him the alternative. “You don't have to decide at first sight.”

“Follow it with where to ask questions.”

“You would make a very good button.”

“As long as it doesn't say Take Home Now.”

Eve laughed down at her notebook and wrote Ask First. He didn't remain behind her shoulder while she worked. Once the relevant information was checked, he went back to his own job.

Oddly, that made her more willing to ask another question.

“If Pepper never looks toward the camera, could I use an existing photograph? I'll check permission and when it was taken.”

“Ask the volunteer responsible for him. They'll know whether it still shows him accurately.”

He hadn't collected every answer merely because people might let him.

Near the end of the agreed filming time, Eve returned to the screen. Wendy had packed the supplies. Pepper had changed position; his face no longer pointed at the wall.

“This is enough for today,” Eve said. “We don't need to get him walking toward us.”

She put her camera into its bag. At the sound of the zipper, Pepper raised his head, stood slowly, and went to Wendy. He didn't jump or bark. He simply rested his nose near the hand holding the towel.

It was such a good image that Eve's fingers automatically sought the zipper again.

Wendy hadn't said she couldn't. In fact, she was looking down at Pepper and hadn't noticed the movement.

Eve stopped.

Not every unrecorded moment was a loss. Especially when putting the equipment away might be what had allowed the moment to happen.

She kept the sight of that relaxed ear in her mind instead.

“Could we describe that? Say he's more willing to approach with someone familiar beside him?”

“Yes,” Wendy said. “Just don't say he already likes you.”

“We've reached the stage of knowing each other's names.”

“You know his. He might not know yours.”

“Right. A one-sided acquaintance.”

Back home, Eve placed Button's lens-licking clip on the timeline, then inserted the empty chair before it. The first music track was too buoyant. She tried the second version without music at all. The sound of paper and a few short footsteps made her want to pause rather than hurry through.

The photograph arrived in two versions. In the first, Pepper's coat had been brightened until the gray looked silver, and his eyes had acquired a sparkle the original didn't have. Eve asked for the unfiltered file. It wasn't less flattering, exactly; it was simply a dog in ordinary light, with a creased blanket behind him.

She cropped out an address visible on a parcel in the background, leaving enough of the blanket to avoid making him float in a featureless studio. Then she checked the new crop with Wendy. The reply was brief: Yes. That's him.

Pepper's card used that recent photograph, whose creator had confirmed permission. She hadn't labeled him a last chance. There was no please save him. His slow adjustment was visible, not hidden beneath promises of uncomplicated affection.

She sent Tessa the unfinished cut for a factual check, clearly marked as not ready for publication.

Dumpling dozed beside her chair. A brief bark from the footage made his ear turn. She lowered the volume and put on headphones. He didn't get up to reveal which clip would go viral. He only buried his face more deeply.

Later, Wendy passed along one correction through Tessa: Pepper still needed someone to help him become comfortable. An otherwise quiet room wasn't a substitute for attention.

Eve opened the text and added it.

That might discourage people who wanted an effortless dog. She knew that.

She also knew that if fewer people asked about him tomorrow for that reason, it wouldn't necessarily mean she'd failed.

She watched the cut once more, this time without counting how often anything cute happened. The practical information was readable. Button still got his wet-nosed entrance. Pepper wasn't invisible, and he wasn't pretending to be Button.

There was a short silence in the middle she was tempted to cover. She left it. A visitor would need time to turn a card over; a viewer could survive two quiet seconds.

Then she saved the project, wrote down the final checks for tomorrow, and closed the laptop rather than invent another version to justify the afternoon.

On the darkened screen, her reflection remained faintly visible above Dumpling's orange back. He had climbed only far enough onto the cushion to steal the warm patch beside her leg, and now his sleeping weight kept her from immediately standing up.

“All right,” she told him. “Two minutes.”

For once, she didn't reach for her phone to make those minutes useful.

In the film, the empty chair waited at the entrance.

It was the only shot she'd taken all day that didn't need someone to fill it.
