# Chapter 174 — Sold Out and Still Unsold

The sold-out notice was smaller than Eve's thumb.

She wrote it on a scrap of card, propped it against the empty tray, and stepped back to admire the least impressive triumph of her working life.

Plain reusable labels. Six packs. Gone.

Beside the tray, twelve carefully designed keepsake cards remained arranged in a fan. Eve had adjusted that fan three times during the afternoon. The cards depicted a little door, an empty chair, and a pair of paws beneath the words You, Me & Paws. They were tasteful. They were beautifully printed. They had attracted one compliment and no purchases.

Robin came over with a pencil behind one ear.

“Someone wants to know if there are more labels.”

“There aren't.”

“I thought so. Shall I tell them?”

“I'll do it.”

Eve could feel herself smiling before she reached the customer. Being asked for something she no longer had was an unexpectedly pleasant problem. Then she remembered that the person asking still didn't have the thing they wanted.

“I'm sorry,” she said. “That was the small trial quantity.”

“Can I order a pack?”

“I haven't confirmed a next delivery yet. I can tell you where this type came from, or note that you'd like to hear when I know.”

The woman chose the supplier information. Eve wrote it down without pretending that losing a possible future sale was generosity. It was an ordinary object, not a secret recipe the venue had invented.

As the woman left, she paused at the keepsake cards.

“Those are lovely.”

“Thank you.”

Then she continued toward the door.

Eve watched her go, looked at the cards, and heard the compliment become even less useful than silence.

The open demonstration period was nearly finished. Robin had brought the last round to its planned end, and the remaining visitors were collecting bags and looking at the small display. There had been no crush, no rush to buy everything, no grand pile of orders. Eve had wanted modest expectations. Apparently wanting them did not mean she enjoyed them.

Mia arrived to say hello on her way past, without Toby. She had already arranged her dog's ordinary care for the day rather than assuming a social visit required him to attend. She stopped in front of the sold-out note and looked delighted.

“You sold something out!”

“Six packs.”

“Still six.”

“Very true.”

Mia glanced at the souvenir cards. “These are pretty.”

Eve laughed before she could stop herself.

“What?”

“Nothing. They're receiving excellent reviews as things people don't want to own.”

Mia's face changed. “Oh. I could buy one.”

“Please don't do that.”

“I like them.”

“Then like them. You don't have to fix my stock.”

Mia picked up a card after asking and turned it over. “What would I use it for?”

“Keeping.”

“Right.”

Eve folded her arms, then unfolded them. It was not Mia's fault that the entire product explanation consisted of one word and a defensive posture.

“I imagined people writing a little memory on the back,” she said. “Something from an exchange. Or giving it to someone.”

“Have you told anyone that?”

“No.”

Mia looked down at the card again. “It might help.”

It might. It might also not turn an unnecessary purchase into a necessary one. Eve thanked her, placed one card flat with its blank back visible, and wrote a simple example on a separate display slip. She didn't lower the price in panic or make Mia perform enthusiasm for the next visitor.

Two people looked at the revised display. One smiled. Neither bought a card.

The result was almost comforting in its refusal to become a lesson with an instant reward.

Mia went to look at the public booking card held to the metal cupboard by her ugly dog-shaped magnet. The creature's shape remained open to interpretation. From where Eve stood, it resembled a potato with a strong opinion about ears.

“Still here,” Mia said proudly.

“It's doing actual work.”

“Good. I chose a useful gift.”

“You chose it after admitting it was hideous.”

“Two qualities can coexist.”

Eve took a photograph of the display after the last visitor had moved away, making sure no people or private information appeared. She almost cropped the empty label tray to make the whole shelf look sold out. Her thumb stopped on the screen.

The unsold cards belonged in the photograph too.

She saved the ordinary wide view for the day's records and put the phone down. No public announcement was required before she knew what the numbers meant.

When the published closing time came, Eve turned the sign and thanked the remaining guests. One asked if she could finish reading a card. Eve allowed the few minutes already left for departures, then closed the door once everyone was out. Robin began the agreed clearing tasks. Mia said goodbye rather than turning her visit into an unpaid shift.

“Will you tell me what happens to the cards?” she asked.

“If they stage a rebellion, you'll be the first to know.”

“Tell the potato to negotiate.”

After she left, Eve gathered the sales slips while Robin counted the small items. They worked at the clear table with the display in sight. It was easier to believe the figures when she could see the twelve cards refusing to evaporate.

Six packs of labels. Two simple bag clips. One small notepad. Several paid session places from the morning, some booked earlier. No sales from the decorative cards.

Robin read the counts aloud while Eve checked them against the payment record. A canceled booking had been refunded according to the terms; she kept the refund separate rather than forgetting it because the money had first appeared as income. A visitor who had merely asked about next month was not a booking. A compliment was not a sale, however beautifully delivered.

One slip seemed to show two notepads, though only one was missing from the tray. Eve checked the time against her payment record, then remembered the customer changing her mind before paying. She had corrected the amount and left the first mark on the paper.

“Mine,” she said, drawing a clear line through it and adding the explanation.

Robin counted the remaining notepads again. This time the physical pile and the record agreed.

Eve felt an absurd relief. A small mistake she could locate and correct was preferable to a large hopeful number she couldn't explain. She put the amended slip with the others, keeping the correction visible rather than rewriting a cleaner history.

The total looked larger before she put the costs beside it.

“I dislike this part,” she said.

“Counting?”

“Subtracting.”

Robin smiled without rescuing her from the calculation.

They included the goods themselves, the paid preparation and hosting time, the clearing allowance, and the day's ordinary share of the venue costs. Eve did not treat the opening sign as something that would be paid for by six packs of labels. It belonged to the setup figures, where it was already waiting with other expenses she would recover slowly if the business worked.

The day had brought in money. It had not demonstrated that the venue could sustain itself.

Eve sat with that answer for a moment. Through the window, someone paused to read the closing hours and then moved on. The room continued to exist after its first public day, whether or not the spreadsheet produced a satisfying headline.

“Maybe I put the cards in the wrong place,” she said.

“Maybe.”

“Maybe the description wasn't good enough.”

“Could be.”

“Maybe I should make a proper video.”

Robin put down the pencil.

“Do you want suggestions, or do you want me to agree that the cards are wonderful?”

Eve looked at them. Then she laughed, a little painfully.

“Suggestions.”

“The labels were used in the activity. People saw a problem they had and an object that might help. The cards are a different purchase. They might want them later. They might not. We only saw one day.”

Eve nodded. She knew all of that in the abstract. It felt considerably less elegant with her own unsold design in front of her.

“What would you change next time?”

“Ask a few people what they'd use. Keep the quantity small. Don't buy twelve more because somebody says these twelve are pretty.”

“That was unnecessarily accurate.”

“I've worked a lot of events.”

They put the remaining goods back carefully rather than sweeping the unpopular ones into a box as punishment. The cards were undamaged and could remain available. Eve wrote down the actual question people had asked about them: What would I use it for? It was a better starting point than deciding the audience lacked taste.

She checked the supplier's minimum quantity for the labels. The next order would cost more than the six packs she had tested because of the minimum, and she would have to store the remainder. The convenient word sold out did not remove that constraint.

“I could split an order if someone else actually wants the same product,” she said. “Otherwise I can wait.”

Robin pointed toward the requests page. “How many confirmed?”

“None. One chose the supplier details, two asked whether we'd have them later.”

“Then those are inquiries.”

Eve marked them that way. She would check demand before spending. Next month's display could favor useful items without becoming a warehouse assembled from one encouraging afternoon.

Her phone chimed with Sophie's message asking how the opening had gone. Eve began typing, Sold out of our first product, then erased it.

She wrote that the activity had worked, the plain labels had sold, the keepsake cards hadn't, and the financial result was too small to judge the whole plan. Sophie replied with a photograph of her own abandoned lunch beside a dismantled stage prop.

Sounds like a real opening. Eat something.

Eve showed Robin, who agreed with the last instruction.

Their paid clearing time had a little left. They finished the stock count, checked the room, and wrote one change for the next session: put a sample of each useful item on the table where people could try it, with clear prices and no obligation to buy. It was a manageable experiment. They did not redesign the entire business while tired.

Eve filed the day's record, then picked up the tiny sold-out notice. She could throw it away or keep it as a souvenir more personally meaningful than any of the cards she had printed.

She slipped it into her notebook beside the unflattering figures.

Robin caught the movement and smiled.

“Private collection?”

“Very exclusive. One item.”

“Excellent stock control.”

Eve laughed. The shelves still held unsold things, the next order remained unplaced, and the day had not paid for her future. But every number on the page referred to something that had actually happened.

For tonight, she could work with that.
