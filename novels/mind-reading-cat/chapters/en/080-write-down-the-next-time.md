# Chapter 080 — Write Down the Next Time

Julian confirmed Friday at lunchtime on Thursday, while Eve was eating rice from a bowl she had successfully remembered to wash.

*Seven, if that still works for you. I've checked the arrangements. I won't promise that the world can never interrupt, but I'm not leaving a known unfinished handover behind me.*

She read the message twice, then answered before turning it into a document requiring consultation.

*Seven works. Same noodle place? I want the dinner we started.*

*So do I.*

There was no grand replacement offer, no expensive restaurant intended to make an ordinary disappointment look petty. Eve liked that. She had wanted the noodles and the conversation, not compensation in a more formal chair.

On Friday, her afternoon delivery finished early enough that she could take a short walk before going home. She bought nothing. She returned a book she had finished, kept the walking book a little longer within its loan period, and checked the new due date before leaving the library.

At home, she fed Dumpling and put away the laundry. The cat tried to sit in the empty basket. She let him inspect it rather than interpreting every interest in a container as a clue. When he climbed out, she returned it to the cupboard.

She chose the same coat as Wednesday. Reusing it felt pleasantly unceremonious.

The travel book went into her bag because Julian had asked about one of its drawings of old bridges. It was light enough for the repaired strap, and she wanted to show him the picture rather than describe a structure she barely understood. She did not bring the notebook of cat observations or a client draft.

At six fifty-five, she reached the restaurant.

Julian was already at the door. Seeing him made her realize that a small part of her had been preparing to receive a cancellation message all the way there.

“You're here,” she said.

“I am.”

She laughed at herself. “An impressive opening from me.”

“Clear. Difficult to misunderstand.”

He offered his hand, and she took it. His coat was unbuttoned, his hair slightly flattened on one side, and she liked the visible evidence that he had come from a day rather than an advertisement.

Inside, the waiter recognized them only enough to ask whether they wanted the same table. Eve chose one nearer the back, away from the draught she had noticed on Wednesday.

“I'd prefer this one,” she told Julian.

“Good. I was going to ask whether that seat was cold.”

“It was. I didn't notice until you went outside.”

“Then this is an improvement already.”

They sat. Julian put his phone away after checking that the agreed contact arrangements were in place. He didn't switch it off with a flourish or ask her to admire the gesture. Eve put hers in her bag too.

The waiter brought menus.

“Same?” Julian asked.

“For the main dish, yes. But I want to try the little side plate I kept looking at last time.”

“Which one?”

She pointed. They ordered it to share after both agreed they wanted it. The decision took ten seconds, leaving Eve mildly embarrassed by how many times she had previously disguised not choosing as generosity.

“How did the delivery go?” he asked.

She began with the version she would have told a colleague: completed on time, agreed revisions included, no remaining issue. Halfway through the sentence, Julian leaned back with a small smile.

“What?”

“That sounds like the email.”

“It was a good email.”

“I believe you. Was it a good afternoon?”

Eve stopped. Then she laughed and told him about the correction that had asked her to make a sentence warmer while also using fewer friendly words. He asked what she had done. She showed him the two versions on a scrap of paper the waiter gave them, changing the particulars so no private client wording was exposed.

Julian studied them.

“They mean the same thing.”

“Yes.”

“The second is easier to read.”

“Also yes.”

“Then the temperature is probably adequate.”

She laughed hard enough that the waiter glanced over to see whether they needed anything. Eve shook her head, smiling, and folded the scrap before putting it in her bag.

The noodles arrived. Julian lifted his chopsticks with a seriousness that made her suspicious.

“What?” she asked.

“I'm determined to eat some this time.”

“An achievable ambition.”

He tasted them while they were hot, then looked genuinely offended on behalf of the packed version he'd eaten on Wednesday.

“Better?”

“Considerably.”

They ate for a while without using every pause to check whether the other person was having a good time. Eve liked the sharp taste of the side dish. Julian liked it too, though he wanted less of it than she did. She took the last piece after asking, rather than abandoning it to demonstrate a lack of appetite she did not possess.

He finished the printer story.

The machine hadn't merely jammed; it had produced a page announcing that nothing was wrong while refusing to print the page that mattered. Tessa had taped its own announcement above it until the repair visit.

“A professional rebuttal,” Eve said.

“She was very restrained.”

“Did you fix it?”

“No. Someone qualified is coming.”

“Another excellent ending.”

He grinned.

After they had eaten enough for hunger to stop directing the conversation, Eve took out the travel book. The page showed a stone bridge with irregular arches. Julian turned it carefully, keeping the corner away from the sauce.

“I thought that middle arch looked wrong,” she said. “But the caption says it was rebuilt later.”

He read it, then studied the picture again.

“I wouldn't have noticed.”

“I didn't either until the book told me.”

They compared the bridge with the next photograph, which showed a newer crossing farther downstream. Eve liked the old one. Julian said he would prefer walking over the one that looked less slippery.

“You can appreciate something without volunteering to fall off it,” he said.

“That may become the title of my travel memoir.”

He looked at her over the book. “Would you actually like to travel more?”

The question was ordinary, and for once she didn't know the answer she wanted to perform.

“Yes. But not constantly. I like having somewhere familiar to come back to. And I don't want to spend three months paying for two photographs and a hotel breakfast.”

“What would you choose?”

“A nearby place for a day first. Enough time to look around without proving I saw everything.”

He nodded. “I like museums when I can leave before I've seen all of them.”

“All the museums?”

“All the rooms. One museum is usually sufficient.”

She laughed. “Then we have at least one compatible travel policy.”

They didn't book a trip over the empty bowls. The conversation moved to the kinds of weekends they preferred, how much company felt good after a difficult day, and which invitations they tended to accept out of habit.

Eve admitted that she sometimes wanted to go home halfway through an event she had been excited to attend.

“So do I,” Julian said.

“But I stay because leaving feels like rejecting everyone.”

“I stay because I think I should be enjoying it more.”

They looked at each other, then laughed at the amount of time that could be lost by two people standing politely in different rooms.

“If that happens when we're together,” Eve said, “I'd like to be able to say it.”

“Yes.”

“And I don't always want you to solve the thing I'm complaining about.”

Julian put down his cup.

“I know I do that.”

“Sometimes it's useful. Sometimes I just want five minutes to be unreasonable before I do the reasonable thing myself.”

“How will I know?”

“I can tell you.”

He nodded. “That would help.”

“What about you?”

He thought for a moment.

“If I'm quiet, I may need time rather than a better question. I can say that instead of expecting you to work it out.”

Eve remembered the tea conversation about becoming a funny explanation for everything. She didn't quote it back as proof she'd learned the lesson.

“All right,” she said. “But please do say it.”

“I will try.”

It wasn't an elegant vow. It sounded usable.

They ordered tea and stayed while the room grew busier. Eve put the book on the empty part of her chair, then draped her coat over it when someone squeezed past carrying a bag. Julian moved his feet to let them through.

“About Wednesday,” he said after a while.

She looked up.

“I checked the process with the team yesterday. There was a gap, and we clarified who confirms the final handover. I can't promise no call will ever come during dinner.”

“I know.”

“But I can do better than leave you with an estimate I don't believe.”

“You did tell me not to wait.”

“Yes. I want to keep doing that part.”

Eve nodded. She didn't need to make the interruption a romantic test he had passed by returning. He had done what he said he would do. She wanted to notice that without handing him a certificate for basic reliability.

“I'd like another date,” she said.

His smile came quickly enough to make her glad she hadn't wrapped the sentence in humor.

“So would I.”

“Not because this one owes us anything. Just because I want one.”

“Good.”

They compared the following week's free time. There were actual limits on both sides. They chose a short weekend outing rather than trying to stretch a work evening into a whole day, and agreed to decide the place after checking the weather.

Eve wrote the time in her calendar. This time she didn't stare at the word Date afterward.

When they stood to leave, she put on her coat and picked up her bag.

“The bridge,” Julian said.

She turned. Her library book lay on the chair.

“Oh. Thank you.”

“I thought you'd miss the memoir.”

She tucked it safely into the bag, checking the strap rather than trusting the repair with a careless swing. Outside, the air felt cooler than it had when they arrived. Julian asked which way she was taking home, and they walked together until their routes separated.

At the corner, Eve reached for his hand before he could offer it.

They stood for a moment, smiling at each other in the light from a closed shop. She had not said everything there might eventually be to say. Neither had he. There was still a difference between choosing more dates and naming a whole relationship, and she didn't need to force the difference closed tonight.

“Goodnight,” she said.

“Goodnight, Eve.”

On the way home, the book pressed a reassuring square against her side. The next time was written down. This time had been lived, hot noodles and imperfect sentences included.
