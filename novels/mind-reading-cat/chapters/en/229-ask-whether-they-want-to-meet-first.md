# Chapter 229 — Ask Whether They Want to Meet First

Nora's first message contained a question Eve had not expected.

Would she prefer to speak without photographs first?

Eve read it twice at the kitchen table on Thursday morning. She had been preparing to ask for more pictures, clearer dates, one useful scrap of certainty. Nora was offering to begin with a voice.

She replied that a call would be welcome and suggested the time they had already kept free that evening. Nora confirmed. Neither of them promised a meeting with the cat.

Eve worked through the day with the appointment written in her personal calendar, separate from the venue bookings. Robin noticed her checking the time near closing.

“Do you need to leave earlier?”

“No. I've allowed enough time.”

“An astonishing development.”

“I'm attempting consistency.”

They finished the planned clearing. Eve didn't add one last task to prove that a private concern could never affect her professionalism. She went home when she said she would.

At seven, the phone rang.

Nora's voice was lower than Eve had imagined from the photograph. She apologized for a noise in the background, moved somewhere quieter, and asked whether now still suited.

“Yes,” Eve said. “Thank you for agreeing.”

“I may not be able to tell you very much.”

“That's all right. I'd rather know what you remember than ask you to fill things in.”

There was a small pause.

“Good,” Nora said. “Because I remember the curtain perfectly, and some much more important things badly.”

Eve smiled despite her nerves.

“Leanne said the fabric you were carrying was table linen.”

“It was. I was trying not to put it on the floor. Somebody had just mopped.”

The detail made the person in the photograph become inconveniently real. Nora hadn't been standing there to preserve a clue. She had been trying to keep clean cloth clean.

Eve asked whether she had helped with animal care in the room.

“For part of that period. Short turns. Some evenings, some mornings. I didn't run the arrangement, and it wasn't my room.”

“Were you caring for an orange-and-white cat?”

“Yes. More than one animal passed through, but I remember the cat in the pictures Leanne showed me.”

Eve gripped the pen too tightly.

“Do you mean you recognize the markings, or that you remember a similar cat?”

“Both, I think. But I'd like you to look at the photographs I found. I don't want you relying on me sounding certain.”

Nora had two original image files from that period and a small print that showed the cat more clearly. She could send the files privately, with the unrelated people cropped from a copy while keeping the originals available to compare later. She also had a few notes from the turns she had covered. They were not a complete care record.

Eve asked permission before describing the information Daniel and Ada had already supplied. She kept it to the place, the dates supported by records, and Daniel's own account of feeding the cat at his step.

“I remember Daniel,” Nora said. “He fixed a wheel on a trolley we kept borrowing. I couldn't tell you what month without checking.”

“The invoice helped him place that.”

“That would be better than my memory.”

Eve looked at the blank space beneath her notes and felt an odd relief. Nora didn't seem to be auditioning to become the person who knew everything. Eve could stop auditioning to ask the perfect question.

They agreed Nora would send the two pictures and the relevant dates after the call. Any other people's contact details would remain private. If a note mentioned another carer, they would not turn it into permission to contact that person without asking.

Then Nora said, “He's still with you?”

“Yes.”

“How is he?”

Eve's prepared questions fell away.

“He likes the wrong cushion. He steals socks if we leave them where he can get them. He recently rejected a very expensive toy.”

Nora laughed, unexpectedly warm.

“That sounds familiar.”

“He's eating and doing his ordinary things. We had a normal check recently because I was concerned about a change I couldn't describe very well. There wasn't an evident problem that day.”

She didn't tell Nora about the strange sensitivity. The old caregiver had asked how the cat was, not acquired a right to every private uncertainty Eve held about him.

“I'm glad he has a settled place,” Nora said.

Eve swallowed.

“I'd like to hear what you remember. But I don't want this to become something you're obliged to keep doing because I asked once.”

“I'd like to help with the part I can.”

For a moment, neither spoke.

Dumpling came into the kitchen and rubbed against Eve's chair. She rested a hand on his back when he paused beneath it. He hadn't heard a magical summons through the phone. He had followed her into the room where she usually sat.

Nora asked whether Eve might want to meet.

The question tightened something beneath Eve's ribs before she could decide what she felt.

“We could talk in person,” Nora added. “Some of the papers would be easier to compare. It doesn't have to involve him.”

Eve looked down at the cat.

She had wanted a person from his past to become real. Now that a real person was offering to come closer, an unlovely part of her wanted to keep everything safely inside a folder.

“Can I think about the arrangement and reply tomorrow?” she asked.

“Of course.”

“Not because I don't want to meet you.”

“You don't have to explain it tonight.”

Eve thanked her for that more quietly than for the photographs.

After the call, she put the pen down and sat without moving until Julian came in from the other room. He had left her to the conversation she had chosen, not listened from the doorway.

“How was it?”

“Kind. Incomplete.”

“Both useful things to know.”

“She asked about meeting. Said it didn't have to include him.”

Julian sat opposite her.

“What would you prefer?”

“I don't know yet. I think I'm afraid she'll be important to him.”

The confession sounded smaller and less reasonable aloud. Eve hated it a little.

Julian didn't hurry to tell her that Dumpling would obviously prefer them.

“She might be,” he said.

“I know.”

“That doesn't tell us what the meeting would be like.”

“No.”

Eve stroked the cat once more, then let him walk away rather than holding him in place for reassurance.

The photographs arrived while they made dinner. Eve waited until they had eaten before opening them. One showed an orange-and-white cat beside a shallow bowl. The other showed the side of his face in better light. The visible markings were consistent with their clearer current images and with Daniel's photographs. The original-file dates preceded Eve's rescue and fell within the period already supported by the room records.

That made Nora's account worth following. It didn't prove every remembered scene or explain where the cat had gone afterwards.

Nora's accompanying note said she had kept a clearer paper photograph too, and would bring it if they met. She had not written a triumphant message announcing that she had found her cat.

Eve read that absence of a claim, then reminded herself not to turn absence into a legal conclusion. They could ask plainly what the contact meant rather than making the cat's affection settle it.

She sent a short question the following morning.

Nora answered directly: she had provided temporary care, had not been the cat's owner, and was not asking to take him. She wanted to help establish the limited history she actually knew and, if welcome, see that he was doing well.

Eve showed Julian the reply.

“I'd like her to come here,” she said. “For a short visit. Not to make him travel somewhere because we're curious.”

“All right. Do you want me there?”

“Yes. If you're available.”

They checked rather than assumed. The following Tuesday afternoon offered a clear hour when both were home without asking the clinic or Robin to cover an invented emergency. Their planned portrait session on Saturday could remain separate. There was no reason to combine a stranger's visit with cameras, clothing, or wedding preparation.

Eve wrote to Nora with the proposal. About an hour, with the option to stop earlier. They could compare photographs and the limited notes first. Dumpling would have his usual quiet place and might choose not to approach. No public filming, no expectation of an affectionate scene, and no disappointment directed at the cat if he preferred to sleep.

Nora replied that those arrangements suited her.

She asked whether she should bring something for him.

“Just the photographs and the relevant papers would be helpful,” Eve wrote. “We can keep his ordinary routine.”

The answer felt almost comically plain after all the emotion the possibility had raised.

She shared the address privately only after they had agreed the visit, checked the arrival details, and put the appointment in the household calendar. The public story remained exactly what it had been before: nothing. There was no teaser photograph or announcement that the mystery might finally be solved.

On Friday evening, Eve took the care folder down again, removed papers unrelated to the meeting, and put a blank sheet at the front for corrections. She would ask Nora to distinguish what she had seen from what she had been told. She would not bring a completed theory for her to endorse.

Printing the comparison pictures produced one small problem that had nothing to do with the past. The printer cropped a white patch at the edge of Dumpling's shoulder. Eve nearly accepted it because the face remained clear, then noticed that the missing edge was one of the details they wanted to compare.

She changed the setting and printed the complete frame.

On the table, she placed the two versions together before putting the cropped copy aside. The difference was ordinary and important. A photograph could look persuasive while omitting the thing that made it useful.

She wrote where each image had come from on a separate sheet, leaving the originals untouched. Beside the file dates she added what they actually established: when the photograph was recorded, not every night a cat slept in the room. The papers took less space after she stopped trying to make each one tell the whole story.

Julian brought over the current photographs they had chosen for comparison.

“Enough?” he asked.

“Yes.”

Eve closed the folder.

Dumpling was in his box, asleep on the blanket that had survived the move because they had made room for it. She looked at him for a moment without trying to imagine his reaction next Tuesday.

Then she set the folder on the shelf and went to help Julian finish dinner.

They had arranged a visit between people who wanted to speak. That was enough to promise. Whatever the cat did when the door opened would be allowed to remain his own.
