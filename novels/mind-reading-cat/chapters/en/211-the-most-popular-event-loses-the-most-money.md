# Chapter 211 — The Most Popular Event Loses the Most Money

The photograph showed a full room. The spreadsheet, unfortunately, had access to information outside the photograph.

Eve put them beside each other on the front table and wished she hadn't.

In the picture, taken with everyone's permission before the rain, the large Saturday program looked exactly as she had intended. Paper scenery framed the demonstration table. People leaned forward. A woman near the window was laughing at something Robin had said. Every place except one had sold.

On the screen, the same Saturday ended in red.

“That can't include the damaged stock,” Eve said.

“It doesn't.” Robin turned the receipt folder toward her. “The rain is separate.”

The quarter had closed. The following week had brought the end of the extra Sunday content clinic, a dinner Eve had actually reached on time, and a little less panic around the repaired window. None of those changes had made the accounts assemble themselves.

They were doing that now, during a paid administrative block, while the venue was closed to visitors.

Eve checked the formula.

The formula was offensively correct.

“Preparation?” she asked.

“Here.”

“Clearing?”

“Underneath.”

“Those hours look high.”

Robin opened the shift records. They did not sound angry. Eve thought she might have preferred anger; it would have given her something other than the numbers to resist.

There was the early furniture move. The printed packs needed sorting and stapling. The backdrop needed assembling, then moving because it blocked a cupboard. Afterwards came borrowed equipment, cleaning, stock checks, and three follow-up messages that belonged to the event rather than to the following week's ordinary administration.

Each piece was small enough to have felt negligible when she agreed to it.

Together they occupied most of a working day beyond the hours the customers saw.

“And your time,” Robin said.

Eve looked at the blank line.

“I haven't invoiced the business for that.”

“I know. We need to see it anyway.”

She took out her calendar. The first entry was easy: ninety minutes editing the explanation sheet. Then she remembered that she had started it again after dinner because the example photographs were unclear.

“That was another hour,” she said.

Robin waited while she entered it.

The number became worse.

Eve made herself record the supplier collection too. There was no benefit in pretending she had been casually passing the supplier with an empty afternoon and a folding cart.

Outside, someone stopped to read the opening times. Finding the door closed, they moved on. Eve watched them go with the unreasonable feeling that a potential answer had just walked away.

“Perhaps the next one needs more places,” she said.

Robin leaned back.

“We already set the capacity for this layout.”

“I meant after the rear section is cleared.”

“The old layout. The one in the photograph. It was nearly full.”

Eve looked at the picture again.

There was the passage they had fought to preserve. There was the space beside the entrance that could not become another paying chair merely because the accounts looked unpleasant. She could remember people moving through it comfortably, carrying coats and bags. That comfort had been part of the product too.

She closed the photograph.

“Fine. Higher ticket price.”

“Could be an option.”

Robin pulled a blank page closer instead of rejecting it. They calculated what price would cover the whole event, including reasonable preparation and clearing time. Then they compared it with actual booking inquiries, earlier cancellations, and the smaller activities people had chosen when they were offered alternatives.

The necessary increase was not impossible. It was large enough that Eve could not assume the same almost-full room would follow her there.

“We could test it,” she said.

“We could,” Robin agreed. “But I need to tell you something before we build the next calendar around that.”

Eve took her hand off the mouse.

“I can't keep doing these Saturdays in their current form.”

“You've been paid for the shifts.”

As soon as she said it, Eve heard the defensive edge.

“Yes,” Robin said. “And I want to keep being paid for work I can continue doing. The late changes use the time I arranged around the shift. Even when we add the hours properly, I can't make the rest of my weekend expand.”

Eve glanced at the calendar where she had already pencilled in the next three large programs.

Robin followed her eyes.

“I haven't agreed those dates.”

“I know.”

She had known. She had also felt better each time she looked at the pencilled blocks, as though her confidence might quietly become somebody else's availability.

“I shouldn't have put your name there yet.”

“Thank you.”

Eve removed it. The three pale blocks remained, suddenly less convincing.

For a while they worked without speaking. They separated exceptional repair costs from regular operating costs, so the leak could not be blamed for a format that had been tiring them before water entered the wall. They checked a supplier credit against the actual confirmation rather than counting the amount twice. They found that one small session had done poorly because only two people came. Another had earned a modest surplus without requiring anybody to carry scenery after closing.

The three-person instructions exchange in the temporary front corner had not rescued the month. It had covered its direct costs and paid time. Earlier small exchanges showed a similar pattern across several dates.

“It's so little,” Eve said, looking at the amount.

“It stays little on the cost side too.”

Robin set the small-session record beside the larger one.

The difference was not glamorous. One needed a table, a limited set of reusable samples, a host, and time to let people talk. The other required Eve to transform the room, persuade everyone to book quickly, and then take it apart again before the next ordinary booking.

She remembered the first time she had described the large program to Sophie. She had drawn the whole room on a paper napkin. People would come because the format was unlike anything else nearby. It would be hers, recognizable before anyone read the account name.

Now she could see how much of the decision concerned keeping that napkin alive.

“I like making it,” she said.

“I know.”

“Parts of it.”

Robin smiled a little. “I've noticed the difference between the parts.”

Eve laughed, then rubbed her forehead.

She liked the moment somebody understood the activity. She liked a good visual joke. She liked the warm noise of people settling into a room she had prepared. She did not like spending Friday night repairing scenery because she had sold Saturday as a completely different experience from the previous month.

A knock came at the glass.

The building manager stood outside with a document folder. Eve let him into the front room. He had the next drying-check appointment confirmed and wanted to arrange access without interrupting bookings.

She checked the calendar with him. The rear area would remain out of use until the checks and remaining work were finished. He did not offer a completion date merely because she had a large event to plan.

When he left, Eve put the appointment in before considering any activity.

For once, the empty block looked useful.

“Can we leave the next big date unsold?” she asked Robin.

“Yes.”

“The one already booked needs a separate decision. We can't quietly change what those people paid for.”

“We can talk to them once we know what we're actually offering.”

Eve nodded. She was not going to turn a full production into an intimate conversation circle and hope everyone appreciated the new language.

They made two practical changes that afternoon. New bookings for future large programs stayed closed while Eve reviewed the format. The existing booked date remained a commitment requiring either the described event under suitable conditions or a clear alternative and refund choice. The smaller exchanges kept their ordinary descriptions and capacity limits.

Before Robin gathered the receipts, Eve brought up the page advertising the next season. A bright button still invited people to register interest in three dates she had not staffed.

“That's not a booking,” she said, then stopped defending it. “But it makes the dates look decided.”

She changed the page to show that future dates were under review and removed the three dates from the inquiry form. The existing booking information stayed intact. One woman had already registered interest; Eve sent her a direct note explaining that no new date was confirmed and no payment had been taken.

It was a smaller action than announcing a new direction. It cost her the pleasant appearance of a full calendar immediately.

Robin checked the public page on their phone. “Now it says what we actually know.”

Eve nodded and closed the editing window.

Robin gathered the receipts into their folder.

“What do you need from me before my time ends?”

Eve wanted to say that they could stay until she felt sure.

Instead, she asked them to check the final cost comparison and mark any doubtful entries. They found one duplicated printing charge. Removing it improved the result a little, not enough to turn it into a different story.

After Robin left, Eve opened the photograph once more.

Nobody in it had been wrong to enjoy the day. The program hadn't been a fraud because she might stop making it. There were moments in that room she remained proud of, and costs she should have understood sooner.

She saved a copy beside the accounts without using it to argue with them.

At home, Julian was cutting an apple into uneven pieces.

“Bad meeting?” he asked when she put her bag down.

“Accurate meeting.”

“Worse.”

She took a piece of apple and told him what they had found. He listened without proposing a clinic appearance that would make the tickets easier to sell.

“The nearly full one loses the most,” she said. “Once I stop treating my evenings as an imaginary resource.”

“What do you want to do?”

“I want it to be a different answer.”

He nodded, which was kinder than telling her that the right answer would feel wonderful eventually.

Dumpling climbed onto the sofa, turned twice, and settled facing away from both of them. Eve could see one pink ear above the cushion. He had never shown any particular respect for her most successful promotional compositions.

“I haven't decided every detail,” she said. “But I think the old program has to end.”

Julian offered her the last apple slice.

She took it, then went to wash her hands. After dinner she would write the two customer messages that genuinely needed sending, within the time she had set aside. She would not redesign an entire business before bed.

On the kitchen table, her notebook lay open to the next month's calendar. Three pencilled Saturdays waited without Robin's name. Eve rubbed out two of the boxes, leaving the already-booked date visible.

The paper looked worn where she had pressed too hard.

She shut the notebook before drawing anything new there.
