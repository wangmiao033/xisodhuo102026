# Chapter 043 — Whose Thought Was It?

Mia arrived with a cardboard tube that was longer than the umbrella she had borrowed to protect it.

“It's only wet at one end,” she said.

“Which end?”

“The end I stopped protecting while I checked the other end.”

Eve took the tube and carried it to the kitchen, where they could unroll its contents without spreading rain across the sofa. Inside was a poster for the volunteer laundry rota, printed at Mia's expense after three people had mistaken last month's handwritten collection times for dates.

The new version was admirably clear from a distance.

Unfortunately, the bottom row had absorbed enough water to become an interpretation of Saturday.

Mia put her umbrella in the bath and came back looking at the curling paper.

“I can have another printed.”

“You can. Or we can find out whether the wet part contains anything we need.”

They spread clean towels under the poster and weighted the corners with mugs. Eve read through the times while Mia checked the original on her phone. Only the blank space below the last row had become illegible. They could trim it, once it dried, and put the whole sheet in the protective sleeve Mia had brought.

Mia exhaled, then began twisting the umbrella strap around one finger.

“There's something else,” she said. “They've asked me to explain the new rota before the collection this weekend. Just a minute or two. Could I try saying it to you?”

Eve had a delivery to finish before five, but the file was nearly done. She checked the time.

“I can listen now. Do you want me to tell you what's unclear, or just be a person in a chair?”

“Both, if the chair person isn't very frightening.”

“I'll lower my professional standards.”

Dumpling appeared in the doorway while Eve pulled out a chair. He stopped to investigate the umbrella smell on Mia's trouser cuff, then moved to the hall rug.

Mia looked down. “He remembers me.”

“Probably your laundry bags,” Eve said. “They bring a lot of information.”

For once she was content to mean ordinary smells.

They were halfway through the introduction when the doorbell rang. Sophie had texted earlier to ask whether she could leave her jacket at Eve's while collecting a bulky equipment case from a nearby repair shop. She was now wearing the jacket and carrying no case.

“It isn't ready,” she said. “They found another broken latch.”

“Do you want tea while you wait?”

“Please. If that's all right with the conference.”

Mia hurried to gather her papers. Sophie told her to leave them where they were and sat on the sofa, out of the way of the kitchen table. She had less of the worn-out smile Eve remembered, but her fingers kept working the tab on her bag's zip.

Eve filled the kettle. When she turned back, Dumpling had got up from the rug. He was looking at the apartment door.

There was nothing remarkable about that until he took two quick steps toward it, stopped, and looked over his shoulder at the room.

Sophie had just checked her phone.

Mia had stopped speaking in the middle of a sentence.

A familiar prickle moved across Eve's scalp. The cat's attention seemed to arrive at exactly the place where somebody had swallowed a thought.

She looked first at Sophie. Waiting for the repair shop? Wanting to get away from another conversation about work? Then Mia shifted her pages, and Eve saw that the corner of one was worn almost soft under her thumb.

Both women were tense. Eve herself had become tense watching them. Beyond the closed door, somebody might already be approaching the stairs. Dumpling's head turned slightly, as if he were following a sound too faint for her to distinguish.

Whose thought was she imagining he had noticed?

The question had no useful answer. She could choose whichever person fitted the story she had begun telling herself, but the cat wouldn't certify the choice.

The kettle clicked off. Eve reached for the mugs.

“Milk?” she asked Sophie.

“A little.”

“Mia?”

“None, thank you.”

The answers gave her something she could actually do.

As she carried the cups in, a trolley rattled along the landing outside. Dumpling withdrew from the door, passed behind the sofa, and slipped into his open carrier under the sideboard.

Mia watched him disappear.

“I haven't upset him, have I?”

“I don't know what sent him over there,” Eve said. “But you haven't crowded him or done anything I need you to stop.”

“Should I go?”

“No. He has somewhere quiet. We can leave him there.”

Eve shifted the nearest chair a little so the route out of the carrier remained clear. That was something visible she could improve. It didn't require deciding whether the trolley had explained everything or merely happened at a convenient moment.

Sophie tucked one foot beneath her and set her phone facedown on her knee.

“I'm waiting for a work message,” she said. “If I keep looking, that's why.”

“Okay.”

Eve didn't ask what message. Sophie had given them enough information to keep the glances from becoming a puzzle everyone needed to solve.

Mia returned to the table. She started her introduction again, reading at a speed that made the collection instructions sound like terms attached to a dangerous investment.

Eve let her reach the end before saying, “I understood the days. I lost who takes the bags afterward.”

“That's the next part.”

“How long is the next part?”

Mia showed her the second page.

“Oh,” Eve said.

Sophie made a small, sympathetic noise behind her teacup.

They laid out the pages beside the drying poster. Half the introduction explained why clean supplies mattered, which everyone attending already knew. Another paragraph apologized for the changes. The actual request had been squeezed into two lines near the bottom.

Eve pointed to those lines.

“Try starting here.”

Mia read them aloud. The new opening took less than fifteen seconds. She looked up with the alarm of someone who had just discovered she had packed for the wrong climate.

“That's hardly anything.”

“It tells me why I'm standing there.”

“What if they think I haven't prepared?”

Eve held up the large poster, careful of its damp corner.

“You've brought a portable wall of preparation.”

Mia laughed and sat back. Then, rather than begin again, she stared at the rota.

“They asked whether I could do a weekday collection too,” she said. “I said I would check.”

“Have you checked?”

“Yes. I can't.”

There was no mystery to interpret now. Mia's own voice had put the problem on the table, separate from every possibility Eve had imagined at the door.

“Do you want that in the introduction?” Eve asked.

“Probably. Otherwise I'll get asked again while everyone's there, and I'll say maybe because they're looking at me.”

Sophie leaned forward, elbows on her knees. “Would you like another chair person?”

Mia nodded.

Sophie stood, moved her cup to the counter, and took a chair beside Eve. She didn't become the production expert conducting a rescue. She sat with her bag on her lap while Mia tried the practical sentences she actually needed.

Mia stumbled once, then said she would continue Saturday deliveries but couldn't add Tuesdays. She left a space after the statement instead of using it to suggest impossible alternatives.

Eve found herself waiting too. The old itch to fill the gap was almost physical. She could name people who might cover it, or redesign the entire rota, or volunteer her own time before checking it.

Sophie asked, “Where should I put my name if I can do Tuesday?”

Mia pointed to the poster.

“Here. Or message the coordinator if you need to check first.”

The conversation moved on. Nothing caught fire in the silence.

When they returned the poster to the sleeve, the damaged lower margin folded neatly behind it. Eve noticed that the small-print contact line was hard to read through the plastic and fetched a black pen. Rather than reprint everything, Mia wrote the contact details in the blank space with large, careful letters.

The sleeve caught on one corner as they slid the sheet inside. Mia pulled harder; the paper began to buckle. Eve put a hand under the middle to support it while Sophie held the plastic open. For a moment the three of them were crowded around a task none could have described as important, moving their hands an inch at a time.

“Left,” Sophie said.

“My left?” Mia asked.

“The paper's left. Apparently it has its own.”

They got it in without tearing anything. Mia wiped the sleeve with the dry end of a towel, and the new contact line emerged darker through the plastic. Eve had imagined spending this visit giving good advice. Holding a piece of paper level had turned out to be equally necessary, and considerably easier to know she was doing correctly.

“It's less pretty,” Mia said.

Eve held it at arm's length.

“I can read it from here.”

Sophie stood beside her. “So can your oldest audience member.”

“You're not old.”

“I've spent this week being addressed as the responsible adult. Let me have the benefits.”

By the time Mia packed up, she was speaking at an ordinary speed. Her umbrella had stopped dripping. Eve wrapped the tube in a spare grocery bag for the journey and walked her to the door.

“Thank you,” Mia said. “I was about to change it all back.”

“Tell me how the explanation goes.”

“I will.”

After she left, Sophie checked her phone again, sighed, and stood. The repaired case was finally ready. The other message hadn't arrived; she said so without offering further detail.

“Do you need a hand carrying the case?” Eve asked.

“No. It has wheels. The repair shop has now restored its exciting ability to remain closed.”

Eve found her own shoes to walk as far as the building entrance. Sophie's bag slid from her shoulder as she fastened her jacket, and she caught it against her hip with a tired little grunt.

“Tomorrow afternoon,” she said. “Are you working?”

“Until one.”

“Could we go somewhere afterward? Outside, preferably. Somewhere that doesn't have a delivery entrance I need to locate.”

Eve thought of three pleasant routes and six questions.

“Yes,” she said. “You can pick the direction.”

Back upstairs, she finished her caption file before opening the notebook from the cup exercise. Dumpling had left the carrier and was sitting in the window. The trolley was long gone. Mia's nervousness had become a conversation. Sophie's remained partly private. The cat had supplied no labels for any of it.

Eve wrote only what belonged to her: *I watched a room with several people in it and chose the explanation I was already worrying about.*

She didn't list what Sophie might have been concealing. She didn't preserve Mia's difficulty as evidence of a successful prediction. There had been none.

Even if Dumpling caught something people couldn't ordinarily catch, she couldn't follow it backward to a named person and a complete sentence. His looking at a door gave her a door. The rest had come from her.

Outside, the rain returned, lightly enough to make the windows look dusty before the first drops began to run. Eve moved the emptied mugs from the table and put away the pen Mia had used.

Tomorrow she would go for a walk with her friend.

She didn't need to decide in advance what the walk would reveal.
