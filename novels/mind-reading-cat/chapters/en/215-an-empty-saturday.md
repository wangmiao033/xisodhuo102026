# Chapter 215 — An Empty Saturday

At nine on Saturday morning, Eve woke with the conviction that she had forgotten a room full of people.

She reached for her phone, found the edge of the pillow instead, and sat up.

The apartment was quiet. Julian was asleep beside her, one hand folded beneath his cheek. Through the partly open bedroom door, she could see Dumpling washing a shoulder in the patch of sunlight that had reached the hallway.

There was no event today.

She remembered arranging that. She remembered sending the notice, checking the bookings, and leaving the venue locked on Friday after the week's final small session. Remembering did not immediately convince her body.

She got up without opening the work messages.

In the kitchen, the cardboard ears leaned against an empty jar. Julian had refused to let her use them as the household reminder board on the grounds that he would feel watched by his own recent humiliation. She had put them there temporarily, which was how most household landmarks began.

Eve filled the kettle. Dumpling followed, checked the place where breakfast usually appeared, and looked at her.

“Yes. Your appointment remains in force.”

She fed him his usual breakfast, refreshed the water, and watched him settle to eat. The ordinary sounds made the kitchen seem less like a waiting room for something she had failed to do.

Her notebook lay closed on the table.

Friday's figures had not improved merely because the old program had ended. Fewer ticket sales meant less money coming through the account. Some staffing and supply costs had fallen too, but the rent remained entirely unimpressed by their new philosophy. The repair reserve needed rebuilding. She had not bought a new sign.

She had, however, finished two paid content assignments that week within the time she quoted. One invoice had been paid; the other had an agreed due date still ahead. She had entered them differently in the cash record instead of treating promised payment as money already available.

That had been yesterday's work.

Today the notebook could remain closed.

Julian appeared while she was making toast. His hair had flattened on one side and risen on the other, producing an effect that suggested the ears had left a permanent influence.

“Are we late?” he asked.

“No.”

He looked at the clock, then at her.

“That's unfamiliar.”

“I nearly apologized to the kettle.”

He took down two plates. “It's had plenty of notice.”

They ate at the table rather than standing in the kitchen. Eve had expected the free day to feel expansive. At first it felt awkwardly unallocated, like a budget line she had forgotten to name.

A spoon fell from the edge of Julian's plate. He caught it against his knee, transferred a stripe of jam to his trousers, and looked down with great disappointment.

“Now we have an activity,” Eve said.

“Laundry wasn't my first choice.”

“You were concealing a first choice?”

“Clean trousers.”

She fetched a damp cloth and passed it over. While he dealt with the jam, she began calculating how long a wash would take and whether they could fit a walk around it. Then she caught herself arranging their whole day around a stain smaller than a coin.

“The washing machine can have its own plans,” she said.

Julian looked up. “Has it joined a club?”

“I was about to organize everything.”

“Perhaps it enjoys surprises.”

She sat down again. He had removed most of the jam without scheduling a single journey. The remaining mark could survive until an ordinary wash. Eve spread her toast and let breakfast take as long as eating it required.

They washed the breakfast things, then drifted into separate small tasks. Julian put away dry laundry without asking her where every item belonged. Eve mended a loose loop on her bag, something she had carried past for weeks because it never became sufficiently urgent to compete with work.

The repair took twelve minutes.

She held the bag up, offended by how little trouble it had required once she actually sat down.

Dumpling climbed onto the empty chair and settled against the warm patch she had left. Eve put away the needle and thread in the closed sewing box, checking the floor before moving it beyond his reach.

By eleven, she wanted to look at the business phone.

Not because she had seen an alert. Because she hadn't.

She had left the usual opening hours and response times visible. Existing customers knew when to expect an answer. No session was running that needed remote support, and the building manager had the separate contact arrangement for a genuine premises problem. Robin was off duty too. There was no invisible person keeping the free day possible by doing their unpaid work elsewhere.

Still, Eve picked up her personal phone.

Sophie had sent a photograph of a sandwich collapsing over its wrapper, accompanied by a complaint about a production meeting. Eve replied about the sandwich, not about staffing the venue. They exchanged three increasingly unreasonable suggestions for keeping the filling inside.

When the conversation ended, she put the phone down.

Julian glanced over from his book.

“Emergency bread?”

“Advanced structural failure.”

“Outside my field.”

“Everyone's, apparently.”

They went out for a short walk before lunch. Eve did not take the camera. At the crossing, a family struggled to fold an enormous paper map, and she resisted offering them a better communication format. Julian bought apples. She bought nothing, which made their return journey surprisingly simple.

At home, Dumpling inspected the shopping bag and lost interest when it failed to contain either food for him or a satisfactory box.

They ate leftovers with the apples. Rain began against the window afterward, light enough to make staying indoors feel chosen rather than enforced. Eve moved the plates to the sink and returned to find Julian examining the cardboard ears.

“Don't put them on again,” she said. “I have limited reserves.”

“I was wondering whether they would fit in the recycling.”

“Not yet.”

He put them back. “Then we need an alternative occupation.”

They found a small box of word cards among the things unpacked after the move. The original instructions had disappeared. Eve remembered a guessing game in which one person described a word without saying it; Julian remembered enough rules to insist that pointing was cheating.

“Those are different games,” she said.

“We can agree on one.”

“Listen to us. Very mature. Extremely dull.”

They chose a modest version: one word, no pointing, no spelling it out, and no expectation that either of them would be good. Eve fetched paper for a score they both claimed not to care about.

Her first card said umbrella.

“A portable roof,” she said.

“Car.”

“For someone who has made less of an investment.”

“Hat.”

“You hold it.”

“An unusually troublesome hat.”

She laughed and showed him the card. He insisted he had been about to get it. She awarded him no points, which immediately made the score important.

Dumpling sat near the sofa, watching their hands.

Once, Eve might have expected a well-timed interruption to settle the joke. She noticed herself waiting for his gaze to shift toward the window or the umbrella stand. Instead, he bent to inspect something on the rug and sneezed once at a bit of dust.

Julian took the next card.

“An object which is largely purchased in order to lose its partner.”

“Earring.”

“Lower.”

“Sock.”

He showed her the card.

“One point,” she said.

“Your turn was not objectively harder.”

“Mine involved meteorology.”

The next few rounds went worse. Eve described a ladder so elaborately that Julian guessed a promotion. He described a pineapple with the grave precision of somebody giving evidence about an assault. She failed to recognize a clock while sitting beneath one.

Their score acquired several crossed-out numbers and an argument about whether giving the answer away while laughing counted as successful communication.

Dumpling remained interested in neither their triumphs nor their mounting accusations of incompetence.

He had settled beneath the closed cupboard where his wand toy was stored, entirely out of reach between supervised games. Eve put the cards down and opened the cupboard herself. Once she had the whole toy in her hand, he rose to follow its movement. She checked that the attachment was secure before sitting on the rug.

“You would prefer a game with a visible opponent,” she told him.

He crouched.

She moved the soft lure across the clear floor, allowing him to stalk it and catch it rather than jerking it away endlessly. He sprang, landed with both front paws around it, and looked briefly astonished by his own competence.

Julian sat on the sofa watching.

“Full marks,” he said.

“He understands the assignment.”

Dumpling released the lure, chased it again, then lay on his side with one paw resting on it. Eve let him finish before collecting the whole toy and storing it out of reach. No loose cord remained on the floor.

She sat back against the sofa.

“He hasn't done that thing today,” she said.

“Which thing?”

“The oddly exact interruption. When we're thinking very loudly.”

Julian considered the cat, who had begun washing a hind foot.

“I wasn't watching for it.”

“Neither was I. Mostly.”

She could not give a useful example of a missing event. They had played a silly game, laughed, and failed to communicate the word pineapple. Dumpling had been an ordinary nearby cat throughout. That didn't establish what he could or couldn't sense.

Eve looked at the cards scattered on the table.

For an instant she imagined choosing a particularly familiar object and concentrating, just to see. The urge was small and recognizable: uncertainty asking to be converted into a task.

She left the cards where they were.

“He ate normally,” she said. “He's been playing. Nothing else I've noticed is different.”

“Then we can keep noticing how he's doing normally,” Julian said. “We don't need to turn the afternoon into an experiment.”

Eve nodded. He hadn't offered a medical conclusion about an unmeasurable ability, and she hadn't asked him to.

Dumpling finished washing, stood up, and stepped over her ankle to reach his box. He turned twice before settling. Neither turn supplied an answer.

The rain softened. Eve could hear someone moving a chair in the apartment above, followed by a brief burst of laughter. Their own room smelled faintly of toast and the apple peel still on a plate.

“Another round?” Julian asked.

“Only if we abolish points.”

“Because you're losing?”

“Because this household deserves peace.”

He handed her a card.

She read it, looked at him, and began to laugh before she could speak. The word was rabbit.

Julian followed her glance toward the cardboard ears and sighed.

“I should have recycled those when I had the chance.”

Eve tried to give a clue and failed. Dumpling slept through the entire performance.

By evening, they had made no photograph worth posting and no plan that would earn money. Dinner was simple. The work notebook remained closed, ready for Monday rather than calling her back to it through the weekend.

Before bed, Eve checked the cat's water and found him occupying the cushion she had intended to use. She sat elsewhere. There was room.

Tomorrow she might notice something different. Tonight, nobody needed to demonstrate a special talent before the lights went out.
