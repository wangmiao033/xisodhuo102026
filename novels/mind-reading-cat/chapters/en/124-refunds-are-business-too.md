# Chapter 124 — Refunds Are Business Too

Leah's message arrived while Eve was spreading jam on toast.

Thank you for yesterday. I think I misunderstood the event. I wanted help turning stories about my cat into posts, and the session wasn't the right fit. I'd like to use the trial refund, please.

A perfectly reasonable message. Eve read it twice, then put enough jam on the toast to suggest she was repairing a wall.

She had known the request might come. She had offered the refund in person. Knowing did not prevent the first small, unpleasant thought: Leah had stayed for the whole session and received a useful instruction sheet.

The second thought was worse. Perhaps if Eve offered a short content consultation instead, Leah would be happy and the accounts would still show three paid places.

She carried the toast to the table and opened the booking terms.

A refund for poor fit, requested by the end of the following day. Not a refund after proving that nothing useful had happened. Not a refund unless the host could persuade the customer to accept a different service.

Eve put the toast down.

She answered that morning, confirming a full return of the ticket price through the original payment method. She did not deduct the payment charge, which the service would retain, or ask Leah to complete an explanation form first.

The refund button required a reason for Eve's own records. She selected the appropriate category, checked the booking twice, and completed it.

A confirmation appeared.

She sent Leah the reference and the provider's stated processing time, with a simple note that no further action was needed from her. Then, in a separate sentence, she asked whether Leah would be willing to point out which wording had led her to expect content coaching. Optional; the refund was already arranged.

When the message was sent, Eve looked at the accounts.

Two retained ticket fees. One returned. Room hire, materials, and charges unchanged.

The trial had cost more than the three-person version she had first calculated, but less than the maximum amount she had set aside. She entered the true number, including preparation and clearing time, and resisted renaming the loss an investment in bold letters. It could be an experiment without receiving a flattering title.

The toast was cold.

Dumpling sat opposite her, looking at the plate with a professional interest he had not earned.

"No," she said. "This is a poor fit for you too."

He settled his chin on the chair back.

Leah replied after lunch. She had attached a screenshot of the announcement as it appeared in the account's feed, with a line drawn beneath owner-story exchange.

I read this with your account in mind. I thought we'd work on stories for an audience. I should have looked more carefully at the full page, but the first bit made me think of that.

Eve opened the screenshot on her larger screen.

She had spent so long refining the full booking page that she had neglected the little piece most people would see first. The feed preview cut off before the plain description of everyday arrangements. Above it sat the account name people associated with videos and photographs.

Nothing in the preview explicitly promised a content workshop. Nothing in it clearly prevented that interpretation either.

She wrote back to thank Leah for the specific example and asked if she could use the feedback privately to revise the wording, without quoting her publicly. Leah agreed.

Eve opened a new version of the announcement.

The first draft overcorrected so severely that it sounded like instructions for surviving a dangerous appliance. No animals. No filming. No medical advice. No content training. No guaranteed outcome.

She read it aloud and closed her eyes.

Nobody had asked to spend seventy-five minutes being told what couldn't happen.

She began with the actual activity instead: A small discussion for pet owners who want to compare everyday routines, organize useful information, and leave with one practical next step.

Below that she added a concrete example. The first trial had included ways to keep shared feeding notes clear and make short care-visit instructions easier to use. No names, no private details, and no claims that those ideas suited every household.

Then she stated the limits in an ordinary paragraph. The activity did not include veterinary consultation, animal training, or advice on making social-media content. Animals stayed at home. Participants could speak or listen.

The preview now contained the actual service before the reader reached the link.

She viewed it on her phone, where the line broke awkwardly after pet. She shortened two words and tried again.

This was the work she had expected to do for clients, not for her own slightly bruised pride. The distinction made it easier to continue.

In the afternoon she sent the promised blank sheets to all three participants individually. Leah received hers too. The refund had not canceled a promise Eve had already made about materials.

She included two short feedback questions: What was useful enough to keep? What was missing or unclear? There was no request for a public rating, no suggestion that supporting a small business required generosity.

Ruth answered first.

The card idea helped us start a conversation. We changed the words when I got home. I liked knowing when we'd finish. I'd try another session if the topic suited me.

Eve smiled at we changed the words. Ruth had not framed the card, admired its creator, or waited for permission to make it her own. It was already doing a job in a kitchen Eve would never see.

Ben answered later. The appointment arrangement was in hand through his practice. He found the information sheet useful but wasn't sure he needed another group soon. He would keep the template.

Eve had wanted three glowing reports and three immediate repeat bookings. Instead she had one possible return, one useful completed purchase, and one refund.

The evidence was considerably less photogenic than ambition.

That evening she met Sophie for the walk they had arranged. Eve wore shoes that had looked comfortable in the shop and began arguing with her heels within ten minutes. Sophie noticed her shortening stride.

"New?"

"Deceptive."

They stopped at a bench. Eve adjusted one sock while Sophie took a folded leaflet from her bag and used it to brush a wet leaf from the seat.

"How was your week?" Eve asked.

Sophie had attended an information session about a programming role with clearer hours than the previous one. She hadn't decided whether to apply. The interesting part was the mix of ongoing activities and short projects; the doubtful part was how much evening cover the team actually shared.

Eve listened, asked what the staff had said, and did not turn the leaflet into a job application over Sophie's knee.

When Sophie asked about the pilot, Eve told her the numbers.

"Three came. One refund. Two useful sheets now living in the world without me. Possibly three."

"How do you feel about the refund?"

"Better after doing it. Before doing it, briefly like a person I wouldn't want to buy anything from."

Sophie smiled. "A feeling isn't an invoice."

"It nearly became a free consultation."

"Did she ask for one?"

"No. She asked for her money."

"Then that sounds clearer."

They got up and walked more slowly. Eve described the preview problem, not because she needed Sophie to solve it, but because it was embarrassing in a way her friend would understand. She had checked the whole page and missed the doorway into it.

"That happened with our session description," Sophie said. "We kept explaining the middle. People hadn't got that far."

"I remember. Your tutor asked whether anyone knew what they'd actually be doing."

"Something like that. Less politely, after the third version."

They passed a small shop displaying lamps in a window. One had a base shaped like a bird with a shade large enough to conceal its head.

"Worst object?" Eve asked.

Sophie considered it. "Strong contender. But perhaps the bird owes someone money."

They spent the next few minutes inventing a modest criminal history for the lamp. Eve's heel still hurt. The trial accounts remained disappointing. Neither problem prevented her from enjoying the walk.

At home she found a message from the neighborhood center asking when she expected to send the trial summary. The coordinator was reviewing room use later that week.

Eve set a delivery time she could meet and opened a document.

She reported the actual attendance, the timing, the activities that worked, the mismatch that led to a refund, and the health question she had directed outside the session. She included her clearing time and the fact that she had needed no additional room or staff assistance beyond the normal hire arrangements.

In the next section, she described what she would change: clearer first lines, a narrower practical topic, the same small maximum, and an explicit route for questions outside her scope.

Then came the financial result.

For a moment she considered omitting it. The center had asked about the session, not her accounts. But if she was proposing another trial, the scale mattered. She included a brief statement that this one had run at a planned, limited loss, increased by the refund, and that another session would need a minimum paid attendance she could sustain. She did not submit a fantasy full-house projection in its place.

She stopped before adding a request for a regular room. The feedback supported trying again with changes. It did not support pretending she had found a permanent business model in a box of pens.

Before sending it, she opened the calendar beside the accounts. The first trial had occupied a much larger patch of the week than its seventy-five minutes suggested. Interviews, a venue visit, the booking page, printing, replies, and clearing had all happened somewhere. She marked which tasks would recur and which belonged only to setting it up.

A second session could use the existing sheets. It would still need preparation, paid room time, and attention afterward. She reserved a single working block for those jobs instead of scattering them invisibly across evenings.

That left one fewer slot she could offer a client. She checked the coming deadlines before committing the time, and moved the possible trial to a quieter week. A workable experiment had to fit among the work she already had. She would not discover its real cost by making every other promise late.

The summary went out the following morning.

Leah sent a short message that afternoon: the refund had appeared. Thank you for making it straightforward.

Eve checked the payment record, marked the return complete, and closed the entry.

No persuaded customer. No triumphant recovery of a sale. One cleanly finished transaction, which left neither person owing the other an emotional explanation.

She moved the spare printed sheets into a folder for reuse and found the failed pen from the session at the bottom of her bag. For no good reason, she tried it again.

It produced a vigorous black line.

"Of course," she said.

Dumpling opened one eye from the sofa.

Eve wrote a label for the folder before the pen could reconsider. Everyday owner exchange. Trial materials.

Then she put it away and returned to the client work that was paying for the next careful attempt.
