# Chapter 226 — Whose Wedding Template?

The first wedding venue arrived before Eve had finished telling her mother she was engaged.

It appeared on her phone as a photograph of an enormous staircase, followed by three messages about how beautifully people could stand on it.

“We could,” Eve said. “But we haven't decided what we're doing.”

“You should see the staircase.”

“I'm looking at it.”

“In person.”

Julian, sitting at the other end of the kitchen table while waiting to make his own family call, looked at her face and quietly moved the bread nearer.

Eve took a piece.

“I don't want to arrange viewings yet,” she said. “I wanted to tell you the news.”

There was a brief silence, then her mother's voice softened.

“I am happy, you know.”

“I know.”

“I can be happy and useful.”

“Today, just happy would be lovely.”

Her mother laughed. They stayed on the phone long enough to discuss the proposal properly, without turning every detail into evidence for a preferred wedding style. Eve described dinner and the private question at home. She did not send a photograph of the ring until she and Julian had chosen one they were comfortable sharing.

By lunchtime, his family had proposed a garden, somebody else's cousin had recommended a hotel, and a friend had sent a picture of a ceremony beside a lake.

“Three opportunities to fall over,” Eve said, surveying the locations.

“The hotel has carpet,” Julian replied.

“A dangerous confidence.”

They thanked people. They didn't reserve anything.

For the first several days, that felt easy. Happiness arrived in ordinary forms: Sophie's startled laugh on the phone, Tessa remembering to ask how Eve was before joking about whether Julian had used complete sentences, a message from Mabel containing congratulations and a warning not to choose a meal nobody could identify.

At the jeweler they checked the ring properly and arranged the adjustment it needed. Eve liked being able to ask practical questions while Julian stood beside her without looking as though they were dismantling the proposal. The ring did not have to hurt to be a meaningful object.

Then the suggestions began to acquire dates.

A hotel could hold a possible Saturday for a limited time. The garden was prettier in a particular season. Someone knew a photographer whose calendar was filling, and someone else knew someone cheaper, though nobody had actually asked what cheaper included.

Eve opened a document.

It was only to keep the messages in order.

By the following Sunday, it had six columns.

Julian found her at the table comparing the distance between the staircase and the dining room on a floor plan.

“Are we going there?” he asked.

“No.”

“Then why are you measuring it?”

“The route looks awkward.”

He stood behind her chair for a moment.

“Would you like to go there?”

Eve stared at the screen.

She had not put that question in a column.

“I don't know,” she said. “I think I'd like everyone to stop sending me places.”

“Have you asked?”

“I've thanked them.”

“That may have produced the wrong impression.”

She leaned back, annoyed because he was right and because he had managed to be right in such a small number of words.

On the floor, Dumpling was investigating a shopping bag Julian had put away badly. Julian removed it before the cat could become tangled in the handles, folded it, and returned it to its proper place. Then he sat opposite Eve.

“Can we begin without the places?” he asked.

“With what?”

“Who we'd like to be there.”

She closed the document reluctantly. The columns had been reassuring. They gave her problems she knew how to solve: access, timings, capacity, a route that didn't send everybody through the same narrow door at once. Wanting something felt less professional.

They took two sheets of paper.

Neither wrote supplier at the top.

Eve began with the people she would want to see even if the occasion supplied no photographs. Julian did the same. They compared the lists after a few minutes, finding several overlaps and some names the other needed explained.

“There are people I'd want to tell,” Eve said, “but that doesn't automatically mean inviting them to a whole day.”

“Agreed.”

“And customers aren't a social category I can invite collectively.”

“No.”

“I know that. I'm saying it before I accidentally invent a public reception at the venue.”

Julian looked up sharply.

“You were considering that?”

“For about four seconds.”

“An energetic four seconds.”

She laughed. Then she crossed out a note that said possible community celebration, because what she had imagined was mostly herself arranging chairs, making signs, and pretending to enjoy being the host.

They didn't decide that a large wedding was foolish. Julian had enjoyed one recently enough to describe the enormous, joyful noise of it. Eve remembered a friend's formal wedding at which the careful arrangements had helped an elderly guest take part comfortably. Expense and size did not determine whether something belonged to the people choosing it.

They asked what would belong to them.

“I want to hear what you're saying,” Eve said.

“During the ceremony?”

“During the whole day. I don't want to speak to you in three intervals between managing other people.”

“I'd like a meal where we can sit down.”

“Radical.”

“And I'd like not to be responsible for judging whether everyone is enjoying it.”

“That may require you to take my phone away.”

“I'd rather you chose to put it away.”

She drew a little box around meal, then stopped herself making a timetable.

They talked about the parts they would remember: familiar voices, food they liked, a place people could reach without an expedition, enough space for someone to leave a noisy room. They wanted the necessary formal arrangements handled correctly, not a pretend ceremony invented because paperwork was boring. They would check the actual requirements separately before setting dates.

The lake photograph fell toward the bottom of the possibilities. Neither wanted an outdoor plan whose success depended entirely on the weather. The staircase didn't survive much longer. Eve could admire it without hiring a building to accommodate admiration.

A small ceremony and a meal began to look like their preference, not merely the remains after everything glamorous had been rejected.

Julian put his pen down.

“Would that disappoint you?”

“Which part?”

“Not having something you could make spectacular.”

The question made Eve unexpectedly quiet.

She did know how to make an ordinary room look impressive in a photograph. She could already imagine the lighting and a composition in which nobody saw the folded chairs waiting nearby. Part of her wanted to prove she could produce the beautiful day people would expect from somebody in her line of work.

“I might miss making it,” she admitted. “That isn't quite the same as wanting to spend my wedding doing it.”

He nodded.

“I'd like to marry the person at the table. Not hire her.”

“You couldn't afford her wedding rate.”

“I suspected.”

Eve laughed, and the document with six columns lost some of its authority.

Before putting the lists away, Julian noticed that Eve had written one name twice, once under friends and once beside possible help.

“Which invitation is that?” he asked.

She followed his finger to Sophie.

“Friend,” she said immediately.

“Then perhaps we should remove the second one.”

Eve crossed it out. She had not even assigned a task. The duplication had happened because she could picture Sophie making an entrance work, finding a missing supplier, rescuing a badly timed meal. It was a picture of competence she had mistaken for a gift already offered.

“If she wants to help, we can discuss it,” Eve said. “But I want her there even if she does absolutely nothing useful.”

Julian put a small mark beside the name, indicating the invitation they actually meant.

They left room for travel needs and accessibility questions without attempting to answer them for anybody. The list looked less complete afterwards. It was also less dishonest.

They took a break before telling anybody else their tentative decision. Julian made lunch. Eve put away the papers and sat on the sofa for ten minutes without reviewing the guest list. Dumpling climbed onto her lap, turned awkwardly, and settled with one hind foot against her stomach.

There was nothing ceremonially elegant about it.

She rested her hand beside him until he was comfortable.

Later, she called her mother.

“We're leaning toward a small ceremony and a meal,” she said. “We haven't booked or chosen the date. Please don't hold the hotel for us.”

Her mother sighed.

“They wouldn't charge for holding it.”

“I understand. We still don't want it held.”

“What about the relatives who'd expect to come?”

“We're making the list ourselves. I'll talk to you about the people we're considering, but I don't want anyone invited on our behalf.”

The conversation was less smooth than Eve had hoped. Her mother thought a smaller celebration might look as though they were reluctant to include people. Eve explained that they were choosing a size they could enjoy and afford, not ranking everyone's affection. That explanation didn't remove every disappointment.

At one point her mother said, “I only wanted you to have a proper day.”

Eve looked toward Julian, who was making his own call in the bedroom. She did not summon him to rescue hers.

“So do I,” she said. “This is how we're starting to work out what proper means for us.”

After a pause, her mother asked what food they were considering.

“Actual dinner,” Eve replied. “No decisions beyond that.”

“Well, that is an improvement over standing on a staircase.”

Eve laughed with relief.

When the calls were finished, she and Julian compared what they had actually said, not the more graceful versions they wished they had delivered. His family wanted advance notice for travel. Hers wanted to know whether helping was still allowed.

“Specific help, when we ask,” Eve said.

“Yes.”

“And people are allowed to enjoy making suggestions.”

“Yes.”

“We just aren't obliged to build every one.”

He reached for her hand across the table.

The next day at work, Robin congratulated her again and asked whether she would need leave when they had dates.

“I'll give you notice,” Eve said. “And we'll arrange cover properly. This doesn't become an extra job for you.”

“Good. I'm exceptionally bad at folding decorative napkins.”

“Then you're safe from my relatives.”

They returned to the small-session preparations. Eve approved an ordinary print run, answered a client's question, and left the wedding document closed during paid work.

That evening she opened it at home and deleted four of the six columns.

In their place she wrote three plain headings: people, practical needs, things we actually want.

Beneath the last one she put dinner with Julian.

He read over her shoulder.

“That can probably be arranged.”

“It had better be. It's the strongest item on the brief.”

He kissed her cheek, and she closed the laptop before the brief could become a production she would have to supervise instead of attend.
