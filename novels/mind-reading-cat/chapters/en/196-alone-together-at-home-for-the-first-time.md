# Chapter 196 — Alone Together at Home for the First Time

By eleven in the morning, Eve had offered Julian a walk, a film, a trip to the market, and the opportunity to help her sort a drawer neither of them used.

He accepted a piece of toast.

She stood beside the table holding the jar of jam. Outside, somebody was reversing a van with a series of patient beeps. Inside, the washing machine had finished its last spin. There was no immediate reason to stand there holding jam.

“Do you want to do something?” she asked.

Julian swallowed. “I'm eating this.”

“Afterward.”

“I thought I might read.”

“Oh.”

He looked at her. “Did we have a plan?”

“No. That's what I'm asking.”

Their old homes had been handed back. The last meter readings, keys, and boxes had ceased to require decisions. Dumpling now crossed the living room openly, although a dropped shoe still sent him behind the sofa. Most of Eve's work things occupied their intended shelf. The remaining box, marked MISCELLANEOUS, was becoming a permanent piece of furniture through force of indifference.

For the first time since moving, they both had a whole day off and nowhere else to be.

Eve had expected this to feel wonderful. Instead, she felt faintly as though six guests were due and she had forgotten the crackers.

Julian put his plate in the kitchen and returned for the book he had left face down beside his chair.

“Would you rather go out?” he asked.

“I don't know. It's a nice day.”

“It can be a nice day while we're in here.”

She glanced at the window. A narrow patch of sunlight lay across the floor. Dumpling was approaching it with more certainty than she had brought to any suggestion that morning.

“I thought we should make use of it.”

Julian turned the book over in his hands. “Eve, you don't have to host today.”

“I know you live here.”

“I was hoping you'd noticed.”

That brought a smile, but only a small one. She set the jam down.

“When we used to have a whole day together, we'd arrange something.”

“Sometimes.”

“Usually something with a time and a place.”

“We've managed the place.”

He gestured around the apartment. The sagging sofa stood beneath an unremarkable stretch of wall. A clean towel hung over the back of her dining chair because she had carried it out intending to put it away and then become distracted. Their home looked unprepared for a significant romantic occasion.

Julian held out his free hand. She took it, and he drew her close enough to kiss her forehead.

“I'd like to be here with you,” he said. “I also want to find out what happens to this very unpleasant man on page eighty.”

“Is he a colleague?”

“No. He owns a fictional hotel.”

“That sounds safer.”

He kissed her mouth this time, then went to his chair.

Eve put away the towel. She carried the jam into the kitchen. She checked the washing machine, although it had finished and nobody had implied it might start a second life without her.

From the living room came the sound of a page turning.

She could have opened her laptop. There was always something to tidy at the venue, and a client had supplied captions that needed attention on Monday. Working would immediately make this hour explicable. She stopped with her hand on the workroom door.

On the desk lay a paperback Sophie had lent her before the move.

Eve had moved it between three bags and two addresses. A receipt marked page twenty-three. It concerned a woman who had inherited a house and immediately discovered that the roof was wrong, which at present sounded less relaxing than Sophie had promised.

She took it to the sofa anyway.

The left cushion swallowed her at an angle. She fetched the folded blanket Julian had been using to prop it up, adjusted it, and sat again. Dumpling watched the operation from the sunny floor, then shut his eyes without offering professional advice.

For several minutes Eve read the same paragraph.

Julian shifted in his chair. She looked up.

He reached for his mug.

She looked down.

A bus passed outside, quieter here than it had been beside that beautiful apartment they had rejected. Eve read about a broken gutter. Julian made a small disgusted sound.

“What?” she asked.

“He's stolen the money.”

“Which man?”

“The hotel owner.”

“Already? You only started disliking him at breakfast.”

“He's efficient.”

She laughed and returned to her own roof. Three pages later, the woman in the book discovered something in a locked cupboard, and Eve forgot to listen for the next page turning across the room.

When she next looked up, the sunlight had moved off Dumpling's paws.

He rose, walked a few inches, and lay down again.

Eve checked the time. It was nearly one.

For a ridiculous instant she felt guilty. They had been given a rare shared morning and spent it failing to speak. Then she remembered Julian laughing at something she had read aloud half an hour earlier. There had been a conversation. She simply hadn't planned it in advance or recorded whether it justified the occasion.

“Lunch?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“What do you want?”

“What's left?”

They investigated together. There was bread, a little cheese, and the remains of yesterday's roast vegetables. Eve suggested warm sandwiches. Julian took down a pan.

“There's the toastie maker,” she said.

“Where?”

They both looked toward the miscellaneous box.

It contained two picture frames, an extension lead, a scarf, and the toastie maker. Eve lifted the appliance by its handle and felt something rattle inside. A teaspoon fell out when she opened it.

“Is that one of the bathroom spoons?” Julian asked.

“They've moved on.”

They washed the plates of the machine and set it on a clear stretch of counter. Julian assembled two sandwiches. Eve tried to close the lid and watched cheese squeeze out of one corner.

“That seems confident,” he said.

“It will flatten.”

“It already has. Sideways.”

She removed some filling. He ate the displaced piece of pepper before she could find a plate for it.

While the sandwiches cooked, Eve leaned against the counter. She could see the blue cup on the table, full of water today rather than hardware. Beyond it stood his reading chair and her uneven pile of cushions. Nothing needed to be arranged for somebody's arrival.

“I'm still doing it,” she said.

“Overfilling the sandwich?”

“Checking whether you're having a good time.”

He looked at her. “Are you?”

She thought about the cupboard in her book. “Yes. I think I forgot for a while that I was supposed to be checking.”

“I had a very good morning.”

“You barely moved.”

“I've wanted to barely move for several days.”

The appliance clicked. Eve opened it too early and lifted one sandwich with the bread still pale. She shut it again.

“Your book's annoying you,” she said.

“In an enjoyable way.”

“So you'd like more of that?”

“Yes.”

They ate beside the window, the sandwiches finally crisp enough to deposit crumbs on their laps. Julian told her he used to read the endings of mysteries when he was younger because he couldn't bear not knowing.

“That's terrible,” she said.

“I stopped.”

“Because you developed moral character?”

“Because I guessed one correctly and wanted to see whether I could do it again.”

She considered this. “Vanity has done some useful work.”

“Occasionally.”

After lunch they cleared the plates and opened the window only to its checked, secure setting. Air moved through the room. Eve went to wash her face and returned to find Julian asleep in the chair, one finger still inside his book.

She nearly woke him to suggest moving somewhere more comfortable. His head rested against the cushion, his shoulders loose. The decision could wait until he made it himself.

She took her book into the workroom and sat in her old chair with her feet tucked beneath her. The desk lamp was off. Without the laptop open, the desk offered a surprisingly good place for a cup of tea and nothing else.

For an hour, the room belonged to her reading rather than her deadlines.

Then a kitchen cupboard clicked, and Julian appeared at the doorway.

“Tea?”

“I've got some.”

“That was an inadequate opening. I'd like to sit with you for a minute.”

“Oh. Yes.”

He perched on the spare chair and rubbed the back of his neck.

“Bad sleeping position?”

“Possibly an ambitious reading position.”

She put down the book. His hair stood up on one side. There was a faint crease on his cheek from the cushion. She liked him with both features and without an activity to justify keeping him nearby.

“How's the roof?” he asked.

“Still wrong. But there's a letter.”

“Promising.”

“Your criminal hotelier?”

“I'm beginning to think his wife did it.”

“Stole the money?”

“Everything.”

He stayed until his tea cooled enough to drink, then returned to the living room. Eve read the letter. Beyond the open door, Dumpling began batting something against the skirting board.

She checked. It was the cloth mouse, not a packing fastener, and the cat abandoned it as soon as he saw her watching. She went back to her chair before his private entertainment became a performance.

Late in the afternoon, hunger drew her out again. Julian had finished his book and was looking betrayed.

“The wife?” she asked.

“The brother.”

“Was there a brother?”

“Apparently there was a brother on page twelve.”

She sat on the sofa and patted the cushion beside her. He came over, sinking slightly toward her when the left side gave way.

“Furniture-assisted affection,” she said.

“We should mention it in the review.”

For a while she listened to his account of the implausible ending. Then she read him a passage she had enjoyed, stumbling once because she was laughing. He waited without taking the book from her.

When the room dimmed, neither got up immediately to turn on a light.

Eve could hear a child in the building asking someone where a shoe had gone. A pan clattered in another kitchen. Dumpling climbed into his familiar cardboard box, circled with painstaking care, and settled facing away from them.

“We could go out for bread,” she said.

“We could.”

“Or eat what's here and go tomorrow.”

“Also possible.”

She leaned against him. The day had produced no photograph, no completed household project, and no particularly good story to tell anyone else. She had read nearly half a book. He had been mildly cheated by another. Lunch had tasted better after they stopped overfilling it.

“I'd like another day like this,” she said.

Julian rested his cheek against her hair. “We live here. Our chances are fairly good.”

She smiled and reached for the lamp.

In the new light, the room looked exactly as untidy and ordinary as it had that morning. This time she left it that way.
