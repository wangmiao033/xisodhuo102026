# Chapter 198 — Whose Side Is the Cat On?

Dumpling stepped over Eve's outstretched hand and climbed onto Julian's lap.

“Of course,” she said.

Julian looked up from the two curtain samples spread over his knees. “What?”

“The witness has chosen.”

“The witness is standing on the blue one.”

“Exactly. Your blue one.”

Dumpling turned twice, displacing both samples, and settled with his tail over Julian's wrist. Eve sat on the floor beside the coffee table, holding the yellow fabric she preferred. It was a warm, cheerful yellow in the shop. In their living room, against the kitchen tiles visible through the doorway, it had developed the confidence of a warning sign.

Julian had said this more tactfully.

Eve had nevertheless defended it for seven minutes.

“I'm not choosing curtains on his advice,” she said.

“I hadn't suggested it.”

“You look smug.”

“I have a paw in my tea.”

He lifted the cup before Dumpling could investigate further. Eve laughed, but the joke left a small, unreasonable sting behind.

This was the third time in two days that the cat had gone to Julian while they disagreed about something.

Yesterday, Julian had asked whether the small speaker could stay in the workroom instead of playing while he read. Dumpling had immediately followed him to the reading chair. Later, they had differed about where to hang a framed photograph, and the cat had rubbed against Julian's ankles while Eve stood holding it against the opposite wall.

Neither disagreement had been large. The speaker had moved, the photograph was still unhung, and no one had slept on the sofa. Yet Eve kept noticing the orange-and-white addition to his side of the room.

She waggled a corner of the yellow fabric.

Dumpling watched it briefly, then tucked his nose beneath his paw.

“Excellent taste,” Julian murmured.

“In curtains or humans?” she asked.

He looked at her more carefully.

“I meant sleeping through a discussion about curtains.”

“Oh.”

She put the sample down.

Outside, the morning was bright but cool. Their existing curtains worked perfectly well; the fabric samples had come home after an errand for a replacement lampshade. They could return every piece without losing anything except Eve's vision of a room filled with golden light.

At present the golden light looked mostly like mustard.

“Perhaps another color,” she conceded.

Julian touched the yellow with one finger. “We could see it in the other room.”

“Don't rescue it. I've made a convincing case for something ugly before.”

“Your career contains many challenges.”

She flicked the edge of the sample at his sock. Dumpling lifted his head at the movement, ears turning toward her, then jumped down and walked away.

Eve watched him go beneath the dining table. The familiar cardboard box was tucked against the wall beside it, moved there two evenings earlier when she cleared space for a better view of the framed photograph. The box stood clear of the walkway. She had thought it an improvement.

He sniffed its edge and continued past.

“Still yours,” she told him.

He went into the bedroom instead.

They carried the samples back to the shop before lunch. In daylight outside the apartment, Julian's blue looked almost purple, and Eve was relieved to dislike it without needing anybody else to be wrong. They returned both and bought the plain lampshade they had originally gone to choose.

On the way home, Julian stopped at a hardware display to examine picture hooks.

“We should decide where it's going first,” Eve said.

“I was checking what fits the frame.”

“Oh.”

She was doing that too: hearing the next argument before he actually supplied it.

The photograph was an ordinary picture from one of their walks. Julian stood at the edge, cut off at the elbow because Eve had been trying to include a leaning tree. He liked it by the bookcase. She wanted it near the table, where the light caught the leaves.

Outside the hardware shop, she opened the picture on her phone to check the frame's proportions. Julian leaned over her shoulder.

“You've included almost all of the tree.”

“It stayed where I put it.”

“I was getting out of your shot.”

“I know. I kept telling you that you were supposed to be in it.”

In the picture, his blurred hand reached toward her. Eve remembered lowering the camera after taking it and walking on with him, neither of them checking whether the leaves looked sufficiently golden. The day itself had been easy. The argument about displaying its evidence was requiring considerably more effort.

She enlarged the image until his face filled the screen, then returned it to its original size.

“I don't want our walls to look like a client approved them,” she said.

“Then we should keep my missing elbow.”

“We're keeping the elbow situation.”

It mattered to her that the room had something they had made together. It mattered less which wall received it. Somehow the smaller question kept getting louder.

They took the lampshade home and ate leftover rice with eggs. Dumpling emerged from the bedroom at his usual meal time, ate normally, and then went straight to Julian when he sat down.

Eve watched the cat stretch across his thigh.

“Shall I leave you two alone?”

Julian stopped stroking him.

“You can sit here too.”

“I know. I'm joking.”

“I know you're making a joke.”

The distinction was irritating enough to be useful.

She sat at the other end of the sofa. The repaired cushion arrangement tilted her slightly toward them, but not far enough for accidental affection to settle the question.

“He keeps choosing you when we're arguing.”

“We weren't arguing just now.”

“We were about to discuss the photograph.”

Julian looked at the frame propped safely on the shelf.

“Were we?”

Eve rubbed her forehead. “Apparently I was.”

Dumpling raised his head, looked between them, and pressed his front paws into Julian's leg before settling again. Once that movement would have encouraged Eve to decide he understood something she didn't. She still felt the old pull. There might be fragments of strong feeling in a room that he noticed in ways she couldn't explain. None of that translated into an informed opinion about picture placement, or which person had been fair.

“He might like the blanket,” Julian said.

“He might.”

“Or the fact that I sit still.”

“I can sit still.”

Her hand was tapping against her knee. She stopped it.

Julian wisely said nothing.

After a moment she leaned back. “When he goes to you, I feel as though I've lost an argument nobody agreed to have.”

“Do you want me to put him down?”

“No. That would be worse.”

“Good. He's quite heavy.”

She laughed, and the cat's ears moved at the sound. He got up, crossed Julian's knees, and stepped onto the folded blanket between them rather than onto Eve.

She let him.

“Maybe we could put the photograph by the table for a week,” she said. “Without making holes yet. Just on the low shelf beneath that wall. Then try your place.”

“I'd like that.”

“You actually would?”

“Yes. I like being able to see it from my chair. That's why I picked the other wall.”

That was a different explanation from the one she had invented, in which his bookcase was quietly annexing the apartment.

“I wanted it where the leaves looked bright.”

“Then let's see both.”

They moved the frame, securing it on the deep shelf where it couldn't slide forward. Eve stepped back. Julian returned to his chair. They could both see it, although the afternoon light put a reflection over one corner.

The matter failed to become a referendum on love.

A few minutes later, a delivery notification sounded from Eve's phone on the dining table. Dumpling jerked awake in the box he had finally entered. Before Eve could mute the phone, the washing machine in the adjoining kitchen began its final spin.

He left the box.

She watched him walk behind Julian's chair and settle on the small rug there, out of the line of movement between kitchen and hall.

“Oh,” she said.

Julian looked up.

Eve crouched beside the box. From this height the table legs formed a narrow avenue of human feet. The chair she pulled out to check her messages stood close to its entrance. The washing machine made a low, regular vibration she hadn't noticed from the sofa.

She had moved the cat's bed to improve a photograph's possible background.

The discovery wasn't dramatic. It was worse in a small, specific way.

“His box used to be over there,” she said, pointing toward the quieter wall beyond the sofa. “I moved it for the frame.”

“I remember.”

“I thought under the table would feel tucked away.”

“It might. Until we use the table.”

Eve lifted the box. Dumpling watched from the rug without approaching. She moved it back to its previous place, checked that nothing blocked the entrance, and laid the familiar blanket inside with its worn corner facing outward.

Then she stepped away.

He remained behind Julian's chair.

“No immediate testimonial,” she said.

“Very unprofessional.”

She smiled, but did not pick him up and put him in the improved arrangement. The box could be available without an audience waiting for him to endorse it.

Later, while Julian was in the kitchen, Eve sat on the floor with the cloth mouse. Dumpling came to sniff it, batted it once, and followed when she drew it slowly beside the sofa. For several minutes she let the game be a game. She did not look up to see whether Julian was watching her regain favor.

The cat pounced badly, missed, and caught the edge of her slipper instead.

“That's my foot.”

He released it and sat down to wash one shoulder, as though the whole encounter had been a misunderstanding on her part.

When he wandered off, Eve put the toy away. She found Julian fitting the new shade onto the unplugged lamp according to its simple instructions, the old shade standing beside him.

“Does this look straight?” he asked.

She checked from the doorway.

“A little left.”

He adjusted it.

“Now?”

“Good.”

Dumpling passed them both and entered his relocated box.

Neither spoke for a moment.

Eve felt the pleased words gathering: See, he wanted my place after all. She caught them before they came out. Even this could become another point awarded to herself if she insisted on turning every movement into a result.

Julian plugged in the lamp. Warm light fell across the sofa and stopped short of the box.

“Better?” he asked.

“Yes.”

They stood looking at the room. It held no new curtains, a photograph whose final destination remained undecided, and a cat who was turning his back on both of them to sleep.

At dinner, Julian disagreed with her about the best route to the station. Eve fetched the map, discovered the crossing she remembered had changed, and refused to accept his suggestion that this meant he should choose all future routes.

Dumpling slept through the whole discussion.

Afterward she passed the box carrying her plate. One orange ear turned toward the sound, then relaxed.

She kept walking, leaving the question of sides unanswered and, for once, unasked.
