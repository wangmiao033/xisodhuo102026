# Chapter 134 — Each Handles Their Own Side

Eve's mother answered the phone with a question about curtains.

“The darker ones look purple in the evening. Is that possible?”

“Yes.”

“They didn't look purple in the shop.”

“The shop wasn't your sitting room.”

“I know that, Eve.”

Eve put the laundry basket on the floor. She had delivered the client revision, eaten something that qualified as lunch, and allowed herself ten minutes before calling. The ten minutes had become sorting towels because towels did not ask why she sounded serious.

“Did you keep the receipt?” she asked.

“Of course.”

“Then you can decide in daylight.”

Her mother made a doubtful sound. Eve waited until the rustling at the other end stopped.

“I wanted to clear something up about the flats you showed us.”

“Oh, those. Did Julian have a look?”

“No. He isn't going to.”

The words sounded abrupt in the quiet kitchen. Eve resisted adding a cheerful excuse about how busy he was.

“I only thought he might know the area,” her mother said.

“You asked whether he would review the development. Then you asked me again afterward. We aren't looking to buy or move in together.”

“I understand that. There's no harm in knowing what's available.”

“I don't want to spend our visits making arrangements for a home we haven't decided to share.”

Dumpling approached the laundry basket and inspected a towel. Eve lifted it before he could settle on the warm surface. He regarded her as though she had withdrawn an advertised service.

Her mother was silent for several seconds.

“Has he told you to say this?”

“No.”

“Because you didn't sound so firm last time.”

“I hoped saying we weren't house hunting would be enough.”

“It was only a leaflet.”

“It keeps becoming a question for him to answer.”

Her mother sighed. Eve could hear the television at low volume in the background, then the click that silenced it. That small action made her sit down.

“I liked him,” her mother said.

“I'm glad.”

“He seemed decent. I thought you were happy.”

“I am happy with him.”

“Then I don't understand why talking about the future has become an offense.”

Eve pressed a fold into the towel across her knees. She had rehearsed a clear refusal, but not the disappointment now inside her mother's voice. It would have been easier if the woman only cared about square footage.

“It hasn't,” she said. “You can ask how we're doing. I don't want you asking him to secure housing for me.”

“I wasn't asking him to buy you a flat.”

“I know you didn't say that. But his salary and his rent and his savings were all part of lunch. You didn't ask me how I wanted to live.”

“I've asked you before. You say you don't know.”

“Sometimes that's still the answer.”

“And I'm meant to find that reassuring?”

“No. I'm asking you to let it remain unanswered without giving him the job.”

Eve stopped folding. The towel had become a narrow, useless strip.

Her mother said, “You told me things were all right at the company too.”

There it was, familiar and not entirely unfair. Eve had hidden difficult months until there was nothing left to hide. She could understand the memory without agreeing to spend the rest of her life proving she was not doing it again.

“I did,” she said. “I wasn't honest about how bad it felt. I'm trying to be more honest now. My work isn't guaranteed. My relationship doesn't come with a housing plan. I'm paying my bills, and if that changes I'll deal with it. I won't pretend Julian has solved it.”

“I didn't ask you to pretend.”

“No. But I think you've been waiting for him to provide a more comfortable answer.”

Her mother did not reply. Eve unfolded the towel and started again.

Eventually her mother said, “You sound very grown up when you're correcting me.”

The sentence stung. Eve almost made a joke to remove the sting from both of them.

“I don't enjoy correcting you,” she said instead. “I'd rather talk about whether the curtains are purple.”

That produced a short, unwilling laugh.

“They are definitely purple.”

“Then return them.”

“I haven't decided.”

“All right.”

For a moment they shared the same silence from different directions. Eve wondered whether her mother heard the parallel and decided not to help her find it.

“The leaflet can stay in the drawer,” her mother said at last.

“You can keep whatever you like. Please stop asking Julian to look.”

“Fine.”

It was not a warm word. It was nevertheless an answer.

Eve accepted it without asking whether her mother understood everything now. They spoke about a book she had borrowed from a friend and the nuisance of carrying a large hardback on the train. Her mother asked whether Eve still wanted the recipe for the rice.

“Yes.”

“I'll write it down when I have time.”

“Thank you.”

They ended the call without arranging another lunch. Eve sat for a minute with the folded towel on her knees. Then she put it away, because finishing the laundry was still part of Monday.

Julian called at the time they had agreed. His voice sounded tired, though she could not tell whether it was the day or the conversation he had just had.

“I spoke to my mother,” he said after they had exchanged greetings.

“How did it go?”

“She asked which Sunday you couldn't do.”

Eve closed her eyes.

“I said that wasn't the issue,” he continued. “I'd promised a recurring visit without asking you. There isn't a standing lunch arrangement.”

“What did she say?”

“That she hadn't meant to pressure anyone. I told her she had asked whether it suited and I was the one who answered.”

Eve let out a breath.

“Thank you for saying that.”

“She wanted to know whether she'd upset you about work.”

“What did you tell her?”

“That you answered her questions, and she can speak to you about your work when you want to discuss it. I wasn't going to give a second account of it for you.”

Eve looked at the client folder on her desk. Earlier, she would have wanted him to describe her competence until his mother could only approve. Now she felt relieved that he had left her actual words where she had spoken them.

“Was she angry?”

“Disappointed. She'd written the next lunch down.”

“That was quick.”

“She likes using the calendar.”

“Apparently you do too.”

A pause. Then Julian said, “Deserved.”

Eve smiled reluctantly.

He had told his mother he would still see his parents on his own, according to what he could arrange, and that dating did not mean every visit had to include both of them. He had not offered Eve a replacement date.

“My father still wants the shelf measurements,” he said.

“Did you send them?”

“Yes. That was a promise I made using my own afternoon.”

“Good.”

She told him about her mother and the leaflet. She described the difficult part without performing either woman's voice.

“I don't think she's persuaded,” Eve said.

“Mine either.”

“Does your mother still want the plant?”

“I didn't think to ask for its status.”

“A critical omission.”

The laugh came more easily this time.

They stayed on the phone for another few minutes, talking about their days. Julian had found his repaired coat pocket more useful than expected. Eve had delivered an edit, discovered that a caption was too long on a smaller screen, and fixed it before the client had to ask.

When he asked if she wanted dinner together later in the week, she did not hear a demand concealed inside it.

“Thursday?” she suggested. “At yours?”

“Yes. I can cook.”

“What?”

“Food.”

“Breakfast,” she reminded him.

He groaned softly. She was pleased to have earned the sound without anyone else being present.

The recipe arrived on Tuesday as two photographs of handwriting. Her mother had crossed out one quantity and replaced it with another, then sent a third photograph because the first had cut off the oven temperature.

Eve enlarged the pictures at her kitchen table. At the bottom of the page, beside the serving instructions, her mother had written that a smaller dish would give her more corner pieces.

She sent a thank-you and asked whether the uncooked rice should be rinsed first.

Of course, came the answer.

It wasn't on the page.

Well, it is now.

Eve laughed. She copied the missing step into her own notes and planned to try the recipe the following week. There was no mention of the development. She didn't reopen the argument to check whether the silence meant agreement, annoyance, or simply that her mother had other things to do that day.

On Thursday, he opened the door with a dish towel over one shoulder and asked her to watch the pan while he drained the rice. Eve put down her bag, washed her hands, and stirred where he showed her. The vegetables were slightly overdone. Neither of them gave a formal assessment.

His phone vibrated on the counter. He glanced at it and left it there.

“Everything all right?” Eve asked.

“Yes. My mother says she hasn't erased the next Sunday yet in case we change our minds.”

Eve looked at the pan. Her first irritation was directed at him, which was unfair; he had not sent the message.

“What will you say?”

“I'll ask her to erase it. There isn't a booking to hold.”

He picked up the phone and typed while she turned off the heat. When he put it away, he did not ask her to approve the wording.

She carried the plates to the table.

“My mother sent the rice recipe,” she said.

“Does it include a viewing appointment?”

“No. It does include the phrase until it looks right four times.”

“A precise method.”

“I may need a second conversation.”

They ate. At first Eve kept noticing the phone, expecting another vibration to turn dinner back into a negotiation. It remained quiet. Julian asked about the photograph Mia had chosen for her story, and Eve described the practical decision without sharing details Mia had kept private.

Then he told her about a narrow reading room he had loved when he was young, attached to the local library, with a window that looked onto the back of a grocery shop. Eve could not remember what had prompted him. Something about the badly placed table in her rented community room, perhaps.

“I used to think it was wonderfully private,” he said. “You could see deliveries, but nobody could see your book.”

“What were you reading?”

“Anything that wasn't the book I was meant to read.”

She laughed. “Is it still there?”

“The library is. I haven't checked the room in years.”

“I'd like to see it.”

He looked at her across the plates.

“All right. We can go when it's open.”

“There was somewhere I used to go too. Behind the shops near my mother's. Nothing impressive.”

“That sounds promising.”

After dinner they checked opening hours rather than property prices. The library was open on Saturday morning. Eve's place required only daylight and a separate trip she actually wanted to make.

Their parents had not become easy people to agree with. The next invitation might still need explaining. But Saturday now contained something they had chosen while sitting together, with both their calendars in front of them.
