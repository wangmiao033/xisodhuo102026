# Chapter 098 — We Don't Need More Proof

The camera had been in the drawer beneath Eve's spare cables all afternoon.

She remembered it when she opened the drawer for a charging lead. A client had sent a photograph that needed to reach her computer before tomorrow, and her phone was nearly empty. The camera's black strap lay across the lead, an accidental suggestion.

She moved the strap aside.

Julian, still on the sofa, asked whether she had found what she needed.

“Yes.”

She plugged in the phone and saw the unopened account notifications above the client's message. Somebody had asked, under an old clip, whether Dumpling really knew what people were thinking.

It was not a new question. Until that afternoon, Eve had regarded it as an audience member making a playful claim. Now the familiar wording looked much less harmless.

She didn't turn the screen toward Julian immediately. First she opened the client message, checked the file had arrived, and confirmed receipt. Work did not need to become the vehicle for a personal discussion simply because both occurred on the same device.

Then she set the phone down.

“There's something I should decide about the account,” she said.

Julian shifted so she had room beside him. “What?”

“People sometimes ask whether he reads minds. Usually they're joking. I've answered with jokes too.”

“What kind?”

“Things like his consultation hours being full.”

The words sounded different without a laughing face after them. Eve remembered typing them quickly, pleased that a comment had offered an easy reply.

“Do they think you're making an actual claim?” Julian asked.

“I don't know. Some probably don't. That isn't the same as none.”

She picked up the phone and opened the visible comment. She could reply with an ordinary clarification: she didn't know what explained certain behavior, but the videos were not evidence of mind-reading. That was accurate, if rather heavy beneath a clip of a cat sitting on a bag.

For a moment she considered simply leaving it alone. Nobody had demanded an official position. Explaining might draw more attention than the joke had.

“What do you want to do?” he asked.

“I want the account not to rely on people believing it.”

“That sounds different from announcing what happened today.”

“It is.”

Eve put the phone down again. “I wasn't going to post the cups.”

“I'd prefer not to become part of a public investigation.”

“You won't.”

“Not as a vet confirming it, or as your boyfriend making it more interesting.”

“Yes.”

She could picture the edit with embarrassing ease. Three plain cups. A close view of a folded card. Julian's quiet face. A caption carefully phrased as a question, legally and emotionally convenient because it did not quite assert anything.

A question could still make a promise to viewers. She knew that better than most people.

“I could make it look very persuasive,” she admitted. “That doesn't mean I'd have made it true.”

Julian looked at the drawer she had closed.

“And you'd have to keep making it.”

The prospect opened out: stronger reactions, better conditions, another witness, a more surprising choice. The successful clip would become an appointment for the cat. A failure would be a reason to try again before the light went.

“No,” she said.

It was not a dramatic refusal aimed at an unseen audience. There was nobody to applaud her. She simply moved the camera bag farther into the drawer, where it belonged, and closed the drawer. The phone continued charging on the desk.

They went into the kitchen because Eve needed to put dinner on. She had onions to use, purchased before the bag had split, and enough rice for a straightforward meal. Julian asked whether he was invited to stay for it.

“I'd like that. Do you have time?”

“Yes.”

“Then you can chop something if you want. No experimental conditions.”

He washed his hands. “A reassuring menu.”

Eve handed him the onions and a board. She started the rice, then returned to the counter with a pan. The room grew less solemn as the ordinary work divided itself into things that had to happen now and things that could wait.

Julian's eyes watered before the first onion was finished.

“You can swap,” she said.

“I've committed.”

“To an onion?”

“It seemed smaller at the start.”

She gave him a clean towel for his hands and took over the last one. They opened the kitchen window a little, keeping the cat safely away from the work area. Dumpling observed from the hall and eventually left, having established that nobody was preparing his dinner.

At the stove, Eve said, “Somebody once suggested I should bring him to client meetings. As a joke, I think.”

Julian looked up from rinsing the board.

“I didn't do it,” she added. “But I remember enjoying the suggestion.”

“Being able to identify a bad client?”

“Yes. Imagine never wasting a week before finding out they don't intend to pay.”

“Would you know that from a cat avoiding a chair?”

“No.”

She stirred the onions until they softened. “And I'd still need a deposit and a written scope. Which are less entertaining.”

“Usually more useful.”

She smiled, then thought of the opposite mistake. A friendly meeting, an animal curling against someone's shoe, a reasonable-looking promise. Warmth was not a guarantee either. She didn't want to build a method that could fail in both directions while always finding a reason to blame the person using it.

“I don't want to do customer identification,” she said. “Not for my work. Not to help anyone else decide whom to trust.”

“Nor patients or owners at the clinic.”

“No.”

The pan hissed when she added the remaining ingredients. She lowered the heat, appreciating that this particular warning had a visible cause.

“What if I notice something without trying?” she asked. “I'm not going to stop noticing him.”

“I wouldn't expect you to.”

“Say he's suddenly worried by a noise near the door.”

“Then you can check the door if there's an ordinary reason to. You don't need an extraordinary explanation to notice something has fallen over.”

“And if there isn't?”

“Then perhaps you don't know why he reacted.”

She nodded. The unsolved part could remain unsolved without requiring a public theory or an interrogation of the nearest person.

They discussed no catalogue of every future danger. Eve had already spent enough evenings imagining she could prevent mistakes by writing increasingly precise rules. Instead, when the building made a sharp knocking sound, they both looked toward the hallway.

Dumpling had stopped beside the bedroom door.

Eve went to see. The sound came again, followed by a neighbor's voice and the scrape of furniture on the landing. Through her peephole she saw two people moving a narrow shelf, its top catching the wall.

She stepped back and returned to the kitchen.

“A shelf. It appears to be losing an argument with the staircase.”

“Does it need us?” Julian asked.

“They've got it. Nobody asked.”

She stirred the pan. Dumpling stayed where he was for a moment, then returned to his chair. No one needed to decide which human emotion he might have perceived in order to leave his quiet route open.

Dinner took longer than Eve had estimated. Julian set the table, found the wrong drawer for the spoons, and asked rather than continuing to open every cupboard. She told him. It was almost laughably easy to help somebody who named what he needed.

While the rice rested, she returned to the account comment.

She wrote: *He's a cat whose behavior I sometimes misread. Please don't use his reactions to judge people—or mine as proof that he can.*

The last part sounded defensive. She read it again, deleted the complicated sentence, and wrote something plainer.

*No reliable mind-reading service here. I sometimes guess wrong about what he's doing. People need to speak for themselves.*

She showed Julian only her proposed reply, not the commenter's profile or unrelated messages.

“Does that make you uncomfortable?”

“It doesn't involve me.”

“I know. I wanted to check whether it sounded like I was presenting today as a conclusion.”

“No. But it's your account. You don't need me to approve each sentence.”

He was right. She was offering him another small job in exchange for the reassurance of having done something properly.

Eve posted the reply herself. Then she closed the app before she could watch for approval.

Before they sat down, Eve's phone vibrated with the client's answer. The photograph was the approved version, but the lettering on the sign had changed. She opened the attached correction and saw a clear request rather than a hidden test of her patience.

She replied that she would use the new wording in tomorrow's agreed edit. She did not offer to finish it while dinner cooled. Then she placed the phone on the desk, far enough away that she would have to make a decision to pick it up.

“Everything all right?” Julian asked.

“Yes. A correction I can make tomorrow.”

He pulled out his chair. “Good. This seems worth eating while it's hot.”

At dinner, Julian tasted the food and asked for the pepper. Eve passed it without asking whether the request meant he disliked the meal. A tiny achievement, but dinner tasted better without a review panel.

“I still think there are things I can't explain,” she said after a while.

“I know.”

“I don't want putting the cups away to mean I lied about that.”

“It doesn't.”

She took another mouthful before answering. The relief was too quick to trust with a speech.

“Thank you.”

Julian set his fork down.

“I don't need more proof this evening. I need ordinary time with you. To see what we actually do.”

“Not another explanation.”

“Not tonight.”

Eve thought about all the good sentences she could construct around that request. Then she asked if he wanted more rice.

He did.

After dinner, they cleared the plates. The three plastic cups were dry. She put the clips back into one, spare rubber bands into another, and left the third empty because an empty container was allowed to exist without an immediate assignment.

When Julian left, they arranged Wednesday evening for a walk after their separate working days. Eve didn't ask whether the date proved he was no longer upset. She asked where he wanted to meet.

At the door, he took her hand.

“I liked staying for dinner.”

“I liked having you.”

Their fingers tightened briefly, then parted so he could put on his coat.

Later, the account notification count had risen by a small number. Eve checked the client file had transferred, set tomorrow's work reminder, and left the comments unread until her normal time for them.

Dumpling climbed onto the chair beside her desk and settled with his nose beneath his tail. His whiskers twitched against the white curve of his paw. Eve reached for the light switch quietly.

Then she turned off the desk light and went to wash her face.
