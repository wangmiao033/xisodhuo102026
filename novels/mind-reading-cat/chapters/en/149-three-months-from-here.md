# Chapter 149 — Three Months from Here

The landlord's reply began with a question about paint.

Eve had asked to test whether the room suited the work. The landlord wanted to know whether testing involved painting it pale green and then deciding to leave.

“No painting,” Eve said on the arranged call. “Nothing fixed to the walls. Removable furniture, a few setup visits, and small sessions only after we've agreed what each involves.”

“And no leaving animals here.”

“No boarding, no animals left without their owners.”

The landlord described the problem from her side. Opening the unit for visits took time. Cleaning still cost money. She could not take it off the market indefinitely while Eve considered colors.

Eve looked at the photograph pinned beside her desk. She had, privately, considered colors.

“I can pay for a defined evaluation period,” she said. “I can't pay a full lease's costs while we haven't settled whether the use works.”

They did not solve it on the phone. They agreed to exchange a short proposal and meet again after Eve had taken advice.

The first draft arrived two days later. It covered arranged access for an initial block of weeks, specified charges, a refundable amount against agreed damage, and advance agreement for any visitors beyond setup helpers. The landlord would retain the keys and arrange opening and closing. The unit could still be shown to other prospective tenants outside Eve's confirmed times.

Eve read that sentence twice.

She had wanted a low-risk trial that somehow also guaranteed nobody else could take the room. Seeing both wishes on the page revealed why they could not cost the same.

She took the draft to the adviser who reviewed commercial occupancy terms. They went through what the arrangement did and did not grant, which costs applied to booked sessions, and what would happen if either side ended it early. The adviser flagged several phrases that sounded flexible until money was involved.

“What does reasonable notice mean here?” she asked.

Eve did not know. Neither did the document.

They replaced the question with something to negotiate: a stated notice period, treatment of confirmed dates, and a clear refund method if the landlord cancelled access. Eve also wanted to know what happened to equipment left between visits. The draft did not promise secure permanent storage, and she could not treat it as if it did.

The adviser checked that the temporary terms did not automatically turn into a longer commitment. A future lease would need its own agreement, including issues they were not pretending to settle now.

Eve left with a marked draft and a fee to record. She had spent money on something that made the proposal less exciting. On the bus home, she found that she felt steadier rather than cheated.

The revised version took several exchanges. The landlord would allow removable items in a designated part of the room during the evaluation, with an inventory and removal deadline. Eve would pay agreed access and cleaning charges. Each paid test session needed advance approval of the activity, numbers, timing, and arrangements for any animals. There would be no public drop-in hours, no building work, and no permanent sign.

The landlord would not promise that evaluation guaranteed a lease.

Eve did not ask her to.

She kept her already booked center session and checked another available date as a fallback. The unit could answer questions without becoming the place where every promise had to be fulfilled.

Before signing, she returned for a condition visit. The landlord opened the door properly this time, showing Eve the repaired catch. They photographed the existing marks on the floor and the pale counter outline, with copies for both of them. Eve tested the lights and noted what belonged to the premises.

At the sink, the landlord asked whether refreshments were part of the plan.

“Not during the evaluation beyond what we've agreed for people bringing their own drinks,” Eve said. “I need to check the requirements and costs before promising anything else.”

“Good. The last conversation I had about a small drinks corner ended with a request to move a drain.”

Eve crossed a tiny cup symbol off the margin of her notebook before the woman could see it.

Julian came for the last part of the visit because Eve had asked him to look at specific questions about occasional animal attendance. He arrived without clinical equipment. The landlord greeted him, then continued directing questions about the agreement to Eve.

He walked the room with them and looked at the proposed separation between activity space and the rear room. When Eve described a quiet area for an animal who wanted distance, he asked how the animal and owner would reach it without passing through the busiest part of a session.

She had drawn the route around a table that did not yet exist.

“I need to test that with furniture,” she said.

“Yes.”

He also asked about cleaning products, shared water containers, and how the booking description would distinguish owner activities from medical advice. For questions about building requirements or permitted occupancy, he told her to use the relevant professional checks rather than his opinion.

The landlord nodded toward the rear wall.

“Could you say whether this is suitable for animals?”

“Not as a general guarantee,” he said. “Different activities and animals need different arrangements. I can comment on the specific proposal within my work, but Eve still needs the other checks.”

The woman made a note. Eve felt grateful that the conversation did not turn into a performance of his authority on her behalf.

Afterward, she walked him to the corner. He had to return to the clinic. She had to decide whether the remaining uncertainty was a reason to stop or the reason for paying to test anything at all.

“Does it look wildly foolish?” she asked.

“The route needs work.”

“I meant me.”

“I know.”

She nudged his sleeve with her knuckles. He caught her hand briefly, then let it go before they separated.

At home, Eve put the final proposed costs beside the amount she had marked as available for the experiment. The number fitted, but only if she kept the first activities small and postponed most of the furniture she had imagined.

She checked what remained afterward. Existing client expenses were covered. Her ordinary living reserve stayed where it was. Tax money and potential refunds did not become available merely because she could see them on the screen. She included the cost of ending the evaluation and removing her things.

Then she made the less pleasant calculation: what if the paid tests earned nothing?

The loss would hurt. She would have to wait longer for equipment she wanted and preserve more commission days. It would not require borrowing from her family, using their property, or asking Julian to fill the gap.

Her mother called while she was looking at that version.

“Have you eaten?” was the first question.

“Not yet.”

“Then why do you sound so serious?”

“I'm doing sums. They dislike me.”

Eve explained the limited arrangement. Her mother asked whether it meant she was opening a shop at last. Eve said no, not yet; she was paying for access to test a place, while keeping the work she already had.

“If you need money, you should say,” her mother said.

The offer warmed her in a way the figures had not. It also made her shoulders tighten.

“Thank you. I've set an amount I can use myself. If the test needs more than that, I'll change the test.”

Her mother wanted to know whether Julian was involved. Eve described his limited advice and his own clinic work. She did not enlist his career as evidence that everything would be safe.

There was a small silence.

“Well,” her mother said, “at least eat before deciding anything.”

“That part I can agree to immediately.”

Eve made dinner. She ate the first few bites standing up, caught herself, and sat at the table.

Dumpling joined her long enough to sniff the chair leg, then returned to the familiar bed near the wall. She had moved no household objects for the new room. Nothing needed to leave home simply because she was trying somewhere else.

After dinner, she chose a date three months ahead and wrote it at the top of a review page.

The evaluation agreement was shorter than three months. There could be another limited block if both sides chose it, or separate lease discussions if the evidence justified them. The date was her own checkpoint for the business decision, not an automatic renewal hidden beneath optimism.

By then, she wanted to compare paid demand, actual hours, session costs, and the work she had turned away. She wanted to know whether the room improved an experience customers valued, not merely whether she enjoyed possessing a cupboard. She also wrote down reasons to stop: costs above the agreed limit, access that did not suit the customers, or work that could be delivered better elsewhere.

She left space for reasons to continue.

It seemed important that the test be allowed to answer yes too.

The next morning, she confirmed the specific temporary activities with the insurer and completed the checks required for the evaluation arrangement. One planned visitor slot had to move to fit the landlord's opening availability. Eve changed it before taking any booking money.

Sophie returned the proposed setup dates with one crossed out and another marked possible. Eve booked the paid session Sophie could actually do. For a later visitor test, she obtained a support quote and checked that the minimum booking fitted the access period. The cheaper slot did not; the landlord could not open early enough. Eve selected the other date and reduced the materials purchase to cover the difference. It was a small adjustment, but for once a real person had displaced a convenient square in the calendar before the square became a promise.

She read the final document once more, signed in her own name, and paid the agreed initial amount from the business account. When the countersigned copy arrived, she saved it beside the schedule and sent the first setup-access request.

There were no keys to collect.

The landlord replied with a time when she would meet Eve and open the unit. Eve put it in the calendar, including the time to clear up and leave.

Then she opened a client's unfinished draft. The introduction needed trimming; a caption had the wrong reference; the delivery was still due the next afternoon.

For several minutes the new agreement sat beside the old work, and Eve felt an urge to photograph it. Instead she moved it out of the way of her tea.

Three months from now, she would have something to review. Today she had bought a limited chance to find out, and a client was waiting for a better first paragraph.
