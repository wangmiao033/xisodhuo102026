# Chapter 220 — No Need to Take the Test Again

The first receipt was smaller than Eve remembered.

She held it by its blank edge, trying not to rub the faded printing. Somehow she had expected the paper to retain the size of the fear that came with paying it. It was only a narrow rectangle, tucked among Dumpling's early care records.

Friday evening had begun with a shelf.

A folder stuck out far enough to catch the kitchen door when it opened fully. Julian had moved it while putting away the groceries, revealing three other folders nobody had properly sorted since the move. Eve volunteered to separate her personal records from the business papers before the small obstruction became another household landmark.

She had not planned to spend the evening in the rain again.

Yet there she was, looking at the date and remembering how carefully she had once counted the money she could spare.

Dumpling sat on the rug nearby. He had already inspected the empty storage box and found it insufficiently comfortable without a blanket. The elaborate new toy was packed away, pending the shop's reply about Sophie's receipt. It would not become permanent furniture simply because the cat had cost someone money by disliking it.

Julian was making tea.

“Keep or discard?” he asked, holding up an envelope containing an expired store promotion.

“Discard.”

“Are you certain? It promises excitement.”

“Two summers ago.”

“Delayed excitement.”

He put it in the recycling pile.

Eve smiled, then looked down at the little receipt again.

There were newer records behind it: ordinary appointments, follow-up notes, the practical history of feeding and keeping and worrying. Monday's receipt lay at the front, waiting to be filed. None of the papers contained an explanation for the strange thing she had come to believe Dumpling sometimes did.

They contained quite a lot of evidence that he had lived with her.

She set the first receipt on a clean sheet so she could make a legible digital copy before the ink faded further. It belonged in his care history, not in a public story about the moment she became a good person.

The phone camera refused to focus.

She moved the lamp, tried again, and finally got an image clear enough to keep privately with the other records. The amount looked sharper on the screen than on the paper. She remembered the old account balance behind it and felt a sudden, unreasonable tightening in her throat.

“You all right?” Julian asked.

“Yes,” Eve said automatically.

He brought the tea over and sat at the other side of the table, giving her space rather than taking the receipt from her.

Eve put the phone down.

She was all right now. That did not mean the memory had become painless merely because she knew how it ended. Back then, she had wanted someone to assure her that keeping a cat would not be one more thing she failed at after losing her job. Instead she had made lists, counted costs, and told jokes about him paying rent.

He had not understood the jokes as a contract. At least, she hoped he hadn't.

The thought caught her more sharply than she expected.

Dumpling stood.

He crossed the rug without approaching the empty box, came to Eve's chair, and reached one paw toward the hand she had lowered beside her knee. His claws stayed sheathed. When she turned her palm, he pressed his forehead into it, then stepped toward the space beside Julian's chair and looked back.

It was so close to the interruption she had been missing that Eve stopped breathing for a moment.

The cat had not discovered an object or supplied a hidden answer. Still, the timing felt exact: the instant her thought had narrowed from a general memory to the sharp wish that she had never made belonging sound like a bill.

She could have followed the gaze back to Julian and called it proof.

She could also see the steam rising from the mugs, feel her own lowered hand, and remember that Dumpling often preferred company when the floor grew cool.

“Eve?” Julian said.

She took a breath.

“No,” she said. “Not entirely.”

He waited.

“I found the first receipt. I was remembering being scared I couldn't keep him. And the stupid things I said about him earning the expense.”

Julian looked toward the papers, then back at her.

“You stopped saying them.”

“Yes. I know.”

“That doesn't mean you can't dislike remembering.”

She nodded, annoyed to find that her eyes had become wet in the presence of an ordinary filing task.

Dumpling rubbed against the chair leg. Eve set the papers safely away from the edge, moved her chair nearer Julian's, and sat with both feet on the floor. The cat leaned briefly against her ankle before settling between them.

“Did you see that?” she asked.

“I saw him come over.”

“The timing.”

“Yes.”

“He hasn't done something that exact all week.”

Julian rested his hand beside hers, leaving her free to take it.

“Do you want me to say what I think happened?”

“What do you think happened?”

“You were upset. He came over. I don't know how much of that he noticed, or how.”

Eve let out a small laugh through her nose.

“Very unsatisfying.”

“I can make it longer. I can't make it more certain.”

She took his hand.

For a few minutes they said nothing. Tea cooled. Dumpling washed one shoulder, having apparently completed whatever business had brought him between the chairs. Eve's pulse steadied without any explanation acquiring a final shape.

Then her attention drifted to the old folders still standing on the shelf.

Their earlier voluntary observations were among the records they had kept somewhere, separate from the cat's medical papers. She remembered the careful attempts to distinguish an ordinary cue from an odd coincidence, and the equally careful failures to turn either into something dependable.

She could retrieve them.

The thought was immediate and practical. One familiar observation, in a quiet room, to see whether the week had meant anything. Nothing unpleasant. Nothing dramatic. Just enough to remove the uncertainty that had occupied the edge of every day.

Her chair scraped as she shifted.

Dumpling stopped washing and looked up.

Eve sat still again.

“What?” Julian asked.

“I was about to find the old notes.”

He glanced at the shelf.

“Do you want to?”

“I want an answer.”

“That wasn't quite the same question.”

“No.”

She looked at the cat. He had gone back to his shoulder, giving the matter less attention than a patch of fur that wasn't yet arranged correctly.

An answer might change what she called the week. It would not change his dinner, his quiet place, or the fact that an ordinary health concern belonged with ordinary veterinary care. She did not need a renewed demonstration to know whether he should remain here.

“No,” she said. “Leave them where they are.”

Julian nodded. He did not congratulate her or make refusing an experiment into a new test she had passed.

They drank the tea while it was still worth drinking.

After a while Eve picked up the care folder again. The real task remained unfinished, and completing it no longer felt like an attempt to answer everything else.

She filed Monday's examination record where she could find it. She checked the saved copy of the faded receipt, then placed the original inside a plain protective sleeve with the date visible. A business printing invoice had slipped into the wrong folder during the move. She separated it and put it with the accounts instead of treating every old paper as part of the cat's story.

Julian dealt with the expired promotions.

“This one promises a fresh beginning,” he said.

“When?”

“Last spring.”

“We missed it.”

“Apparently.”

They kept the few documents they needed, set aside uncertain ones for a later proper check, and put the rest of the harmless packaging and old advertisements in the recycling. Eve did not make a grand attempt to discard her whole frightened former self along with the leaflets.

The shelf no longer caught the door.

It was a satisfying, limited success.

They made dinner from the remaining vegetables and a portion of food they had planned to use that evening. Eve forgot to turn the kitchen timer on. Julian noticed because he looked at it, not because Dumpling intervened. They checked the pan together and found that nothing disastrous had happened.

The cat watched them from his usual place clear of their feet.

“You can remain off duty,” Eve told him.

“He wasn't on,” Julian said.

“I know. I'm reminding myself.”

After dinner, Eve sent Sophie a message about the toy: Dumpling had declined it twice, politely by his own standards, and Eve would follow the shop's response about returning it. Sophie replied with a photograph of herself holding an equally expensive coffee she regretted buying.

Everybody's tastes were costing somebody money today.

Eve laughed and put her phone down. The ordinary exchange helped more than another hour comparing the cat's behavior would have done.

Later, she and Julian sat on the sofa with their books. Dumpling chose the small box beside the bookcase, now furnished with his familiar blanket. He did not come over when Eve silently remembered the receipt again. He did not react when Julian smiled at a sentence without reading it aloud.

The one unusually timed interruption remained one interruption.

Eve was glad she hadn't asked the evening to repeat itself until it gave her a result she liked.

“On Sunday,” Julian said, marking his page, “we should look at that loose fastening on the kitchen chair. Before somebody trusts it with their weight.”

“The one we put aside?”

“We have only one that I'm prepared to accuse.”

“All right. In daylight.”

“Not now?”

“I'm practicing leaving a reasonable job until its reasonable time.”

He looked at the chair they had moved out of use and agreed. They would inspect it properly rather than tighten random parts while tired. If it needed more than a simple repair, it would stay out of use until it was safe.

Eve returned to her book. For once, the weekend contained a task without expanding to swallow all their time.

Near bedtime, Dumpling got up, stretched, and walked past them toward his water. Eve watched him drink, then turned off the reading lamp after Julian had closed his book.

She could not say his strange sensitivity had disappeared. She could not say it had returned. There had never been a reliable gauge to consult, and a week of quiet did not provide one.

She could say he had eaten, played, slept, stolen laundry, disliked a gift, and leaned against her on an evening when she needed to admit she was upset.

The last thing was precious even if it was also entirely ordinary.

Before she went to bed, Eve moved the empty filing box out of the walking route and left his familiar sleeping place where it was.

Dumpling curled into the blanket without looking up for permission.

She left him to it.
