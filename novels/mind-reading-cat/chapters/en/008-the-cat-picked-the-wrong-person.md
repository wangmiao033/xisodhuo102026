# Chapter 008 — The Cat Picked the Wrong Person

The man stopped in the doorway and said, “No. Not that one.”

Dumpling retreated so quickly that his orange face vanished behind the towel in his carrier.

Eve looked from the carrier to the man.

He wore a dark jacket despite the mild morning. One hand rested on a cane; the other held the event card folded into four neat squares. His mouth was set in a line that made her feel she had already answered something incorrectly.

“I haven't offered you one,” she said.

The man's eyes moved toward her. “I was speaking to the volunteer.”

Behind him, Wendy lifted a hand in acknowledgment. Button, a few steps away on his lead, had been delighted by the arrival of someone new. Apparently, the delight wasn't mutual.

Eve bent to adjust the carrier towel. Dumpling stayed back. She felt an immediate, unreasonable solidarity with him.

He wasn't usually shy with everyone. He had allowed Sophie to visit, had settled when Julian spoke, and had interrupted Eve at moments she still found difficult to dismiss. Now he seemed to have made up his mind about this stranger before she had.

Not that she was making up hers.

She was simply noting the evidence.

“There's a seat farther inside,” she told the man, in the tone she used for people who had already wasted several revisions.

He looked past her. “Is there one closer?”

The chair by the wall was occupied by a visitor completing a form. Eve nearly pointed toward the row at the back. Instead, she noticed the cane had a worn rubber tip, and the man was leaning more heavily on it than he had at the door.

“I'll find one.”

She put the carrier in the quiet side room Tessa had arranged while she finished checking Dumpling's follow-up instructions. He hadn't come to entertain visitors. Once his clinic visit was finished, Eve would take him home before returning for the event's main filming period. The short overlap had seemed manageable on paper.

In reality, a table was already in the wrong place, the registration pens were missing, and a man with a cane needed the chair she had just allowed someone to move.

She fetched another from the wall and set it near the entrance, leaving a clear route beside it.

The man tested its back before sitting.

“Thank you,” he said.

She had been prepared for a complaint. The thanks landed awkwardly.

Wendy came over without Button. “We can talk here, Mr. Bennett. No hurry.”

“I don't want a young one dragging me down the street.”

“We can discuss what sort of care you could manage before introducing anyone.”

Eve picked up a fallen card and straightened it with unnecessary precision. From the man's voice alone, she would have described the conversation as a rejection. He spoke as if dogs were faulty products being presented to him.

Then he unfolded the card.

The creases crossed Pepper's photograph.

“I called about this one,” he said. “But I couldn't tell how big he is from the picture.”

Eve's attention caught.

Wendy explained that a photograph wasn't enough to assess a match. They could arrange a calm introduction, and there were questions to discuss first. Mr. Bennett nodded, then rubbed the side of his knee through his trousers.

“No promises,” he said sharply. “I won't promise something I can't do.”

Eve's first instinct was to dislike the sharpness. Her second, less comfortable thought was that those were sensible words.

Tessa appeared at her elbow with the missing pens, which had been packed inside a box labeled Cups.

“His instructions are ready,” she said quietly, indicating the carrier room. “I've kept your camera bag behind the desk.”

“Thanks. I'll be back before the agreed filming time.”

Eve went to collect Dumpling. Julian was waiting near the side-room door, reviewing the note he had prepared for her. He went through the follow-up plan and what she should contact the clinic about, then asked whether she had questions.

“About him? No.”

Julian waited a fraction longer. Eve realized she was looking toward the entrance.

“The man out there,” she said. “Dumpling really didn't like him.”

“He's had a busy morning.”

“Mr. Bennett?”

“The cat.”

She looked down at the carrier. A white paw rested on the towel now. Dumpling's face remained partly hidden, but he wasn't making the dramatic verdict she had been imagining.

“There was Button, too,” Julian added. “And people moving chairs.”

“I know. I wasn't saying—”

She stopped before defending a claim he hadn't accused her of making.

Julian didn't offer a character assessment of the visitor. He reminded her to keep Dumpling's journey quiet and stepped back so she could lift the carrier without twisting around him.

Outside, the morning had grown brighter. Eve carried the cat home, listening to the traffic and the occasional protest from behind the towel.

“You may be an excellent judge,” she told him when they reached her door. “You also hissed at the laundry rack.”

Dumpling offered no clarification.

She settled him, checked everything he needed, and stayed until he had emerged to inspect his bowl. When she left again, he was arranging himself in his usual resting place, apparently unconcerned about Mr. Bennett's moral standing.

Back at the event, the entrance chair was empty. Eve asked Wendy whether the man had left.

“He's in the quieter area. We're talking through his routine.”

“I didn't mean—” Eve hesitated. “I was a little abrupt with him.”

“He can be abrupt himself. You can still speak to him.”

No reassuring explanation followed. Wendy didn't tell her he was secretly wonderful, or that she was obliged to like him. She simply returned to the conversation.

Eve checked her filming plan. Mr. Bennett hadn't agreed to appear, so she kept the camera in its bag before approaching.

He sat with his folded cane beside the chair. Pepper remained several feet away, with Wendy between them. Nothing about the arrangement resembled the instant connection Eve might once have wanted to film.

“May I ask whether the card was useful?” she said. “I made the layout. You don't have to be in any footage.”

Mr. Bennett looked at her, then at the card.

“The print was large enough. The size wasn't clear.”

“I can make that clearer.”

“And this.” He tapped slow to warm up. “I didn't know whether it meant he never wants anyone near him.”

Wendy explained what she had observed, carefully distinguishing familiar settings from what might happen somewhere new.

Mr. Bennett listened with his hands flat on his knees.

“My wife used to say I'd ask a shop assistant whether the kettle enjoyed its work before I bought it,” he said at last. “I'm not trying to interrogate you.”

“You can ask questions,” Wendy replied.

He gave a short, dissatisfied breath. “My knee isn't what it was. Some days I can walk farther than others. I thought an older dog might mean less running, but that's an assumption, isn't it?”

“Yes,” Wendy said, without making the word a reprimand. “Age alone won't tell us what a particular dog needs.”

He nodded slowly. “That's what I was afraid of.”

The words were quiet enough that Eve nearly missed them.

She remembered his first sentence. No. Not that one. She had heard dislike. He had been looking at an enthusiastic dog pulling toward a new person and imagining himself unable to keep up.

That didn't make him automatically suitable for Pepper. It didn't make his manner pleasant, either. It did make him a person who had arrived with a question rather than a sentence passed on every animal in the room.

“Would it help if we put the introduction process on the back?” Eve asked. “So it's clear you can talk before meeting a dog?”

“That would have helped me.” He glanced toward the doorway. “I thought I was supposed to know already.”

Eve looked at the chair, the cards, and the lead lying loosely in Wendy's hand. She had designed an invitation to ask questions, then almost resented someone for asking in the wrong tone.

“I made that harder this morning,” she said. “I'm sorry. I should have found you a seat without making you ask twice.”

Mr. Bennett studied her for a moment.

“Well. I could have said good morning.”

“It's still technically morning.”

He glanced at his watch. “Good morning, then.”

She smiled. This time it wasn't part of her event face.

Pepper turned his head toward Wendy. Mr. Bennett didn't reach for him. Instead, he asked what support the volunteer group could discuss if an introduction eventually went well, and what he would need to arrange himself.

Before she stepped away, Mr. Bennett asked whether she had a spare pencil. The event pen kept slipping against the glossy card.

Eve found one in her bag and offered a blank sheet as well. He wrote three questions, slowly, pressing hard enough to leave marks on the page beneath. One was about the days when his neighbor couldn't help. Another concerned transport. She didn't read the third; she had noticed herself looking and turned toward Wendy instead.

“Keep the pencil,” she said. “The pen may be better at looking professional than doing the job.”

He gave a dry little laugh.

Eve left them to it. Her camera stayed in the bag until she reached the area where filming had been agreed.

At the registration table, she asked Tessa whether the entrance chair could stay where she had put it.

“As long as we keep this part clear.” Tessa indicated the route past the table. “Someone moved it because it looked untidy in a photo.”

“That may have been me.”

Tessa waited.

“It was me,” Eve admitted. “Yesterday, when I was checking the frame.”

They moved the brochure stand instead. The entrance looked less symmetrical, and nobody would need to walk to the back merely to sit down. Eve took a new reference photograph for herself, with the chair plainly in it.

Then she added a note for the next revision: Explain that asking does not commit you to adopting.

She didn't write Mr. Bennett's personal circumstances. They were not hers to turn into a moving anecdote about appearances.

Later, when she collected a wide shot of the information table, she caught sight of him leaving. He thanked Wendy. He carried no dog, only his folded card and a number to call after thinking over the practical questions.

Nobody announced a happy ending.

Eve found herself relieved.

That evening, she would probably still wonder why Dumpling sometimes reacted before she expected him to. There were small incidents she couldn't neatly explain, and dismissing all of them as nothing felt as lazy as declaring them proof.

But one thing had become embarrassingly clear.

A cat moving away from a man could tell her that a cat had moved away from a man.

The rest required a conversation.
