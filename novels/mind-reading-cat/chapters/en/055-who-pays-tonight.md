# Chapter 055 — Who Pays Tonight?

By Tuesday evening, the scarf had spent two days on the highest shelf in Eve's kitchen.

She took it down at six thirty, checked the corner that Dumpling had carried, and folded it into a paper bag. It looked less like evidence of a romantic conspiracy and more like something waiting to be returned after a school trip.

This was probably healthy.

Dumpling watched from a chair while she put on her jacket. When she lifted the paper bag, he stood, apparently prepared to reopen negotiations.

“No,” Eve said. “You have your own things.”

She left his familiar blanket where it was and put the mouse beside it. He sniffed the toy with an expression that did not suggest gratitude, then sat on the blanket anyway.

Julian rang at six forty-six.

“Late,” he said when she opened the door.

“Tragically. I've sold your table.”

“To someone with more scarves?”

“To someone who understood punctuality.”

She handed him the bag. He took out the scarf, examined the corner without visible alarm, and folded it back inside.

“Thank you for not putting it in the washing machine.”

“I considered a heroic rescue. Then I read the label.”

“Many disasters have been prevented that way.”

He had changed out of his work clothes. His hair was still slightly damp at the edges, and he carried no folder or laptop. Eve noticed these things before she noticed that she was noticing them.

Dumpling came to inspect his shoes. Julian greeted him, waited for the brief exchange of sniffing and rubbing to finish, then stepped back to let Eve leave.

The cat remained just inside the doorway. Eve checked his position, closed the door carefully, and turned the key.

No one had needed to lose another object.

The restaurant near the library occupied a narrow corner with windows on two sides. Its sign was smaller than the lettering advertising the laundrette next door. Eve had walked past it often enough to know that people stayed after their plates were empty, which seemed a promising recommendation for a place where she wanted to talk.

Inside, a server showed them to a table near the window. It was not the most secluded table; a couple sat behind Eve, and a woman farther along was reading a paperback while waiting for food. There was room between the chairs, and no loud music forcing every sentence into a performance.

Julian put the scarf bag beside his feet.

“Good choice,” he said.

“We haven't eaten yet.”

“The chairs are already ahead of the one in the furniture shop.”

Eve pressed her back against hers. “Retrieval included.”

They opened the menus.

The soup was not potato and leek. Julian studied the blackboard, where someone had written lentil, then added a small drawing that might have been a leaf or an unsuccessful spoon.

“You can be disappointed,” Eve said.

“I like lentils.”

“An adaptable man.”

“Within reason.”

She chose baked chicken with rice and vegetables. He ordered the soup and a toasted sandwich, asking what was in the dressing before deciding on it. The question was unremarkable, but Eve liked hearing him choose something instead of saying that anything would do.

When the server had gone, she reached for the water jug.

“This is my treat, by the way.”

Julian looked up. “I was going to pay.”

“I invited you.”

“And I asked you to the film.”

“The film was free.”

“The tea wasn't entirely theoretical.”

“I bought potatoes.”

“Exactly. So I can buy dinner.”

Eve set the jug down with more force than she intended. Water moved against its sides, not enough to spill but enough for both of them to notice.

“I don't want you to feel you have to.”

“I don't.”

“You said it very quickly.”

“So did you.”

That was inconveniently true.

At the next table, someone was having a much more successful conversation about a dog who had learned to open the laundry basket. Eve could hear the laughter over her own sudden discomfort.

She had chosen the restaurant partly because she could afford it. She had wanted the small pleasure of taking Julian somewhere, giving him a meal, being the person who asked rather than the person grateful to be included. His quick offer had made that pleasure feel as if it had been removed before she could explain it.

But he hadn't said she couldn't afford dinner. He hadn't looked at the prices and rescued her.

She moved the jug farther from her elbow.

“I wanted to take you out,” she said. “That's why I asked.”

Julian rested the menu he had still been holding against the edge of the table.

“I liked being asked.”

“Then let me do it.”

He was quiet for a moment.

“All right.”

The ease of the answer left her with nowhere to put the argument she had been preparing.

“All right?”

“Yes.” He looked at the jug. “Unless you want a more complicated response.”

“No.” She let out a breath. “No, I don't.”

“I'd like to take you somewhere another time.”

Warmth moved through her so suddenly that she looked down at the folded napkin.

“We could take turns,” she said.

“We could.”

“Not matching every amount.”

“I wasn't planning to audit the potatoes.”

Her laugh arrived with a small, embarrassing amount of relief.

She poured water for them both. This time the jug touched the table quietly.

“What did you want to do by paying?” she asked.

Julian considered the question instead of giving her a polished answer.

“Something pleasant for you. And I usually reach first before thinking much about it.”

“I think I was doing that second part too.”

“Then we've both improved our evening by approximately one bill.”

The server returned with bread and two small plates. They moved their menus aside. Eve was grateful for the ordinary interruption, for a task requiring only that she pass the plate nearest her.

She tore a piece of bread and found it still warm.

For a while they talked about the library. Julian had recently returned a book he hadn't managed to finish; Eve wanted to know whether he felt guilty about unfinished books.

“Sometimes.”

“I think of them waiting on the shelf, disappointed in me.”

“That seems an exhausting relationship with paper.”

“Do you never feel judged by your belongings?”

He glanced toward the scarf bag.

“Recently, yes.”

She laughed and told him about the repaired pocket in her carrying bag, including the coins it had been quietly collecting. He admitted he owned a drawer containing three unrelated keys and had been unable to throw them away for years.

“What if you suddenly find the locks?” she said.

“Exactly.”

“This is why people need friends. Someone has to defend these choices.”

“Or identify the locks.”

Their food arrived. Eve's plate was hot enough that the server warned her twice, then moved it by the edge of a cloth. Julian's soup came in a wide bowl with the sandwich on a separate plate.

The first spoonful made him pause.

“Good?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Not a professional assessment. You can use more words.”

He tasted it again. “Warm. Slightly smoky. Better than the soup I made last week.”

“What happened to yours?”

“Nothing dramatic. I forgot salt until I was halfway through eating it.”

“Did you add it?”

“Eventually.”

She liked him best, perhaps, when he was allowed to be unimpressive in a way that cost nobody anything. Not the person expected to know the answer, not a handsome face being asked to smile properly for the camera. A man with a good sandwich and three mysterious keys.

He asked how her Tuesday had gone.

Eve described a client who had sent the correct photograph under a filename that said final, revised, new, use this one, followed by a number that suggested several previous disappointments.

“Was it the right one?”

“Yes. Which made the name technically accurate.”

She had finished the agreed edit before leaving. One question remained for tomorrow, but it did not need Julian to solve it. She stopped before turning dinner into a full account of her working methods.

“And yours?”

“A lot of ordinary work. I took my break.”

“Outside?”

“For part of it.”

He told her about a delivery driver struggling to read a faded building number while standing directly beneath a much larger new one. Julian had pointed to it; the driver had looked up, sighed, and said it had been a long day.

“I know that feeling,” Eve said.

“So do I.”

There were no private patient stories, no borrowed emergencies making the conversation important. Eve found she didn't miss them.

When their plates were cleared, Julian asked whether she wanted dessert.

“Yes,” she said, before calculating what answer would seem easiest.

The dessert board offered apple cake or ice cream. They chose one portion of cake with two forks because both wanted a little, not because the shared plate required a meaning. The cake was dense, warm at the center, and unevenly divided by the time they finished discussing which corner had the best crust.

Eve asked for the bill.

Julian left his wallet where it was.

It was such a small act of listening that she almost missed it. Then she took her card out and paid the amount she had expected, plus the service charge clearly printed on the slip. She did not need to announce that she could manage it. He did not thank her as though she had performed a feat.

“Thank you for dinner,” he said once the server had gone.

“You're welcome.”

Outside, the library windows were dark. A return slot beside the door accepted someone's book with a hollow clunk. The laundrette still glowed, a row of machines turning behind glass.

Julian took the scarf out of its bag and looped it around his neck. Eve watched him find the ends.

“You remembered,” she said.

“I've been given a second chance.”

At the corner, their routes divided. Her building was a short walk to the left; his stop lay to the right. This time she did not want to keep him by pretending there was something they had forgotten to discuss.

“I'd like to see you again,” she said.

The words came out quietly, but they came out whole.

Julian turned toward her.

“I'd like that.”

She smiled before she could moderate it.

“You said you wanted to take me somewhere.”

“I did. I'll check when I'm free and ask you.”

“Good.”

Neither of them named the arrangement more precisely. There was room for the next invitation to be what they chose when it came.

They said good night at the crossing. Eve walked home alone, warm from the meal and from having asked for something without borrowing a reason.

At her door, she checked her bag for the key.

It was exactly where it belonged.
