# Chapter 011 — Returned for Revision

The envelope said APPLICATION RETURNED in a red rectangle large enough to announce a failed restaurant inspection.

Eve arrived just after three on Friday, with the remaining printed copies in her bag and Julian's pen in her hand. She noticed the envelope before she noticed the young woman holding it. The woman stood at the clinic counter with a tote bag full of new pet supplies and the rigid expression of someone determined not to cry in a public place. A yellow fleece blanket protruded beside a packet of toys. The toys still had their price sticker.

“It isn't rejected,” Tessa said. “We need the household section completed.”

“It's my cat. Not my household's cat.”

“It would live in the household.”

Eve stopped beside the chairs, Julian's forgotten pen warm in her fist. She had planned a thirty-second visit. Hand over pen, exchange a normal greeting, leave before finding an imaginary reason to stay. Instead she found herself staring at the red stamp.

At her old job, RETURN FOR REVISION had meant that someone above her disliked an idea but wanted her to discover the reason alone. She had a sudden, unreasonable urge to confiscate Tessa's stamp.

The young woman turned. “You made the video at the event, didn't you?”

“The short one. Yes.”

“You said we didn't have to be perfect.”

Eve recognized her then: Mia, who had lingered by the information table while everyone else packed up. Not one of the two households moving into assessment. She had asked where to apply and taken a form, then helped stop a stack of leaflets blowing across the pavement.

“I said the first conversation didn't have to be perfect,” Eve said.

“Well, mine apparently did.”

She held out the envelope. Eve could see handwriting beneath the stamp. Three spaces in the household section were empty.

“Could we talk over there?” Eve asked, pointing toward the chairs. “You're going to lose that blanket.”

Mia shoved the yellow fleece deeper into the tote, caught a falling toy with her elbow, and followed. Eve handed over the printed copies before Tessa returned to the telephone without looking relieved, which Eve appreciated. This was not a rescue from an unreasonable customer. At least, she hoped that wasn't how it looked.

Mia had chosen a cat pictured on the volunteer group's website: a black-and-white adult called Socks. Eve had seen him at the event only briefly, resting in his quiet enclosure away from the busiest corner. Mia opened a photo. His white feet looked unreasonably neat.

“I bought a covered bed,” she said. “And bowls. I didn't buy the food yet because it said to ask what he's eating. I've read everything.”

“What did your roommates say?”

“They said we'll discuss it.”

“And have you?”

“We're all working different hours.”

Eve looked at the three empty spaces again. She could remember how humiliating it felt to have enthusiasm treated as evidence of incompetence. Mia's things were new; her eagerness was painfully visible. Perhaps the group could accept the form provisionally. Perhaps a conversation could happen after approval, when the roommates understood that she was serious.

“I can ask what the next step actually needs to be,” Eve said.

“You know the vet.”

“I know where to return his pen.”

Mia glanced at her closed hand. “That's more than I know.”

A consulting-room door opened. Julian came out carrying an empty carrier for a staff member, caught Eve's eye, and stopped at the counter. She approached with the pen extended like a small official document.

“Your property.”

“Thank you. I was beginning to suspect the stationery drawer.”

“It was found under an orange suspect. No charges have been filed.”

He clipped it into his pocket. Eve should have left it there.

“Can I ask you something about the applications?”

Julian looked toward Mia, then back to her. “I don't make adoption decisions for the volunteer group.”

“No, I know. But do they have to send it back like that? She has bought everything.”

“That sounds expensive.”

“I meant she's committed.”

“Possibly. What did the other people in the flat say?”

Eve felt herself bristle. “That they'll discuss it.”

“What are they concerned about?”

She glanced at Mia. The young woman was rubbing the edge of the red rectangle with her thumb, as though it might come off.

“I don't know.”

“Then perhaps start there.”

It was a practical answer, delivered without drama. Eve disliked it for precisely three seconds.

“She thinks she's being told she isn't good enough.”

“That would be a difficult thing to hear. It still wouldn't tell us whether the other residents agreed.”

He was called from the corridor before she could argue. He lifted one hand to acknowledge the call, then added, “Tessa has the coordinator's details. You don't need me to carry a question across the room.”

“I wasn't asking for special treatment.”

His gaze stayed on hers. Not accusing. Not reassuring her out of the discomfort, either.

“I hope not,” he said, and went back to work.

Eve stood beside a display of dental-care leaflets, annoyed that the exchange had ended with her understanding him. Across the waiting room, Mia pulled another toy from the tote and attempted to remove its sticker. It tore into white shreds.

Eve sat down again.

“I can't get the requirement waived.”

Mia's shoulders dropped.

“But if you want, we could call your roommate and find out what the discussion is waiting for. Not to persuade them. To ask.”

“I already know. They think I'll get bored.”

“Did they say that?”

“Not in those words.”

“Then maybe we should hear their words.”

Mia looked at her for a long moment. Eve could see the exact point at which she became another adult standing between Mia and the cat, though Eve was twenty-seven and still ate cereal out of a measuring jug when the bowls were dirty.

Finally Mia unlocked her phone.

“Rachel's on her lunch break.”

They took the call outside, away from the clinic's waiting room. Eve asked whether Rachel was comfortable speaking with both of them. Mia held the phone without switching on the speaker until Rachel agreed.

“I haven't said she can't have a cat,” Rachel said immediately.

“I told you.” Mia stared at the pavement.

“You asked if I liked cats. That isn't the same question.”

Eve rested her hip against the low wall. A delivery bicycle clattered past. She waited.

Rachel worked nights twice a week. The living room was sometimes where she slept when the bedroom beside hers was noisy. Mia proposed keeping Socks in her bedroom, but expected him to have the flat when she was out. The flat's only good place for drying laundry stood in the kitchen, where Mia wanted the litter tray. Their third roommate had not been asked at all.

“Also,” Rachel said, “who's the emergency contact when you're at work?”

“I would come home.”

“You can't always come home.”

“I'd find someone.”

“Who?”

Silence gathered around the phone.

Eve had been ready for a heartless roommate with a list of arbitrary dislikes. Instead she could picture the drying rack, a damp sleeve hanging over the sink, someone trying to sleep behind a thin door. Ordinary objects made inconvenient witnesses.

“I don't want to stop her,” Rachel said more quietly. “But last month the parcel return sat in the hall for three weeks because everyone thought someone else was taking it. A cat isn't a parcel.”

Mia looked away. Eve resisted the temptation to soften the sentence on her behalf.

“What would be useful?” Eve asked.

“A conversation when we're all home. With the actual plan. Not a picture of a cute cat in the group chat.”

They agreed on an evening. Mia repeated the time, flatly at first, then typed it into her calendar. Rachel thanked them and went back to her lunch.

After the call, Mia folded the envelope in half.

“She could have said all that to me.”

“Perhaps she thought she had started.”

“Are you on her side now?”

Eve watched the traffic light change at the corner. A woman with a dog waited through the first few green seconds because the dog was still investigating a leaf.

“I was on your side before I knew what the situation was,” she said. “That wasn't terribly useful.”

Mia gave a short, reluctant laugh. “Great. So now no one's on my side.”

“I can help you make the questions clearer. That's different from helping you get a yes.”

For a moment Eve thought Mia would leave. She shifted the tote higher on her shoulder instead.

“Could the volunteer person come? Not to inspect us. Just to explain what they want.”

“We can ask.”

Inside, Tessa gave them the coordinator's email. Her name was Nora. She had run the assessment table at the event and possessed, according to Tessa, a miraculous ability to find chairs that had gone missing.

Mia wrote her message herself. Eve watched her delete “I've already spent a lot of money” and replace it with “Could we arrange a conversation about what needs to be agreed?”

When it was sent, Tessa offered to hold the unopened toys until Mia decided whether to return them. Mia chose to keep the yellow blanket and take the rest back to the shop.

“There's a receipt somewhere,” she said, digging in the tote. “If I haven't thrown it out.”

Eve found it stuck to the fleece with static. It peeled off with a tiny crackle.

“Your first successful retrieval,” she said.

Mia rolled her eyes, but the envelope was no longer folded tightly in her hand.

After she left, Eve returned to the counter.

“May I make a very small suggestion about the form?”

Tessa looked toward the telephone, then at Eve. “How small?”

“The stamp looks as if they've failed an exam. What if it said 'More information needed'?”

“We inherited the stamp.”

“I can tell. It has the moral confidence of old equipment.”

Tessa pulled a blank form toward her. “The requirement stays.”

“Yes.”

“And I'm not ordering anything from my own money.”

“I was thinking a printable note. One page. I'll send it to Nora to approve. Not a whole new campaign.”

Tessa considered this, then slid the form across.

Julian came through as Eve folded it into her bag.

“Did you find out?” he asked.

“The drying rack is involved.”

“Frequently underestimated.”

She smiled despite herself. “They're going to talk. With the coordinator, if she's available.”

“Good.”

“You could look slightly less correct about it.”

“I'll try in private.”

Outside, Eve checked her phone. No new client messages. No miraculous opportunity waiting because she had spent an afternoon being helpful. Only a photograph she'd taken that morning of Dumpling asleep with his face pressed against the wall, apparently unwilling to confront his own living room.

She put the phone away and headed home.

At the crossing, she remembered Mia saying, You said we didn't have to be perfect. The line would sound lovely in a video. It had sounded lovely when Eve wrote it.

Next time, she thought, she would leave enough room around a lovely line for the work it didn't explain.
