# Chapter 169 — Not Everyone Can Have a Key

Leah's suitcase stood beside the kitchen door.

Eve noticed it before Mia did.

It was small, dark, and entirely ordinary. Somehow it made the carefully arranged care notes on the table look much less finished.

“Oh,” Mia said.

Leah put her cup down. “I told you about the trip.”

“You said later in the month.”

“And then I sent the dates.”

Mia checked her phone. Eve looked away while she searched, suddenly wishing she had arrived ten minutes later or several days earlier. Mia had invited her to help test the revised household instructions before a call with the familiar paid carer. Nobody had invited her to referee a housemate's calendar.

Toby was resting in the next room after his normal outing. Mia had settled him there before answering the door, leaving his usual quiet space undisturbed. The ordinary success of that little arrival had made Eve smile. Now she could see Mia gripping the edge of the table as though a previous version of herself might be waiting beneath it.

“I missed it,” Mia said finally.

Leah nodded. She did not add a speech.

“I thought you might be here for the evenings,” Mia continued. “Not doing everything. Just—”

“Being here and being responsible are different.”

“I know.”

“I'm away anyway. Rachel's shifts haven't changed. She can't cover three days by being in the building.”

Eve kept her hands around her cup. This was not a new betrayal. It was a piece of information arriving late in a plan that had borrowed certainty from the word probably.

Mia sat down.

“I haven't booked the travel,” she said. “I said I'd confirm once I knew I could go.”

“Then there's still time to arrange it.”

Leah's answer was practical, neither angry nor soothing. She reached for the suitcase and moved it away from the doorway, where it had been waiting for her to finish packing.

“I can read your cupboard instructions now,” she said. “I can't become the missing evening.”

Mia pushed the page toward her.

“That would still help.”

The distinction changed the room. Eve could feel herself unclenching, though nobody had asked her to be tense on their behalf.

Leah read the first three lines and found the correct supplies without difficulty. At the fourth, she stopped.

“Which lead?”

“The one on the hook.”

“There are two.”

Mia closed her eyes briefly. “Of course there are.”

One belonged to an old arrangement and had not been put away. Mia checked the current routine, removed the unused item, and corrected the page. Leah tested the instruction again, then returned everything before going back to her packing.

No one applauded. It was simply better than it had been.

Mia's call with the carer came at the agreed time. She asked whether Eve could stay while they discussed the practical gaps; the carer agreed. Eve took notes only where Mia requested them, keeping the conversation in Mia's hands.

The usual visits were not enough for an absence of three days. Mia said that plainly at the start, before listing the dates and the actual periods she needed covered. She described the current support arrangements and what had changed since the carer's last booking. The carer asked questions Eve would not have known to ask, then said she could offer a different arrangement for those dates, including staying in the home, subject to confirming that the household agreed and the full routine was workable.

It would cost more than Mia's ordinary help.

Mia's pen stopped.

“Can you send the details and the price?” she asked.

“Yes. Don't confirm until you've read them.”

They arranged a visit before the trip to go through the routine, access, and the intended cover. Any gaps had to be solved before Mia accepted the work travel. A familiar person was a useful starting point, not proof that every new arrangement would suit Toby or the people sharing the flat.

After the call, Mia sat looking at the number she had written.

“I wanted this to be easy because it's a good opportunity.”

Eve waited.

“And now I want to be angry at Leah for owning a suitcase.”

“That would be a difficult argument to win.”

“I know.”

Mia laughed once, unhappily, then opened her own budget. Eve did not offer to pay the difference. She also did not call the amount small. It was Mia's money, attached to a job that mattered to her.

“Could I say no to the trip?” Mia asked.

“Yes.”

“You answered very quickly.”

“You asked whether you could.”

Mia looked at the calendar again. “I don't want to.”

“Then find out whether this version works. You haven't reached the answer yet.”

They used the remainder of Eve's agreed visit to test two more instructions. Mia found an old contact number on the sheet, corrected it from the current record, and checked that the carer would receive the appropriate private information directly. Nothing was going to be left on the venue's table for a passing participant to photograph.

At the door, Eve asked what Mia needed from her next.

“Nothing tonight,” Mia said. “I need to speak to Rachel and Leah, read the quote, and answer my manager.”

“Then I'll go home.”

“Thank you for not making the face.”

“What face?”

“The one where someone is already preparing to explain why having a dog means never wanting anything else.”

Eve shook her head. “I was mainly thinking about how many instructions I've written that begin with obviously.”

“Please retire that word.”

“I'll try.”

Two afternoons later, Mabel arrived at the venue carrying no map at all.

She came during an agreed short materials pickup, collected the blank labels Eve had set aside for her, and stood by the counter turning the packet between her fingers.

“I asked my old neighbor about helping if I have to be out,” she said. “The one who used to live on the floor below me.”

“What did they say?”

“That they could do a short afternoon, with notice. Not mornings. And no lifting Biscuit.”

“Is that useful?”

“Yes.” Mabel looked faintly surprised by the word. “Not everything I imagined. Still useful.”

She had made the call herself. The neighbor had remembered the kitchen differently, because it had changed since their last visit. Mabel had invited them over for tea and a proper look at what help would involve, without Biscuit being expected to tolerate unfamiliar handling for the sake of practice.

“I nearly gave them a key before we discussed any of that,” she admitted.

Eve glanced toward her own keys behind the counter.

“What stopped you?”

“They asked what I expected the key to do.”

Mabel's mouth twitched. Eve smiled back.

Apparently the neighborhood contained several people capable of asking a better question than the one on the sheet.

When Mabel left, Eve wrote no grand conclusion in her event notes. She recorded that participants wanted time to try arrangements outside the room and report back voluntarily. The space could help people compare confusing cupboards. It could not manufacture available neighbors.

Mia messaged later: Household agreed to the proposed stay after talking directly with the carer. Trial tomorrow. Still not confirmed until afterward.

Eve replied that she hoped it went well, then returned to measuring a shelf for opening supplies. She did not refresh the conversation every ten minutes.

The following evening Mia invited her for a brief tea after the trial, and Eve found the suitcase back by the kitchen door. This time it was Mia's.

Toby lay at the edge of his familiar rug while the carer finished a note with Mia. The visit had included working through the routine with Mia present, then a short planned absence within what was suitable for him. Eve had not witnessed the whole trial and didn't pretend that the quiet room now proved everything. She listened as they discussed what had worked, what needed adjusting, and the remaining practical detail about the morning handover.

Mia corrected it on the page before either could forget.

The carer checked the dates once more. Rachel and Leah had each agreed to the parts affecting their home; neither had been assigned animal care by default. The actual coverage, contact arrangements, payment, and what to do if plans changed were settled between the people responsible. Mia retained a workable alternative if the arrangement fell through before departure, rather than treating a travel booking as a reason to proceed regardless.

Only then did she confirm her trip.

Eve watched her send the message to her manager. The tiny sound from the phone seemed much quieter than the effort it had taken to earn it.

“I thought I'd feel completely relaxed,” Mia said after the carer had left.

“Do you?”

“No. I feel prepared and worried.”

“That sounds possible.”

Mia took a key from an envelope and put it with the agreed handover things, out of casual reach. She had not copied keys for the whole exchange group. One person had agreed to a defined job; the household knew who would be there and when.

Toby got up, wandered toward his water, and returned without commenting on the arrangement.

“He doesn't know I'm going to see a conference room with no windows,” Mia said.

“A glamorous opportunity.”

“They've sent photographs. There are twelve identical chairs.”

“You could make instructions for finding yours.”

Mia laughed. This time it lasted.

Then she opened the suitcase and discovered she had packed two left shoes from different pairs. Eve sat on the floor to help find their partners while Mia muttered that apparently all her competence had been allocated to the dog.

“Your shoes haven't attended the workshop,” Eve said.

“They're letting the household down.”

They found one beneath the bed and the other exactly where it belonged, which Mia considered particularly unfair. She put the matching pair in the case, checked both before closing the lid, and laughed again at her own exaggerated care.

They had tea while she checked the travel details. Eve asked what the work itself involved, and Mia began describing it with increasing animation. There were people she wanted to meet, a task she had prepared for, and a chance to be trusted with something new. The dog did not disappear from the conversation. He also did not occupy every sentence in it.

At the door, Mia thanked her again.

“I didn't do the care plan,” Eve said.

“I know. You helped me stop pretending my first answer was already a plan.”

Eve accepted that without making it smaller.

On the way home she passed the venue's dark window. The empty corner was just visible beyond the reflection of traffic. In a few days the room would have its proper opening. Before then, there were orders to check and a sign to collect, none of which would solve every difficulty of owning an animal or running a business.

Still, six people had sat around a table with pens, and one of them could now go away for work without relying on a housemate who wouldn't be home.

Eve found that worth the rent for an evening.
