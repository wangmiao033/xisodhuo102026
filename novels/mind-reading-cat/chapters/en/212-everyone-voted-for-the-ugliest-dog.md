# Chapter 212 — Everyone Voted for the Ugliest Dog

Eve's new sample had a pale blue border, a useful writable space, and a small illustration of a window with plants arranged along its sill.

Mia looked at it for several seconds.

“That's lovely,” she said.

Then she put her voting sticker beneath the potato dog.

Eve watched the sticker go down.

“You understand the question?”

“Which one makes me want to stop and look.”

“Yes.”

“The dog.”

“You didn't even hesitate.”

“I said something nice first.”

The samples occupied a portable board beside the front table, not the wall awaiting its final repair. Eve had invited comments during an ordinary open session before commissioning any new materials. The board asked what people would actually keep or use. Nobody had to buy anything, leave an email address, or pretend to be an enthusiastic focus group.

Three designs were Eve's. The fourth space held a photograph of the ugly magnet Mia had brought back from her trip, with a handwritten note explaining that it was a conversation prompt, not a product for sale.

The actual magnet remained on the metal cupboard, holding a public date card with the grim competence of a very small administrator.

It had five brown lumps, uncertain legs, and a red collar carrying almost the whole burden of being a dog.

Eve had added its photograph because Robin suggested including something familiar that was not designed for this exercise. She had assumed it would loosen people up before they discussed the real samples.

So far, it was defeating the real samples decisively.

“Perhaps it's the photograph,” Eve said.

Robin stood beside her. “Your photograph.”

“I can resent my own work.”

Mia laughed and went to put away her coat. She had come without Toby; this was an afternoon she could fit around his ordinary care, not a new test of how long someone else could cover for her.

Eve picked up a stack of blank cards and tried to look interested in evidence.

The first new visitor chose the dog too.

“Why?” Eve asked.

“It looks how I feel when someone asks whether I've finished packing.”

“Do you own an animal?”

“A very tidy rabbit. This isn't about him.”

The woman took one of the writable samples and studied the lines.

“This would be useful, though. Are these meant to go on the fridge?”

“Possibly. We're comparing a small reusable reminder card with a printed pack. Nothing's in production.”

“I'd want more space. I write enormous letters when I'm trying to make my husband notice something.”

Eve handed her a plain card and a pen. “Show me how much.”

The woman wrote CHECK THE BAG in letters large enough to be seen from the far side of the room.

“That's an impressive bag,” Robin said.

“It contains everything except the thing we need.”

A man beside her laughed. He described once taking his dog's empty food container on an overnight visit while leaving the filled one on the kitchen counter. Eve asked whether he would rather tell that story to the group or keep it between them. He said the dog had already told everybody who mattered, but he was happy to repeat it himself.

By the time he finished, two more people had added stickers to the dog photograph.

Eve resisted the urge to explain that stickers were not a substitute for answering the actual product question. The answers were arriving; they simply weren't following the order she had imagined.

She wrote down the request for larger space.

Mabel arrived near the end of the first hour, without Biscuit, who was resting at home while familiar cover stayed with him. She took her usual time removing her scarf and looked at the board.

“Still a potato,” she said.

“You can choose something else,” Eve told her.

Mabel placed her sticker beneath the dog.

“Apparently I cannot.”

“You haven't seen the plant design properly.”

“I have. I don't want to wash all those windows.”

Eve laughed despite herself.

Mabel sat at the table and pointed to the blank reminder card. “I'd use that. With less pale writing.”

“The pale writing is the example.”

“Then make the example easy to see.”

Eve changed it with a dark pen. The alteration made the design less delicate and immediately better at the job it had been given.

Across the table, Mia was describing the magnet's arrival. She did not tell anyone private details from the care arrangements for her trip. She told them that she had found an appallingly ugly souvenir after managing to go away for work, and that the venue had seemed the right home for it.

“It wasn't a reward for being perfect,” she said. “Everything took longer than I thought. I still got there.”

The woman with the rabbit nodded.

“That's why I like him. He looks as if he got there.”

Eve looked from the photograph to her carefully composed window.

Her illustration described a home where someone had placed every pot at a thoughtful angle. The magnet reminded people of a room in which they could admit that the normal food was not a clear instruction, that three biscuit tins could conceal three completely different things, and that even Julian could misinterpret an arrow.

The memory was the attractive part.

She had been trying to replace expensive scenery with an inexpensive beautiful object. The visitors kept pointing toward a different kind of afternoon.

Robin brought over a sheet headed THINGS WE HAVE ACTUALLY FORGOTTEN.

“May I?” they asked.

“Please.”

They put the sheet in the middle of the table. People could add a harmless example or leave it blank. No addresses, contact details, or real access instructions belonged on it.

Within minutes it contained a lead, glasses, a lunch, and the word THURSDAY underlined twice.

“Someone forgot a whole day?” Mia asked.

“I attended a Wednesday appointment,” the man said. “With great punctuality.”

Eve drew a tiny, indignant calendar beside it.

Mabel watched her pen. “That looks more like you.”

“Chronologically distressed?”

“The drawing. It knows something has gone wrong.”

Eve made the calendar's eyebrows more offended. The table laughed.

She had not erected a backdrop or given anybody a role. The joke had come from a thing someone chose to tell them, and now there was an object that might help him remember a date rather than pretend he never forgot one.

“Could we try a small session built around these?” she asked Robin quietly.

“Drawing the problem?”

“Or finding a way to make it easier to notice. People bring a harmless example. We use made-up details in anything displayed. They take their own version home.”

“That's close to what the exchanges already do.”

“Yes. Better than calling it something enormous.”

Robin nodded. “We could run one within the usual small-group hours. Without a new set.”

Eve asked the visitors what would make such a session worth attending. One wanted help choosing what information to leave out. Another wanted to compare whether a photograph or a sketch made an object easier to recognize. Mabel wanted time to try reading the result from farther away.

Nobody requested a painted skyline.

One woman said she would not pay for a drawing workshop because she could already draw. Eve thanked her and asked what she had come for today.

“Other people's mistakes,” she replied cheerfully. “It's encouraging.”

That answer went into the notes too. Eve did not convert it into a promise that the woman would book something later.

When the open session ended, Robin counted the stickers while Eve collected the written feedback. The dog had a clear majority. Her window had two votes, one from a person who genuinely preferred quiet designs and one from Mia's second visit to the board.

“You voted twice,” Eve said.

“I felt sorry for your window.”

“The window will recover.”

They removed the sympathy vote with Mia's permission and wrote the actual count. The people who liked the window still mattered. A popular choice did not require everyone else's taste to become a joke.

Before taking the board down, Eve added a second question beneath the winning photograph: WOULD YOU ACTUALLY USE IT?

The answer was less triumphant. Some people wanted to look at the dog but had no need for another object. The rabbit's owner preferred the plain card she had tested. Mabel wanted a larger example, not a souvenir. Eve separated those answers from the popularity count instead of treating affection as an order.

Robin tapped the two columns. “That saves us buying a box of applause.”

“A beautiful box,” Eve said.

“Still difficult to pay the rent with.”

She marked the next step as a small paid activity trial, not a stock purchase.

Eve put the sample away intact rather than dramatically tearing it up. It might belong in another project, or nowhere at all. Neither possibility required her to punish it.

Mia lingered to help carry her own cup to the return tray.

“Are you going to make lots of dogs?” she asked.

“No. I didn't design that one, and a gift isn't a manufacturing permission.”

“Good. I wouldn't know where to find the shop again.”

“We might make our own silly drawings in a session. Different creatures. Or bags. Or that furious calendar.”

“I'd come to that if the time worked.”

“When there's a confirmed date, you can decide.”

Mia nodded and left. Eve watched her wave to the cupboard through the window, maintaining the magnet's unusually active social life.

After clearing, Robin put the blank cards into the reusable supply box. They had used fewer materials than Eve expected. The paid hours ended without an extra trip to storage or an apology about the time.

“Would you host the trial?” Eve asked.

Robin paused with their coat in their hand.

“On the terms we agree. And I want to choose the order of the exercise.”

Eve's first impulse was to describe the order she had already imagined.

She held it long enough to hear what Robin had actually asked.

“Tell me what you'd change.”

They suggested beginning with the object or mistake rather than an example made by Eve. Participants could discover the need before being shown a polished answer. Eve could supply plain materials and limits, then step back.

“That may look messier,” she said.

“It may.”

She looked at the board, where the triumphant dog had acquired so many stickers that its photograph was beginning to resemble a decorated official.

“All right. Let's cost that version.”

At home that evening, she showed Julian her original sketch of the angry calendar, not anyone's private story or the feedback sheets.

“Wednesday,” he said at once.

“What?”

“It looks like Wednesday.”

“It was supposed to be Thursday.”

“A difficult week for the artist.”

She leaned against his shoulder, laughing. On the floor, Dumpling was washing a paw with the concentration of someone who had never attended the wrong appointment in his life.

Eve opened a fresh page in her notebook. Instead of drawing an entire room, she drew a small table and left space for whatever the people sitting there might bring.
