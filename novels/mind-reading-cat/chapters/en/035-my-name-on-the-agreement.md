# Chapter 35: My Name on the Agreement

The payment arrived while Eve was unclogging the kitchen sink.

There was no music. No sudden wash of sunlight. She was wearing an old shirt with one sleeve pushed above her elbow, and the small strainer in her hand contained something she would prefer never to identify.

Her phone lit up on the dry end of the counter.

She glanced at the notification, then leaned closer.

Pocket Path had paid the invoice.

The full amount. On the agreed date.

Eve looked down at the sink.

“Could you have given me five minutes?”

The sink declined to improve the occasion.

She finished cleaning it, washed her hands thoroughly, and opened the banking app. The money was still there. She checked it against the invoice, marked the payment received, and saved the record in the project folder.

The task took less than a minute.

She sat down afterward as though it had required a great deal more.

The film had not needed rescuing at the last moment. Felix had requested one change: the first caption should say what the bag organized rather than call it an answer to chaotic mornings. Eve had thought the original line was livelier. On rereading it, she admitted that it promised rather a lot from a pocket.

They changed the caption.

He approved the finished file. She delivered the agreed version, returned the sample, and sent the invoice. Pocket Path published it with the paid-work disclosure intact and the use limited to what they had signed.

That was the sequence.

It was so ordinary that part of her kept looking for the twist.

Dumpling jumped onto the chair beside her and stared at the empty space where the borrowed tote had spent several days.

“It's gone home,” she told him.

He sniffed the chair seam.

“You may find the new arrangement less commercially exciting.”

He sat down.

Eve opened the folder one last time. Inside were the first proposal, the discarded storyboard, the trial agreement, the receipt for the failed session, the smaller production agreement, the approved film, and the invoice.

Her name appeared on the documents.

Not *Dumpling's owner*, which was how several people introduced her lately. Not the woman with the clever cat. Her own name, attached to work she had described and delivered.

She had still lost money on the trial. The smaller job didn't erase that cost. She entered Sophie's fee, the simple lighting board, and transport in the expense column, then looked at what remained.

Less than the original offer had promised.

More than she would have made by accepting a job she couldn't fulfill and spending the week trying to force it into existence.

She resisted turning the calculation into a philosophy. There would be other jobs and other mistakes. Today, she could pay herself for a piece of work without pretending the entire month was solved.

Before celebrating, she moved the amount she needed for the next household bill into the account she used for regular expenses. She left a little aside for work costs and wrote down the balance rather than trusting the happy feeling of a new payment.

Then she opened the grocery list.

Eggs. Rice. Washing liquid. The less expensive apples that were perfectly good once she stopped expecting every piece of fruit to resemble a photograph.

There was something oddly satisfying about being able to buy ordinary things with an ordinary payment. She had spent so long imagining a breakthrough that she had forgotten a week could also become easier one completed job at a time.

She sent Felix a short acknowledgment.

*Payment received, thank you. I've saved the approved version and delivery record.*

His reply came a few minutes later.

*Thanks. Clear process. I'll let you know how the test does.*

No declaration that she had transformed his business. No immediate offer of a year-long contract. A test would produce information, and the information might or might not lead to something else.

Eve put the phone down before she could ask when.

Then she picked it up again to message Sophie.

*Are you free for dinner this week? My treat. Not a working dinner.*

Sophie answered with a photograph of a stack of chairs blocking a doorway.

*Thursday. If civilization survives until then.*

*Somewhere without folding furniture?*

*You understand me.*

On Thursday, Eve arrived at the noodle shop early enough to claim a table near the back, away from the door that slammed whenever someone forgot to catch it. She had chosen a place they used to visit before her former job made every weekday meal feel like stolen time.

The menu had changed. The tea still came in cups that were too hot to hold immediately.

Sophie appeared twelve minutes late carrying a bag full of cables.

“I said no work,” Eve said.

“These are emotional-support cables.”

“Do they need a chair?”

“They've had a difficult day.”

Sophie dropped the bag under the table and sat with the careful exhale of someone whose feet had begun negotiations with the rest of her body.

Eve slid the menu over.

“I haven't ordered. I remembered you hate it when people decide you're going to want the usual.”

“I do want the usual.”

“I was prepared for that complication.”

They ordered two bowls of noodles, cucumber salad, and dumplings that Eve immediately regretted naming aloud because Sophie raised an eyebrow.

“Your household is expanding into side dishes.”

“He hasn't approved the licensing.”

When the server left, Eve said, “First, thank you for the feedback session. The shorter opening was better, and the sound was much clearer.”

Sophie reached for her tea.

“You paid for that.”

“I know. I can still say you did it well.”

“Accepted.”

“And this dinner isn't payment. You already got that. I wanted to see you.”

Sophie paused, then set the cup down again.

“Good. Because noodles are a terrible invoicing system.”

Eve laughed, relieved that she had managed to say the distinction without making it sound like an office policy.

She asked about Sophie's week.

Not whether Sophie had a spare hour. Not whether a venue could be borrowed. Not whether she knew someone who could help with a shoot.

The week itself.

Sophie looked almost suspicious.

“Which part?”

“The part you want to talk about.”

That turned out to be a delivery of identical black cases that had arrived with no labels, three volunteers who had been sent to the wrong entrance, and a client who kept referring to a staircase as an accessible route because it was a wide staircase.

Eve listened. Once she began to suggest a labeling system, caught herself, and took a drink instead.

Sophie noticed.

“You can say it.”

“I was about to solve a problem you've obviously already solved.”

“Color tape. Photographs. A list. Then a second list for the person who lost the first list.”

“Right.”

“The problem wasn't that nobody knew how to label a box. The problem was that nobody thought it was their job until it became mine.”

There was enough irritation beneath the joke that Eve didn't answer with another one.

“That sounds exhausting.”

“It was. We got it done.”

Sophie reached for a dumpling, and the conversation moved to a man who had spent twenty minutes arguing that his name was on a guest list while refusing to tell anyone what his name was.

Eve laughed so hard she had to put down her chopsticks.

She had missed this: stories that didn't need to become content, an audience of one who already understood the embarrassing parts.

Halfway through the meal, Sophie asked how the paid film had gone.

“Paid,” Eve said.

“A strong opening.”

“Normal revision. Normal delivery. Normal payment.”

“Suspicious.”

“Extremely.”

She described the trial, the cost, the smaller concept. Sophie had known pieces of it, but not the way Eve had sat staring at the original number, hoping that wanting the money badly enough might make the cat comfortable in a new room.

“I didn't actually believe that,” Eve said.

Sophie waited.

“I may have been willing to postpone finding out whether I believed it.”

“That sounds more like you.”

Eve pushed a cucumber slice through the dressing.

“I keep wanting the right decision to feel good immediately.”

“Mine usually feels like sending a message and then wanting to throw my phone into a canal.”

“That's encouraging.”

“I offer realistic friendship.”

They ate for a while without talking. Around them, the shop filled with the scrape of bowls and other people's conversations. Eve wasn't scanning the room for a backdrop. She wasn't making a note of the warm light against Sophie's hair.

She was noticing that Sophie always saved one dumpling until the very end, then complained that it had gone cold.

“You can eat that now,” she said.

“I have a system.”

“Does the system include regret?”

“Of course. It's a complete system.”

After dinner, Eve paid before Sophie could reach for her bag.

“Next time's mine,” Sophie said.

“Fine.”

“No grateful speech.”

“I've used today's allocation.”

Outside, the air had cooled. They walked toward the tram stop slowly, Sophie no longer pretending that her shoes were comfortable.

At a shop window full of notebooks, Eve stopped.

“Don't,” Sophie said.

“I was only looking.”

“You look with your wallet.”

There was a small blue notebook near the front. Plain cover, thick pages, a neat band holding it shut. Eve could imagine writing plans in it that didn't collapse by Wednesday.

She smiled and kept walking.

“You're growing,” Sophie said.

“I have nine at home.”

“Then you're doing inventory.”

At the stop they stood together without searching for something useful to say. Sophie's tram arrived first. She climbed aboard, turned, and waved with the hand not holding the cables.

Eve waved back.

On her own journey home, she checked the account once, not the bank balance but the page where her recent posts lived. The walking guide was still there. So were the less polished everyday clips, the corrected caption, the quiet moments that hadn't become a campaign.

The paid film did not need to replace all of them. The account could remain an experiment rather than a machine that demanded a profitable expression from Dumpling every morning.

She answered one practical comment about the map's update date, then closed the app.

At home, Dumpling met her in the hall. He sniffed her trousers with great seriousness, perhaps offended by the restaurant smells, perhaps simply collecting the news.

“No work followed me home,” she told him, setting her keys in their dish.

That wasn't entirely true. There were future emails to answer, and a few revisions on another small assignment. But none of them needed doing tonight.

She filled his water bowl and sat on the rug. He approached, rubbed once against her knee, and settled just beyond reach.

Eve let him.

The paid invoice was filed. Sophie's dinner was not in the business-expense column. The borrowed tote was back at the shop, and the chair beside the table belonged to whichever creature reached it first.

Nothing had become permanent success.

Still, when Eve turned out the kitchen light, she knew exactly whose work had paid for the evening.
