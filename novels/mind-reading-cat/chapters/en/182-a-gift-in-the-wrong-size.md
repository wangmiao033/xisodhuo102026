# Chapter 182 — A Gift in the Wrong Size

The delivery driver looked at Eve, then at the door of You, Me & Paws, then back at the long cardboard box balanced against his trolley.

“Is there another entrance?”

“Not a useful one.”

Together they turned the box sideways. It made it through with the sound of corrugated cardboard brushing brick, and Eve signed for it while holding the door open with her hip. The label carried her name. Below it, in smaller type, was Julian's.

Her phone vibrated before she had found the scissors.

A photograph followed his message: the corner behind her worktable, taken during the opening preparations. She remembered complaining about the stack of loose supplies. He had remembered too.

*Something for the mountain of paper. It looked more useful than flowers.*

Eve smiled down at the box.

Then she opened it.

The shelf in the picture on the enclosed instructions was narrow, white, and very pleased with itself. The shelf emerging from the packaging was wide enough to require a second person to maintain eye contact across it.

Robin, arriving for their paid preparation hour, stopped with their coat half off.

“Are we expanding?”

“It folds.”

“Into what?”

Eve found the hinge and lifted. A second panel unfolded with a confident clack. The shelf became wider.

Robin put their coat back on its hook.

“Right,” they said. “Shall I get the tape measure?”

They had forty minutes before a small session about preparing home instructions for a carer. The guests would need chairs, table space, and a route through the room that did not include climbing over a romantic gesture.

Eve measured the recess behind the worktable. Then she measured the shelf. Then she measured the recess again, because she was briefly willing to believe the wall had changed its mind.

It had not.

“We could move the table,” she said.

Robin looked at the strip of floor they had worked so hard to keep clear.

Eve looked too.

“No,” she said. “We couldn't.”

They folded the shelf back down, keeping its protective sleeves in place, and carried it into the supply area. The cardboard box took up nearly as much space as the paper it was meant to solve. Eve's knees bumped it whenever she reached for a clean cloth.

She sent Julian a photograph cropped so that the shelf filled the frame.

*It arrived. Thank you for thinking of this.*

Nothing in that sentence was false. That made her feel worse rather than better.

The session went well until Eve needed spare paper. She went into the back room, shuffled past the box, retrieved the paper, and caught her cardigan on an exposed corner. The knit stretched. She unhooked it slowly, breathing through her nose.

On returning, she discovered that one of the guests had written *the obvious cupboard* in their draft instructions.

“Could we show which cupboard?” Eve asked.

The guest laughed and crossed it out.

Eve thought of her own photograph, which showed exactly enough to be misleading, and had to look very hard at the table.

After everyone left, Robin held up the packaging sleeve she had nearly thrown away.

“Keeping this?”

“Yes.”

“For a return?”

“I haven't decided.”

Robin slid it carefully back into the box. They did not ask why she was making a furniture problem into a private ordeal. Eve appreciated that, although it left her alone with the question.

Her phone lit up.

*Good fit?*

Julian had added a hopeful little face. She stared at it with the resentment usually reserved for software updates that arrived at precisely the wrong time.

He had chosen it while away, between an early class and whatever inadequate lunch he had found. He knew the supplies were annoying her. He had wanted to help. She could picture his pleasure at having solved something from a distance.

The worst possible reply was the honest one.

She typed *Lovely*, deleted it, then set the phone down.

At home, Dumpling inspected her damp shoe and abandoned it. He had no interest in the problem she carried through the door. She filled his water bowl, put away her coat, and found a loose white thread dangling from her cardigan.

“Oh, don't start,” she told the thread.

She called Julian during the gap they had agreed that morning. He answered from a quiet stairwell, his hair flattened at one side.

“Have you been asleep?”

“Attempting to understand a diagram. Similar appearance.”

He smiled. Eve felt a powerful temptation to give the shelf another chance.

“About the present,” she said.

His smile changed slightly. “Broken?”

“No. Much more successfully constructed than our room can accommodate.”

She told him the measurements. He asked her to repeat the larger one, then moved the phone as he reached for something. She heard paper rustling.

“I ordered the two-panel version.”

“Yes.”

“I thought that was the number of shelves.”

“It is very generous with panels.”

He disappeared from view for a second, leaving her looking at a patch of painted wall.

When he came back, he was grimacing.

“I read the depth. Then I looked at the photograph and apparently stopped participating.”

Eve laughed before she could stop herself.

“I thought you might have been using a different scale.”

“Optimism.”

“The room doesn't support it.”

“Can we send it back?”

The relief was so quick it embarrassed her. She had imagined a wounded silence, explanations, an entire conversation about whether she valued his effort. Instead, he was looking up the returns page.

“I kept the packaging,” she said.

“Excellent. I can't find the receipt.”

“The order email?”

“Yes. That would be the sensible place.”

She watched his eyes move as he searched. Then she said the part she had nearly hidden inside the measurements.

“I don't like it very much either.”

He looked up.

“The white finish?”

“And the little decorative curves. It looks as though it wants me to start selling bath salts.”

For a moment his mouth stayed perfectly straight. Then he laughed, leaning back against the stair rail.

“I thought the curves made it less industrial.”

“They do. Unfortunately, the industry they're replacing is mine.”

He held up a finger, still laughing, and turned aside to let someone pass on the stairs. Eve heard a greeting, then footsteps fading. When his attention returned, she had stopped gripping the end of her cardigan.

“You can tell me that,” he said.

“I know.”

“You didn't sound as if you knew.”

“I was grateful. Then I thought being grateful meant finding somewhere for it.”

“I'm grateful you haven't knocked a wall down.”

She gave him a look.

He softened. “I wanted to get you something useful. I'd rather know when it isn't.”

It was not a miraculous removal of every awkward feeling. Eve still felt a faint pinch, and Julian admitted he was disappointed in his grand solution. But neither feeling required a shelf to remain in a doorway.

They opened the shop's page together. He found the return window and the packaging instructions. She took a clear photograph of the item label, including the code she had accidentally cropped out of the first picture. The seller offered an exchange if the replacement was available.

A smaller model came in a plain grey finish. It had straight sides, adjustable shelves, and dimensions printed in a diagram large enough for two chastened adults to read.

“Don't buy it yet,” Eve said.

“I haven't.”

“I want to measure the boxes that actually go on it.”

“An alarming development in furniture shopping.”

Their call ended before they finished choosing. Julian needed to prepare for the next morning. Eve needed dinner, and the supplier did not require their relationship to solve the problem in one sitting.

The following morning she took three storage boxes to the recess and arranged them on the floor. One had a lid that needed extra room to lift. Another was more useful under the table. She marked the required width with two pieces of tape and opened the cupboard beside it all the way.

Robin arrived to find her kneeling on the floor.

“Structural optimism?”

“Being reduced.”

They tested the space with an empty carton. The smaller shelf fit, but only if it stood a little farther from the corner than Eve had first intended. That left the cupboard usable. She sent Julian the measurements and a photograph that included the whole room.

He replied with a screenshot of the correct model.

*This one? Including the absence of bath salts?*

*That one.*

He arranged the exchange through his order, and she confirmed a collection slot outside their next session. The difference in price would go back to him. There was no need to turn the leftover money into another surprise.

Before confirming, she nearly selected a taller version. The extra shelf would take the unsold souvenir cards, which still sat accusingly in their shallow tray. She could imagine the cards arranged at eye level, made desirable by better furniture.

Then she put one box on top of another and tried reaching behind them for the cloths.

The top box tilted. Eve caught it against her chest.

“No audience for that, please,” she told Robin.

“I saw nothing worth charging for.”

She returned to the shorter model. The cards needed a decision about whether anyone wanted them, not a tower from which to observe their own lack of popularity. She wrote their location on the inventory sheet and gave the cloths their reachable place.

It was a small distinction, but it changed what she sent Julian. Instead of a photograph of an attractive possibility, she sent the arrangement she and Robin could use on a busy afternoon, including the rather battered carton under the table.

His answer was not an improvement proposal.

*Looks as if you can get to everything.*

She could. That was the point she had kept losing among other people's hopeful pictures, including her own.

Packing the original took longer than opening it. Eve and Robin worked out how the corners had fitted into the foam, failed twice, and finally consulted the photograph she had taken before dismantling everything.

“Photographic evidence earns its keep,” Robin said.

“Unlike my first photograph.”

Once it was taped shut, she stood the box in the agreed collection place, clear of the exit. She turned around and found the worktable accessible again. A very ordinary patch of floor had returned to her.

Julian sent one more message before his class.

*Next time I might try flowers.*

She typed back without the earlier anxious pause.

*I don't have a vase here.*

His reply appeared almost immediately.

*Good information. Learning already.*

Eve put the phone away, picked up her scissors, and cut the loose white thread from her cardigan. The little snag remained, but it would not unravel if she left it alone.

When the driver came, she could thank Julian for the gift and wave the wrong shelf away with equal sincerity.
