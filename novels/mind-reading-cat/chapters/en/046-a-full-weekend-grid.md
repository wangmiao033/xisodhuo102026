# Chapter 046 — A Full Weekend Grid

At nine on Friday morning, Eve discovered that two promising inquiries could occupy the same square.

She had drawn the weekend grid by hand because the empty spaces looked more generous on paper. Eight days remained before the proposed events. That had seemed like plenty until she entered the times.

The neighborhood hall wanted an owners' story table during its Saturday exchange morning, ten until twelve. People would bring practical suggestions for short outings: a useful pocket, a place to sit, an item they always forgot. The coordinator had seen Eve's walking film and wanted conversation rather than a lecture.

Pocket Path wanted her at its showroom from eleven until one. Felix was planning a demonstration of the revised bags, with customers trying the pockets and discussing what they carried. He wanted a short film afterward, and somebody who could make the live explanation less like reading the contents of a drawer.

Both requests were for her work. Neither mentioned a clever cat.

Eve looked at the overlap and felt almost offended by it.

Dumpling put his chin on the blank Sunday column.

“You've chosen the stronger business model,” she told him.

She lifted the paper before he could settle fully and opened the messages again. The hall needed a proposal by Saturday afternoon. Felix needed an answer by the same evening. Neither booking was confirmed; she had time to ask questions before saying anything foolish.

She asked about access, staffing, the expected audience, and exactly what each client meant by coverage. Then she completed the caption revision already due that morning. She sent it without adding a new version nobody had requested.

By lunchtime, the replies had made the square smaller.

The hall's exchange was already staffed, but its coordinator couldn't manage Eve's table as well as the entrance. Pocket Path had a shop assistant available for sales, not a second person for customer interviews. Both clients expected Eve to remain onsite for their full sessions.

She made a second grid. In this one, travel took twelve minutes.

The map said seventeen.

Eve changed it to fifteen, then stared at the number until she changed it back.

At two, she went to see the hall.

The coordinator met her with a ring of keys and a roll of paper stuck beneath one arm. Several folding tables were already standing for a sewing group. A woman at the far end was trying to persuade a stubborn machine to release a sleeve.

“Your corner would be here,” the coordinator said.

The corner contained a table, six chairs, and a stack of unused display boards. Eve had imagined a wall of route cards. In person, the boards would block a window and reduce the clear passage to the toilets.

“Can those stay in storage?” she asked.

“Certainly. Are you thinking photographs instead?”

“I'm thinking people might want to get past us.”

They moved one table together. Its feet squealed across the floor, drawing an indignant look from the sewing machine's owner. Eve apologized, then walked the route from the entrance with her bag held at carrier height. Even without pets attending the indoor discussion, people would have coats, shopping, and children.

The coordinator watched her turn sideways around a chair.

“We pictured something a little more flowing,” she said. “People stopping whenever they like. Could you interview them as they arrive?”

Eve pictured a camera, a consent conversation, six chairs, late arrivals, and herself watching the clock for a journey across town.

“We could,” she said, too quickly. “I need to work out the staffing.”

The coordinator nodded. “Put that in the proposal. We've only budgeted for the one table, not a production crew.”

On the way out, Eve stopped to let the woman with the sleeve pass. The machine had won; she carried the whole thing toward the caretaker's room.

“Some jobs take two pairs of hands,” the woman said, shifting the weight.

Eve held the door. It wasn't advice intended for her, which made it unexpectedly difficult to ignore.

Outside, Felix called.

He had received a question about the showroom event: could customers bring their dogs?

“That depends on your space and arrangements,” Eve said. “The demonstration doesn't need animals. We should say that clearly, whatever you decide about ordinary visits.”

He agreed to a people-only demonstration. She felt a brief, sensible satisfaction. She could hold a boundary when it concerned a cat being expected to perform. Apparently her own presence remained a more negotiable resource.

“Would eleven thirty work for you?” he asked. “For the main explanation.”

“Yes,” she said, then caught herself. “Potentially. I'm checking a clash. I'll confirm tomorrow, as agreed.”

“No problem. I don't want to print a name and then replace it with an apology.”

“Neither do I.”

She went home and wrote *MAIN EXPLANATION 11:30* in the middle of the overlap.

By five, she had designed a way to hand over the last ninety minutes of the hall session without moving either event. Someone would receive the next group, keep the table going, handle the questions, protect the consent forms, and pack away when it ended.

The hall would have to agree to someone else handling that part. She had not asked yet.

She wrote *Sophie?* in a small box.

Then she enlarged the box.

There would still be two sets of preparation, two visits, a short film, information cards, and the paid work she already owed. To fit everything in, she moved the film edit to Sunday night. Then she moved a different edit to Friday night. The new blocks extended beyond midnight into a part of the page where she had usually written nothing.

For a moment the plan looked solved.

Her phone lit with a message from Julian. He was stopping at the grocery near her building after work. Did she still want the name of the radio play with the confusing town?

She did. She also needed bread.

They met beside a display of oranges, where Julian was inspecting the bottom of a paper bag with unreasonable concentration.

“Is there a problem?” she asked.

“The last one opened halfway home.”

“Did you lose everything?”

“One onion. It went under a parked car and declined further discussion.”

She laughed and chose a loaf. At the checkout, he took a reusable bag from his pocket instead. The precaution ought to have pleased her. Instead, some restless part of her envied how readily a problem could be answered by something already folded and available.

Outside, he asked about her day.

“Two clients want the same Saturday,” she said. “I think I can make it work.”

“Both events?”

“Yes.”

They moved out of the shop doorway. The evening air smelled of damp pavement and bread from the bakery next door. Eve balanced her loaf against her bag and showed him the photograph she had taken of the grid.

He enlarged the picture.

“What happens at one?”

“Finish at the showroom.”

“I meant one in the morning.”

“Oh. Editing.”

“And this?”

“Preparing the questions. That's only one late night.”

He looked at the other end of the page.

“Two.”

She took the phone back.

“They're eight days away. This is a first pass.”

“Then cancel one while there's still time.”

Something in her chest tightened.

“They aren't confirmed. I can't cancel something I haven't agreed to.”

“Decline one, then. Keep the hall. You care about that kind of work.”

“I care about paid product work too.”

“I didn't say you shouldn't.”

“You just chose the one I should keep.”

Julian shifted his grocery bag to his other hand. “You showed me a schedule with no sleep in it.”

“I showed you a problem I'm working on.”

A woman approached with a wheeled shopping trolley. They stepped aside again, their conversation becoming awkwardly mobile. Eve stopped beside the closed florist, where buckets had left dark circles on the pavement.

Julian faced her.

“If you already know it needs two people, taking it alone isn't going to prove you're capable.”

The sentence landed closer to an old bruise than he could have known.

“I don't need you to tell me what proves I'm capable.”

“That wasn't—”

“Please let me finish.”

He closed his mouth.

Eve looked at the loaf in her arms. She was squeezing it through the paper.

“I've spent weeks trying to get people to hire me for something besides a cat reacting in a room. Now two people have. One is a repeat client. I can't make those things unimportant because the sensible answer is inconvenient.”

“I don't think they're unimportant.”

“Then don't dispose of one while we're standing outside a grocery.”

He looked down at the bag, then back at her.

“All right,” he said. “I shouldn't have chosen for you.”

She had wanted that acknowledgment. Hearing it didn't immediately release the anger.

“You don't have to agree that I can do both,” she said.

“I don't agree that you can do the version on that page.”

“No. You've made that clear.”

A silence opened between them. Yesterday they had been laughing at imaginary bus routes in her kitchen. Today she couldn't find anything pleasant to do with the actual route home.

Julian rubbed a thumb along the bag handle.

“Would you like me to look at alternatives with you?”

“Not tonight.”

He nodded. “Okay.”

She nearly softened it into an invitation she didn't want to give. Instead she wished him a good evening, and he gave her the radio play's title before they parted, as if the ordinary reason for their meeting could still be honored.

At home, Dumpling came to inspect the groceries. Eve put the bread away, checked his bowl, and sat at the kitchen table.

The grid was exactly as unreasonable as before.

That annoyed her most of all. Julian could overstep and still have noticed something real. She couldn't repair the timetable simply by winning the argument about who controlled it.

She opened her reply to the hall coordinator. *I can provide full coverage* waited at the beginning of a sentence she hadn't sent.

Eve deleted it.

She wrote instead that she was working on the scope and would send a specific proposal tomorrow. To Felix she confirmed only the promised time for her answer. No names for print. No agreement concealed inside enthusiasm.

Then she messaged Sophie.

*Can we talk about a job tomorrow morning? I have a scheduling problem.*

Sophie replied after several minutes. She could meet for coffee at ten, for half an hour.

Eve looked at the little box bearing her friend's name. She imagined Sophie arriving, seeing the clash, and immediately identifying the missing arrangement. Relief rose before they had discussed a single task.

She turned the page over.

Dumpling stepped onto its blank back, kneaded once, and lay down across the whole weekend.

Eve moved her water glass out of reach. Then she took a separate sheet and began calculating what the two jobs actually paid, including every hour she had tried to hide after midnight.
