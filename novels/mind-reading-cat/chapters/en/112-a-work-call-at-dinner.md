# Chapter 112 — A Work Call at Dinner

Sophie had ordered the baked rice before Eve arrived.

"Self-defense," she said, moving her coat from the other chair. "They run out."

"I'm four minutes late."

"An entire era in cheese."

The restaurant was narrow, warm, and full of the sound of cutlery touching heavy plates. Eve put her bag under the table and sat down with the relief of somebody who had narrowly caught a train. She had finished the customer's changes, exported the corrected file, and sent the link while walking from the station.

All that remained was confirmation that it opened.

She placed her phone beside her water glass.

Sophie had changed her hair. Not dramatically; the ends were shorter, and she had pushed one side behind her ear with a green clip Eve hadn't seen before.

"I like that."

"The haircut or my expression of patient suffering?"

"Both. Did you get it done today?"

"Saturday. It was meant to be a trim. Apparently my definition lacked ambition."

Eve laughed and picked up the menu. For a few minutes they debated whether the vegetable dish was enough for two people who had both eaten unconvincing lunches. They ordered it, and an extra portion of bread.

Then Sophie said, "The exercise you asked about went reasonably well. After I stopped trying to explain the whole project in the first sentence."

"The activity description?"

"Yes. One of the others tried it on her father. He thought it was an evening of listening to somebody talk about books. Which was useful, because—"

Eve's phone lit up.

Can't open. Is this the right link?

"Sorry. Just confirming a delivery."

She opened the message, checked what she had sent, and copied the link again. The customer had selected an earlier one in the thread. It took less than a minute to explain. She put the phone down.

"Sorry. Her father thought it was a lecture?"

"Yes. So we changed the first line to say people would make something."

"Good. Concrete action."

Sophie looked at her for a moment. "You sound like the tutor."

"A bargain, considering my usual consultation fee."

The joke landed weakly. Eve reached for her water.

"Did the new version work?"

"Better. And we did the room exercise. I thought I'd stand in a corner and observe quietly. Within four minutes I was explaining where the spare chairs ought to go."

That made Eve laugh properly. Sophie imitated the tutor asking whether the chairs had requested representation. The bread arrived, warm enough that Eve pulled her fingers back from the first piece.

Her phone rang against the table.

The customer again.

She looked at Sophie. "I should take this. They may still be stuck."

"All right."

At the door, squeezed between the coat hooks and a family waiting for a table, Eve answered. The file worked. The customer now wondered whether the final slide could be moved earlier, and whether a second version might fit a different screen.

"I can look tomorrow," Eve said.

"We only need to know if it's possible."

It was possible. Describing how became a discussion of which slide should precede it. Eve could see the sequence in her head and started solving it aloud. A server asked her to move away from the doorway. She stepped outside, where a bus hissed at the stop and she had to ask the customer to repeat a question.

By the time she returned, the baked rice was on the table.

A spoon-shaped hole interrupted its golden top. Sophie was eating.

"I didn't wait," she said.

"Good. You shouldn't have."

Eve sat down. Her own plate had arrived too. She thanked the server, put her napkin on her lap, and reached for the bread. It was no longer warm.

"So the room exercise."

"We finished that story."

"Right. The tutor. The chairs."

Sophie nodded and took another mouthful.

Eve wanted to recover the evening quickly. "How's the festival project going?"

Sophie's spoon stopped above her plate.

"The bookbinding session."

"Yes. Sorry. I meant the event."

"There isn't a festival. There is a man in the class who talks about one."

Heat rose into Eve's face. She knew that. She had listened on the phone. Somewhere between the client's sequence and the restaurant door, the details had slid into a pile labeled Sophie's Work, available for later sorting.

"I know. That came out wrong."

"Okay."

The phone vibrated again. Eve glanced down before she could stop herself.

Sophie put her spoon on the edge of the plate.

"Can you tell them you're having dinner?"

"I did. They're just sending the screen dimensions."

"Then the dimensions can sit there."

"Yes. Of course."

Eve turned the phone facedown. For several seconds the table seemed too small to contain both plates and the silence.

"I'm sorry," she said. "It's been a complicated week."

"I know."

"And this customer might become regular work."

"I know that too."

Sophie wasn't raising her voice. Eve almost wished she would. Then Eve could respond to something visible instead of this calm refusal to make her excuses useful.

"I don't want to be difficult about one call," Sophie said. "Things happen. I've taken calls at dinner. But you were already halfway inside it when you arrived."

Eve pressed her thumb against the folded edge of her napkin.

"I thought I'd finished."

"And then every new thing became the last thing."

At the next table a child triumphantly announced that he had found the missing fork. Someone laughed. Eve could see a crumb on Sophie's sleeve and wanted absurdly to brush it away, as if a tiny service might put them back on familiar ground.

She kept her hands on the table.

"You're right."

Sophie looked toward the window before answering.

"When something's gone wrong for you, we can talk for an hour. You remember every part of what I say. When it's just my ordinary life, sometimes I feel like I'm talking while a file loads."

The sentence hurt more because it did not accuse Eve of never caring. She did care. She had noticed the haircut. She had remembered to ask about the exercise. She could assemble these facts into a defense, and for one ugly second she wanted to.

Instead she said, "I made you compete with a customer who wasn't even having an emergency."

"I don't want to need an emergency."

"You shouldn't."

Sophie picked up her water, then set it down without drinking.

"I'm glad things are happening for you. I am. I just don't want every meal to become the space between them."

Eve nodded. There wasn't a useful joke to put here.

She took her phone and checked the latest message once, deliberately. It contained dimensions and a request for the additional version by morning. She had not agreed to that deadline, but her enthusiasm outside had plainly left room for the customer to expect it.

She wrote that she could confirm timing and the additional cost the following morning, and that tonight's delivered version remained available at the working link. Then she switched off work notifications until the morning and put the phone inside her zipped bag.

"I should have said that outside," she said. "Not let them think I was starting tonight."

Sophie watched her, but did not reward the action with immediate cheerfulness.

"Yes."

Eve took a mouthful of her cooling food. It tasted mostly of having been somewhere else while it was hot.

For a while they ate. The silence stopped being punitive, if it had ever been that, and became two people not yet knowing where to put the next sentence.

Finally Eve asked, "Do you want to keep talking about the course, or have I spoiled that for tonight? Either is all right."

Sophie considered it.

"Not the course. I talked about it all evening yesterday."

"What would you like?"

"To complain about the haircut, actually. The front section is too short to stay in the clip. It keeps escaping into my soup."

Eve looked at the green clip, holding its small, increasingly unsuccessful assignment.

"Would you prefer sympathy or a practical answer?"

Sophie met her eyes.

"Sympathy. Don't design me a hair management system."

They both laughed, cautiously at first.

The green clip eventually lost. Sophie caught the loose section of hair, twisted it, and tried again using the restaurant window as a mirror. Eve held up the little mirror from her bag instead. Sophie accepted it without turning the offer into an announcement of renewed friendship.

"Is it straight?"

"The clip? No."

"Good. Honesty at last."

Eve shifted the mirror a little. "Now it's straight."

"My head's crooked."

This time the laugh didn't require care. They returned the mirror to its scratched case, and Eve found herself grateful for a problem too small to become anybody's rescue.

Dessert did not repair the whole evening. Sophie ordered coffee; Eve ordered a small pudding they shared because neither wanted an entire one. When the server brought two spoons, Sophie said the pudding looked unusually defensive, standing so firmly in its pool of sauce.

Eve nearly told her how Dumpling had looked in the opposite chair the previous week. Then she did tell her, briefly, because being attentive did not require erasing herself from the conversation. Sophie supplied an indignant cat voice that made Eve snort into her coffee.

When they stepped outside, the air felt cooler than the restaurant had promised. Sophie wrapped her scarf twice and checked the bus display.

"I've got Sunday morning free," she said. "I was thinking of going out to the riverside museum. There's a small exhibition about ordinary household objects. It looks pleasingly pointless."

"I'd like that."

"Not a work visit."

"I understand."

Sophie looked sideways at her. "And I'm not setting a test. If you've got a deadline, say so."

Eve checked her actual calendar rather than the hopeful version in her head. Sunday was clear until late afternoon. She would need to finish the additional client work on Friday if they agreed to it, or set a later delivery date before taking it on.

"I can do the morning and lunch. Home by three."

"Same. I've got laundry. Very exclusive engagement."

They chose a train and agreed to buy their own tickets. Sophie did not ask Eve to promise never to answer another phone. Eve did not offer it.

At the bus stop, she said, "I'm sorry I wasted part of tonight."

"You did," Sophie said, and touched her arm. "Don't waste the rest apologizing."

Her bus arrived before Eve could invent a more elaborate response. Through the window Sophie lifted a hand, then turned toward an empty seat.

Eve walked to the station with her phone still in the bag. She wanted to tell someone that she had behaved badly, hear that she was not a bad person, and sleep easier.

That could wait.

At home she fed Dumpling, washed his bowl, and put Sunday's departure time beside her keys. Then she sat down to eat the last piece of bread she'd brought home, which had survived the evening better than her pride.
