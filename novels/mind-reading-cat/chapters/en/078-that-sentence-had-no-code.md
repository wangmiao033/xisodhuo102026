# Chapter 078 — That Sentence Had No Code

Julian asked beside a hedge that needed cutting.

There was no sunset positioned behind him, no music from an open window, and no cat delivering a meaningful object. Eve had just moved her bag to the other shoulder because its strap was pressing the same place for too long.

“Would you like to hold hands?” he said.

She stopped walking.

He stopped too, leaving enough space that the question remained a question.

For one absurd moment, Eve thought she must have missed something preceding it. A joke about cold fingers, perhaps. A practical reason for crossing a busy road. They were on a quiet path beside the river, with no road immediately available to excuse anybody.

“Yes,” she said.

Then, because the first answer had emerged rather quietly, “I would.”

He offered his hand. She took it.

His palm was warm. Their fingers needed a moment to settle, an ordinary adjustment she was grateful nobody had promised would be effortless. When they began walking, they briefly chose different speeds and laughed at the resulting tug.

“Sorry,” she said.

“Same direction is a good start.”

She looked down once, then made herself look at the path. If she spent the rest of the afternoon inspecting their joined hands, she would eventually walk into a tree and give the moment a very different ending.

The wind moved dry leaves along the hedge. Somewhere behind them a bicycle bell rang; they stepped to one side together, released hands to make room, and joined them again when the cyclist had passed.

That second joining surprised her more than the first.

Neither had needed another explanation. He held out his hand, she put hers into it, and they continued as though they could choose a thing twice.

“I thought you were going to ask about the bridge,” she said after a while.

“Why?”

“You looked as if you were deciding how to phrase something.”

“I was deciding how to phrase that.”

“Ah.”

“What did you think it meant?”

“I was preparing several options. None of them was the actual sentence.”

He smiled. “That sounds exhausting.”

“It has been a busy few weeks.”

They reached a broader stretch of pavement where an older couple walked ahead, discussing a shopping list with considerable force. Eve found the noise reassuring. The world had not stopped to judge whether she was doing this correctly.

“Did you know,” she said, “that I once spent a very long time deciding whether a sandwich was personal?”

Julian looked at her.

“The sandwich I gave you after closing. Early on.”

“I remember the sandwich.”

“Not its possible symbolism?”

“I was hungry.”

She laughed, then groaned. “A devastating answer.”

“I was also glad you stayed to eat.”

Her laughter faded into a smile she couldn't hide.

“There. That's the part I would have spent an hour trying to obtain from the sandwich.”

“You could have asked.”

“I could have done many sensible things.”

His thumb moved slightly against her hand, not in a way she needed to interpret beyond the fact that it felt pleasant.

“I didn't ask enough either,” he said.

She waited.

“It was easier to suggest something useful. Return a container. Discuss an edit.”

“The container was becoming very well traveled.”

“Yes.”

“And now?”

He looked at her. “Now I'm asking about something I want.”

Eve felt her grip tighten before she consciously decided to answer.

“Good,” she said. “I want it too.”

They walked a little farther without speaking. She didn't need him to repeat the question in a more elaborate form. He hadn't promised a whole life. She hadn't been asked to decide one. They had chosen to be here, holding hands on a Sunday afternoon that had already survived a broken exhibition and an unsuccessful shortcut.

Near the bridge, the path divided. Eve recognized the road that would lead back toward her neighborhood. Julian asked whether she needed to head home soon.

“In a little while. Dumpling's dinner doesn't need to become late because I've acquired a social life.”

“Shall we take this road?”

“Yes.”

She was relieved that he hadn't made the cat into a rival for the rest of the day. She was also relieved by how much she wanted the day to continue.

A small dog on a lead stopped across the path to study a fallen leaf. Its owner apologized and tried to encourage it along. Eve and Julian waited, leaving room instead of stepping over the lead. The dog finally chose a different leaf and moved on.

“An extensive consultation,” Eve murmured.

“No fee, fortunately.”

She looked up at Julian. He was smiling at the dog, not trying to become its veterinarian in the middle of his afternoon. She liked this glimpse of him too: interested, fond, and able to keep walking when nobody needed his help.

At a narrow gate, they let go again. Julian held it while she passed, then followed. This time Eve reached first when they were through.

He took her hand without a comment.

The road smelled faintly of bread from a bakery near closing. A worker was taking in a chalkboard, erasing tomorrow's first line by accident with her sleeve. Eve watched the woman sigh and carry it inside rather than fixing it on the pavement.

“Would you like something to take home?” Julian asked, noticing where she was looking.

“Not hungry. I was admiring someone leaving a task for tomorrow.”

“A rare sight.”

“Endangered in certain professions.”

They passed the bakery and a bus stop where several people stood looking in the same direction with expressions of disputed hope. Eve recognized a poster she'd once designed in another job, altered enough that her favorite part was gone.

Usually she would have stopped to inspect the changes. Today she glanced at it, felt the familiar small irritation, and kept walking.

Julian asked what had caught her eye. She told him briefly.

“Do you want to look?”

“No. It isn't mine to fix now.”

He nodded. They continued, and she liked that he had offered the choice rather than treating the date as a protected room into which her working life must never enter.

A few streets from home, her hand had become uncomfortably warm inside his. She hesitated, then laughed.

“I need to change hands or stop for a minute. This is less graceful than I'd hoped.”

“So do I.”

They separated, both flexing their fingers with embarrassing relief.

“Excellent,” Eve said. “We've achieved honesty before permanent circulation damage.”

“I don't think we're at that point.”

“Please don't bring professional accuracy into my excuse.”

He grinned. She liked his grin enough that she briefly forgot what they had been saying.

They walked the last stretch with their arms occasionally brushing. It didn't feel like a retreat. The first touch hadn't created a requirement to maintain continuous evidence of it.

At her building, Eve took out her keys. Julian waited beside the step.

“Would you like to come in for a little while?” she asked. “I need to feed him, but there's tea.”

He glanced at the time. “I'd like to. For half an hour, if that's all right. I still need to do some things at home.”

“Washing powder.”

“Among other failures.”

“Half an hour is good.”

Dumpling waited inside the flat, round and orange and apparently unimpressed by the significance of anyone's fingers. Eve put her bag away before crouching to greet him. He sniffed her shoes, investigated Julian's, and went toward his usual food place with an unmistakable preference for what should happen next.

She fed him while Julian filled the kettle after asking which socket to use. There were no missing objects to explain, no need to arrange a return visit for a stolen scarf.

Eve watched the cat eat and felt an impulse to tell him everything.

*It worked. You can stop now.*

She didn't say it. Dumpling had not conducted the afternoon, and she didn't know what any of his earlier arrangements had meant. Whatever he sensed nearby, he hadn't been at the restaurant or the river. She was not bringing home proof that he had chosen correctly.

“What?” Julian asked from the counter.

“I was thinking I had a good afternoon.”

“So did I.”

She took two mugs from the shelf, pleased by how easily the actual sentence had survived being spoken aloud.

They drank tea at the table. She showed him the blank postcard with the red door, leaning it against the sugar tin because she hadn't decided where to put it. He studied the tiny painted steps.

“Still for your wall?”

“I think so.”

“It will need a very small tenant.”

“I've had applications from several dust particles.”

Dumpling came back after eating and settled near the window. Eve resisted placing the postcard beside him to see whether he would perform some clever response. Its door belonged to a picture, not his lost history.

Julian asked whether she had time for dinner later in the week. Eve checked her calendar rather than announcing she was free whenever he wanted.

“Wednesday evening,” she said. “After six thirty.”

“That works for me. Shall I send you a couple of options?”

“Yes. Somewhere with chairs that don't require formal clothes.”

“A demanding brief.”

“I have extensive experience with briefs.”

When his half hour was up, he stood. Eve walked him to the door, wanting him to stay and glad he trusted her enough to leave when he'd said he needed to.

He took her hand briefly on the landing.

“Wednesday,” he said.

“Wednesday.”

He let go before she needed the keys. There was no kiss, and no awkward apology for the absence of one. Eve watched him go down the first flight, then returned inside and closed the door.

Dumpling had occupied her chair.

“Efficient,” she told him.

She took the other chair and opened her private notebook, the one that didn't contain client deadlines or observations she might later confuse with facts about the cat. On a blank page she wrote the date and one sentence.

*Julian asked to hold my hand. I said yes. I was happy.*

It looked almost embarrassingly simple.

She considered adding the closed gallery, the bread, the coat caught on cupboard handles, and the strange relief of being able to say her hand was too warm. There was room for all of it, but she didn't need to write the whole afternoon before she was allowed to keep it.

Instead she placed the postcard between the pages until she found somewhere for it.

Her phone lay nearby. She could have told Sophie, who would probably have sent an affectionate and merciless answer. She could tell her another day, when they were both free. For now, there was nobody to convince and no response she had to collect before the day became real.

Eve closed the notebook.

Across the table, Dumpling washed his face with the concentration of someone entirely satisfied with his own Sunday.
