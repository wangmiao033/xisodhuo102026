# Chapter 216 — A Morning Without Hints

On Sunday, Dumpling failed to discover the butter.

It stood behind the bread bin, precisely where Eve had put it while clearing breakfast. Julian searched the refrigerator twice, then opened the cupboard containing plates. The cat sat in a square of sunlight and watched a small moth beyond the window.

Once, an interruption at just the wrong moment might have sent a human hand toward the right place. This morning, Eve found the butter because she remembered moving it.

She put it away without announcing the achievement.

Dumpling followed her into the kitchen when she took down his bowl. He ate. Afterwards he drank, used his litter tray, and attempted to sit on a cloth Julian was folding. There was nothing especially mysterious about wanting the cloth. It had recently been warm.

Nevertheless, Eve kept looking at him.

By eleven, she had looked so often that he left the chair and went beneath the sofa.

“That may be an answer,” Julian said.

“To what?”

“Whether he likes being stared at.”

Eve lowered herself onto the rug, leaving the cat space to come out or remain hidden. She could see the white end of a paw beneath the upholstery.

“I'm trying not to make anything of it.”

“I can tell. You're doing that very intensely.”

She laughed unwillingly.

Yesterday's guessing game had supplied no evidence worth testing. Today's missing butter supplied even less. Dumpling had never been an infallible finder of misplaced breakfast. Eve knew that perfectly well, yet a peculiar absence had settled at the edge of the morning. She had grown used to certain interruptions without noticing how often she waited for them.

The white paw withdrew.

Eve moved away from the sofa.

“I'm not worried because he hasn't helped,” she said.

Julian put the folded cloth away. “I didn't think you were.”

“I don't want to miss something because I'm trying so hard to be sensible.”

That was harder to say than the joke about butter.

He sat at the other end of the rug.

“What have you noticed about him, apart from that?”

They went through the ordinary things she could actually describe. His meals, his water, his litter tray, how he moved and whether he wanted to play. Eve resisted turning each answer into a sign with a hidden meaning. He had finished breakfast. That meant he had finished breakfast, not that she had received permission never to worry.

There had been no new problem she could point to.

“If that changes, we deal with what changes,” Julian said. “We don't have a medical measurement for the other thing.”

“I know.”

“And if you're concerned about his health, we can arrange a normal appointment. You don't have to explain it with a theory first.”

Eve looked toward the sofa. Dumpling had put his nose out, perhaps investigating the fact that neither of them was looking directly at him now.

“Would that be ridiculous?”

“No.”

“Would you tell me if I was trying to turn a checkup into an experiment?”

“Yes.”

“Very quickly?”

“Apparently I have already started.”

She leaned against his shoulder for a moment. Then she stood, found the clinic's normal contact details, and asked for an available nonurgent appointment rather than expecting Julian to make room during his own work.

The reply came during Monday's opening hours. There was a place later that morning with Dr. Clark. Eve rearranged the order of her own editing work and confirmed it. She did not cancel anyone else's paid session or summon Robin to cover her absence. The venue wasn't opening until the afternoon, and the appointment fitted before the preparation time she had already allowed.

Getting Dumpling into his carrier took longer than arranging the appointment.

He walked halfway in, reconsidered the entire institution of travel, and sat down with his hindquarters outside.

“You've left the complicated part behind,” Eve told him.

She waited. There was no need to shove him along for the sake of a schedule she could still meet. Eventually the familiar blanket interested him more than resisting did. She secured the carrier and checked it before carrying him downstairs.

At the clinic, Tessa asked how he had been eating.

“Normally.”

“Any vomiting or change in the litter tray?”

“Not that I've seen.”

Eve answered the questions as they were asked. When Tessa handed over the information sheet, she realized she had been preparing to apologize for bringing a cat who wasn't visibly unwell.

Instead, she said thank you.

The waiting room smelled of the same cleaning product it always did. A dog across the room pressed against his owner's knee while the owner read a notice twice. Eve kept the carrier positioned so Dumpling wasn't required to face him. Through the side opening she could see the cat adjusting his blanket with one paw.

Nobody needed a performance from him to justify the appointment either.

Dr. Clark listened to the changes Eve could describe. She said the cat seemed less responsive to some familiar social moments but was otherwise doing his ordinary things. She did not translate an uncertain ability into a symptom she hadn't observed.

“Less interested in people generally?” he asked.

“Not exactly. He still comes to us. He just hasn't had those unusually well-timed reactions I sometimes notice.”

“All right. We'll check him, and you can tell me if anything else occurs to you.”

The examination was ordinary, which made it more reassuring than an impressive explanation would have been. Dr. Clark handled Dumpling carefully, paused when he stiffened, and asked about routine changes at home. Eve explained the quieter weekend and the recent reduction in large events at the venue. She kept the business story short. The cat did not require the quarterly accounts attached to his medical history.

Dumpling objected to one part of being handled with a low, affronted sound, then accepted a pause with his usual dignity.

Eve held the carrier steady rather than telling him he was being brave for an audience.

At the end, Dr. Clark said he hadn't found an evident health problem in that day's examination. That was not a guarantee about every possible cause of every change. They discussed the ordinary signs that should prompt another call, and Eve repeated the relevant instructions back to make sure she understood.

There was no recommendation to provoke him into responding.

There was no box on the record marked magical function.

At reception, Eve paid for the appointment. Julian came through the corridor while she was putting the receipt away. He greeted her, bent slightly to look through the carrier, and asked how it had gone.

She gave him the short version.

“Good,” he said. “I'll see you tonight.”

Then he returned to his work.

Eve liked that he hadn't turned her visit into his own examination, or stayed to demonstrate special concern in front of the waiting room. She could tell him more over dinner, when she was his partner rather than somebody occupying a space between his appointments.

At home, Dumpling emerged from the carrier and inspected the apartment as if she might have replaced it while he was out. Finding the familiar furniture, he went directly to a cushion.

Eve left him in peace.

She had forty minutes before she needed to leave for the venue. A new client assignment waited on the laptop: a separate, paid follow-up clip the storage-box proprietor had commissioned after approving the first demonstration. He had supplied footage of an additional product, and they had agreed a fresh editing fee rather than reopening the finished job. She opened it, watched the first comparison shot, and caught herself glancing toward the cushion again.

The cat was asleep.

She turned her chair so the screen, rather than Dumpling, occupied the center of her view.

For half an hour, she worked. A lid obscured the product label in the second take. The sound needed a small correction. The client's requested sentence fitted better over a close shot than over his hands. Those were real problems with available answers, and she solved two of them before getting her bag.

At the venue, Robin was checking the plain materials for the afternoon's small session. The rear room had been cleared after the repairs, but they had kept the simpler table arrangement. Extra floor did not have to become extra chairs.

“How was the appointment?” they asked.

“No evident problem today. Normal watching for changes.”

“Good.”

Eve put her coat away.

Robin held up two versions of an example card. “Which one can you read from there?”

Eve stepped back to the entrance and tested them. The darker writing won. She was relieved by the directness of the question, then embarrassed by her relief. She had been trying to make the whole day prove she could manage without a hint from the cat, as though he had routinely chosen type sizes for her.

He hadn't. She had done this work herself before, and she could do it now.

The afternoon brought a missing pen, a visitor who had the wrong date, and a useful discussion about whether an arrow pointed to a drawer or beyond it. Eve found the pen in the supply tray. Robin helped the visitor check the correct booking. The group disagreed about the arrow until its owner turned the sheet around.

No miracle was noticeably missing from any of it.

After closing, Eve checked the time and stopped editing the sample at the end of her planned work period. The urge to stay had little to do with Dumpling. She wanted to leave something perfectly clear in a day that had contained a question she couldn't answer.

The sample was clear enough.

She put it in the folder.

That evening, Julian found her sitting beside the sofa rather than peering beneath it. Dumpling lay on top of the cushions, one paw dangling over the edge.

“I've watched him less,” she said.

“Has he noticed?”

“He hasn't sent feedback.”

Julian sat down carefully, leaving the dangling paw undisturbed. Eve described Dr. Clark's examination and the instructions, then told him about the arrow. He listened to both with approximately equal seriousness.

After dinner, Dumpling approached the closed cupboard containing the wand toy. Eve took it out and checked it. They played for a few minutes on the clear floor. He stalked, caught the soft lure, and lay down with his paws around it when he had had enough.

She did not add one more round to see whether he could predict the direction.

When she had stored the whole toy, she joined Julian at the table. He was trying to mend the cardboard ear that had begun to peel apart beside the jar.

“I thought you wanted to recycle that,” she said.

“I still do. You said not yet.”

“That doesn't oblige you to maintain it.”

He considered the narrow strip of paper in his hand, then put it down.

“Excellent.”

They cleared the little pieces away before the cat could inspect them. Eve looked once toward Dumpling, who had occupied her abandoned place on the sofa.

The morning had begun without hints. The day had still contained a doctor who listened, a piece of work she could finish, and someone who knew when to stop repairing a cardboard ear.

She sat beside the cat, not moving him to claim the warm part of the cushion.

He opened one eye and closed it again.

This time she let that be the whole conversation.
