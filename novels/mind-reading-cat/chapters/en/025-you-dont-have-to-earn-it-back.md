# You Don't Have to Earn It Back

The first version of Eve's new budget required her to stop eating lunch.

The second required her to stop paying for electricity.

The third was less imaginative and therefore more useful.

She printed it before she could negotiate with it again.

Dumpling sat beside the printer, offended by its noises but unwilling to surrender the opportunity to watch paper emerge. Eve caught the first sheet before he could put his paw on the warm edge.

“This one isn't yours.”

He looked at her.

“None of them are yours. I realize the distinction has not always been clear.”

The account name at the top of the page made her pause.

Cat Pays Rent.

It had been a joke before it became a small plan. A better joke than Unemployed Woman Learns Video Editing Is Still Work. She had liked its cheerfulness, the implication that something ridiculous and lucky could walk into a room and put the furniture back in order.

Now she had made a column for income she could actually rely on. The account was not in it.

The clinic work had been paid work. A future editing client, if she found one and signed a sensible agreement, might also become paid work. Views were numbers, not a promise that rent would arrive by the due date.

Eve wrote THREE MONTHS across the top of a second sheet.

Not three months until she became successful. Three months to test whether the account could be useful, manageable, and worth making alongside the work that paid her bills.

She divided the period into weeks, then immediately reduced the posting schedule she had written.

Dumpling yawned.

“Yes,” she said. “I also thought that was ambitious.”

---

The folder on the shelf had become a stack.

At the bottom was the first receipt from the afternoon she had brought Dumpling to the clinic. Above it were care notes, a copy of the search notice, dates when information had been checked, and several loose pieces of paper she had apparently believed would organize themselves through proximity.

She spread them across the table.

She did not write ADOPTED across the front. There were still questions about his previous home, and wanting a word did not make the questions disappear.

Instead, she labeled one section CURRENT CARE and another SEARCH AND CONTACTS. Any future adoption paperwork could belong in its own place when it actually existed. The decision was dull, which made it unexpectedly comforting.

She checked the dates against the copies Tessa had given her. Nothing had changed since the last conversation. She had no new clue to report and no reason to telephone the clinic simply because putting the papers in order had made her feel emotional.

Dumpling stepped onto the chair beside her. His old mark was hidden beneath the angle of his fur now. Eve resisted the urge to turn him toward the light and look again.

“You don't have to explain yourself every time I get nervous,” she said.

He settled with his back to the papers.

She put the original receipt in a clear sleeve.

The total still made her stomach tighten. She remembered standing under the clinic lights with rain in her shoes, trying to calculate what kindness would cost before she had even learned what was wrong.

She had made the choice. He had not signed an agreement to repay it.

“You don't have to earn it back,” she told him.

Dumpling lifted one paw to wash his face.

“Your medical bill. Your food. The rent. None of it.”

The cat was unimpressed by the dissolution of his financial obligations. Eve found that rather touching.

She finished the folder and put it upright on the shelf, where she could reach it without moving the kettle.

---

The laundry video took most of the morning.

Mia had sent corrections: one load in the footage had belonged to a different collection day, and the caption made it look as if she took every blanket home herself. Eve changed both. The best shot was still the plainest one—clean towels being counted, with a hand smoothing the corner of a stack.

She sent the revised version back and waited before exporting.

While she waited, she opened the account profile.

She tried three replacements for the name. They sounded like newsletters distributed by a committee. One contained the word “journey.” She deleted it on principle.

The old joke could remain a name for now, she decided, but it did not need to be a command. She changed the profile description instead.

Small stories about pets and the people learning to care for them. Filmed when everyone has the time—and the cat feels like participating.

Then she added a separate contact line for editing and planning inquiries.

No promise of daily miracles. No claim that Dumpling could discover a stranger's character by sniffing a shoe.

She saved it.

Almost immediately, she wanted to explain the change at length. She wrote the beginning of a post about expectations, the pressure of starting over, and the dignity of animals. Halfway through, she imagined someone opening the account to see a cat and being handed her entire private crisis.

She reduced it to two sentences beneath a photograph of Dumpling asleep beside the closed folder.

The cat has been relieved of rent duty. We'll be trying a slower schedule while I work out what useful things we can make here.

Before posting, she checked that no personal information on the folder could be read. Then she let the small announcement be small.

---

In the afternoon, she went to the market with a list that included actual lunches.

The man at the vegetable stall held up two bunches of greens. “These are cheaper together.”

“I'll take one.”

“Two for only a little more.”

“One that I eat is cheaper than two that become a science experiment.”

He considered that and selected the better bunch.

She bought eggs, noodles, and a packet of something she did not have to persuade herself was economical. On the way home, she sat on a low wall to adjust the handle of her bag.

Her phone had accumulated a few notifications.

One person had written that the cat looked underpaid anyway. Another asked whether the clinic videos would continue. Eve replied that there would be more when she had something accurate and useful to share.

A third message was a work inquiry. Could she edit twelve short clips by tomorrow for a very small amount because the footage was “basically already done”?

Yesterday, she might have counted it as evidence that declining the agency had worked out after all. Today, she asked to see the footage and the actual requirements before quoting. The reply could wait until she was home.

She put the phone away and carried the groceries upstairs with both hands.

Dumpling met her at the door, sniffed the bag, and accompanied it to the kitchen with the expectation of a customs officer.

There was nothing for him in the noodles. He supervised anyway.

---

Sophie came by briefly on her way home, having checked that Eve was free. She arrived wearing the expression of someone who had spent the day moving other people's tables and would like her next conversation to remain seated.

Eve made tea.

“It's a three-month trial now,” she said, showing her the page.

Sophie read it without offering encouragement until she had reached the bottom.

“You've put a review date in.”

“So I can't keep calling a bad week temporary forever.”

“You've also put rest on the calendar.”

“In pencil.”

“Coward.”

Eve grinned and handed her a pen.

Sophie did not take it. “You write it.”

Eve made the entry permanent.

They drank their tea while Dumpling attempted to occupy the space occupied by Sophie's bag. Sophie moved the strap out of his reach and left the bag where it was.

“What if it goes nowhere?” Eve asked.

“The account?”

“Yes.”

“Then you'll know more about what you can offer. And you'll have made some things people used.”

“That sounds less impressive than becoming wildly successful.”

“It is. You can still prefer wildly successful.”

Eve looked at the week she had planned. It had ordinary tasks in it: edit, invoice, look for jobs, buy cat food. Nothing about proving that the agency had missed its chance or that her old employer would regret losing her.

Sophie finished the tea and stood. “I have to go before I become part of your furniture.”

“You'd be the most expensive chair.”

“And I wouldn't fold.”

After she left, Eve checked the work inquiry. The footage required more than a quick edit. She sent a quote for a smaller, realistic delivery rather than accepting all twelve clips overnight. Whether the client agreed was now a separate question from whether she had been good enough to ask.

---

At nine that evening, Julian sent a message.

Did you get a proper dinner?

Eve read it standing beside the sink.

No file was attached. No appointment time followed. He was not asking about Dumpling's food or reminding her of a production date.

For an unreasonable moment, she wondered whether she had missed the beginning of a conversation.

She wrote, Yes. Noodles with things in them. Very advanced.

His reply came a minute later.

That does sound like progress.

She leaned against the counter.

How about you?

Dinner. Also with things.

A photograph might have made the exchange more interesting. She considered asking for one and decided against turning a greeting into an assignment.

Instead, she wrote, I didn't take the job. The actual role wasn't the one advertised.

She had not meant to put that into the conversation so quickly. There it was anyway.

I'm sorry it wasn't what you wanted, he replied.

Not congratulations for being brave. Not a prediction that something better was certainly coming. She found she could breathe around the answer.

Thanks. I'm trying a smaller plan for a while.

Smaller can still be a plan.

She smiled at the screen, then set it on the counter before she began improving the exchange into something it wasn't.

Dumpling had found a warm hollow in the blanket on her chair. She sat on the other one and opened her book. The folder was put away. Tomorrow's work was written down. For the first time in several evenings, she did not position the phone so she could film the cat if he happened to be charming.

He stretched one paw out of the blanket and went back to sleep.

Outside, someone wheeled a bin across the courtyard. Dumpling opened one eye at the rattle, checked the room, and closed it again. Eve held her place in the book with a finger until the noise passed.

Nothing had been earned by it.

Nothing needed to be.
