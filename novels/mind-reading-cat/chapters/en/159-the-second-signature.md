# Chapter 159 — The Second Signature

Daniel arrived with pastries and a spreadsheet.

Eve had known him since an old content job where he could turn a missing invoice into a joke without making the person responsible feel foolish. They had not been close lately, but when he heard she was negotiating premises, he offered to look at the figures. Then he mentioned that he might be interested in putting in a small amount of money himself.

She had made coffee before realizing that she was nervous.

They met at her kitchen table. Dumpling examined Daniel's bag, decided the pastry box was the only relevant part of it, and sat beneath his chair.

“I remembered the cinnamon ones,” Daniel said.

“You remembered correctly.”

The familiarity helped. Eve put out plates and opened the version of the budget she had sent him. She had removed personal account details, but the estimated expenses were all there. The modest rental proposal, the contingency, the movable equipment. Even the embarrassing line for replacing a mat that still had not stopped curling.

Daniel took off his jacket.

“First thing,” he said. “I like that you aren't trying to build the whole thing at once.”

Eve felt herself relax another inch.

They went through the figures for nearly forty minutes. He asked useful questions. How many paid sessions would cover a slow month? Which costs were fixed if she cancelled? How much of her client work could she keep without answering emails during an event?

She didn't know every answer. She said so and wrote down the missing ones.

When he proposed a cushion large enough to cover the initial furniture and part of the deposit, she stopped turning her pen.

That amount would change the room immediately.

It would also change the tightness in her chest when a client took an extra week to pay.

“I'd need to understand what you wanted in return,” she said.

“Obviously. We'd get proper advice before signing anything.”

“Good.”

“I'm thinking a small stake, not a loan you have to service from day one.”

Eve wrote down the words without treating them as an agreement. A stake had consequences after the first difficult month ended. Still, she could feel herself moving the ugly storage cabinet out of her imaginary room and replacing it with something whose doors closed.

Daniel leaned back.

“Julian sounds sensible,” he said.

“He is.”

“I'd want him involved in the decisions.”

“He isn't an owner.”

“No, I know. But he's the person with the practical background.”

Eve glanced at the spreadsheet she had spent three evenings building.

“What kind of decisions?”

“The bigger spending. Changes in direction. Staffing, probably.”

“Those would be my decisions, subject to whatever terms we actually agreed.”

“Of course. You'd still handle the content and the look of it. That's your strength.”

For a moment the kitchen seemed to become slightly too warm.

Daniel reached for another pastry. He wasn't smirking. That made it harder to locate the exact place where their conversation had gone wrong.

“My work includes more than how it looks,” Eve said.

“I don't mean that as an insult.”

“I understand. But I'm organizing the operation too.”

“Right. I just think we'd all be more comfortable with another signature.”

“On what?”

“Anything substantial. I'd talk it through with him, and then you could get on with the parts you're good at.”

Dumpling emerged from under the chair and pressed his head against Eve's ankle. She lowered a hand automatically, grateful for something solid to touch.

“If you need an investor approval threshold, we can discuss the threshold,” she said. “If you need professional advice, we can identify the professional. Julian isn't my authorization system.”

Daniel smiled as though she had made a sharp but entertaining joke.

“You know what I mean.”

“I think I do. I'm trying to check.”

She moved the budget between them and pointed to the session staffing line.

“Suppose I want to pay a host for an extra hour because the room took longer to close. Would you need Julian to approve that?”

“Not every little thing.”

“Then what amount?”

“We'd work that out. I just want to know somebody with his head screwed on is keeping an eye on it.”

Eve put the pen down carefully.

The sentence hurt because she had been proud of the meeting until then. She had brought real figures, admitted uncertainty, and resisted decorating the projection with attendance she couldn't justify. Daniel had seen all of that and still wanted somebody else to translate it into competence.

“He's a vet,” she said. “He hasn't run this kind of business. And he has his own work.”

“Which is why he'd be good at seeing the bigger picture.”

She almost asked which profession qualified her to see her own picture. Instead she looked at the sum he had proposed and let herself understand what refusing it would cost.

The deposit would still come from her savings. The cabinet would stay. The nicer lamps could wait. She would need the client edit she had hoped to decline.

“I'm going to stop this discussion here,” she said.

Daniel's expression changed.

“Because I mentioned Julian?”

“Because you want him to supervise decisions I'm responsible for. I don't want that arrangement.”

“We haven't even written anything.”

“That's why this is a good point to stop.”

He looked disappointed, then a little annoyed. “I was trying to help.”

“I know you offered time and money. Thank you for looking at the figures. I don't think we're a match as business partners.”

She heard how formal she sounded. She couldn't find a warmer version that didn't reopen the door.

Daniel closed his laptop.

“Talk to him before you decide.”

“I've decided about this offer.”

There was a long silence. Dumpling jumped onto the empty chair and investigated a flake of pastry. Eve brushed it onto her plate before he could lick it.

Daniel put his laptop away. He did not slam the bag or insult her. The meeting ended with an uncomfortable goodbye and half a box of cinnamon pastries on the table.

After the door closed, Eve sat down again.

She had imagined refusing unsuitable money might feel clean and powerful. Mostly she felt nauseated. She opened the budget and looked at the contingency column until the numbers blurred.

Then she checked the client email she had been avoiding and accepted the additional edit, with a clear fee and delivery time.

It was work she knew how to do. It was also three evenings she had wanted back.

Julian arrived later carrying groceries for the dinner they had already planned. Eve took the bag from him and found a lemon rolling around beside a packet of rice.

“Did you buy one lemon or did it escape from something?”

“One. An intentional lemon.”

She laughed, unexpectedly close to tears.

He noticed. “Bad meeting?”

“Useful beginning. Bad ending.”

She told him while they unpacked. She quoted the specific condition rather than trying to explain Daniel's whole character. Julian put the vegetables down and listened.

“He sent me a message,” he said when she finished.

Eve went still.

“What did it say?”

“He asked if I could help you reconsider. I haven't answered.”

“Can I see it?”

He handed her the phone.

The message was polite. It said Daniel thought Eve had misunderstood, and that a little reassurance from Julian might put things back on track.

She read it twice, then returned the phone.

“I don't want you negotiating it.”

“I don't want to.”

“Or telling him what I really meant.”

Julian leaned against the counter. “You told him what you meant.”

The simplicity of that made her shoulders drop.

He wrote a short reply and offered to show it to her before sending. She read that he would not take an approval role in Eve's venture, that her answer stood, and that he wasn't available to discuss an investment on her behalf.

“Thank you,” she said.

He sent it, put the phone away, and asked where she wanted the cutting board.

For a little while they cooked without talking about money. Eve rinsed the rice. Julian chopped vegetables more slowly than she expected from someone so competent with small tools.

“You're judging my carrots,” he said.

“Only their inconsistent ambition.”

“They'll cook.”

“Some before the others.”

He handed her the knife with exaggerated dignity. She took over, and he washed the board she had used for the onions.

At the table she admitted the offer would have made a genuine difference.

“I know,” he said.

“I wish you could tell me I didn't need it.”

“You may need money. That doesn't make this the right arrangement.”

She pushed a carrot through the rice.

“I don't want to become somebody who rejects every question because it hurts.”

“Did the questions about the budget hurt?”

“Some of them.”

“Did you reject those?”

“No. I wrote them down.”

Julian nodded toward the notebook still on the sideboard. “Then you have useful questions and an offer you don't want. You can keep one.”

She looked over at it. Daniel had identified a cancellation cost she had underestimated. That was still true. She would correct it without inviting him back into the venture.

After dinner Julian washed up while she recalculated the deposit balance. She did not ask him to fill the gap. He did not offer money as proof that he was different from Daniel.

The revised cancellation figure required a second calculation. She had allowed for returning a room fee but forgotten that a booked helper might still be owed the agreed minimum. Eve found the message confirming the terms and changed the line. It made the margin worse. She left the correction in.

On a separate sheet she wrote three questions for the professional who was checking the proposed rental documents. None was about persuading somebody to believe in her. She needed to know what she would owe, when she could leave, and which promised repairs had to be written into the agreement before she relied on them.

Julian glanced at the clock but didn't hurry her. She told him she needed ten more minutes, set a timer, and used all ten. At the sound, she stopped with one question still unanswered.

When she finished, the plan was smaller again.

She moved the cabinet replacement to a later column, cancelled an unconfirmed order for decorative display stands, and left the quiet-corner chair untouched. That chair wasn't the reason the budget was tight.

Julian dried his hands and came to stand beside her.

“Walk?”

“In a minute.”

She saved the file, then closed it before she could start bargaining with the figures again.

Dumpling occupied Julian's abandoned seat, paws neatly together, as though waiting to be consulted on the investment.

“You don't get a signature either,” Eve told him.

Julian laughed. She took his hand on the way to the door.

The money was still missing. The evening wasn't.
