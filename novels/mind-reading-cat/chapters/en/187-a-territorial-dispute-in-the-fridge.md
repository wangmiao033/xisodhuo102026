# Chapter 187 — A Territorial Dispute in the Fridge

The refrigerator made its objection in the form of a soft, repeated knocking.

Eve pushed the door again. Something round rolled back against it.

Her mother, seated at the kitchen table with a train receipt and a pair of reading glasses, looked up. “You have to move the cabbage.”

“I did move the cabbage.”

“You moved it into the way of the milk.”

The cabbage was not a whole cabbage. It was a quarter, wrapped in a bag, and somehow that made its influence more offensive. Eve took it out and put it on the counter beside two containers of soup, a dish of cooked vegetables, and a small tub whose contents had become invisible beneath condensation.

Saturday had been pleasant until everyone arrived bearing refrigeration requirements.

Julian's aunt had come over from his parents' house in the late afternoon, invited for dinner, carrying a cooler which she set down with the satisfied sigh of a person completing a delivery. Eve's mother had opened the fridge to help. Between them, the women had produced enough containers to suggest that Eve had previously lived by licking rainwater from a windowsill.

Now the aunt was washing her hands, Julian was still on his train, and Eve was calculating whether she could eat an entire cabbage standing up.

“You don't have to fit all of it,” her mother said.

Eve looked at her.

“We thought you'd want some soup.”

“I do want some soup. The difficulty is that both of you thought it very thoroughly.”

Her mother laughed. The aunt came back, drying her hands on the towel Eve offered rather than the one hanging beside the oven.

“I can take mine back.”

The speed of the offer made Eve stop pushing things around.

“No one has to retreat in disgrace. I just need to see what's here.”

They cleared the table. Eve found a marker and a roll of tape, then realized she was standing over two grown women as though about to conduct a baggage inspection.

“Tell me which is which,” she said instead. “I'll forget before you leave.”

The aunt's soup contained barley. Her mother's did not. The vegetables were intended for tonight. The little tub contained a sauce, not another meal, which felt like receiving an unexpectedly modest bill.

Dumpling appeared in the doorway and stopped at the sight of three people surrounding the table. His ears moved separately, taking inventory. Eve left the route to the sitting room clear. After a brief investigation of her ankle, he chose the cushion at the far end of the sofa.

“The sensible member of the household,” the aunt said.

“He'll change his mind when I sit down.”

With the door open, Eve discovered a jar of mustard which she had bought because a recipe required one teaspoon. The recipe had been disappointing. The mustard had been living with her ever since.

Her mother picked it up. “Keep?”

“Yes. It's practically paying rent.”

They worked through the shelf without throwing anything away on another person's guess. A container Eve had genuinely forgotten went into the bin after she checked it. The remaining fresh food fitted more comfortably when she stopped trying to preserve the original arrangement as a matter of pride.

There was still too much soup.

“I could take one of your mother's portions home tomorrow,” the aunt suggested. “If she doesn't mind. I've heard about this soup for twenty minutes.”

“You've heard about the missing lid for twenty minutes,” Eve's mother said. “The soup is less interesting.”

Eve found two smaller containers. They divided the batch her mother had brought, leaving enough for Eve and setting the aunt's portion back into the cooler. One portion of the aunt's soup would go home with her too, since she wanted it for lunch. Nothing needed a new owner found through an emergency message to the whole neighborhood.

By the time Julian knocked, the refrigerator closed on the first attempt.

Eve opened the front door with a small, unreasonable sense that she had passed a test set by an appliance.

Julian stood outside holding his bag and a plain white bakery box.

“Please tell me that's a scarf.”

He looked down. “It would be a disappointing scarf.”

She stepped forward to kiss him before either of them could explain. The box tilted. He saved it with one hand and laughed against her mouth.

“Now I have to ask,” he said. “Is there a refrigeration crisis?”

“There's been a negotiated settlement.”

“I've brought two portions of bread pudding.”

“Of course you have.”

He had bought them near the station after messaging that he was hungry. The box contained two dark-topped squares in a shallow tray, with a separate little pot of custard. Eve made a space beside the milk while he went to greet the others.

The sound of his aunt laughing reached her before his answer did. She asked if the course was feeding him. He said yes. She asked whether it was feeding him vegetables. He said that question had acquired an unexpected audience.

Eve put the pudding away and joined them.

Dinner was an uncomplicated assembly of the food already there, supplemented by rice. Julian set the table. His aunt tried to give him the chair nearest the kitchen because he had traveled, then took it herself when he pointed out that she had traveled farther the previous day.

Eve's mother described a colleague who had booked a meeting room on the wrong floor and conducted half a conversation before realizing the people inside belonged to another company. Julian's aunt asked whether that had improved the meeting. Eve nearly dropped the serving spoon.

There was no moment at which the food became a competition. Her mother wanted to know how the aunt kept the barley from absorbing all the broth. The aunt wanted the name of the spice in the sauce. They exchanged practical disappointments about lids that vanished in drawers.

Julian's knee touched Eve's beneath the table. When she looked at him, he lifted his glass slightly, a private greeting belatedly delivered among four plates.

She had missed him most in ordinary places. Beside a draining board. Reaching past her for a clean spoon. Saying something that did not need to survive a faulty connection.

After dinner, his aunt took a call from his mother in the sitting room. Julian went to retrieve his overnight bag from the hall, checking that he had not left his charger on the train. Eve began rinsing the rice pan.

Behind her, the refrigerator opened.

“Is this the old rice thing?” her mother asked.

Eve turned. Her mother held the white box over the counter, the lid lifted. She had mistaken the dark top for something abandoned after reheating.

“No! That's tonight's dessert.”

Her mother froze.

Eve took the box, perhaps a little more dramatically than the situation required. “A survivor of the journey. Please don't finish it off.”

“I wasn't going to throw it without asking.”

“I know.” Eve looked down at the pudding and started laughing. “Sorry. I heard 'old rice thing' and saw my evening disappear.”

Julian came back with his charger held up in triumph.

“What's disappeared?”

“Your signature dessert,” Eve said.

“I bought it.”

“Your carefully selected commercial masterpiece.”

Her mother peered into the box again. “I thought that top was an accident.”

“It's caramelized,” Julian said, with the confidence of someone who had read the label while waiting to pay.

“An expensive accident, then.”

He put the charger down and laughed.

Eve cut the two portions into four without making a ceremony of anyone sacrificing their share. The pudding was rich enough that a smaller piece felt entirely sufficient. They ate it from mismatched plates, and her mother conceded that the top was better than it looked.

It was while returning the custard pot to the kitchen that Eve noticed her mother's hand hesitate above the fridge shelf.

“Where do you want it?”

“Here. And this side is usually the things I'm saving for something.”

“I didn't know you had sides.”

“Neither did I until tonight.”

Eve shifted the milk and showed her the space she usually kept for workday lunches. Her mother listened without that injured expression Eve had briefly expected and felt unfair for expecting.

“At home,” her mother said, “I put anything that needs using at eye level. Otherwise it becomes archaeology.”

“That would explain why you moved my cheese.”

“It was hiding.”

“I was saving it.”

They looked at each other and laughed again, quieter this time.

The apartment was small enough that a visitor's helpful gesture could change an entire routine. Eve had been stepping around bags all day without saying which corner she needed clear. Her mother had been trying to infer a system from three shelves and a cabbage. Neither of them was especially good at inference when tired.

“Tomorrow I'll clear this end of the counter before you go out,” Eve said. “Your breakfast things can stay there.”

“Thank you. And I won't reorganize your cheese.”

“That's all I ask of family.”

Later, the aunt packed the agreed soup portions into her cooler for the short journey back to Julian's parents. Eve added the sauce only after asking whether she wanted it. The aunt said she did, very much, and wrote its ingredients on the back of a receipt because she knew she would forget.

At the door, she invited Eve's mother to compare travel notes the next day. They established a time themselves while Eve stood holding a scarf that belonged to neither of them.

When the aunt had gone, Julian stayed to help wash up. He would sleep at his own flat, where he wanted clean clothes for the final stretch of the course. They had a little time before he left, and Eve was determined not to spend all of it discussing containers.

She dried her hands, took his wrist, and drew him toward the sofa.

Dumpling opened one eye.

“Move over,” Eve told Julian, who had not yet sat down.

“I see the hierarchy is unchanged.”

They fitted beside the cat. From the kitchen came the soft click of her mother making tea.

Julian rested his shoulder against Eve's. No train announcement followed. No screen dimmed between them. She leaned there long enough to feel the difference.

He told her about a vending machine which had kept his coins and dispensed a packet of crackers to the next person instead. She told him about a client who had approved a photograph only after she returned to the version he had rejected first. Neither story was important enough for a scheduled call. Both were exactly what she had wanted to hear and tell.

When her mother brought the mugs, she also carried the quarter cabbage.

“Where was this supposed to go?”

Eve stared at it. They had left it on the counter throughout the entire victory.

Julian took it from her mother and went to the kitchen. A moment later, the refrigerator began knocking again.
