# Chapter 111 — She's Not Available

At half past five, Eve discovered that an evening could fall out of her calendar without making a noise.

The client had moved their call to the following morning. Her files were delivered. There was food in the refrigerator, but most of it required the sort of preparation that began with believing in oneself. She looked from the eggs to the empty chair opposite her desk. Last night she had noticed how often their conversations began with her needing something. She had meant to ask when Sophie was free. Now an actual free evening seemed like a better offering than another vague promise, and she reached for her phone before considering whose evening it was.

Dinner? That place with the enormous dumplings. My treat if you can get there in twenty minutes.

She had already found her other shoe when the reply arrived.

Can't. Training tonight.

Eve sat on the edge of the sofa, shoe suspended from one hand.

Training for what?

Venue programming. Told you about it. Thursday evenings.

Under the message appeared a photograph of a paper cup beside a thick blue workbook. A corner of Sophie's lunch was visible: something wrapped in foil, already being eaten over the pages. Beyond it were the legs of other chairs.

Eve enlarged the picture. On the cover she could make out the words Audience, Access, and Practical Delivery.

She knew Sophie's preferred coffee order. She knew which ankle hurt after a long day standing and which supplier would deliver tablecloths in the wrong color while insisting the colors were identical. She did not know why her friend was spending Thursday evenings with that workbook.

Was this the thing with the community hall?

Different thing. That was a job.

Before Eve could compose an intelligent response, another message arrived.

Starting. Talk later.

She typed Good luck, then deleted it. It sounded as if Sophie were about to take an examination Eve had forgotten. In the end she sent Enjoy it, which was not necessarily any better.

Dumpling appeared in the doorway and looked at her shoe.

"An administrative error," she told him.

He rubbed his face against the bare foot instead.

She put the shoe down and opened her message history.

There it was, six weeks earlier, between a question about a broken tripod clip and a photograph of a cat asleep inside a laundry basket.

Thinking of doing this. Evening course, six sessions. Could be useful.

A link followed. Eve's answer had been a thumbs-up, then Can I call you for five minutes? Something ridiculous has happened with the venue.

She remembered the call. She remembered Sophie asking whether she wanted sympathy or a practical answer. She remembered saying both.

She did not remember asking about the course afterward.

The eggs were still waiting when she returned to the kitchen. She broke one too sharply against the bowl, fished out a piece of shell, and found herself irritated with the egg. This was an unreasonable expansion of its responsibilities.

Her phone buzzed on the counter.

Julian had sent a photograph of a biscuit whose chocolate coating had melted into the paper packet.

This was described as individually wrapped, he wrote. Technically true.

She laughed before she meant to.

I have also been misled by food. Omelet under construction.

He asked whether her call had finished early. She explained that it had moved, and nearly added that Sophie had abandoned her for a mysterious new life. The joke sat in the typing box, too easy and unpleasantly close to what she had felt.

She erased it.

Sophie has a class tonight. I forgot she'd told me about it.

What class?

That's the embarrassing part. I've had to ask.

Julian's reply took several minutes. During them, the omelet acquired a browned bottom and an uncertain center.

I hope she enjoys it, he wrote. I'm heading home. Speak tomorrow?

Tomorrow, she answered.

There was no rescue offer and no substitute dinner. She had not asked for either. Eve folded the omelet, watched it split down the middle, and ate at the table instead of carrying it to her laptop.

Outside, a delivery bicycle's bell rang twice. Dumpling climbed into the opposite chair and settled with his back toward her.

"At least someone accepted."

His ear moved. That was the extent of his contribution.

After washing up, she found the course description Sophie had sent. It covered planning public activities in established venues, working with facilitators, communicating with audiences, and evaluating something beyond attendance totals. A short supported practical assignment concluded the six sessions.

Eve could imagine Sophie being good at all of it. She could also imagine herself saying exactly that, then offering to design promotional materials before learning whether Sophie wanted them.

She closed the link without sending anything.

On her desk lay a market invoice Sophie had annotated with three neat corrections. Eve had kept it because the final amount needed to match her accounts. She could find that document immediately; the course message had required a search. She turned the invoice over, embarrassed by how easily her records preserved what other people did for her.

The embarrassment tempted her toward an extravagant response: flowers, a long apology, perhaps a surprise visit outside the classroom. All three would require Sophie to interrupt the evening again, this time to reassure her that she was a good friend. Eve put her phone beside the sink and took out the rubbish instead. Downstairs, somebody had wedged a pizza box sideways across the bin opening. She wrestled it flat with her elbow, glad to have a small problem that actually belonged to her hands.

At nine fifteen, Sophie's message arrived.

We have homework. I thought adulthood was meant to prevent this.

What is it?

Watching how people use a room without trying to reorganize them immediately. Apparently a challenge for my personality.

Eve smiled. Then she stopped herself from answering with a story about the clinic reception film.

Do you like the course?

Yes. Mostly. There is one man who turns every question into a story about the festival he worked on in 2014.

A respectable vintage.

He has not reached the final day yet.

For a while they exchanged theories about the festival, including the possibility that it was still happening. Then Eve asked whether Sophie had time for a call.

Ten minutes. I'm on the bus and my dinner is leaking.

The background was an uneven mixture of engine noise and somebody else's music. Sophie answered with, "If I sound distracted, I'm trying to save an orange from a sauce incident."

"I didn't remember you telling me. About the class."

"You were busy."

The words were matter-of-fact. Eve found that harder than accusation.

"I went back and looked. You sent the whole description. I immediately asked you to solve something."

"You did have an actual problem."

"That doesn't make your message disappear."

The engine noise deepened. Sophie said something away from the phone, thanked someone, and came back.

"I've got a seat. And a rescued orange."

"Why this course?"

"Because I'm tired of arriving when everything has already been decided badly. Not always, before you start reviewing every job we've done. But a lot of the time. Someone sells an idea, somebody else makes promises, and I meet it in the form of a truck arriving at the wrong door."

Eve leaned against the kitchen counter.

"You want to be involved earlier."

"Sometimes. I want to understand that work properly before I say I want it. Programming isn't just choosing interesting activities. There's the venue's audience, and what a facilitator actually needs, and whether the thing belongs in that room at all."

"That sounds like you."

"Which part? The room?"

"The part where you notice a plan isn't attached to any actual people."

Sophie made a small sound Eve could not quite identify over the bus. A laugh, perhaps, or the effort of retrieving dinner.

"Well. I'm learning how much I haven't noticed. We had to review an activity description tonight. I thought mine was clear. Then another person asked if they'd be expected to speak in front of the whole group, and I couldn't tell them from what I'd written."

"Was it yours for the practical assignment?"

"An exercise. The practical is a group project at the learning center. I'm coordinating one part, with the venue lead supervising. Not running a festival, despite what one member of the class might prefer."

Eve wanted to ask for the description. She could look it over; she was good at this kind of thing. Her mouth had already shaped the first word when she remembered the paper cup and half-eaten meal.

"What's your part?"

"A small bookbinding session and display. People make a simple folded notebook, then there's a table of local makers' work. The facilitator does the teaching. I handle the session arrangements and visitor information."

"Are you nervous?"

"Yes. In a useful way, currently. I'll update you when it becomes the other kind."

A stop announcement broke across the line. Sophie said she had two stops left.

Eve looked at the clock. Their ten minutes were nearly gone.

"Would you like dinner on a night you choose? Not as emergency entertainment because someone else canceled on me."

"Was that what tonight was?"

Eve winced.

"My client moved a call. I realized I was free and assumed you'd be standing in storage until required."

"I've had a shelving upgrade."

"Very pleased for you."

Sophie laughed properly this time. "Tuesday? Seven? I want somewhere I can sit down without hearing a loading-bay alarm."

They chose a small restaurant near the station, not because it was useful to either of their projects, but because Sophie wanted the baked rice there. Eve put it in her calendar while they were still talking.

"And don't bring the workbook unless you want to," she said.

"I wasn't going to let you copy my homework."

The call ended with Sophie trying to stand, gather her bag, and keep the orange from rolling down the aisle.

Eve remained at the counter for a moment. She had learned more about her friend's last six weeks in ten minutes than she had from dozens of messages. None of the information was secret. Sophie had offered it in the first place.

She took the plate from the drying rack and put it away. Then, because she had promised a client a short reply before morning, she finished that reply without turning it into another hour of browsing.

On Tuesday afternoon, the restaurant sent a booking reminder. Eve forwarded it to Sophie, who responded with a photograph of an orange sitting on the blue workbook.

Still alive.

The orange or the student?

One of us is doing better than the other.

Eve started to type Tell me everything. It sounded generous, and it was far too large a promise for a message sent between two exports. Instead she wrote, Tell me about the exercise when we eat. I want to hear how it went.

Then she moved the dinner reminder above a client note, where it would not vanish beneath the next convenient emergency.

Her phone rang immediately afterward. A customer wanted to know if she could have a quick look at something before the evening.

Eve glanced at the clock. There was still time, she thought, and answered.
