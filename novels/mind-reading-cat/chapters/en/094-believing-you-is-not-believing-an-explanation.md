# Chapter 094 — Believing You Is Not Believing an Explanation

The second cup of tea was too hot to drink.

Eve found this unreasonable. The first had become cold while she tried to explain a secret, and now the replacement required another interval in which neither of them knew what to do with their hands.

Julian set his on the table.

“I believe you're telling me what you think happened,” he said.

Relief moved through her so quickly that she almost missed the careful shape of the sentence.

He had not said the cat could read minds.

“Thank you.”

“I don't know what explains it.”

“Neither do I. Not properly.”

“And I'm not agreeing that there's only one explanation.”

“I understand.”

She did understand. She also felt the small, unreasonable disappointment of having offered something frightening and not received a matching certainty in return. It took effort not to turn the disappointment into a fresh argument for her preferred answer.

Julian watched her for a moment.

“Does that feel like I haven't believed you?”

“A little,” she admitted. “Even though I can hear the difference.”

“I don't think you're sitting here inventing a performance for me. That's different from knowing what the cat perceives.”

Eve nodded. She could work with the distinction if she stopped asking it to do something else.

Dumpling emerged from beneath the sideboard. He crossed the room, sniffed the corner of Julian's coat, and stepped onto the empty chair Eve had left nearer the window. Nothing about the route explained his choice. Nothing required an explanation from them.

Julian looked at him, then returned his attention to Eve.

“Have you been waiting for him to tell you whether I like you?”

Her throat tightened.

“Sometimes I wanted a sign before I was willing to ask you anything. But you told me yourself. I know that.”

“When I ask to see you, I mean the invitation. When I say I like you, I mean that too. I don't want every sentence to have a second version that matters more because you think he supplied it.”

“No.”

“I also don't want to have to keep proving that the second version is wrong.”

That was the part Eve had not fully imagined. She had pictured herself carrying the burden of a strange uncertainty. She hadn't pictured handing him the labor of disproving interpretations he hadn't helped create.

“I won't ask you to answer for a thought I've assigned you,” she said. “If I'm unsure about something between us, I'll ask about it plainly. You can answer or say you're not ready. I have to use what you actually say.”

“And if you think you notice something anyway?”

“I may not be able to prevent a thought occurring. I can stop acting as if it gives me a right to question you until you confirm it.”

Julian picked up his tea. This time he could drink it.

Eve let the silence remain while he swallowed. She had already interrupted him once tonight by announcing she understood before he finished. There was no need to improve her record by repeating it more gently.

“I'm trying to work out which parts of our conversations you were actually having with me,” he said.

The words hurt because they were specific.

She looked at the blue album on the shelf. Their photograph could not answer that question. Neither could the pleasant meal yesterday, the walks, or the care with which he had put bread away. Real moments did not cancel an incomplete explanation.

“The things I said about liking you were mine,” she said. “Choosing to keep working independently was mine. Asking for dates was mine. But there were moments I watched him because I was afraid to ask you, and I didn't tell you why. I can't make those moments honest retroactively.”

“I'm not asking you to say everything was false.”

“I know. I think I'm trying to prevent you from having to wonder.”

“I already wonder. You can't remove that by finding a better summary.”

Eve looked down at her fingers. A faint pen mark remained beside her thumbnail from the page she had prepared. She had considered that preparation a form of care. Perhaps it was, partly. It had also allowed her to imagine enough good wording might control the effect of the truth.

“I can answer what you ask,” she said. “And accept that it may take longer than tonight.”

He nodded.

The radiator made a short clicking noise. Dumpling lifted his head, decided against further involvement, and tucked his nose along his foreleg.

Julian glanced toward the notebook.

“Do those pages contain things about other people that they wouldn't expect you to keep?”

“There are my interpretations. I stopped writing them as facts. Some earlier entries are still careless.”

She reached toward the notebook, then stopped before pushing it across the table.

“I shouldn't hand you other people's private circumstances to prove how careful I am now.”

“No.”

“I can review what I've kept without turning it into evidence about them.”

“That's for you to do. I don't need access to it.”

She withdrew her hand. The notebook stayed closed between them, no longer a proposed exhibit.

Eve had once imagined telling him would mean finally having somebody qualified to decide what was happening. Now that seemed another way of handing him work he had never agreed to perform. Being a veterinarian did not make him responsible for resolving every frightening theory she formed about an animal.

“I don't expect you to examine him to explain this,” she said. “If his health changes, I'll describe ordinary symptoms and ask for proper care. I won't treat this as a diagnosis.”

“Good. And don't manufacture a situation to make him do something.”

“I won't.”

“No frightened people, no pressure on him, no patients.”

“Agreed.”

She felt the speed of the answer and made herself add something more precise.

“I nearly did arrange a situation yesterday. That's why I need the rule to include things that look harmless from outside. Not only things I can persuade myself are obviously bad.”

Julian set down his cup.

“I appreciate you saying that.”

The sentence did not absolve her. It made the discussion possible to continue without either of them pretending it had achieved a cure.

“What about the card project?” Julian asked.

Eve looked toward the cupboard where she'd put the tray and blank reminder. “Tessa hasn't approved the quote yet.”

“That isn't quite what I mean.”

She waited this time.

“If we're working together, I need to know you won't use my hesitation as material for this. I may be unsure about a layout because I haven't checked a detail, not because there's something you're meant to uncover.”

“The card can be reviewed in writing,” Eve said. “Ordinary comments, ordinary corrections. You don't have to come here to do it. He doesn't need to be involved at all.”

“I don't want us to invent a system where every work conversation becomes proof that you haven't watched him.”

Eve had been about to suggest exactly that: separate rooms, specified times, a visible precaution for every imaginable moment. It would make his work more cumbersome while allowing her to feel impressively accountable.

“No,” she said. “You're right. I need to do my job by the information people give me. Not make you supervise whether I'm doing it.”

She picked up the folded sheet and turned it over. On the blank side she wrote a short reminder to herself: ask what needs checking. She didn't show it to him for approval.

The project might go ahead or might not. If it did, the final design still needed to help a visitor prepare, not demonstrate that Eve had become trustworthy in one evening. She would send Tessa the quote she had actually requested, with no additional promise to make up for a personal conversation Tessa knew nothing about.

They moved the tray to the kitchen because the cups were empty and neither wanted more tea. Eve carried the notebook separately, placing it on the desk. She didn't hide it beneath anything. There was no need to turn putting a book away into a dramatic promise of destruction either.

At the sink, she ran water into one cup and watched a ring of tea loosen along the side. Julian stood near the doorway, leaving her room to move.

“Do you want the plate from yesterday?” she asked, then winced. “The bread plate. I borrowed it when you brought the loaf.”

“It isn't mine.”

Eve looked at the plain white plate on the draining rack.

“No. You're right.”

“It was already here.”

For the first time that evening, she laughed. A small, tired sound, but real.

“Excellent evidence of my powers of observation.”

Julian's mouth moved into a brief smile.

“Keep the plate.”

She dried it and put it in her own cupboard. The ordinary mistake let her breathe without making the more difficult one disappear.

When they returned to the sitting room, Dumpling had spread across the chair. Julian gathered his coat from its hook rather than trying to recover space beside the cat. Eve watched him check his pockets, the familiar small sequence preceding departure.

“I don't need an answer about the explanation tonight,” she said.

“I don't have one.”

“I meant I won't keep adding examples until you do.”

He looked at her then, and she could see that the clarification mattered more than the first sentence.

“Thank you.”

At the door she wanted to reach for his hand. She also wanted to let him choose whether closeness was comfortable after this conversation. The uncertainty tempted her into a new performance of carefulness, as though she could stand so respectfully that he would be obliged to reassure her.

She asked instead.

“Would you like a hug before you go, or would you rather not tonight?”

He paused.

“A brief one.”

She stepped close when he opened his arms. The embrace was neither a promise that everything was settled nor a punishment reduced to its minimum. It was the contact they had both agreed to. Eve held him, then let go when he moved back.

“I still need to think,” he said.

“All right.”

There were practical questions about when to talk again. For a moment both of them stood with those questions waiting, Julian holding his coat and Eve trying not to substitute an invitation for an answer.

She would have liked to say tomorrow was completely free. It wasn't. She had a paid edit due and a call she had already arranged. Making herself perpetually available would not make the disclosure easier for him to absorb, and it would undo the work choices she had only recently made.

“I have work tomorrow morning,” she said. “I'm free later, if you want to talk. If you need longer, say so.”

He nodded, considering rather than immediately choosing.

Eve stayed beside the door without reaching for the notebook again. She could hear Dumpling move on the chair behind them, claws briefly catching the fabric before coming loose. She did not turn around to see which person he faced.

Julian was here, within ordinary speaking distance. Whatever happened next needed to come from him.
