# Chapter 057 — A Night Worker's Morning

Ben's breakfast came in a paper bag darkened by butter.

He stood beside the low wall at seven twenty-eight, eating something folded around an egg. Eve arrived with a notebook and the uncomfortable awareness that her own day was beginning while his had already demanded most of him.

“Do you want to finish first?” she asked.

“If I stop eating every time I speak, I'll be here until lunch.”

“Fair.”

He indicated the space beside him. She sat, leaving the notebook closed until he said he didn't mind notes. No camera hung from her shoulder. He looked at the absence before he looked at her face.

“They said you make animal videos.”

“I do other communication work too. This isn't going to be a video about you.”

“Good. I don't look especially sympathetic in the mornings.”

“You don't have to.”

He took another bite. A bus sighed to a stop on the main road, beyond the row of bare shrubs. Ben watched it until it moved again.

“I get home around seven,” he said. “Sometimes later. I need to sleep before the afternoon.”

“What can you hear from your room?”

“The side door closing. People talking by the bins. The dog. The dog answering the side door.”

“Every time?”

“No. If I say every time, somebody will find a time it didn't happen and tell me the whole thing's imaginary.”

Eve opened the notebook.

“Then let's not write every time.”

He gave her a quick look, as though testing whether she meant it. He described short bursts just after he lay down, a longer spell on Tuesday, and two quiet mornings he remembered because he had slept through his phone alarm afterward.

“Have you kept times?”

“Not properly. I sent messages when I was angry.”

“Would you be willing to note what you hear for a few days? Approximate start and end. No recording anyone through their door.”

“I don't want another job.”

“I know. It could be a few lines. If it becomes a burden, tell the committee rather than filling in guesses.”

He folded the empty bag between his hands. “I'm not asking her to get rid of him.”

“Would you be willing to say that directly?”

“I thought it was obvious.”

Eve waited.

Ben looked toward the building. “Perhaps not from that notice.”

A woman came through the side entrance carrying rubbish. The door closed behind her with a sharp metal clap. From somewhere above them came three barks, a pause, then two more.

Ben lifted one hand without speaking.

Eve noted the time. She could hear the sound here, but that didn't measure how loud it was beside his pillow. She said so before he could mistake her writing for an official finding.

“I know,” he said. “You'd have to come inside. I don't particularly want that.”

“You don't have to let me.”

He rubbed the heel of his hand over one eye. They agreed to finish after twenty minutes. His sleep was not an illustrative detail she could keep collecting because the conversation had become useful.

Before leaving, she asked where he had put the first note.

“Under the little wooden wedge beside her door. It was folded.”

“Did it have a flat number?”

“My name. I assumed she knew where I lived.”

“Did she?”

His expression changed by a fraction.

“I don't know.”

At eleven, Nora opened her door only as far as a secured barrier allowed. A small brown dog stood several feet behind it, barking with his whole chest. His ears flicked between Eve and the hall.

“This is Pip,” Nora said. “Give us a moment.”

Eve stayed where she was. Nora moved the dog farther from the entrance, then invited her into the kitchen after checking the barrier. By the time Eve sat down, Pip was in the adjoining room with his bed and a familiar toy. He could see Nora without having to approach the visitor.

“He doesn't do that all day,” Nora said.

“I've only been here two minutes.”

“People decide quickly.”

The kitchen table held a laptop, a calculator, a bowl containing two pegs, and several lengths of fabric. Nora cleared a chair, apologizing for the cloth.

“You don't have to tidy the work away,” Eve said. “What do you do?”

“Alterations. Some shop work, some from home. I've started taking more early collections.”

“Through this door?”

“Yes. People bring things before their own work.”

From the other room came a single bark. Nora turned her head, waited, and continued when Pip settled again.

She had started early collections three weeks before. Previously she worked two fixed days at a shop and saw home customers in the afternoon. A change in the shop's staffing had altered the arrangement. She needed the work, and customers liked leaving a hem to be shortened before catching the train.

“People think I'm sitting here all day watching him bark,” she said.

Eve glanced at the calculator with a sleeve draped over it.

“Are you able to hear him while you're working?”

“Usually. Not when I'm on a call with the shop. Or when I go downstairs to collect a delivery.”

“Have you noticed a change?”

Nora smoothed the cloth on the table.

“I thought he was cross with the door. The closer's louder. They adjusted it last month.”

“The committee can ask the caretaker to check that. I can't say whether it's working correctly.”

“You heard it?”

“This morning.”

Nora's shoulders dropped a little. Eve recognized the invitation to join her in blaming a piece of metal. It would be satisfying to locate the entire quarrel in something that couldn't be embarrassed.

“The door may be part of what people hear,” she added. “It doesn't tell us everything about Pip.”

“No.” Nora picked up a peg, then put it down. “He's been following me more. I thought he wanted whatever I'd put in my pocket.”

Eve asked whether Nora wanted general information about finding appropriate professional help. She didn't ask to examine the dog or offer a reason for the change. Nora had a regular vet and could contact that practice herself.

“Will they tell me to buy a hundred things?”

“I can't answer for them. You can tell them what you're seeing and what you can manage. Asking for an appointment isn't agreeing to every possible expense.”

Nora nodded but didn't reach for her phone.

Eve left the choice there.

Then Nora showed her the note she had received. It had been pushed beneath a different paper, lacked a name, and said only that everyone was sick of the noise.

“That isn't the note Ben described,” Eve said.

“Ben?”

“The resident I spoke to this morning. May I tell you the practical point he wanted you to know?”

Nora agreed.

“He works nights. He's trying to sleep when the early collections happen. He said he isn't asking you to get rid of Pip.”

Nora looked toward the adjoining room.

“I thought he wanted me out.”

“Did he say that?”

“No. Somebody wrote that people should move if they can't behave.”

“Was that signed?”

Nora shook her head.

Eve drew two separate boxes on her page. One for the conversations people had actually had, one for unsigned notes. She didn't write a culprit in either.

“Would you sit with him for half an hour?” she asked. “With the coordinator present. To talk about the next week, not decide who's a good person.”

“Not with everyone watching.”

“A small room. No filming. You can stop if it isn't useful.”

Nora took a loose thread from her sleeve and wound it around a finger. “I'll think about it.”

Before leaving, Eve waited while Nora checked the hallway and secured Pip away from the open front door. The dog didn't offer a photogenic farewell. He stood beside his toy, watching the visitor go. Eve thanked Nora for her time, stepped over neither barrier nor animal, and discovered she had spent an hour in a home without once needing to call it a content opportunity.

That afternoon, Eve walked to the clinic to collect a general information card Tessa had agreed to leave for her. It described contacting a practice about changes in behavior, without promising a diagnosis from a symptom list. Eve checked which wording the clinic allowed her to quote.

Julian appeared from the staff corridor as Tessa was helping another visitor. He waited until Eve had moved away from the desk.

“Did you get what you needed?”

“The card, yes. I have a question about what I shouldn't say.”

“A large category.”

“Briefly.”

He glanced at the clock. “I have five minutes before my next appointment.”

She explained that the owner had reported a recent change but had not given permission for personal details to be shared with him. Her question was about a public sentence: could she write that barking often reflected anxiety?

“I wouldn't narrow it that way,” he said. “There can be different causes. A general notice should encourage someone to contact their own vet about a change, not tell the neighbors what the dog has.”

“Even if the sentence sounds helpful.”

“Especially if it sounds like an answer.”

She crossed it out in her notebook. “Would you check one short paragraph later, through Tessa, if the clinic can fit that in?”

“Send the scope. Don't put my name beneath it yet.”

“I wasn't going to.”

“I know. I'm practicing saying the whole sentence.”

She smiled. He looked tired, but he wasn't leaning against the counter as if it were the only thing holding him upright. When Tessa called him, he left with a small wave rather than stretching five minutes into a favor he couldn't afford.

Outside, Eve received Nora's message.

She would attend on Friday at eleven. Her sister could stay with Pip in the flat, so she wouldn't bring him into an unfamiliar room. She had also called her own practice and arranged to discuss the change.

Eve thanked her. She did not forward that medical detail to Ben.

Ben agreed to the meeting after checking his shift. Friday was his day off. He asked whether he could bring the times he had already written down.

*Yes,* Eve replied. *And please bring one thing you'd like to change in the coming week.*

At home, she found Dumpling asleep with his nose pressed into the seam of a cushion. She sat at the far end of the sofa, letting the notebook rest closed on her lap.

The morning's two accounts had not cancelled each other out. Nora needed paid work. Ben needed sleep. Pip still needed whatever appropriate assessment and care his owner arranged. Eve could not settle those needs by choosing the most attractive face for a thumbnail.

Her phone sounded again. Ben had sent a photograph of his handwritten times, then immediately apologized for the handwriting.

Eve enlarged it. The letters were uneven but legible.

*I can read it,* she wrote.

For this particular piece of work, that seemed a useful first result.
