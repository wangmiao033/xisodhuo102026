# Chapter 147 — The Chair Someone Has to Fill

Sophie arrived at the cafe with a roll of paper under one arm and a sandwich in a bag.

“This is my lunch,” she said before Eve could greet her. “The paper is somebody else's seating plan. Neither is available for your project.”

“I was only looking at the sandwich.”

“You looked professionally.”

Eve moved her notebook to make space. They had arranged forty minutes to discuss the unit, followed by a separate paid visit if Eve still thought an operational assessment would help. Sophie had squeezed the conversation between work at two locations. Her newer programming role had finally come back with the disputed closing shift shared, and she was trying the revised rota before committing to more outside work.

“Does it feel better?” Eve asked.

“The hours do. Ask me about the chairs after Thursday.”

“What happens Thursday?”

“Two hundred people discover that chairs are individually movable.”

Eve wished her luck and passed over the page of costs.

Sophie read it while eating. Near the bottom, she tapped a line with her clean finger.

“Who's this?”

“Session support.”

“I mean who's doing it.”

“I thought I could start with someone for busier sessions.”

“Then this is a wish with an hourly rate.”

Eve had put in a plausible figure. It looked oddly naked under Sophie's finger.

“What do I need instead?”

“An actual quote. Duties. Minimum call. What happens when you cancel. Whether the person can work those dates. And enough time for them to arrive before the visitors.”

Sophie finished a bite and looked again.

“Where are you during this?”

“Leading the session.”

“And when the first person needs the toilet while another person arrives early with a dog?”

“At least one of me is at the door.”

“Expensive, cloning.”

They began with a single six-person event. Eve described the welcome, the materials, the discussion, the break, and the clearing. Sophie asked what could actually wait. In the borrowed room, Eve knew the route and could direct late arrivals from the table. At the unit, a closed front door, a visiting animal, and unfamiliar people would change the work before anyone paid for a seat.

Eve drew another chair beside the entrance.

“Somebody has to fill that,” Sophie said. “Even on the evening when you only sell four places.”

Eve added a minimum staffing cost rather than a cheerful cost per participant.

The difference ate most of the imagined surplus.

For a moment she wanted to explain that Julian might sometimes be there. She had not written his name. She could still feel the blank place shaped like him, ready to absorb whatever her figures could not manage.

Instead she asked Sophie whether she knew anyone whose normal work included small events.

“I can send two contacts who take independent bookings. Ask them yourself. Don't reserve either through me.”

“Would you want any of the work?”

“Occasional planning, maybe. Not regular hosting. I don't know my new rota well enough, and I want to keep some evenings.”

Eve wrote occasional planning beside Sophie's name and left the host line empty.

They had six minutes left. Sophie asked what the landlord had said about overheads. Eve showed her the notes, and Sophie circled two uncertain charges.

“Get those confirmed before you turn this into a monthly figure.”

Then she packed the seating plan and left for the work she had already agreed to do.

Eve stayed to finish her tea. The page looked less encouraging and much more useful.

Over the next few days, she made calls instead of drawing interiors. One event host quoted a minimum booking longer than the session itself. The other was unavailable on the weekday she most wanted but gave a clear rate for a different afternoon. A cleaner asked about floor area, toilets, animals, and how often the room would be used before quoting anything at all.

Eve measured the unit during a second agreed visit. The landlord opened up, watched her wrestle a measuring tape that wanted to return to its case, and supplied copies of the current charge information.

“Will your partner be coming to look?” she asked.

“Julian may look at some animal-related questions for me. He's not taking the premises with me.”

“Oh. I meant whoever would be signing.”

“That would be me.”

The sentence came out without the brave emphasis she might once have given it. The landlord simply corrected the contact note.

At the rear, Eve checked the sink, the door, and the place where a cupboard might stand. She did not decide whether any of them met requirements by staring harder. She put the questions on the list for someone qualified to answer them.

The next appointment was a paid consultation with a small-business adviser. Eve brought the current trial results, not her follower count. The adviser asked what people had bought, how often they returned, and how much paid client work she would lose by opening more hours.

“I could edit there between sessions,” Eve said.

“While visitors can come in?”

“Perhaps.”

“Have you tried producing client work while people can interrupt you?”

Eve thought of a sentence she had once rewritten six times between answering the same question about a booking link.

“Yes.”

“How did it go?”

“I should remove perhaps.”

They made three versions of a month. In one, the sessions filled as often as the recent trials. In another, several places remained empty. In the third, Eve cancelled a session and a client paid late.

The third version was not a catastrophe. It was a recognizable month wearing an inconvenient hat.

The adviser separated money already received from expected payments and asked how much had to remain available for Eve's existing work and ordinary life. Together they marked what could genuinely be risked on an experiment. The space could not consume money set aside for taxes, outstanding project expenses, or refunds merely because those balances happened to be visible in the account.

Eve also booked a short review with someone who handled commercial occupancy agreements. The appointment would happen when she had actual proposed terms. For now she learned which documents to request and which questions she could not settle with a landlord's cheerful assurance.

She asked the center coordinator about storage as well. A small cupboard might become available, but not until another group decided what to keep. Eve recorded the possibility without making it the foundation of her alternative. Even a borrowed room could acquire imaginary advantages if she wanted the comparison to come out neatly.

There were insurance questions too. Eve described the activities accurately, including the possibility of visitors bringing their animals. The answer was not a price for an imagined cafe or a clinic. It was a request for enough detail to assess her actual service.

By Friday, she had a folder of answers and several properly named unknowns.

Sophie met her at the unit for the paid assessment. The landlord let them in for an hour and stayed nearby while they worked. Eve brought an empty carrier, her usual materials box, and six sheets of paper to stand for participants.

“We look very successful,” Sophie said, surveying the paper audience.

“They all paid promptly.”

They walked the tasks rather than admiring the sunlight. Sophie arrived holding the carrier. Eve tried to greet her while moving the materials box away from the route. A sheet representing another visitor slid under Eve's shoe.

“You've stepped on a customer,” Sophie said.

“A small retention issue.”

They tried again, this time leaving the box where it would actually be stored. The room offered possibilities the borrowed venue did not, but it also needed equipment, cleaning, and someone responsible for opening and closing. Eve timed those jobs. Sophie made her repeat the closing sequence after remembering a bag under a chair.

The extra time was not huge. Across a month, it became someone else's evening or Eve's own paid working hours.

Then they sat on two folding chairs the landlord allowed them to use and considered the quiet periods.

“What happens on Tuesday morning?” Sophie asked.

“I work on commissions.”

“Door closed?”

“Yes.”

“And Wednesday?”

“Probably the same.”

Sophie looked around the empty room. “Are you paying for somewhere to leave a box, or does the room add enough useful work?”

“That is an extremely rude question.”

“I included it in the fee.”

Eve laughed, then sat with it. Storage mattered. Predictable time mattered. Neither automatically paid a commercial rent.

On the way out, she asked the landlord whether occasional arranged use might be possible before a longer agreement. The landlord said she would consider a written proposal with specified dates, uses, and responsibility for costs. There was no promise to hold the unit indefinitely.

Eve thanked her and did not ask for a favor disguised as certainty.

That evening she met Julian for noodles near his flat. He had submitted the training application and had no result yet. Eve asked whether the repetitive boxes had survived his editing.

“Two of them. The third received an answer that fit.”

“A radical departure.”

He wanted to hear about the assessment. She described the customer she had stepped on, the cost of opening the door, and the distinction between a room that would be useful and a room that would support itself.

“Would you be disappointed if I don't take it?” she asked.

“I would be disappointed for you if you wanted it and couldn't make it work. I don't need you to rent it.”

She stirred the noodles. That answer left her with an uncomfortable amount of freedom.

“I think part of me wanted us to be the reassuring picture in the window,” she said. “Two capable people, recognizable cat, everything already joined up.”

“Dumpling would sit with his back to the glass.”

“He has never understood brand consistency.”

Julian smiled, then waited.

Eve set down her chopsticks.

“I want to find out if the work deserves the room when you're at the clinic, Sophie is at another job, and the cat is asleep at home.”

“That sounds like the room you'll have most often.”

She nodded. It was also the room she had not properly pictured until this week.

After dinner, they walked to the corner where their routes separated. Julian invited her up for tea; Eve wanted to go home and finish the revised figures before the details blurred. She said so, and he kissed her beside the crossing without looking wounded by an evening that ended there.

At home, Dumpling had commandeered the sweater she had left on a chair. Eve sat at the desk instead of reclaiming it.

She entered Sophie's invoice, the consultation fee, and the real support quotes. The bold monthly total rose. Beneath it, the amount she could safely commit remained stubbornly the same.

Then she made a second page.

It was headed: If I keep borrowing rooms.

There was enough space beneath the heading to do the work properly. She opened the center's calendar and began.
