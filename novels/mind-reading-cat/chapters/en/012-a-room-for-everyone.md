# Chapter 012 — A Room for Everyone

The first obstacle to keeping a cat in Mia's flat was a bicycle named Gerald.

“That's not its name,” Rachel said. “She calls it that because she wants me to feel guilty about leaving it in the hall.”

“It has its own room,” Mia said.

“It has half a corridor.”

Eve stood on the landing with Nora, the volunteer coordinator, trying to decide whether smiling would establish friendly intentions or trivialize a serious housing dispute. Nora solved the problem by asking where they could put their wet shoes.

They had arranged the visit two evenings earlier. Everyone in the flat had agreed to it; it was a conversation, not a surprise inspection. Eve had been invited because Mia wanted someone there who already knew her, and Nora had said an extra pair of ears might be useful. Eve had brought no camera.

This fact seemed to disappoint Mia, who had cleaned her bedroom with the energy of a person expecting it to appear on television.

The third roommate, Leah, sat cross-legged on a kitchen chair eating toast from a saucepan lid. She moved a pair of headphones when they entered and offered the only mug without a chipped handle to Nora.

“Tea?”

“Lovely, thank you.”

“Milk may have to be negotiated.”

Rachel opened the fridge. Three cartons stood in a row, each bearing a different colored dot. Eve recognized the system from office kitchens and instantly trusted its capacity to cause war.

Mia led her toward the bedroom before tea could become another subject on which she had failed to consult anyone.

“See? There's space.”

There was a new covered cat bed beside the wardrobe, a neatly folded yellow blanket, and a cardboard scratcher on the rug. Mia had removed the price tags. A notebook lay open on her desk with SOCKS written at the top and little squares for ticks below it.

Eve could see why she wanted someone to admire the preparation. The room looked like a promise made physical.

“The bed is lovely.”

“It fits under the chair if he doesn't like this corner.”

“And where will your chair go?”

“Here.” Mia pulled it out. Its wheels caught on the scratcher. She lifted the scratcher and set it on the bed, then looked furious with both objects.

Eve sat on the edge of Mia's own bed. “My first setup had a food bowl where the bathroom door needed to open.”

“What did you do?”

“Walked into it twice. Then accepted that architecture wasn't going to negotiate.”

Mia smiled briefly. From the kitchen came a burst of laughter. She glanced toward it as if the conversation there had already become a verdict.

“They think I'm being childish.”

“Let's hear what they think.”

“I know what you keep saying.”

Eve took the rebuke. “All right.”

In the kitchen, Nora had drawn a rough rectangle on an ordinary sheet of paper. Not a printed assessment form, Eve noticed. Rachel was explaining the night-shift arrangement while Leah held the missing bicycle pump aloft like evidence.

“It was behind the bin,” she told Mia.

“I didn't put it there.”

“I'm not accusing you. I'm saying I've found it.”

Nora waited until they sat down.

“The question isn't whether this is a large flat,” she said. “It's what you can agree to do in the flat you actually have.”

Mia opened her notebook. She had written a feeding routine, listed local supply shops, and made a savings calculation. It was more work than Eve had done before Dumpling arrived without an appointment and occupied her life.

Rachel looked through it carefully. “This is good.”

Mia's expression brightened.

“But I'm still not available as the daytime person after a night shift.”

“I didn't say you were.”

“You said you'd need someone here when the plumber comes because Socks might be frightened.”

“That was one example.”

“It still involves me.”

Eve watched the brightness leave Mia's face. She recognized a dangerous habit of her own: treating a small request as if its size meant another person had already agreed.

Nora asked Mia about her working hours. Two days were predictable. The others changed with the rota, sometimes with little notice. Mia took an evening class once a week. Leah was often away on weekends. Rachel could help occasionally but would not commit to a daily job.

No one shouted. That made it harder, somehow. There was no dramatic person to remove from the room so that everyone else could be reasonable.

They moved on to the living room. Mia wanted to keep the door open so Socks could explore when she wasn't home. Leah wanted the door shut while she practiced recorded presentations because outside noise ruined them. Rachel needed the same room dark and quiet after certain shifts.

“We can't plan every minute,” Mia said.

“No,” Nora agreed. “But we can notice when two plans use the same room at the same time.”

Eve drew a second rectangle next to the first. Three people looked at her.

“I'm not redesigning your flat,” she said quickly. “I was trying to put the times next to the rooms.”

“Like a venue schedule?” Leah asked.

“Unfortunately, that's how my brain works.”

Leah fetched a ruler. The two of them drew columns while Rachel retrieved her rota. Mia remained on the edge of her chair, watching her imagined home become squares of occupied space.

For a few minutes it helped. The bicycle could be moved out of the narrow entrance if another hook were approved by the landlord. Mia could shift a shelf. Leah was willing to discuss alternatives for some of her recording sessions.

Then they reached the evenings when nobody could reliably be home at the time Mia had written down, and the empty squares stopped being satisfying.

“I could stop the class,” Mia said.

“Do you want to?” Eve asked.

“Not everything is about what I want.”

“No. But giving something up while you're upset doesn't tell us whether you'll be happy with the arrangement later.”

Mia shut the notebook.

Eve wished she had phrased it differently. She sounded like the kind of person who wrote thoughtful advice beneath strangers' bad days.

Nora asked whether they wanted a break. Leah immediately offered more toast. It turned out she had a perfectly good plate; the saucepan lid was easier to wash.

They stood around the kitchen counter, chewing buttered toast while Gerald blocked the route to the front door. Eve found that the room became less hostile once nobody was pointing at a plan.

Rachel showed Nora a photograph of the cat she had grown up with. Leah admitted she didn't dislike cats; she disliked finding out she was supposed to live with one after the bowls had been purchased. Mia listened without looking at either of them.

“I thought you knew I meant it,” she said.

“We knew you meant it,” Rachel replied. “We didn't know you thought that meant we'd agreed.”

Eve rinsed her mug. Mia came to stand beside her, holding the yellow blanket she had fetched from her room.

“I know how this ends.”

“We haven't decided an ending.”

“You're all going to tell me to wait.”

The sink tap squeaked when Eve turned it off. She looked at the blanket, beautifully soft and unused.

“There might be a way to do something now without moving Socks in now,” she said.

Mia made a tired sound.

“I don't mean pretend you never wanted him. The volunteer group has regular jobs. You could see what it feels like to have something depend on a particular day, even when work changes.”

“So I have to prove I'm responsible by washing things?”

“That isn't what I meant.”

“It's what it sounds like.”

From the table, Nora said gently, “We need volunteers, but volunteering isn't a test that earns someone an animal. It wouldn't reserve Socks for you. It wouldn't skip an assessment later.”

Mia's hands tightened around the fleece.

Eve felt the mistake land. She had offered work as a consolation prize, making it sound as though enough good behavior would buy the answer Mia wanted.

“You're right,” she told Mia. “I made that sound like a ladder. It isn't.”

Mia stared at the floor.

“You don't have to volunteer,” Eve continued. “And you don't have to decide tonight.”

For the rest of the visit, she tried not to turn silence into another recommendation.

They left with questions written down rather than an approval or refusal. Nora would send the ordinary information about household agreements. Mia would not bring an animal into the flat while the arrangements were unresolved. Rachel and Leah would each tell her what help, if any, they could genuinely offer. Nobody promised that the answer would eventually be yes.

At the door, Mia did not hug Eve or thank her for teaching her anything. She said goodnight and closed the door with more force than necessary.

Eve and Nora descended two flights in silence.

“I think I made that worse,” Eve said on the pavement.

“You apologized for the part you got wrong.”

“That's a low bar for an evening.”

“Some evenings require low bars.”

Nora adjusted the strap of her bag. Inside it were the same unremarkable forms she had carried in. Eve had expected expertise to produce an elegant way through the problem. Instead it had produced several accurate questions and a young woman who was disappointed in her.

“Does it always feel this unfinished?” Eve asked.

“Not always. Sometimes there's a biscuit.”

Eve laughed, surprised into it.

At home, Dumpling was asleep beside the drawer she had surrendered to his belongings. He opened one eye when she came in, then closed it again. His care supplies sat together on the shelf, exactly where the clinic's written plan told her they should be. Everything she had rearranged still seemed provisional: bowl, carrier, chair pushed sideways to make a clearer route.

She put down her bag and stood in the middle of the room.

She hadn't been more prepared than Mia. She had simply met a cat at a moment when he needed immediate help, and taken the next step with the clinic. That didn't make her a useful judge of another person's life.

Her phone lit up. A message from Mia.

For one relieved second Eve expected reconciliation.

It said, Please don't use any of tonight in a video.

Eve replied at once. I didn't record anything. I won't use your story without asking.

She wanted to add that she was only trying to help. The sentence hovered beneath her fingers, demanding to be understood.

She deleted it.

Dumpling rose, stretched with careful deliberation, and walked past her to the water bowl. Eve moved her bag out of his route.

Then she sat down and let the evening remain unfinished.
