# Chapter 015 — Not Today Is Not Never

The first version of Eve's film opened with a slow shot of an empty bowl.

Sophie watched it on Eve's phone, finished chewing her lunch, and said, “Has someone died?”

“No.”

“Then why have you made the bowl look bereaved?”

Eve took the phone back. “It's the music.”

“It's very effective music. I was about to send money.”

They were sitting on a low wall outside Sophie's current event venue, where she had exactly twenty minutes before a supplier arrived. Eve had brought sandwiches and asked whether she could borrow ten of them. Not the whole afternoon. Not a favor disguised as an emergency.

Sophie had looked suspicious, then accepted both the terms and the sandwich.

The short film contained the clean-up morning, Beth's approved interview section, and a simple list of jobs people could ask the volunteer group about. No Mia. No waiting-room conversations. No private names on the rota. Eve had checked all of that before choosing the music.

She had somehow failed to check whether the bowl appeared to have passed away.

“Try without it,” Sophie said.

Eve muted the track. The scrape of a cupboard door and the click of a plastic lid returned. Beth's voice sounded less like a person imparting wisdom at the end of her life and more like someone talking in a small flat on a weekday afternoon.

“Oh.”

“Yes,” Sophie said. “There you are.”

Eve watched the sequence again. “It's quieter.”

“It was always quiet. You put a brass band in a cardigan over it.”

A van pulled up beside the venue. Sophie checked the registration, swallowed the last of her sandwich, and stood.

“My ten minutes are gone.”

“Thank you.”

“You're welcome. Don't send me nine new versions tonight.”

“I was thinking eight.”

“Goodbye, Eve.”

Eve went home, removed the music, and spent an embarrassing amount of time realizing the film was better.

By the following morning, Nora and Beth had approved the final cut. The group would publish it on its own page, with Eve credited for making it. They had also checked the practical details in the end card so that nobody would arrive at the clinic expecting to volunteer without arranging it first.

Eve was about to send the finished file when Nora asked whether she could stop by later. Mia had arranged to discuss her application, and afterward there would be a small stack of returned supplies to photograph if Eve still needed a clean image for the post.

No interview of Mia was proposed. Eve replied that she could come for the supplies, and waited until Nora confirmed that Mia knew she would be there.

At the clinic, she found Mia outside with an enormous blue laundry bag.

“Are you moving in?” Eve asked.

“The bag is empty.”

“It's making a convincing argument for being full.”

Mia tried to fold it. The stiff sides sprang back into shape.

“Rachel found it under her bed. Apparently it's mine. I thought it was hers.”

“Shared housing: the suspense never ends.”

Mia smiled, but her fingers continued working the handles. Through the window, Eve could see Nora talking to Tessa. Nobody seemed to be waiting impatiently. Still, Mia looked as though she was late for something irreversible.

“Do you want me to go in first?” Eve asked.

“No. It's fine.”

They entered together. Nora had booked ten minutes in the borrowed back room, which now contained a level table and no paper dog. Eve placed her camera bag beside the door, closed, and sat only after Mia nodded at the spare chair.

“I want to pause it,” Mia said before Nora could ask anything. “The application. Not because they've made me.”

“All right.”

“I talked to Rachel and Leah again. We can change a few things, but not everything right now. My hours are changing next month too.”

Nora waited while Mia unfolded the edge of the laundry bag.

“I could keep saying I'll manage. But I don't actually know what I'd be agreeing to.”

Eve felt an impulse to say she was proud of her. She held it back. Mia hadn't asked for a parent, and Eve had been less than useful at their last serious conversation.

“What happens to Socks?” Mia asked.

“His foster carer continues looking after him while we consider applications,” Nora said. “We won't keep him waiting for you, but we also won't invent a deadline to make you choose today.”

Mia nodded. Her face tightened for a moment at the first part, then relaxed at the second.

“Could I still get updates? The ordinary ones. Not special ones.”

“Through the group page, yes.”

“Okay.”

It was a small word for setting down a future she'd already furnished. Eve looked at the empty bag instead of her face.

Mia reached into her pocket and pulled out a folded piece of paper.

“I also asked about this. The laundry. There's a place near my work where the group already has an arrangement, right? Nora explained it in her message.”

Nora nodded.

“I can collect the packed bags here on Tuesdays and take them over. Then bring back the clean ones Thursday. Not wash everything in our kitchen. Rachel made that extremely clear.”

Her voice steadied as she read the times. They were specific, tied to shifts she already worked, with a backup contact if the arrangement failed. She hadn't promised to become indefinitely available.

“Let's try it for three weeks,” Nora said. “Then see whether it works for you and for us.”

Mia put the paper on the table. “And I know it doesn't move me up a list.”

“Correct.”

“Good. I don't want to be doing it while secretly counting points.”

Eve smiled down at her hands.

The first pickup was that day. Nora showed Mia how the bags were separated and labeled, where the receipt belonged, and which contact to call if anything didn't match. Eve photographed only the closed, labeled containers after Nora covered the collection details that weren't for public use.

Mia tried to hoist both bags at once. One slipped.

“I can carry one to the door,” Eve offered.

“I can carry them.”

“I didn't say you couldn't. There are two handles and one door.”

Mia considered that, then gave her the lighter bag.

At the pavement, they waited for the crossing. The blue bag looked less absurd now that it had a job.

“Are you going to put the film up today?” Mia asked.

“The group is. I'm sending them the final file.”

“What's it called?”

“Still Helping.”

Mia wrinkled her nose. “That sounds like somebody's granddad refusing to sit down.”

Eve laughed. “You could have told me before I made the end card.”

“You didn't ask.”

The light changed. They carried the bags across, and Eve left Mia at the bus stop. Back at home, she changed the title to Not Adopting Yet.

Before sending the file, she checked the new title with Nora. The reply came back with a small change to the end card: the group wanted people to contact them before bringing supplies. Someone had already asked whether a broken office chair would be useful.

Eve changed the sentence and exported again. It took longer than it should have because she had named every earlier version FINAL. She created a dated folder, put the accepted file in it, and sent the exact version to Beth as promised.

Beth approved it with one line: That's the conversation I remember having.

Eve stared at that for a moment. She had received more enthusiastic praise before. She wasn't sure she'd ever been more relieved by a piece of feedback.

The group posted it at four.

At four ten, it had nine views, two of which were almost certainly Eve. At four thirty, a volunteer commented to say the packing shot was useful. At five, a woman asked whether the group accepted old blankets, and Nora replied with the actual requirements rather than a grateful invitation to dump an attic's contents outside the clinic.

Eve refreshed the page at five twelve.

Dumpling sat beside her laptop and watched the arrow move.

“This is a very boring sport,” she told him.

He yawned.

By dinner, the film had fewer views than a picture of a crooked cake Sophie had posted the week before. Eve heated leftovers and tried not to feel betrayed by people who had never promised to watch her work.

A new comment appeared while she was eating.

I thought I'd wasted your time by asking questions and then not applying. This helps.

Eve read it twice. She didn't know the writer. There was no dramatic photo attached, no invitation to turn the comment into another piece of content. She left a brief thank-you and let Nora answer anything about the process.

Then she closed the page.

Her phone rang before she'd washed the plate. Tessa's name appeared on the screen.

“Have I left something at the clinic?” Eve asked.

“Not today.”

“An encouraging record.”

“Dr. Clark watched the film. So did I. We were wondering whether you take on small information videos.”

Eve straightened. Dumpling, startled by the movement, stepped off the table and gave her an offended glance.

“What sort?”

“The questions people ask before a visit. Where to wait, what information to have ready, who to call if they're delayed. Not consultations on the internet. We already answer the same things separately all day.”

Eve reached for her notebook, knocking a spoon onto the floor.

Tessa paused. “Are you eating?”

“Not successfully.”

“I can email the outline.”

“Please do. How many videos were you imagining?”

“One to begin with. We need a quote, not a surprise donation.”

Eve looked at the plate, the notebook, the empty place where Dumpling had been. A month ago, she might have offered three versions before asking a single useful question.

“Send me the scope and where you want to use it,” she said. “I'll quote for the first one. Who will check the wording?”

“Julian for the professional parts. Me for whether anyone can understand them.”

“That sounds balanced.”

“It's not always.”

They agreed that a written brief would come first. No appointment was made for filming; no free afternoon was promised. When the call ended, Eve sat still for a moment, listening to the ordinary sounds of the flat.

Her film had not changed her life at four o'clock. It hadn't filled an adoption waiting list or made her a person whose every idea deserved attention.

It had shown someone what her work could do.

She picked up the spoon, washed it, and put it beside the plate. Then she opened a fresh page in her notebook and wrote three questions for the brief.

Dumpling returned to inspect the cleared table.

“No,” she told him. “You haven't been hired.”

He sat on the notebook anyway.
