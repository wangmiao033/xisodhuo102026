# Chapter 204 — A Group Photograph That Stays Unposted

By Sunday, the rain had stopped, leaving the venue's front window streaked and the street looking unexpectedly ordinary.

Eve stood on the dry side of the room with a cloth in her hand. She could see a cyclist waiting at the crossing, a woman examining a broken umbrella, and the shop opposite lifting its shutters. Nobody moved as though they had entered the final scene of an inspiring film.

Robin was counting usable instruction packs.

“Twenty-three,” they said.

Eve wrote it down.

“Twenty-four. One was pretending to be the bottom of the box.”

She changed the figure.

The rear recess remained closed off. The building manager had confirmed they could use the front room for this short staff visit, and they kept to that area. No public event was taking place. A sign at the entrance explained the closure; people with ordinary questions could use the usual contact details without stepping inside.

They worked through dry stock, wiped the front table, and moved two empty cartons out of the walkway. The damp materials stayed untouched for Monday's assessment.

Eve had expected the room to smell ruined. It smelled mostly of cardboard and the bread Sophie brought at eleven.

“I have forty minutes,” Sophie said at the door. “I am visiting, not joining a cleanup operation.”

“I remember inviting you for lunch.”

“Good. Sometimes invitations acquire duties while travelling.”

She set the bread on the clean table and removed her coat. Her own production job had finished late the previous evening, and she wanted to sit down before going home. Eve cleared a chair without asking how much extra time she might have hidden elsewhere.

Mia arrived a few minutes later to return a sample folder she had borrowed before the weather changed. Eve had agreed to the brief drop-off once the route was clear and the front room available.

“My dog has had an extremely boring morning,” Mia said. “Which is a considerable improvement on my Friday.”

“Did the visits work out?” Robin asked.

“Yes. Nobody needed to live in our flat indefinitely. A disappointing outcome for the dog.”

She put the folder on the table and accepted a piece of bread. Sophie told her about the decorative oranges at her event. The client had finally approved them after half were replaced with wooden bowls.

“So you achieved less orange,” Eve said.

“At considerable expense.”

For several minutes, the conversation had nothing to do with rain.

Eve liked the sound of people in the room again. She could also feel herself composing a sentence about it. Laughter returning. A community stronger than the weather. Words that would make the damp carton behind the barrier feel like the opening of a story rather than an expense waiting for a number.

Mia looked around the table.

“We should take a picture,” she said. “To remember everyone still has a face after all those phone calls.”

Robin lifted an instruction pack beside their cheek. “This has been my face since Thursday.”

They all laughed. Eve moved the booking papers and payment records out of view before anyone raised a phone.

“For us?” she asked.

“Yes,” Mia said.

Sophie nodded. Robin agreed, provided Eve didn't make them hold the pack in a triumphant pose.

Eve set her phone on a stable shelf, checked the frame, and started the timer. She hurried back to the table and caught her sleeve on the back of a chair. Sophie reached to free it. Mia was laughing. Robin held a piece of bread halfway to their mouth when the picture was taken.

The result was crooked and warm.

Eve looked tired. Sophie looked amused. Robin appeared mildly betrayed by the speed of the timer. Mia had one hand over her mouth, as though trying to keep the laugh in the photograph.

“That's good,” Mia said.

“It contains evidence of lunch,” Robin said. “Keep it.”

Eve saved it without correcting the angle.

Mia studied the screen a little longer.

“You could use that with a post about what people did for each other. The calls, and the changed visits. I think people would want to know.”

The idea wasn't cruel or foolish. Eve felt its appeal immediately.

She could write about Nina getting the boxes downstairs, Mia finding a workable visit, the customers accepting refunds, and the team keeping the little venue going. She could include the photograph, warm enough to make people stop scrolling. A few new bookings would matter after this weekend.

Sophie broke a piece of crust in half.

“I said yes to a private picture,” she said. “I'd rather it didn't go on the account.”

“Oh,” Mia said. “Sorry. I wasn't thinking of that.”

“No harm in asking.”

Eve put the phone face down.

“Private,” she said. “That was the agreement.”

Robin rubbed their thumb over a crumb on the table.

“I'd prefer that too. I'm glad we handled the cancellations. I don't particularly want to become the face of handling them.”

Mia nodded. “I would have been fine with it, but that doesn't decide for everyone.”

Eve felt a small disappointment she didn't want anyone else to have to soothe. The photograph would have worked. Knowing that did not make the answer less clear.

They checked who could see the private album. Eve named the four of them, and all agreed. She put the photograph there, without adding it to the venue's public account or forwarding it elsewhere.

Sophie looked at her over the bread.

“You went very quiet.”

“I was writing a caption in my head.”

“A good one?”

“Annoyingly good.”

“That's inconvenient.”

Eve laughed. Sophie didn't tell her the caption was morally ugly. It wasn't. It simply required things other people hadn't offered.

After lunch, Mia left for her own afternoon. Sophie checked the time and followed, taking the remaining bread because she had brought more than anyone could eat. Robin returned to the paid inventory work, and Eve opened the messages she had received since Friday.

She wondered whether a plain text account of the small tasks might still be useful. Before drafting one, she asked the people involved whether they wanted their experience mentioned, with no names or identifying details unless separately agreed.

Nina called rather than replying in writing.

“The plumber's been,” she said. “The cupboard's accessible. The cat has decided the empty hallway is offensive.”

“That sounds about right.”

“And I'd rather not be in a post. Even without my name. I just want to stop thinking about how foolish I felt asking for help.”

“You weren't foolish.”

“I know that now. I'd still rather leave it behind.”

“All right. I won't use it.”

Nina sounded relieved. She began describing the cat's determined attempt to reclaim the cleared floor with a toy, and Eve listened without turning that into a replacement anecdote she could publish instead.

Mabel's answer arrived later.

No story about me, please. My friend came for tea today. Biscuit disliked her umbrella. Normal service restored.

Eve smiled and confirmed she would leave them out.

Mia was willing to talk about changing the care visits, but wanted time to decide what she actually wanted to say. Eve told her there was no deadline. The venue didn't need a lesson from someone else's disrupted Friday by the end of Sunday.

She closed the draft window, still empty.

Across the room, Robin had finished the count.

“Ready for the unpleasant numbers?”

“As ready as I'm likely to get.”

They spread the records over the table where the bread had been. Cancelled revenue appeared beside refunds already processed. Preparation costs stayed in the month they had been incurred. The remaining supplier order would arrive later, with the express charge removed. The printed material that might be damaged went into a provisional line, awaiting confirmation rather than an invented total.

Eve added Robin's paid hours.

For a moment she stared at the gap between what they had expected the weekend to bring in and what it had actually produced.

A warm photograph wouldn't change those entries. It might bring future work, but she couldn't honestly count that money before anyone had booked.

“Do you want to leave the account update until tomorrow?” Robin asked.

“No. If we finish the actual numbers now, tomorrow can be about what needs repairing.”

They found one supplier credit Eve had not entered, then a small bank processing cost she had missed. The corrections moved the total in opposite directions. Reality declined to become either entirely worse or conveniently better.

A message arrived from someone booked for the following Wednesday. They were about to arrange transport and wanted to know whether the session would definitely happen.

Eve's eyes went to the empty corner, where a smaller group might fit. She could almost see the chairs already.

She replied that she couldn't confirm until the repair assessment and offered to refund the booking if the uncertainty made planning difficult. The customer chose to wait until Monday afternoon. Eve put that response beside the booking, with the promised update time, rather than silently treating patience as permission to postpone the decision indefinitely.

When the record matched the confirmations, Eve saved it and put the paper copies away.

The commission she had completed on Friday would help once paid. She recorded its invoice separately from money already received. It was still work she could continue while the venue was partly unusable, and for the first time that afternoon she felt something steadier than the hope of a sympathetic audience.

Robin shut the laptop at the end of their shift.

“Monday at the time we agreed?”

“Yes. I'll send the contractor's assessment when I have it. No need to wait on messages tonight.”

They put on their coat and left. Eve remained to check the front room and lock up.

Before going, she wrote a short public update. The weekend sessions had been cancelled and refunds were underway or complete according to each booking's confirmed status. A small area of the venue required repair assessment. Existing customers with affected future bookings would hear directly once the timetable was known.

No photograph of tired people accompanied it. No one was described as a hero. She did not call the weekend a success.

She read the notice, checked the facts once more, and posted it.

At home, Julian was reading when she arrived. Dumpling occupied the folded blanket beside him, asleep with one hind foot pointing upward at an improbable angle.

“How did it go?” he asked.

“We counted things. Ate bread. Took a photograph we aren't posting.”

“Good bread?”

“Excellent bread.”

She sat beside him and described the day. She didn't show him the group picture, because the album's audience had been agreed. Instead, she told him how Robin had looked when the timer caught them with lunch halfway to their mouth.

He laughed at her imitation, which was inaccurate but affectionate.

Later, Eve opened the private album once while waiting for the kettle. The picture was still crooked. Nothing in it could be measured as reach or inquiries or conversion.

She remembered the sleeve caught on the chair, Sophie's fingers freeing it, and the brief shared laugh before anyone suggested a caption.

Then the kettle clicked, and she put the photograph away to make tea.
