# Chapter 197 — Who Remembers the Tissues?

The tissue box made a hollow cardboard sound when Eve pulled out the last sheet.

She had tomato sauce on two fingers, an onion rolling toward the edge of the counter, and a phone vibrating inside the pocket she could not touch. Across the kitchen, Julian held the lid of a pot as though waiting for instructions from a traffic officer.

“Could you get another box?” she asked.

“Where are they?”

“Top of the hall cupboard.”

He went. She caught the onion with her wrist.

“There aren't any,” he called.

Eve closed her eyes briefly. She had noticed that three days ago. She had meant to put it on the list when she finished unloading groceries, then remembered they also needed washing liquid, and somewhere between the two facts had answered a venue message.

“Use the kitchen roll,” Julian said, returning.

“Also nearly finished.”

“I'll buy some tomorrow.”

“And washing liquid.”

“Okay.”

“The kind for the machine. Not the one for dishes.”

“I know.”

She rinsed her hands. Her phone stopped vibrating, which was the first helpful contribution it had made to dinner.

“Do we need anything else?” he asked.

The question ought to have been kind. It arrived like another object placed in her already occupied hands.

“I don't know,” she said too sharply. “Could you look?”

Julian put down the lid.

“I was offering to go.”

“I heard.”

They looked at each other over a pan of chicken that was beginning to catch. Eve turned down the heat. Julian added a little water from the jug, then stepped back.

The sauce loosened. The conversation did not.

They had been living together long enough for the move's first shopping receipts to fade in the drawer. Most mornings worked better now. Julian prepared his breakfast things quietly; Eve stopped hunting for cables beside a sleeping person's head. Dishes were ordinary dishes again, dealt with according to their shifts and dinner plans.

Yet the household seemed to contain a second, invisible kitchen where Eve was permanently opening cupboards in her mind.

She knew the bin bags were running low. She knew the bathroom bulb flickered sometimes, that the cat litter delivery was due next week, and that the tea Julian preferred came in packaging almost identical to the one he didn't. None of this was arduous on its own. Together it followed her to work and sat beside her while she tried to write captions.

Julian stirred the sauce.

“I'm not refusing to help,” he said.

“I know you're not.”

“Then tell me what you need.”

She leaned both hands on the counter.

“That's the part I'm tired of.”

He looked puzzled, which irritated her more than disagreement would have. She took a breath before she turned the onion into evidence of a much larger crime.

“The remembering. Working out what needs doing, then asking, then remembering whether it happened.”

“I buy things when you ask.”

“Yes.”

“And I put them away.”

“Yes. I don't mean you aren't doing anything.”

He set the spoon down. In the silence, the pot gave a little impatient bubble.

Eve took the plates out. She wanted to explain this well, but she also wanted to eat before clarity reduced dinner to paste.

“Can we finish cooking and talk while we eat?”

Julian nodded. “All right.”

They moved around one another without their usual commentary. Eve cooked the rice she had already measured. Julian finished the sauce and wiped the counter with a cloth. There was no tissue box to solve everything by reappearing.

When they sat down, Dumpling came to inspect the chair legs. Eve had fed him earlier. He sniffed Julian's slippers and continued toward the living room, uninterested in being enlisted into household governance.

Julian tasted the chicken.

“Your mother's version?”

“The accidental version I made when you tried to teach me yours.”

“It's improved.”

“By abandoning the instructions.”

He smiled. She was grateful for the small familiar exchange, and also grateful he didn't use it to declare the tension finished.

After a few mouthfuls, she said, “When you asked what else we needed, I started looking through every cupboard in my head.”

“I thought you'd already know.”

“I usually do. That's how we got here.”

He glanced toward the kitchen.

“I didn't expect you to take it on.”

“I didn't announce that I was taking it on. I just kept seeing things first.”

She picked a piece of pepper out of the sauce, then put it back. She was organizing even her dinner to avoid meeting his eyes.

“At the venue, Robin owns whole parts of the day. I don't have to remember every detail they handle. Here I keep saying, 'Could you get this?' And you're perfectly willing. But I'm still running the cupboards.”

Julian chewed slowly.

“I thought asking you was better than buying things you didn't want.”

“Sometimes it is. Once. Then you could know which ones we use.”

He nodded, without the quick reassurance she had dreaded.

“I've been treating you as the person who knows the house.”

“I've only lived here as long as you have.”

“I know.”

The rice was slightly overcooked. Eve ate it anyway. She did not need dinner to become a symbol too.

“What do you want me to take?” he asked.

She opened her mouth. Then he held up a hand, catching himself.

“Sorry. Let me try first.”

He looked around the room, apparently counting things she couldn't see.

“I can take the household supplies. Cleaning things, paper things, bin bags. Laundry too. Checking what we have, buying it, keeping the clothes moving. Not waiting for you to say the basket's full.”

Eve considered the offer. He had early starts, but he also had predictable days when he could shop. She worked later at irregular intervals. Laundry would no longer drift toward her merely because her workplace was sometimes a few feet from the machine.

“I'd like that.”

“What would you rather keep?”

“Ordinary food shopping and meal planning for now. And Dumpling's supplies and care appointments. We still do the feeding according to who's home.”

“That sounds like more remembering on your side.”

“Maybe. Let's see what it actually involves instead of making the categories look equal.”

Julian scraped up the last sauce with his spoon.

“And if one of us can't do our part that week?”

“Ask early. Hand over an actual task. Not leave an empty bottle and hope love notices.”

He looked toward the hallway. “I may have done that with the soap.”

“You have.”

“I did notice the soap. I thought there was more somewhere.”

“There was. I put it there.”

He winced, and this time she laughed.

After dinner they opened the hall cupboard together. It contained spare sheets, towels, one tissue box that was emphatically empty, and a bottle of something bought during the move with a label neither remembered reading.

“Why did we keep the empty box?” Julian asked.

“I thought I'd remember to replace it.”

“Did it work?”

“Constantly. Just never in a shop.”

He flattened it for recycling. Then he took down the mystery bottle, read the label, and put it beside the appropriate cleaning things rather than guessing what to use it on.

Eve began listing products aloud. After the third, she stopped.

“I'm giving you a tour as though you're taking over a museum.”

“One tour would help. A lifelong audio guide would not.”

She showed him the laundry setting she used for her delicate tops, and he showed her a label on his work trousers she had never needed to know about. They sorted the bottles, checked what was almost gone, and made room so the supplies could actually be seen without lifting a towel mountain.

Julian opened a note on his own phone.

“I'll keep the list here.”

“Do you want me to send you mine?”

“Just anything already ordered, so I don't duplicate it.”

She sent two items and left the rest to him.

That should have been the end of it. At bedtime, she nearly reminded him about the tissues.

The sentence was halfway to her tongue when she realized what she was doing. She swallowed it, kissed him goodnight, and trusted that a perfectly ordinary adult could discover whether shops sold paper.

The following afternoon, Eve came home to a large pack on the table.

“Those are very many tissues,” she said.

Julian emerged from the workroom carrying the laundry basket.

“They were cheaper per box. I checked the cupboard before buying them.”

“Will they fit?”

“Yes.”

She looked toward the cupboard anyway.

He caught the glance. “Would you like to inspect my calculations?”

“No.”

“You sound disappointed.”

“I had prepared several opinions.”

“Save them for the washing liquid. I bought the same one.”

The joke made it easier to leave the pack alone. She went to change her shoes. In the bedroom, the clean laundry lay in separate piles, including a top she had forgotten owning because it had spent so long near the bottom of the basket.

There was also one of Julian's socks draped over a lampshade.

She carried it out between finger and thumb.

“An artistic decision?”

“Dumpling took it while I was folding. I thought he'd abandoned it under the bed.”

The cat, sprawled beneath the table, offered no comment. Julian retrieved the sock and put it away himself.

Before leaving for the shop, Eve checked the litter delivery. The supplier had moved the arrival window to a morning when both of them would be out. She found a later slot, changed it, and put the confirmation in her calendar.

Julian passed carrying the empty basket.

“Everything all right?”

“Delivery moved. I moved it again.”

“Do you need me home?”

“No. I'll be here editing.”

He continued toward the bedroom. She had not asked him to track a parcel that belonged to a task she had chosen. The distinction felt less grand in practice than it had over dinner. It was mostly a few taps, and then letting the question be finished.

Later, Eve stopped at the shop for vegetables. She went to turn down the household aisle, then stopped beside a display of biscuits.

She could check. It would take a moment. She could check the washing liquid, the soap, the bin bags, the vast new tissue supply. Instead, she bought the peppers she wanted and a packet of biscuits she didn't need.

At home, she found Julian putting a new liner in the kitchen bin.

“Do we have milk?” he asked.

“Yes. I bought it.”

“Good.”

There was no ceremony. He tied the old bag, and she put the groceries away. The division would need changing sometimes. She would occasionally remember something on his side, and he would notice something on hers. Tonight, though, she had walked through a shop without carrying the whole apartment in her head.

She put the biscuits on the table beside the blue cup.

Julian opened them, offered her one, and reached for a tissue when crumbs fell onto his shirt.

The box was there.

Eve took her biscuit and let that be enough.
