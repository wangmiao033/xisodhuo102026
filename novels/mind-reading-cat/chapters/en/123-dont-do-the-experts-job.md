# Chapter 123 — Don't Do the Expert's Job

"I can't tell you whether that's expected," Eve said.

The sentence sounded abrupt in the quiet room. She wanted to cushion it with something warm and useful, but the words that came first were precisely the dangerous ones: probably, sometimes, I remember hearing.

She left them unsaid.

Ben looked down at his folded appointment paper. "I wasn't asking for a diagnosis."

"I know. But deciding whether a change in eating is expected needs someone qualified to assess it. Your practice can tell you what to do next."

Ruth shifted in her chair.

"Mine did that when—"

"Could we pause the comparisons?" Eve asked.

Ruth closed her mouth. Eve felt rude, then remembered that letting the story continue might leave Ben choosing among three animals none of them had examined.

"I don't want another pet's experience to steer what you do for yours," she said. "Do you have your practice's contact details with you?"

"On the paper."

Ben unfolded it, keeping the printed side toward himself.

Leah glanced at Eve. "Isn't your partner a vet?"

Eve had never announced Julian as part of this event. That didn't make their relationship a secret; people who followed her work had sometimes met them together. Still, she disliked how quickly his profession had entered the room and begun rearranging her chair.

"He is. He isn't providing this session, and I can't answer on his behalf."

"Could you ask him?" Ben said.

There was no manipulation in the question that Eve could see. He was worried, a possible source of help had appeared, and he had reached toward it.

Her phone was in her bag. She could picture sending Julian a quick message, then waiting for his answer while the group watched. An unofficial consultation would feel reassuring even before it contained any information. That was part of the problem.

"Your own practice has his records and can direct you properly," she said. "If they're closed, their official message should give you their out-of-hours arrangements. Would you like a quiet place to make the call now?"

Ben looked at the clock. "I don't want to hold everyone up."

"You won't. We can continue, and you can rejoin when you're ready."

She showed him the small waiting area the caretaker had identified, away from the open meeting-room door. Ben took his coat but left the next-step sheet on the table.

When Eve came back, Ruth was studying the card she had made.

"I was going to say something reassuring," Ruth said.

"I know."

"My sister says that's usually when I should stop."

Eve smiled, grateful for the small release. "We all want to help. We can be useful without deciding what his cat needs medically."

She sat down rather than standing at the front as though she had delivered an important lecture.

"Leah, would you still like to talk about the example you brought? You can pass."

Leah looked at her card.

"It's mostly about photographs. Probably not what everyone else came for."

"We can leave that for afterward. Is there anything in the everyday arrangements you'd find useful to discuss while we're here?"

For a moment Eve thought Leah would say no. Instead she described trying to coordinate a short visit from a friend who sometimes looked after her cat. She had written instructions, but they filled several pages and the friend kept asking which parts mattered today.

This Eve could help with.

They did not need the cat's medical history or the friend's identity. Eve asked Leah to imagine arriving at the door with ten minutes and a phone in one hand. What would she need to locate first?

"The key," Ruth said.

Leah laughed despite herself. "That has happened."

On a blank sheet, Leah wrote the practical essentials she wanted someone to find quickly. She kept private information out of the version on the table. Eve suggested separating standing information from a dated note for that particular visit, then asked Leah which headings made sense to her.

Ruth pointed out that a heading was no use if it said Important Things above six different paragraphs.

"That is nearly my exact system," Leah admitted.

They tried a shorter example. Eve watched Leah move two lines, cross out another, and leave a space where she would add the proper contact details at home. It wasn't the workshop Leah had wanted, but it was work she could use.

Ben returned ten minutes later. He sat down and put the folded paper inside his coat.

"I've spoken to them. They're arranging the next step with me."

"Good," Eve said. "Would you like to keep listening, or do you need to leave?"

"I can stay until the end."

Nobody asked him to repeat the call. He picked up his pen.

They finished by having each person choose one action rather than recite everything discussed. Ruth would talk to the other adults about the feeding card. Leah would shorten the visit instructions. Ben would follow the practice's directions and put the relevant information where he could find it again.

Eve asked them to mark anything on their sheets that was a personal suggestion rather than a professional instruction. Then she thanked them and ended at the promised time.

No one clapped. Ruth put the cabbage back upright. Ben asked whether Eve would send the blank version of the three boxes, and she said yes, as included in the booking. Leah remained seated while the others collected their things.

"I'll send you a message about what I expected," she said. "I need to look at the announcement again."

"Please do. The refund terms are in your confirmation. I'll honor them."

Leah nodded and left with the pale scarf looped over one arm.

Eve stood alone beside four used cups.

Her first feeling was relief. Her second was a powerful desire to phone Julian and have him declare that she had handled everything well.

Instead she collected the cups, wiped the table, and restored the chairs. The room booking included clearing time, not an indefinite period for deciding what the evening meant.

The coordinator came to check the room just as Eve was testing whether the whiteboard was clean. The eraser had spread a gray cloud across it.

"Use this," the woman said, offering a cloth. "That thing mostly relocates ink."

Eve thanked her and tried again.

"How did the trial go?"

"Three people. Useful in parts. One person expected content advice, and a health question came up that needed a practice rather than a group."

"Were you able to manage it?"

"The owner contacted his practice. I'd like to make that distinction clearer before another session."

The coordinator checked the floor beneath the table. "People bring all sorts of questions to rooms. The description helps, but you'll still need to handle them when they arrive."

Eve folded the cloth. "I noticed."

"Send me your feedback summary when you've had time. Without anyone's personal details. We review trial bookings before agreeing more."

It was neither an invitation nor a dismissal. Eve put it in her work notes as a next step.

At home, Dumpling met her at the door with the usual concern about whether she had forgotten where the food lived. She checked the time, washed her hands, and gave him his meal before opening any messages.

Then she sat on the floor beside the sofa. Not because there was nowhere else to sit. Because the evening had required her to keep looking composed in chairs, and the floor asked less.

Julian called at the time they had agreed earlier. Eve told him how the session had gone, leaving out identifying details and the owner's specific concern.

"Someone wanted you to answer through me," she said. "I sent them to their own practice instead."

"That sounds appropriate."

"I wanted you to say it with more ceremony."

"Shall I find a bell?"

She laughed, and Dumpling looked up from his bowl.

Julian asked what had worked. Eve described the feeding card, the folder, and the visiting instructions. As she spoke, she noticed how much of the evening had been ordinary useful work. The difficult question had filled the entire room in her memory because she had been afraid of answering it badly.

"I kept thinking I should know more," she admitted.

"About what?"

"Everything anyone might bring."

"That sounds like a long course."

"Several courses. An irresponsible number of chairs."

He was quiet for a moment.

"What did you sell them?"

"A hosted conversation and help finding a practical next step."

"Did the medical question need you to become a vet, or help them reach one?"

Eve leaned her head against the sofa. The answer was obvious when he put it like that. She wished obvious answers could arrive before the heat in her face.

After the call she worked for twenty minutes, no longer. She added a short paragraph to the facilitator notes about stopping speculative health comparisons, offering privacy for a call, and using the owner's own professional contacts. She removed a planned prompt inviting people to ask anything about life with pets. Anything was a word with expensive ambitions.

The next morning she asked Tessa, through the clinic's ordinary contact channel, whether the clinic had a public page explaining appointment contact and out-of-hours arrangements. She wanted a link to official information for a resource sheet, not permission to send strangers to Tessa personally.

Tessa replied during working hours with the public page and a reminder to direct existing clients to their own practices when appropriate. She also pointed out that the page could change.

Eve put a checked date beside the link and labeled the clinic clearly rather than calling it recommended help. She included a place for participants to write their own practice's number. The sheet would help people locate the right route; it would not pretend that one clinic was available to everyone for everything.

Before printing, she tried the sheet on herself. She put a finger on the heading and imagined needing the number while distracted. The first version sent her through a paragraph about preparing for appointments before it reached the contact section. She moved the contacts to the top.

The change pushed the source labels onto a second page. Eve reduced the decorative space, not the type, and tried again. One page. Clear headings. Room for handwriting large enough to read afterward.

She had enjoyed designing beautiful things long before she owned a cat or knew Julian. That pleasure remained, but this page had a particular job. A handsome border that made the number hard to find was failing it.

She saved the fuller notes separately. If somebody wanted to read more later, they could. The person holding a carrier and looking for a phone number did not need to begin with her introduction.

Then she printed a test copy.

It was less impressive than the first design. There were no broad promises, no reassuring photograph, and no suggestion that access to Eve included access to Julian. Several lines were simply blank for the owner's own information.

She held it at arm's length and could read the headings easily.

Dumpling jumped onto the desk and sat beside the printer, watching the second copy emerge. Eve moved it before he could pin it with a paw.

"This part is mine," she told him.

He turned toward the window, apparently content to leave her with it.
