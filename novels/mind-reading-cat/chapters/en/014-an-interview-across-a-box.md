# Chapter 014 — An Interview Across a Box

By the time Beth arrived, Eve had hidden the washing in the bathroom and turned a supermarket box into the most attractive object in the flat.

The box had defeated every attempt at disguise. She had draped a clean throw over one side, then removed it because Dumpling kept trying to pull it through the opening. Now it sat beside the chair with BANANAS printed across its longest edge, containing a cat who had rejected both his bed and the afternoon's agenda.

“It looks comfortable,” Beth said.

“It used to be fruit accommodation.”

Beth laughed and took off her coat.

Nora had introduced them by email. Beth had been considering adoption for several months and was willing to talk about the questions that kept slowing her down. She had chosen not to be filmed at the clinic, where she worked herself into feeling she ought to make a decision. Eve had offered her own flat, explained that Dumpling would be there, and sent the proposed questions in advance. Beth had agreed, after asking whether there would be a place to put her bag away from inquisitive paws.

There was. Eve had emptied half a shelf for it.

It felt reassuring to begin with a promise she could actually keep.

“Tea?”

“Water would be lovely.”

“Still or the sort where I drop the glass?”

“Still, if it's not more expensive.”

Beth was in her forties, with a rain-darkened fringe and a laugh that arrived slightly after her words. Eve had imagined someone solemn from her emails. She should have learned by now not to cast people before they appeared.

The camera stood on a short tripod near the window. Eve showed Beth the frame, which included the chair and a plain section of wall but neither the family photograph on her phone nor anything that would reveal a home address. She explained that she could stop at any point, that the recording was for a short piece about the work surrounding adoption, and that Beth would see the selected section before it went anywhere.

“It's possible nothing you say will fit,” Eve added. “That isn't an assessment of how interesting you are.”

“I'd quite like to be moderately uninteresting today.”

“I have excellent equipment for that.”

Dumpling remained inside the box. A white paw lay over the edge, giving the impression that he was supervising from a private booth.

Eve checked the audio twice. The room was quiet enough that the refrigerator sounded like distant machinery trying to escape.

She asked the first question.

“What made you start thinking about adopting?”

Beth looked at the lens. Her shoulders rose.

“I've always believed animals bring people together.”

Eve felt the familiar sensation of a polished sentence leaving no place to put her next question.

“That sounds like something you'd put on a leaflet,” she said, then winced. “Sorry. I meant—”

“It is. I work in communications for a college.”

“Oh.”

“I can keep doing it, if you want. We could finish in six minutes.”

Eve laughed, and Beth's shoulders lowered.

“What would you say if I weren't filming?”

“That I miss having another living creature making demands in my kitchen. And then I feel ridiculous, because my kitchen is already too small.”

The answer was better. Eve leaned toward the camera to check the sound level, and Dumpling withdrew his paw.

Beth glanced at the box.

“Have I frightened him?”

“I don't know. He likes being in there.”

“Should I move?”

“Not unless you'd prefer to.”

Eve asked about the practical questions. Beth's job involved occasional evening events. She rented. A relative had offered to help, but lived far enough away that it wouldn't be a daily arrangement. None of the problems was impossible. Together they formed a landscape she could not cross just by wanting to.

“My sister says I think too much,” Beth said. “Last week she sent me six photos and wrote, Pick one.”

“Did that help?”

“It helped me turn off notifications.”

Eve smiled. Beth smiled back, then looked toward the camera again. Her face changed as she remembered it.

“The thing is, I had a cat before.”

Eve waited.

“A long time ago.”

She pressed her fingers together in her lap. Eve could see the shape of the interview shifting: a past animal, a loss, a reason that wanting another felt dangerous. It would give the short piece an emotional center. The sort of thing clients called human, as though hands folding towels were not.

“What was—” Eve began.

Beth's gaze moved to the window.

Eve stopped herself. The proposed questions hadn't included the old cat. Beth had introduced the subject, but that didn't oblige her to open every door behind it.

“Would you rather leave that part out?”

Beth took a breath. “Yes. Sorry.”

“No apology.”

Eve switched off the recording.

“I can cut what we've just done too.”

“Not all of it. Just that.”

“Of course.”

The refrigerator clicked into silence. In the sudden quiet, they both heard a faint scrape from inside the box.

Dumpling had backed farther into it. Eve could see one round eye between the top flap and the side. She had been moving her chair closer, lifting the camera, leaning forward. Perhaps she was making him uncomfortable. Perhaps Beth's unfamiliar voice was enough. There was no way to ask him which part of the afternoon he objected to.

She moved the tripod away from the box and sat on the floor by the low table instead of reaching inside.

“He can stay there.”

Beth looked relieved. “People always tell me their animals are friendly, as if I'm supposed to pass a test.”

“He doesn't have a customer-service role.”

“Neither do I, technically. Yet here we are.”

Eve laughed. The sharpness she had felt about losing the interview's best moment eased into embarrassment. There hadn't been a best moment. There had been a woman deciding she didn't want to discuss something.

They drank water. Beth asked how Eve had ended up with Dumpling. Eve gave the short version: rain, injured paw, clinic, a temporary arrangement that had expanded to include furniture rights. She didn't perform the story of losing her job. It wasn't necessary to make the cat's arrival meaningful.

“So you're still looking for someone who might know him?” Beth asked.

“Yes. Through the ordinary channels. Nothing confirmed.”

“That must feel odd.”

“It does.”

For once, Eve didn't add a joke quickly enough to cover the truth.

Beth nodded. She looked at the box without leaning toward it.

“I think I'm afraid of making the wrong promise,” she said.

Eve's hand almost moved toward the camera. She kept it on her glass.

“You don't need to say that again for the recording.”

“Thank you.”

They talked about unglamorous things: the price of replacing a damaged chair, who could hold a spare key, why an office sometimes called an evening event optional when it clearly wasn't. Beth had brought a list of questions for the adoption group. Two were questions she had already asked and wanted to ask again because she hadn't understood the answers.

“Ask again,” Eve said. “I do that professionally and charge for it.”

At the box, an orange ear appeared.

Eve noticed it, then deliberately looked back at Beth. The ear stayed. A little later, Dumpling's whole head emerged. He surveyed the room with the sour expression of someone finding the same guests still there after a strategic absence.

Beth smiled without offering her hand.

“He's got excellent eyebrows.”

“They're his management team.”

He stepped out, stopped beside the table, and sniffed the leg of Beth's chair. She remained still. Eve wanted a photograph so badly she could feel the wanting in her fingertips. The light was gentle, the cat was curious, and Beth's face had relaxed into a smile that had nothing to do with performing one.

She left the camera where it was.

Dumpling moved past Beth and climbed into the chair Eve had abandoned. He folded himself onto the cushion with the air of a tenant recovering disputed property.

“Well,” Beth said. “We've all found somewhere to sit.”

Eve looked up from the floor. “Some of us have better legal representation.”

After a while, Beth asked whether they could try the interview again.

“Only the practical part,” she said. “And could I look at you instead of the lens?”

“Absolutely.”

Eve reset the camera farther away. Dumpling was now out of frame, asleep in the chair, and she did not rearrange him to improve the composition. Beth spoke about finding out what help relatives really meant when they offered it. She described asking the landlord a specific question rather than relying on a vague comment from years earlier. She admitted that a list could make a decision clearer without making it for you.

When Eve asked what she wished someone had told her at the beginning, Beth considered it.

“That I don't need to arrive already convinced.”

Eve waited a beat, then stopped the recording.

This time, she didn't ask for a cleaner version.

They watched the useful sections together. Beth chose to remove one mention of her work schedule that felt too specific. Eve marked the time and made the cut while she watched. The earlier personal section went into the deletion queue, not a folder labeled emotional material for later.

“Does everyone get to be this fussy?” Beth asked.

“Everyone I'm interviewing for this does.”

“Good. I'm available to be moderately uninteresting again.”

At the door, she retrieved her bag from the shelf and put on her coat. Dumpling opened his eyes, watched her leave, and made no attempt to stop her. Eve walked her to the landing, where Beth said she would send Nora the revised questions tomorrow.

She did not say she was ready to adopt.

Eve did not ask her to.

Back inside, she reviewed the room before the footage: two empty glasses, the damp patch beneath Beth's coat, a cat occupying her chair, and the banana box abandoned by the table. Nothing in it looked like a successful production.

She sent Beth the agreed clips, then deleted the unwanted recording as promised. For a moment the empty space in the timeline bothered her. A story had almost fitted there.

Dumpling stood up, turned twice, and settled with his back to her.

Eve pulled the box a little farther from the walking route and left the opening clear.

Her phone buzzed with Beth's reply before she reached the doorway. The selected section was fine. Could Eve please send the final version before posting, as agreed? Also, she had accidentally taken the paper coaster.

Eve looked at the low table. One round damp mark had already formed where the coaster ought to have been.

Keep it, she wrote. It may help with the kitchen negotiations.

Beth sent back a laughing face. Eve set a cloth over the mark.

Then she went to the bathroom to rescue her hidden washing before it became part of the architecture.
