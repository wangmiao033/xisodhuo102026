# Chapter 213 — She Is Not the Only One Who Can Do It

Robin put the blank cards at the far end of the table.

Eve moved them to the middle.

Robin moved them back.

They looked at each other.

“I want people to tell the story before they start writing,” Robin said.

“Yes. Of course.”

Eve took her hand off the cards and put it in her pocket, where it could interfere only with a receipt.

The small trial was due to begin in twenty minutes. Four people had booked at the stated price. The front room's temporary arrangement still worked, and the rear remained separate while the final stages of repair were checked. Nobody needed scenery, a microphone, or a promise of veterinary advice. The listing described an owners' exchange about making ordinary household reminders easier to understand.

Eve had approved the cost and practical limits with Robin. She had also agreed that Robin would host it.

Her fingers had apparently missed that conversation.

“I'm going to the client,” she said.

“Good.”

“I mean, I'll be back later because we arranged it, not to inspect.”

Robin smiled. “You can come back to your own workplace. You just can't secretly run two events at once.”

The paid assignment was a short product demonstration for a nearby independent shop. It involved folding storage boxes, a counter, and a proprietor who wanted customers to see the actual sizes before ordering. Eve had already agreed the shot list, fee, and limited revisions. There were no animals in it.

Dumpling, meanwhile, was sleeping in his familiar carrier in the venue's nonpublic quiet area. Eve had brought him for their previously arranged short visit while she completed preparations; Julian would collect him on his own way home before the visitors arrived. Robin had agreed to check the quiet area during the few minutes between Eve's departure and his arrival; if his plans changed, Eve would wait rather than leave them an unplanned care task. They had checked that the timing worked without turning Julian into a clinic courier. The carrier door was open to the quiet space, which remained separated from visitors. The cat was not part of the booking.

Eve checked his water and the collection message, then picked up her camera bag.

At the entrance, she turned back.

“If people get stuck, the sample with the calendar—”

“I've got it.”

“Right.”

She left before adding another sentence disguised as reassurance.

The proprietor met her beside a stack of boxes and immediately handed her the smallest one.

“This is the large.”

Eve looked at it.

“Is that the product name, or a challenge?”

He laughed and checked the label. “Wrong pile.”

They sorted the actual sizes before the camera came out. Eve photographed the labels for her work reference and marked the surfaces to be shown. She moved a display sign that reflected in the clear lid, then tested the sound of the shop door, which chimed over the end of every explanation.

Here, there were useful things she knew how to do. She could hear where a sentence was too long for the action. She could tell that the overhead angle concealed the depth of a box. She could suggest showing the same ordinary objects in two sizes instead of asking customers to imagine what a number meant.

The proprietor tried the demonstration.

His first attempt included a lid that refused to close.

“Keep that,” Eve said. “Not the mistake, necessarily. The comparison. People need to see when it doesn't fit.”

He considered it, then agreed to show the limit honestly.

Her phone vibrated in her bag.

She wanted to check it so abruptly that her hand had reached the zip before she remembered where she was.

It was not the emergency contact tone they had agreed. Robin could make ordinary decisions and would call if something genuinely required Eve.

She finished the take.

During the planned pause, she checked the message. It was Julian confirming that he had collected Dumpling. The cat had objected to leaving his sleep by continuing to sleep until the last possible moment.

Nothing from Robin.

Eve put the phone away, feeling both relieved and slightly unnecessary.

“Again?” the proprietor asked.

“Yes. This time leave your hand still after the lid closes.”

They worked through the remaining shots. When a customer entered, Eve stopped filming rather than capture an unagreed face and try to solve it in editing. The proprietor made the sale. Eve checked her notes while waiting and found that she had enough footage for the brief without the elaborate moving shot she had imagined on arrival.

She didn't invent a need for it.

By the time she packed up, the client had seen the selected takes and confirmed the demonstration facts. The first edit was due the following afternoon. She had completed the on-site work within the booked time.

At the venue, Robin was standing beside the table holding a drawing of a bag with what appeared to be an antenna.

“It isn't an antenna,” a participant said.

“What does it do?”

“Keeps the keys attached.”

“Then let's see whether someone else can tell that.”

Eve stopped inside the door.

The cards were still at the far end. Three visitors were drawing. The fourth had no drawing at all and was talking with her hands. Eve could see the remaining time on the wall clock, and the exercise had not reached the part she had expected by now.

Her mouth opened.

Robin glanced toward her, acknowledged her arrival with a small nod, and returned to the woman speaking.

Eve closed her mouth.

She put her camera bag in the office area and came back with the supply receipt she actually needed to file. There was a chair by the entrance where she could sit without joining the table.

The woman explained that she kept making labels that everybody else in her household ignored.

“Do they know what the labels are asking them to do?” Robin asked.

“Of course.”

“Have you asked?”

The woman paused, then laughed at herself.

“Apparently not as recently as I thought.”

The conversation took several minutes. No impressive object emerged from it. The woman crossed out a heading on her card and wrote a question instead.

Eve had been about to hurry them past that moment in order to protect the exercise.

She looked down at her receipt.

A little later, Robin invited anyone who wanted a private record of their own work to take a photograph. No public filming was taking place. One participant asked Eve whether the venue would post the finished cards.

“Only if there's a separate agreement,” Eve said. “Today's activity doesn't require that.”

Robin nodded and continued explaining the next step.

Eve felt a second correction rise when they skipped her example calendar. It was a good example. She had drawn it neatly for once. Robin used the participant's antenna bag instead, asking two people to interpret it before the owner explained.

Their wrong guesses made the owner laugh without making her the target. She changed the attachment drawing herself. The result was less attractive than Eve's sample and more closely connected to the problem she had brought.

Eve left the calendar in its folder.

Robin showed the group a photograph on their phone: the empty setup, taken for the internal room record before any visitors arrived. During collection, with the outside door closed and Julian beside him, Dumpling had crossed the bottom of that frame, a blurred orange tail briefly replacing two chair legs. Julian had then gathered him into his carrier and left before the event began. The accidental image had become a joke, with no visitors or private notes in view.

“I thought cats were meant to improve pictures,” the man with the bag drawing said.

“He hasn't signed that agreement,” Eve replied.

“Good union.”

The group laughed and went back to their work. Nobody asked for the cat to be brought back to repeat the accident.

When the clock reached the final ten minutes, Robin invited each person to name one change they wanted to try at home. The talking woman chose a conversation, not a finished card. The man chose fewer arrows. Someone else wanted a photograph of the correct container, taken at home and kept privately.

Eve saw the stack of unused materials and almost offered a quick additional exercise so everybody would leave with more.

That was the third time.

She kept her hands folded around the receipt instead.

The guests had paid for the stated session, not for exhausting every sheet they could carry. Robin finished the promised discussion and left time for belongings, questions about the next ordinary date, and unhurried departure.

They ended on time.

Afterwards, Eve joined the paid clearing period they had agreed. She wiped the table while Robin checked the supply count. There was no painted backdrop to dismantle, no heavy furniture to return, and no late rescue message waiting to be written.

“Feedback?” Robin asked.

Eve rinsed the cloth.

“I tried to move your cards before you started.”

“I noticed.”

“I also nearly interrupted three times after I came back.”

“I noticed one.”

“I'm improving invisibly.”

Robin laughed.

Eve told them what she had seen, including the time the woman needed to reach her own question. She asked why they had left the drawing materials at the far end. Robin said some people would start decorating immediately rather than work out what they actually wanted to communicate.

“That would be me,” Eve said.

“I wasn't going to name you.”

The four paid bookings had covered the planned costs and left a modest margin. The short feedback cards included one request for clearer examples at the beginning, which Robin wanted to consider without rebuilding the whole format around it.

Eve agreed to review the next version with them during scheduled time.

Then she opened her laptop to organize the client's footage. Robin finished their own closing tasks without waiting for her to invent more.

For the next hour, Eve worked in the quiet room. She cut a repeated sentence, adjusted the comparison shot, and checked that the product names matched the labels. This was work she could invoice, work that belonged to her professional skill rather than to her ability to be physically present at every table.

She exported a review copy for the client and checked the agreed delivery address before sending it. The message named the revision window instead of inviting unlimited changes. Then she put the project away. Finishing one assignment did not require her to reopen the venue's whole calendar tonight.

The first assembly was complete before she went home.

Julian was sitting on the floor with Dumpling, who was investigating a paper bag with its handles removed. Eve set her equipment down and sat beside them.

“How was the session?” he asked.

“Good. Not quite how I'd have done it.”

“Disaster.”

“Apparently survivable.”

She told him about the antenna and the woman who had left with a question. Dumpling emerged from the bag, stepped across Eve's knee, and settled beyond her reach.

The session had worked without him performing. It had worked without Eve directing every minute too.

She rested her hand on Julian's shoulder and stayed on the floor, where nobody needed her to improve the arrangement.
