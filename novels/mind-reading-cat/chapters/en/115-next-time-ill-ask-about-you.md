# Chapter 115 — Next Time I'll Ask About You

Sophie's answer came the following afternoon, while Eve was trying to draw the same cup for the fourth time.

Debrief done. Nobody died of uneven paper. I have notes and a headache.

Eve put down her pencil.

Want to talk, complain, or be left alone?

Complain briefly. Talk tomorrow. Be left alone after complaining.

A voice message followed. Sophie sounded tired, but not in the strained way she had sounded after jobs that seemed to acquire new duties during the drive home. The room change had worked. The first instructions had been too distant for some participants to see. She had underestimated how long beginners needed to settle their materials before listening, and the tutor had pointed out that she sometimes asked a question while already moving toward the next task.

Eve listened to the entire message before replying.

That sounds irritatingly useful. Did you agree?

Unfortunately.

She nearly wrote a list of ways to structure the demonstration. Instead she asked whether Sophie wanted ideas now.

Not now. I've got enough ideas to trip over. Tell me your notebook hasn't fallen apart.

It has survived two fish and an argument with a cup.

Good. I'm putting that in the evaluation.

Eve sent a photograph of the page, careful to keep an unrelated client document outside the frame. Sophie responded with a laughing face and then, as promised, disappeared.

The absence felt less like a door closing when Eve had stopped standing in front of it waiting for immediate proof that everything was all right.

She returned to the cup. The handle still looked detached, as if it belonged to another household and had only stopped by to visit. She erased half of it, made the paper furry, and decided the cup could remain structurally unsound until morning.

There was work to finish, but not an emergency. The customer from the interrupted dinner had accepted a later delivery for the additional version after Eve confirmed the scope and price. The world had not rewarded her boundary with universal admiration. There had been a slightly terse reply, followed by the information she needed.

Now the work was nearly done, and tonight belonged to something else.

She checked the time she had agreed with Julian, then put a pan on the stove.

He arrived carrying bread and a small bag of tomatoes. Dumpling greeted the bag before the person.

"I see how it is," Julian said.

"He respects a contribution to the household."

"He can't have either of these."

"His standards concern potential, not eligibility."

They put the food away before the cat could become more personally involved. Julian washed his hands, asked what needed doing, and accepted the task of slicing bread without reorganizing the kitchen.

Eve had been looking forward to telling him about the session. Once she began, however, she found herself describing Sophie's work before her own crooked notebook.

"She changed the demonstration halfway through, after talking to the facilitator. It wasn't dramatic. People just understood it better."

"That sounds useful."

"It was. I kept wanting to know the whole schedule. As if they'd sent me an incomplete briefing."

"Had they?"

"No. They'd sold me a ticket."

Julian smiled and put the bread on a plate.

"A difficult role for you."

"I required considerable supervision from myself."

She showed him the notebook once the food was ready. He examined the cover and the uneven corner without attempting to reassure her that it looked professionally made.

"It stays together," he said.

"A glowing review."

"And it's yours. What are you using it for?"

"Things I don't have to be good at."

He opened the first page with her permission. The disappointed fish faced him from beside the failed cup.

"You've started ambitiously."

She kicked his ankle lightly beneath the table, and he laughed.

During dinner she told him about the phone call at the restaurant. She did not reproduce every word Sophie had said, or ask him to determine which of them was right. It was enough to explain what she had done and why she'd been uncomfortable afterward.

"I wanted to call you that night," she admitted. "Mostly so you could tell me I wasn't awful."

Julian considered that while serving himself more vegetables.

"I might have told you to apologize."

"I had already done that."

"Then you'd have been disappointed in my range."

She smiled, then shook her head.

"I wanted to feel finished with it. Sophie wasn't finished just because I understood it."

"Has it been better?"

"Yes. Not because we've had one perfect conversation. I know more about what she's doing now. I can actually ask a second question."

Julian nodded, and they moved on to his week. There had been a supply order mix-up at the clinic, resolved without a heroic story attached. He had bought the wrong size of something for his own kitchen as well, which Eve found more interesting.

"How wrong?"

He held his hands apart.

"That's not a lid. That's a small roof."

"The online photograph lacked a useful comparison."

"Did the measurements also lack one?"

He gave her a patient look. She laughed into her glass.

After dinner they washed up together. While Julian dried the plates, Eve took out the drawing book to show him the exercise that had defeated her. He studied the example, then the cup on the counter.

"May I try?"

She gave him a loose sheet of paper rather than surrendering the handmade notebook. He drew with the careful concentration she had seen him bring to many more useful tasks. When he turned the sheet around, the cup was recognizably a cup, but the handle projected from its side at an alarming angle.

Eve examined it in silence.

"You may say it," he said.

"It looks like it wants to leave."

"Possibly because of the company."

She laughed and drew a second cup beside his, making hers lean toward it. They argued over whether this improved either object. There was no photograph for the account, no charming clip of a veterinarian being unexpectedly human. When the tea was finished, Eve put the paper between the pages of the drawing book, where their two peculiar cups could continue leaning without anyone else needing to approve them. Later, when he left for his own home, she walked him to the door and kissed him without looking back to see what Dumpling thought of the arrangement. The cat was asleep on the sofa, with one paw folded beneath his chin.

The next morning, Eve and Sophie spoke while walking separately to their errands. Eve stopped outside a quiet shop doorway rather than trying to answer messages and cross a road at the same time.

"How did the rest of the debrief go?" she asked.

"You heard the embarrassing part."

"I heard one embarrassing part. I assume there are categories."

"Several. But the venue lead said I handled the late arrival clearly. They couldn't join the practical session without holding everyone else up, so I offered the display and details of future sessions. I hated saying no while there was paper sitting unused."

Eve remembered the visitor by the door. She had almost supplied a cheerful yes on the strength of that same spare paper.

"Did they mind?"

"A little. They'd traveled. But they understood. We checked the booking information afterward to see whether the start time could have been clearer. It was there. We may still make it more prominent."

"And did you enjoy the work?"

Sophie was quiet for a moment. Eve heard a crossing signal in the background, then footsteps.

"Yes. I liked knowing why we were doing it. And seeing the same team before and after, instead of just receiving a list of things that had gone wrong."

"Are you going to look for more of that?"

"I am. Carefully. There's a temporary coordination role advertised at another venue. I'm going to ask about the hours before applying. It includes evenings, which is fine, but 'flexible' can mean several incompatible things."

"Do you want me to look at anything?"

"Not yet. I might ask you to read my application later. Not today. Today I need to collect a parcel and buy something that counts as lunch."

"Understood."

Sophie asked about Eve's additional client work. Eve told her the revised delivery had been accepted, and admitted she had spent an unreasonable amount of time rereading the customer's terse response.

"Did it become warmer on the ninth reading?"

"No. I checked thoroughly."

They laughed, and then Sophie had reached the parcel shop. The conversation ended without either promising to call again tomorrow.

That afternoon Eve put a note in her calendar for the day after Sophie's inquiry about the role. Not an alarm demanding an update, just a reminder of something that mattered to her friend. She had crossed such a note out of the handmade notebook because she'd wanted that object to remain play. Remembering itself was not the problem. Turning friendship into another project she could complete was.

When the reminder appeared a few days later, she sent a short question.

Did they clarify the hours?

Sophie replied that evening. The advertised role involved more late finishes than she wanted at present. She was not applying.

Eve stared at the message for a moment, resisting the urge to call the decision disappointing before Sophie had named it herself.

How do you feel about that?

Annoyed. Relieved. Still interested in this kind of work, just not this version.

That makes sense. Want company this weekend?

Saturday's busy. Sunday late afternoon?

They settled on a walk near Sophie's place. No promise to spend the entire day together, no assumption that a free hour meant a person had nothing else of value to do. Eve would bring back the paperback Sophie had lent her after their trip, after she finished discovering why its characters were so determined not to speak plainly.

She opened the book that evening with Dumpling settled beside her thigh. Her phone lay on the table, silent. Julian had his own plans, Sophie had hers, and for once Eve was not treating that as a gap she needed to fill urgently.

Three pages later, she reached a plot twist so implausible that she sat upright and startled the cat.

"Sorry. Fictional emergency."

He stretched, turned around, and settled facing the other direction.

Eve photographed only the page number and sent it to Sophie.

We need to discuss this on Sunday.

The answer arrived much later, after Eve had put the phone away and returned to the book.

I was waiting for you to get there.

She laughed when she finally saw it. On Sunday they would talk about the role Sophie had decided against, and whatever else had happened since. They would also spend at least ten minutes arguing about a man in a novel who could apparently survive anything except a direct question.

Eve tucked the bus ticket from their trip into the book and turned the next page.
