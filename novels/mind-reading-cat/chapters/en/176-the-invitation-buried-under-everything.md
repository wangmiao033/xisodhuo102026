# Chapter 176 — The Invitation Buried Under Everything

The restaurant had no spare sockets, no noticeboard, and no reason to ask Eve whether six people could sit in a circle.

She liked it immediately.

Julian arrived carrying nothing except his coat. For a moment she almost asked whether he had remembered the small paper bag of cupboard handles still at his flat. Then she remembered what they had agreed under the half-dark lights of You, Me & Paws and closed her mouth around a different question.

“Have you eaten somewhere that wasn't the clinic this week?”

“Yes.”

“Your kitchen doesn't count.”

“Then no.”

They had found an evening a few days after the opening. Eve had delivered a client's revised captions that afternoon; Robin had the next session's running order. The room was locked. Nobody needed her to make a decision about tape.

She put her phone inside her bag.

Julian noticed. He slipped his into his coat pocket with a ceremony so solemn she kicked his shoe beneath the table.

“Don't make it strange.”

“I was honoring the occasion.”

“You look like you're surrendering evidence.”

They ordered aubergine, rice, and a fish dish whose description gave them no practical information. A waiter brought tea in a pot with a lid that rattled whenever Julian tilted it. Eve talked about a stranger on the bus who had carried an enormous unwrapped lampshade, rotating it politely whenever somebody needed to pass.

“What shape?” Julian asked.

“That matters?”

“If I'm going to picture the inconvenience accurately.”

She drew it in the air. He said he respected the man's commitment to ambient lighting. It was a thoroughly useless conversation, and Eve felt something behind her ribs loosen.

They were halfway through the aubergine when he mentioned a colleague lending him a book.

“The one you complained about being out of print?”

“Different one. This is more relevant to the course.”

His chopsticks stopped.

Eve waited for the rest of the sentence. A couple at the neighboring table were arguing amiably over whether they had ordered enough food. The waiter reached past them for an empty plate.

“The training?” she asked. “Have you heard?”

“Yes. I was going to tell you tonight.”

“Oh.” She smiled before she could help it. “Julian. That's good.”

“It is.”

He smiled too, but there was a tired crease between his eyebrows. He reached into his coat for the phone, then set it on the table without unlocking it.

“I need to explain the timing.”

“Okay.”

His screen lit beneath his hand. It was an ordinary reminder, large enough that she could read it without leaning forward: travel booking, Sunday. Under it was the name of the training organizer.

“This Sunday?”

“Yes.”

Her smile went away slowly, which felt worse than if it had simply vanished.

“Monday orientation,” he said. “Three weeks. I would travel the afternoon before.”

Eve looked at the aubergine between them. They had applied themselves to it from opposite ends, leaving a narrow strip in the middle.

“When did you find out?”

“Two days before the opening.”

The teapot lid clicked as somebody's elbow caught the table.

“You knew at the opening.”

“Yes.”

“And when we were sitting in the corner afterward.”

“Yes.”

“You asked for an evening where we didn't talk about the venue.”

“I meant that.”

“I know you did. That's not what I'm asking.”

He moved his hand away from the phone. “You were running on very little sleep. There were things happening every hour. I thought I could wait until the opening was behind you.”

Eve pushed a loose grain of rice back into her bowl. She did it with such care that she knew she was angry.

“Did you think I would ask you not to go?”

“No.” He hesitated. “I thought you would be upset.”

“I probably would have been. For a minute. Maybe for longer. I'd also have been pleased.”

“I know.”

“You didn't let me find out.”

The fish arrived. Neither of them had expected it to be served whole. The waiter explained where the small bones were likely to be, and Eve thanked him with a brightness that sounded terrible in her own ears.

Julian waited until he had left.

“I wasn't trying to make a decision for you.”

“You decided there wasn't room for my reaction.”

He took a breath, then let it out without answering.

She wanted him to disagree so that she could produce more evidence. She wanted him to agree so she could stop feeling like somebody who had spoiled the first quiet dinner they had managed in days.

“I remember the application,” she said. “I asked you about it. I didn't forget you wanted this just because I got a door with my name beside it.”

“I know you didn't forget.”

“Then please don't treat me as if anything that isn't good news for the shop will knock me over.”

He looked down at his bowl. “I made the conversation smaller in my head every day I didn't have it. Then I could see how close it was getting, and it became harder to start.”

That answer was sufficiently unflattering to sound true.

Eve picked up her tea, discovered it was cold, and put it back.

“Have you already said yes?”

“I acknowledged the offer and asked for the placement information. Dr. Clark and I have been checking the cover. I haven't sent final acceptance. They need it tomorrow.”

“Tomorrow.”

“I should have told you when it came.”

“Yes.”

“I'm sorry.”

She nodded once. The apology didn't rearrange the evening, but it gave her something solid to put her foot on.

The couple beside them ordered another dish. Eve asked Julian to pass the rice.

They ate for a few minutes. He took care with the fish bones. She hated that she could still find his concentration endearing while being angry with him.

“Don't reject it tonight,” she said eventually.

“I wasn't planning to.”

“Good. Because then I'd have to be furious about a second thing.”

His mouth twitched, cautiously.

“I'd prefer not to expand the agenda.”

She didn't laugh, quite, but she took the piece of fish he had put on the serving plate.

After dinner they walked to Eve's flat. She had suggested going there instead of trying to discuss accommodation while a waiter waited for their table. It was still an evening away from the venue. Nobody had promised it would be easy.

Dumpling met them at the door, sniffed Julian's shoes, and turned his back on both of them. His bowl had been washed; his usual meal was due. Eve fed him before taking off her coat.

“Do you want tea?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Fresh tea. We've made enough bad decisions with the old one.”

Julian filled the kettle while she cleared two envelopes from the table. He asked before opening the training information on his phone and putting it between them. Eve brought a notebook, not the business one with the unpaid invoices tucked inside.

Three weeks. Monday start. Supervised sessions with a team whose work he wanted to observe, followed by time reviewing what he'd done. Some days' details would depend on the teaching rota rather than an office-hours calendar.

“Which part are you looking forward to?” she asked.

He glanced up, apparently surprised.

“I can be cross and interested. It's a fairly crowded evening.”

He showed her one part of the outline. He had encountered the work before, but not often enough to be comfortable doing more without further supervision. He wanted to see how another team prepared, not just read the summary afterward.

“There are things I do slowly because I'm careful,” he said. “And things I do slowly because I need to learn them properly. I'd like to be better at recognizing which is which.”

Eve wrote Monday on the blank page, then stopped taking notes so she could listen.

He explained a little more. He didn't turn it into a lecture, or pretend three weeks would make him an expert. When he reached a session he was particularly looking forward to, his thumb stayed against the line on the screen.

She had seen him look that way at a difficult passage in a book. It made the missing conversation feel more wasteful.

“Where will you stay?”

“A furnished room arranged through the course. I have the details. I need to confirm it with acceptance.”

“Food?”

“There is a kitchen.”

“That wasn't the question.”

“I can buy food.”

“You once described four carrots as dinner supplies.”

“They were supplies. The rest was already at home.”

She drew a tiny carrot beside Monday. He watched her do it and looked relieved in a way that made her soften and resent softening at the same time.

The first Saturday appeared mostly free after lunch. He could not promise the whole weekend yet. The second week's clinic rota would affect whether a return trip made sense. They looked at travel times without buying anything.

Eve's next paid client deadline sat in the first week. The venue session was later than she had initially planned. Moving either to fit an imagined perfect farewell would cause problems for people who had nothing to do with their argument.

She left both where they were.

“What frightens me,” she said, turning her pen between her fingers, “is that I won't know which bits to ask about. I'll tell you three stories about a cupboard and you'll say your day was fine.”

“I could make more of an effort than fine.”

“You'll have to.”

He nodded.

“And if I'm having a difficult day, tell me anyway. You can ask whether I have ten minutes. You don't have to decide the answer.”

“Okay.”

Dumpling jumped onto the spare chair and tested its cushion with his front paws. He turned twice, rejected the end nearest the table, and tucked himself against the backrest. Whatever qualities he was assessing in this particular square of fabric, he appeared to have found a satisfactory spot. Eve envied the simplicity of that inspection.

She rubbed his shoulder once. He settled without looking at her.

Julian closed the information screen. “I don't expect you to stop being annoyed because we made a list.”

“Excellent. The list is very short.”

He looked at it. Monday, three weeks, and the carrot.

“Strong visual communication.”

This time she did laugh, though it escaped on a sigh.

He left before it grew late. In the hallway she kissed him, then kept her hand around his wrist for another moment.

“Congratulations,” she said. “I wanted to say it properly.”

“Thank you.”

“Tomorrow, after you've spoken to the clinic, call me. Even if I'm still being a nuisance.”

“I hadn't categorized you that way.”

“Don't start a spreadsheet.”

His laugh followed him down the first few stairs. Eve went back inside, crossed out none of her page, and added the name of the course beneath the carrot.
