# Chapter 249 — The First Bowl Is Still Here

A year after Nell and Theo were born, the numerals on their birthday cake refused to dry.

Eve had piped one beside each name. The first had slumped until it looked like a hook. The second had suffered a worse fate when Julian, carrying a basket of washing, said over his shoulder that perhaps she could write eleven and get the next ten parties out of the way.

She'd laughed, leaned down with the icing bag still in her hand, and put a pink comma on the edge of the plate.

He put the washing down.

“I was wrong,” he said. “Now it's eleven and a quarter.”

The cakes they had fed people at the venue had always looked remarkable, mostly because somebody other than Eve had made them. She found a clean cloth and wiped away the comma. Before the guests arrived, she'd cut two soft pieces for the twins to eat, leaving the piped names intact for the adults who would actually notice them. There were no candles to chase out of a baby's reach. Sophie had a camera in her bag and had already agreed not to bring it out until asked.

“Could you find the wider cake stand?” Eve said. “This plate keeps turning on the cloth.”

Julian lifted the plate, removed the cloth and set the plate back down. It sat perfectly still.

“Oh,” Eve said.

“You can look for the stand if you want.”

“No, I preferred it when this was difficult.”

He went away laughing, with the washing and the small pile of clothes that still had to go on two small people. Eve scraped the icing into a bowl to wash later. On her way to the cupboard for something to cover the cake, she remembered why they had put the stand away in the first place. She kept it in its original broad tin, with several other things she didn't need very often. She brought the tin down and lifted the stand off the top.

The first was a towel, folded with the border on the inside. In unraveling the fold she found the edge had gone uneven where the loops had pulled, one longer than the rest. It had been the cheapest towel in the shop. She had paid first and been given the dry box for nothing. She still remembered the shopkeeper making the distinction, as if he could account for even the mercy of a box in his stockroom.

The towel had come back from the clinic that night in a bag, damp and separate. The cat had stayed.

She put it on the table next to her account book, which she had taken out that morning to enter an invoice. The school holiday booking had been paid on time; a small materials expense still needed the proper receipt. She clipped its envelope inside the front cover for the next hours she'd set aside for work. It wasn't the record of the household and it wouldn't become one because the cake had briefly sat beside it. Below the book was her usual ring of keys. Robin had the venue key for the afternoon session already. She didn't need to remember to bring hers to anybody.

She shook out the towel. Something rattled against the tin.

The first bowl was still there, nested beneath it. Eve had bought the bowl and put it on an easy-to-clean mat in the apartment she thought she might not be able to afford. She could remember the reason for choosing the mat more readily than she could remember what she herself had eaten that week. Dumpling had gone to inspect the rim while she was still telling a recruiter, over the phone, that the company had restructured. He had gone from there to a drawer, having apparently assessed her hospitality and settled on an improvement.

She turned the bowl over. Dust clung to the foot. In the tin underneath it, folded in with a few papers, was the slip that had recorded the loan of the clinic carrier. The carrier had gone back long ago. She could still see the clerk's writing and the place where the fold cut part of one number in half. There was no expectation of anyone needing it. She put it aside while she washed the bowl.

From the next room came a yell and a less convincing yell immediately after it. Julian replied in an entirely normal voice to both. Eve heard the click of the press studs on a fresh shirt. A minute later he came to the doorway with Theo on his hip. At his feet, on the clear rug inside the sitting room, Nell was demonstrating that she could get around one of the cushions on her own, even if it meant going the long way past everything in the room.

“Somebody objected to the yellow shirt,” Julian said. “Somebody else objected to not getting to object to it first.”

Theo screwed up his nose and caught his father's collar in one fist.

“Do you need me?”

“I think I'm being kept.”

Julian sat down on the rug. Nell turned as soon as she heard him and went back the way she'd come, towards the knees she could pull herself up on. Eve set the bowl upside down to dry, where neither child could reach it, and took the book and the keys off the table. Then she sat beside them. Theo kept his grip on Julian's collar and pressed the side of his face against Eve's arm.

She had intended to get ready before anybody arrived. She stayed where she was until they heard Sophie ring the bell.

The little party took up the dining table and the space around it. Mabel brought a knitted vest in a parcel large enough to contain four. Mia brought two books with thick pages and put the paper wrapping away before either baby could reach it. Sophie came with bread for later and an opinion on the icing.

“I think it's rather good,” she said.

“Did you see what it says?”

“No. That was before I knew I had to read it.”

There were no requests for the twins to sit beside one another for more than they could bear. When Nell stood holding Eve's fingers, somebody set a paper crown on Eve's head instead. Theo made it from Julian's lap to Eve's and back again. Once they got their pieces of cake, each protected what remained on the tray with a sticky forearm. Eve and Julian stayed with them until they'd finished and wiped their hands. The twins had no intention of going looking for crumbs, but they had an impressive talent for taking crumbs with them when they left.

Dumpling appeared after the first plates had been carried to the sink. He came out as far as a familiar corner, with a perfectly straight tail and the air of someone prepared to discover that all the visitors had come to see him.

“Let him choose,” Eve said, as Mia began to get off her chair.

Mia settled back. “I was going to offer him this chair.”

“He'd prefer to believe you were about to.”

That brought one short laugh. Dumpling looked toward the sound. Eve went with him to his usual place once the others were seated. She put down a little of his ordinary food in the bowl with the old mark on its side, the one he used now. He sniffed it from a distance so small it could hardly be measured and turned his head away.

It hadn't been made in the shape of a flower or changed because it was a birthday. He turned it down anyway.

Eve left the offer without coaxing. At the next burst of voices he went into the hall and vanished upstairs. He had a way past the stair gate that the children didn't. Nell put her palms down on the rug as if to go as far as the door, then saw the shiny corner of a board book and stopped. Julian picked up the book and turned to a page with one door on it. This door, at least, opened whenever anyone asked.

Sophie waited until both babies had gone back to their parents' laps before asking for the camera. Eve agreed to one picture. The vest was nowhere near the frame, Dumpling was gone, and Julian had an oval of icing on his cuff. Eve didn't ask him to change it. Theo looked at the camera for just long enough for Nell to look away.

“Do you want another?” Sophie asked.

“Can I see that one?”

The light from the window had caught Nell's cheek as she turned. Julian was looking at Eve, not quite smiling yet. In the foreground there was half a plate. Eve could tell immediately who had set it down in the wrong place, because it was the mistake she'd made every time she'd tried to take a photograph with somebody else's table in it.

“Keep that one for us,” she said.

Sophie switched the camera off. She hadn't been looking for a reason to keep it out.

They ate most of the cake and put the rest away, then took the cups and plates through in small trips. Eve found she had been speaking quite loudly for an hour. As the voices dropped, she heard the clock from the hall again. Mabel and Mia were the first to go, leaving their two chairs a little farther apart than before. Sophie went to wash her hands and said she'd stay for supper if that was still the plan. Eve was glad she'd asked. It gave her the chance to say yes again when the day had more than one part left in it.

Julian carried out the adults' glasses and came back for Theo. Eve dealt with the last of the butter smeared on the cake knife, then took the dry bowl over to the tin. The towel no longer fitted neatly round it. She could have spent a minute making the edges line up and getting everything back in exactly as she'd had it. Instead she tucked the slip with the other papers underneath, folded the towel over once and left one corner out where she could see it. The dry box from the shop wasn't there. Neither was the first battered box from her office; she had no wish to make room for that one.

It struck her that nobody coming into this kitchen now could guess why the bowl had been kept. It didn't look particularly old or expensive. She liked that it could stay there without making its case.

Before she put the tin away, she went upstairs and looked in at the doorway. Dumpling had settled on the chair, sitting deeply enough in the grey blanket that one fold covered part of his chest. He'd come out again or he hadn't. Now his eyes were half shut. She didn't use the opportunity to call his name and see if he'd come back to her.

Julian met her in the hall at the open sitting-room door, both children on the clear rug within his reach. He had made the iced cuff disappear by rolling the fabric as far as his elbow, which was a solution, not a change of clothes.

“Did he want any dinner?” he asked quietly.

“Not while I was there.”

“There's half a slice of ours in the tin, if you do.”

Eve considered it. “After supper.”

He set one hand briefly on her back as she went by. The clean bowl, towel and papers were back inside the tin. She put the stand back on top of them and found a steady place for the tin at the back of the cupboard. Then she took her place on the rug, in front of the twins and as far from the noise in the kitchen as the room allowed. Nobody asked her to bring anything else.
