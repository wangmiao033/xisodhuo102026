# Chapter 091 — He Notices Her Waiting for the Cat

The cupboard door would stay open if Eve held it with her knee. Unfortunately, she also needed her knee to stand up.

“An ambitious piece of furniture,” Julian said.

“It's expressing a preference.”

“For your remaining here indefinitely.”

He took the stack of shallow trays she handed out, leaving her free to straighten. The door closed with a modest thump. Eve had invited him for tea and a walk, not furniture maintenance. So far they had spent six minutes discussing the correct place for trays.

Being able to call him her boyfriend had not improved the apartment's storage.

Their Sunday walk had passed without either of them opening a work folder. Now, on Tuesday, she still liked thinking the word. It had acquired the slightly ridiculous glow of a new object she wanted to take out of its wrapping several times a day. Julian was standing in the kitchen holding scratched plastic, and she could think boyfriend without quietly adding a question mark.

“Those can go beside the sink,” she said. “I need one for tomorrow's photograph. The rest can return to their life of disappointment.”

“What are you photographing?”

“A way of sorting the things people actually take to an appointment. Tessa asked whether I could price a short update to the preparation cards. I haven't agreed to anything yet.”

The existing cards needed a small revision after repeated questions about what visitors should bring. Tessa had sent a plain list of the questions, with no names or patient details. Eve wanted to use an empty tray and ordinary objects rather than ask somebody to surrender a real consultation for the camera.

Julian put the trays down.

“Do you want me to look now?”

“I was going to ask if you had ten minutes before we went out. But you can say you'd rather look at it at work.”

“Ten minutes is fine. Not the medical wording tonight.”

“That can go through the clinic properly.”

She fetched the blank card and set it beside the smaller tray. An appointment reminder, a securely closed sample container supplied only as a visual placeholder, and a notebook might look clear from above. The clinic would decide which objects belonged in the final version and when any particular sample was appropriate. Eve was considering the photograph, not prescribing what every owner should bring.

Julian picked up the blank reminder.

“Will there be room to write on the printed version?”

“I think so.”

“With a normal-sized hand?”

Eve turned it around. She had left a generous border and a very ungenerous space for an actual date.

“A normal-sized hand will need to negotiate.”

He smiled. She marked the border to be reduced.

Dumpling came into the kitchen while she found a thicker pen. He paused by Julian's shoe, sniffed the trouser cuff, and looked toward the table. Julian stepped back to give him room.

Eve began explaining her alternative arrangement. The reminder could sit upright at the back of the tray rather than lie beneath the notebook. That would keep the most important information from becoming scenery. A low angle might work better than the overhead shot she had first imagined.

Julian stopped turning the card.

“It might,” he said.

Something in his voice seemed absent. Not disapproval, exactly. A pause with its contents removed.

Eve looked down.

Dumpling was beside the table leg, watching Julian's hand.

She waited for him to move.

It was only a moment. A ridiculous amount of time to notice. The cat could go toward the cupboard or the rug or the shoe, and none of those routes came with a reliable explanation. Yet she waited as if his next step might supply a word Julian had left out.

“Eve?”

She looked up too quickly.

“Sorry. Did you say something?”

“No. I was waiting for you.”

“Oh.”

Julian set down the card. “Have I missed something with him?”

Dumpling sniffed the bottom of the cupboard door and began washing his shoulder.

“No. He's all right.”

“Then why did you look at him when I stopped speaking?”

Eve's fingers tightened around the pen.

Because she had been about to use a cat to finish his sentence. Because she had learned not to do that and apparently still wanted to whenever uncertainty touched something she cared about. Because there was an explanation that would sound worse delivered over an empty sample pot.

“He was moving,” she said.

Julian glanced at the cat, who was now enthusiastically occupied with an elbow.

“All right.”

The absence of an argument made Eve feel more dishonest, not less. She had answered a question about her attention with a fact that explained almost nothing.

She put the pen down.

“That wasn't really the reason. I got distracted. I was trying to work out whether you disliked the idea instead of asking.”

“You can ask.”

“Do you dislike it?”

“No. I was remembering that the card needs a place for the clinic number. If it's upright, will the tray edge hide the bottom?”

They both looked at the arrangement.

It would.

Eve moved the card forward. A practical answer occupied the space where she had prepared to invent a personal one.

“Better?”

“Yes.”

He didn't sound annoyed. She refused to turn that into proof he hadn't noticed the part she had omitted.

They spent the remaining few minutes checking the layout. Eve took no photograph; she hadn't arranged the light, and the concept needed Tessa's review before she made anything elaborate. Julian handed back the thicker pen, then asked whether they were still going out.

“Yes. I would like to.”

She packed the work away instead of finding one last question to keep him conveniently occupied while her embarrassment settled.

At the door, Dumpling tried to put a paw inside Julian's shoe. Julian waited until it was withdrawn before putting his foot in.

“You cannot come as an insole,” Eve told him.

The cat sat down on the mat, looking neither enlightened nor particularly offended.

Outside, the air was cool enough that they walked faster for the first street. Eve slipped her hand into Julian's when he offered it. His fingers were warm; the ordinary fact relieved her so much that she became cross with herself.

They had chosen one another. That did not mean every pause had to be cross-examined for evidence that the choice had been withdrawn.

At a small food stall, they stopped for something hot. The person ahead ordered an item from a photograph that was no longer on the menu. While the server explained, Eve noticed Julian looking at a handwritten sign beside the till.

“What?” she asked.

“They've added a collection number system.”

“Do you approve?”

“I've spent several visits being convinced they were calling me when they weren't.”

“You have an unexpectedly common noodle identity.”

“Apparently.”

She laughed. They ordered, paid separately for their own food this time, and stood aside. Their number appeared on the receipt in lettering large enough to satisfy Julian's opinion of normal hands.

Eve wanted to return to the kitchen conversation. She also wanted to leave it where it was, one awkward second concealed beneath an enjoyable evening. The second desire was easier to satisfy. Julian was talking about a bookshop that had rearranged its shelves so completely he could no longer find the section he used to dislike.

She listened. She asked what had replaced it. She did not owe him a disclosure delivered badly in a queue merely because she felt guilty.

But she owed him more than a permanent series of almost-answers.

They ate on a bench, balancing the containers on their knees. Eve tipped hers slightly and caught a loose piece with her fork before it landed on her coat. Julian offered a napkin without turning the incident into a rescue.

“Earlier,” he said, “when you were waiting.”

Her fork stopped.

“I wasn't trying to catch you doing something wrong.”

“I know.”

“You seemed to be looking somewhere else for an answer. If you're worried I'll be difficult about a work idea, I'd rather know.”

There it was: the wrong explanation taking a perfectly reasonable shape because she had supplied only part of the information.

“It isn't only that,” she said.

Julian waited.

Eve watched a person fold a paper bag into a small, determined square across the path. She could feel herself wanting a prepared script, something that would make the next conversation arrive without absurdity or risk.

“There's something about Dumpling I haven't explained properly,” she said. “Not a health problem. Not something I need you to solve tonight.”

He put his empty container beside him.

“What sort of thing?”

“I need to find a way to tell you without making it sound more certain than it is.”

“Does it affect me?”

The honest answer was harder than yes or no.

“It affects how I've sometimes behaved around you. So I think I should tell you.”

Julian looked at her. She couldn't read his expression into a verdict without doing exactly what had started this.

“All right,” he said. “Tell me when you're ready to explain. I don't want to guess from hints for weeks.”

“I won't leave it for weeks.”

She hadn't disclosed the secret. She had, at least, stopped pretending there wasn't one.

They disposed of the containers and walked back by the lit shopfronts. Julian did not resume questioning. Eve was grateful, but the gratitude carried a responsibility she could no longer mistake for permission to forget.

At her building they stopped to arrange another evening. She checked her work calendar; he checked his own. Their next meal could be at her place, with no need to fit a clinic discussion around it.

“Nothing involving sample pots,” she said.

“A strong recommendation.”

They laughed, and when he left she watched him go because she liked watching him go down the steps carefully, one hand on the rail. The liking did not disappear beneath her worry.

Upstairs, Dumpling was on the rug, one forepaw stretched toward a toy he had apparently decided should approach him. Eve crouched and nudged it within reach. He caught it, rolled onto his side, and forgot about her.

She opened the notebook containing her old observations. Not to discover what Julian might be thinking now; there was no route through these pages to him. She wanted to see what she had actually recorded before her memory polished it into a more impressive story.

On one page she had written that she became more certain when she wanted a result.

Eve sat down.

The sentence had not improved with age. Neither, she suspected, had the habit.

She put a clean sheet beside the notebook and wrote two headings: what happened, and what I decided it meant. This time the intended reader was not an audience, and the goal was not to persuade him that she had been right.

Before writing anything else, she crossed out a line that began with the cat's cleverness.

She began again with herself.
