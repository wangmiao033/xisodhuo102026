# Chapter 168 — Even the Vet Guessed Wrong

Julian studied the drawing for so long that Eve began to worry he understood it.

“The line comes back on itself here,” he said.

Mabel nodded encouragingly.

“And this is the door?”

“Part of it.”

Eve leaned against the table, resisting the temptation to exchange a look with Mia. They had already spent fifteen minutes deciding whether the shape beside the arrow represented a handle, a flowerpot, or a very small horse. Julian had arrived to collect her from the venue and found all three of them bent over a photocopy as if awaiting archaeological permission to move.

He had asked what they were doing.

It was his own fault.

The follow-up session had been a small booked continuation, open to those who wanted more time with household instructions. Robin had hosted the first part and left at the end of their agreed hours. Eve was finishing the room while Mabel and Mia waited for a passing shower to ease. The last customer had taken home a perfectly clear photograph of her own labeled towel shelf and looked almost disappointed by its lack of mystery.

Mabel's drawing had retained enough mystery for everybody.

“It looks like a turning movement,” Julian said. “If that's the edge of a lock, perhaps the arrow means—”

“The rubbish bin,” Mabel said.

Julian stopped.

“The arrow points toward the rubbish bin downstairs.”

Eve pressed both hands over her mouth.

Julian looked at the page again. “Of course it does.”

“The bin isn't on the door,” Mia said helpfully.

“I had reached that conclusion.”

Eve tried to draw a corrected line, laughed halfway through, and sent it sliding off the paper onto the scrap sheet beneath. The new version looked like a route taken under considerable distress.

Mabel retrieved the drawing before Eve could improve it further.

“The lock bit is here,” she said, pointing at another small shape. “The arrow is a separate instruction. You take that bag when you go downstairs.”

“What bag?” Julian asked.

“The one I haven't drawn.”

He placed the page flat on the table with the care of a man putting down a question he no longer wished to answer.

“I withdraw every opinion.”

Eve laughed until the muscles beneath her ribs hurt. It wasn't that Julian had made an unusually foolish mistake. It was the serious concentration he had given an arrow whose author had not intended it to carry half the information they had assigned to it.

He could be reliably good at his work and entirely defeated by Mabel's bin.

This should not have been a revelation. It was nevertheless a pleasure.

“Would it help if I signed my conclusion?” he asked her.

“Only if you include the horse.”

“There is no horse.”

“We don't know that yet.”

Mabel folded her arms. “You all wanted instructions.”

“We did,” Eve said, getting her breath back. “And then we tried to be clever instead of asking what the marks meant.”

Mia turned over her own page. She had brought a revised guide to locating Toby's supplies. It included descriptions, not just household nicknames, and a photograph of the cupboard she had taken herself. She was still waiting to finalize who would use it during the trip. At least now it looked like something another person might read without calling her at every line.

“Could you just take a picture of your door?” she asked Mabel.

“Which part?”

“The bit you need someone to recognize.”

“And send a photograph of my lock around the neighborhood?”

“No,” Eve said. “Keep it private. Only the person you've actually agreed will help needs the details. This group doesn't need your keys, your address, or a set of pictures showing anyone how to get in.”

Mabel considered this, then nodded once.

Eve put the confusing copy aside. They had reached the point where the exercise needed less paper, not a larger vocabulary for arrows.

“Can we try one small example here?” she asked. “Something harmless. Nothing to do with anyone's front door.”

She pointed to the materials cupboard. A box of pencils and clips lived on the lower shelf, in an open tray. Nobody needed a code or a key to reach it. The problem was that she had described it in her own notes as spare bits, which meant approximately nothing.

Mabel looked at the cupboard. “You could start by not calling them bits.”

“An excellent clinical observation,” Julian said.

Eve handed him a pencil. “You may sit in the audience.”

He sat.

They photographed the open shelf on Eve's phone, checked that no customer information or private papers were in view, and circled the tray in a copy of the image. Under it, Eve wrote the plain name of the item. The photograph stayed with the venue's internal setup instructions; it wasn't a picture for the account.

Mia followed the new direction and brought back the clips.

“Success,” she said.

“Now put them away,” Mabel replied. “Otherwise your next photograph will be wrong.”

Eve looked at her with admiration. Mabel had found the weakness in the system without leaving her chair.

They added a date to the picture and decided where updated setup notes would live. Eve did not turn every shelf into a photographic catalog. There were three items people regularly failed to find. Those would do for a beginning.

Julian held up the pencil she had given him.

“This one was already on the table.”

“Then you are ahead of the exercise.”

“I'd like that recorded.”

Outside, the shower slackened. Light from the doorway brightened the edge of Mabel's paper. She rubbed a thumb along its familiar fold, suddenly less eager to defend it.

“My sister knew the rubbish instruction because she always asked what to take down,” she said. “I suppose I wrote the answer to her question without writing the question.”

“That makes sense,” Eve said.

“It makes sense to my sister.”

“And to you.”

Mabel looked around the room. “There used to be more people who knew what I meant before I finished saying it.”

For a moment Eve didn't know where to put her hands.

Mabel hadn't said she was lonely. She hadn't asked anyone to turn the afternoon into a speech about aging. Yet the folded page carried suddenly more than bad arrows. It belonged to a home once interpreted easily by people who had moved away, changed routines, become less available, or simply grown old themselves.

Eve sat on the edge of a chair instead of reaching for the paper.

“Would you like a clearer version and to keep this one too?” she asked.

“Of course I'm keeping it.” Mabel gave her a mildly offended look. “It's perfectly good paper.”

Mia smiled down at her own notes.

Julian did not contribute a solution. He moved his chair so Mabel could stand without walking around his knees.

They agreed that Mabel would choose a few photographs at home, with whoever she ultimately asked to help. The recipient would try the ordinary tasks while Mabel was present. If a picture was confusing, they would change the picture. They didn't need to prove the person reading it lacked common sense.

Mia's phone vibrated. She read the message, then turned the screen facedown.

“Leah's home tonight. We'll talk.”

“Good,” Eve said.

Mia gathered her sheets, looking briefly more anxious again. Eve had the urge to reassure her that the arrangements would work out. She kept the sentence to herself. There was still a real conversation between Mia and the trip, and optimism could not attend it in her place.

Mabel put the original map into her handbag. The photocopy remained on the table.

“May I keep this?” Eve asked. “For my own reminder not to guess. I won't show it elsewhere.”

“You may keep the bin arrow. Tear off the rest.”

Eve did exactly that. Mabel took the remaining paper, then paused by the shelf.

“You could label the clips underneath the picture as well. If the light's poor, all those little shapes look alike.”

Eve added the label before forgetting. Then she asked Mabel to find the tray from the doorway without Eve pointing. Mabel brought it back, selected a clip, and fastened the unused sheets together.

“There,” she said. “Now your bits are less likely to migrate.”

Eve thanked her and left the label exactly where she had put it.

Mia and Mabel left together once the rain stopped. Eve checked that neither had left a page with personal details behind. Julian helped move only the table she asked him to move, then stood out of the way while she finished the room's closing checks.

“Sorry,” she said. “You came for dinner and got the municipal infrastructure examination.”

“I failed the first question.”

“The examiner was demanding.”

“The examiner omitted the bag.”

She tucked the little torn arrow into her notebook. “Are you hungry?”

“Enough to trust a photograph of food.”

“Dangerous. Sometimes the portions are entirely fictional.”

They went to a place where the menu contained no photographs. Eve ordered soup and something she could pick up with chopsticks. Julian chose a dish he had eaten there before, then admitted he couldn't remember whether it was the one he had liked or the one he had promised not to order again.

“You need written instructions,” she told him.

“I need to eat here more often.”

The bowl that arrived settled the question. He liked it. Eve tasted one offered bite and disagreed strongly enough to make him laugh.

“Then we won't swap,” he said.

A simple outcome. No one needed to persuade the other into having better taste.

They talked about a book she had started, which Julian had not read, and a bus detour he had taken, which Eve knew nothing about. She liked being able to ask ordinary questions without performing the revelation that they didn't know every part of each other's day.

Halfway through dinner she drew a tiny arrow on the back of a receipt, pointing toward his bowl.

“What does that mean?” he asked.

“You tell me.”

He looked at it solemnly.

“Something downstairs.”

She laughed so abruptly that she had to put her spoon down. When she could speak again, she told him it meant she wanted a second taste, just to be certain she disliked it.

He moved the bowl closer.

At the crossing afterward, they separated for their own homes. Julian kissed her briefly and reminded her to send him the name of the book, not the page about kitchen storage she had almost photographed on their date.

“I can distinguish the two,” she said.

“I believe you.”

“Unlike a lock and a rubbish bin.”

He shook his head, smiling, and walked toward his bus.

Eve watched until he reached the shelter, then went home to Dumpling. The cat met her with an indignant stretch and no useful interpretation of the evening. She fed him according to his familiar routine, put her shoes where nobody would trip over them, and sent Julian the book's title.

For once, there wasn't an arrow in sight.
