# Chapter 044 — An Afternoon Without Questions

Sophie chose the street with the least promising view.

To the left were trees and the pale suggestion of the river. To the right were a repair shop, a row of bins, and a sandwich board advertising replacement heels. She turned right without hesitation.

“There's a bookshop this way,” she said.

Eve followed.

She had finished work at twelve forty-eight, eaten before leaving, and resisted putting a small camera in her bag in case the light became interesting. Dumpling had been asleep when she checked his water. For a moment she had considered whether his stillness meant anything about the afternoon ahead, then remembered that she was leaving a cat after lunch.

Now the streets were damp from yesterday's rain. A delivery rider was balancing a paper cup against the handlebars of his bicycle. Someone had propped open the repair shop's door with a shoe too large to belong in its window display.

Sophie walked more slowly than Eve expected. Not Mabel's measured pace, not the purposeful speed she used between loading bays. She stopped to examine the shoe and then to read a notice about piano lessons in a window above a closed hairdresser.

“Are you taking up the piano?” Eve asked.

“No. I like the confidence of 'all levels welcome.' I'd like to put it on my door.”

“Wouldn't people arrive with instruments?”

“Flaw in the plan.”

They reached the bookshop, which occupied a narrow room beneath an awning that had collected a shallow pool of water. Sophie avoided the dripping edge and held the door for Eve. Inside, it smelled of paper, dust, and the orange somebody behind the counter was peeling.

The owner looked up, nodded, and went back to the orange. There was no music. A radiator clicked beneath a window crowded with books about places neither of them had visited.

Sophie headed toward a shelf marked HOUSE AND GARDEN. Eve considered following, then let herself browse the mysteries. Several covers promised an impossible death in an isolated country house. She selected the least plausible one and read the first page standing beside a stack of atlases.

For ten minutes neither of them had to keep the other entertained.

When Eve found Sophie again, she was holding a thin book about growing plants on windowsills. A previous owner had written in the margin beside an illustration of basil: *This is optimistic.*

“An experienced reviewer,” Eve said.

Sophie turned the page. There was a coffee ring across the instructions for drainage.

“I used to grow things.”

“You had that pot of mint.”

“That pot of mint tried to occupy the kitchen.”

“It had excellent project management.”

“I killed the basil, though.”

“The book seems prepared for that.”

Sophie bought it. Eve bought the mystery after discovering, with quiet delight, that the apparently isolated house had a bus stop directly outside it. They left carrying one small paper bag each. Nothing in either purchase was going to make them more employable.

They continued toward the river by a less efficient route. Sophie stopped once to retie her shoe. Eve waited beside a railing, watching a man wrestle a rain cover over a folded pram. She had room in her head for questions, and questions kept presenting themselves: Had the work message arrived? Was Sophie being promoted? Was somebody making her stay late again?

At the apartment the other day, Dumpling's attention had made those questions feel urgent and justified. Without him, Eve could hear how much of the urgency belonged to her desire to stop worrying.

Sophie stood, tested the knot, and pointed toward a bakery.

“Something with sugar?”

“Yes.”

They bought two buns and asked for them in separate bags so they could disagree about the proper pace of eating. Eve took the first bite on the pavement. Sophie carried hers intact to a bench overlooking the river, claiming she preferred anticipation to crumbs down her jacket.

The bench was dry under the shelter of a large plane tree. Across the water, people moved behind office windows, small figures in rectangles of warm light. A duck pursued a scrap of leaf with entirely misplaced determination.

Eve brushed sugar from her thumb.

Sophie took her book out of the bag and balanced it on her knee. She read the same page for a while, then closed it with one finger still inside.

“I got the message,” she said.

Eve turned toward her without putting down the bun as if a formal session had begun.

“They can't confirm the new rota until next week. Again.”

“That sounds like more waiting.”

“It is.”

Sophie looked across the water. Eve waited, aware of every small thing she might say too soon.

“Two people left last month,” Sophie went on. “Different reasons. Nobody was fired in a dramatic meeting. They just left, and their jobs stayed behind like forgotten bags.”

She moved the book from her knee to the bench between them.

“At first I covered a few things because I knew the events. Then somebody started calling me the lead for that part. It sounded good. I thought it might turn into a real change.”

“What changed?”

“The number of questions people ask me.”

Eve gave a small, unhappy laugh before she could help it. Sophie glanced at her and smiled briefly.

“The guest entrance. The supplier access. Whether a late change needs another person. Everyone sends it to me, but I can't approve extra staff or change what the client has been promised. So I carry it up to somebody who can, wait for an answer, and then carry their answer back down to whoever's annoyed.”

A cyclist rang a bell along the path behind them. Eve moved her feet in though they weren't in the way.

“I keep thinking I'll get the next event finished, and then we'll have the conversation,” Sophie said. “But every time an event finishes, the next one becomes the reason we can't have it yet.”

She finally opened the bun's paper bag. The pastry had cooled. She ate a piece anyway, chewing slowly while a flake of sugar caught on her lower lip.

Eve wanted to tell her to leave. The suggestion arrived fully dressed, carrying examples from Eve's own life and a cheerful certainty that starting again could become good eventually. It would make Sophie's situation look conveniently like a road Eve had already mapped.

She swallowed the suggestion with the last bite of her bun.

“I don't want to quit on a park bench,” Sophie said, as though she had heard the version Eve had wisely kept to herself.

“Probably difficult to give notice to a duck.”

“Poor communication skills. Strong interest in lunch.”

They watched the duck abandon the leaf and head toward something more promising.

“I still like making an event work,” Sophie said. “I like the moment the doors open and the things we've moved around on a screen turn into actual people moving through actual space. I don't want every story about my job to end with how much I hate it.”

Eve nodded. The noodles, the unlabeled cases, the lost volunteers: she'd heard the strain, but perhaps she had begun to mistake it for Sophie's entire work.

“There was a little launch last month,” Sophie continued. “I only ran the floor on the day. Someone else booked the venue and handled the supplier agreements. I went home knowing what I'd done and what I hadn't. It felt almost suspicious.”

“Would you want more of that?”

“Maybe. I don't know whether there's enough of it. I'm not announcing a company. Please don't make me a logo.”

Eve laughed. “I wasn't going to.”

“You had a font expression.”

“That might just be my face.”

Sophie leaned back against the bench. For the first time that afternoon, she stretched her legs fully instead of keeping herself ready to stand.

“The embarrassing part is that I wanted them to ask me,” she said. “I wanted the responsibility. Now I feel ridiculous saying I can't keep doing it like this.”

Eve looked down at the empty paper bag in her hands. She folded its edge, then unfolded it.

“I wanted people to need me at my old job,” she said. “That part took longer to stop hurting than the actual work.”

Sophie turned her head.

“Does it still?”

“Sometimes.”

Eve could have made the answer more reassuring. Instead she let it be the one she had.

For a while they sat without speaking. Water moved around the bridge supports. A child farther along the path was trying to explain something about the ducks to an adult who kept saying yes before the explanation was finished. Sophie finished her bun and carefully collected the crumbs in the bag.

“I've asked for a meeting on Monday,” she said. “I want the duties written down, including who can actually decide things. If they want the larger role, we need to talk about the hours and the pay. If not, I need to stop covering it by accident.”

“That sounds like a conversation worth having.”

“It also sounds like one I might spend Sunday trying to talk myself out of.”

“Do you want me to check in Sunday?”

Sophie thought about it. “One message. Not a motivational campaign.”

“One message.”

“And no offer to write the whole thing. I need to hear what I sound like saying it.”

Eve nodded. The request was small enough to follow exactly. It felt better than inventing a rescue and then trying to persuade Sophie she needed it.

A gust moved through the tree and shook a few collected drops onto the far end of the bench. They stood before the next gust could improve its aim.

On the walk back, Sophie opened the gardening book to a drawing of a narrow wooden planter.

“Would this fit your kitchen window?” Eve asked.

“Mine,” Sophie corrected, laughing.

Eve stopped, then laughed too. She had spent so much of the afternoon trying not to absorb Sophie's problem that she had nearly absorbed the planter instead.

“Yours. Obviously.”

“I have a ruler. I can manage this stage of the business.”

They parted at the tram stop. Sophie took the book out once more while they waited, comparing two plants with very different chances of surviving her kitchen. She didn't claim to feel cured. The meeting was still on Monday. There would still be messages before then.

When her tram came, she tucked the paper bag under her arm and stepped aboard without checking her phone first.

At home Eve found Dumpling in the same patch of blanket, though he had rotated enough to present a different side of himself to the room. She sat beside him with her improbable mystery and let him sniff the bakery scent on her fingers.

She knew more about Sophie now than she had that morning. The knowledge had arrived in sentences her friend chose, with pauses Eve had finally allowed to remain.

There was no need to ask the cat whether she had been told the truth.

Eve opened her book. Outside the fictional country house, the number six bus arrived exactly where a competent murderer would least want it.
