# Chapter 122 — The First Session Doesn't Sell Out

Three names looked less discouraging on a booking page than they did on six chairs.

Eve stood in the meeting room with the list in her hand. Ben. Ruth. Leah. She had checked the page so often during the previous two days that she could have written their names without it, possibly in her sleep.

The fourth registration had never arrived.

At home that morning, she had drafted a cancellation message claiming insufficient numbers to provide the intended experience. It sounded responsible. It also concealed the fact that she had advertised a small exchange, and three people could exchange perfectly well if their host stopped imagining an audience elsewhere.

She had put the message aside and reread the actual minimum she had set for herself: three bookings, with the trial loss already allowed for.

Now there were three bookings.

She folded the list and began moving the chairs.

The first arrangement resembled a committee waiting to dismiss someone. The second resembled a dentist's reception area. In the third, four chairs sat around one end of the table, close enough for conversation without forcing knees to touch. She left a fifth available against the wall, where someone could choose a little more distance.

The sixth went back in the stack.

She walked the route from the entrance once more. The directional sign pointed toward the room, but someone approaching from the toilets would see its blank back. Eve turned it slightly, checked that the corridor remained clear, and asked the caretaker where people should wait if she was already speaking with someone at the door.

"Inside, if you're happy with that," he said. "People standing out here tend to think they're not allowed in."

She put the welcome sheet on the table rather than making a checkpoint of the entrance. Then she checked the name list against the payments, counted the printed sheets, and placed the refund information where she could find it without searching her phone. These tasks made her hands useful while the nervous part of her continued composing a grand opening speech.

She abandoned the speech. They already knew why they had booked.

On the table she put blank cards, pens, and a simple printed sheet headed Useful next steps. She had nearly ordered folders bearing a logo. Instead she had bought a packet of ordinary card that would remain useful if the whole idea failed before dinner.

From the corridor came the squeak of the caretaker's trolley.

"Ready?" he asked through the open door.

"Visually."

He considered the table. "That's usually first."

Eve checked the clock. Fifteen minutes until the start. She had already eaten, checked Dumpling's water, and arranged to return well within his usual evening routine. There was no photograph of him on the table, no comforting mascot to make the empty chairs look deliberate.

Just her, three names, and a room she was paying for.

Ruth arrived first, carrying a shopping bag with a cabbage protruding from it.

"I can put this somewhere," she said.

"Beside your chair is fine."

"It wasn't part of the plan. They were reduced."

Eve introduced herself, pointed out the toilets and water, and explained that Ruth could choose any chair. Ruth chose the chair nearest the door. She put the bag down and looked around.

"Am I early?"

"A little. Three people are booked for this trial."

The number came out without an apology attached. Eve was privately astonished.

"Good," Ruth said. "I thought it might be one of those things where you have to shout your name across thirty people."

Ben arrived while Eve was trying not to look excessively grateful to the cabbage. Leah followed him, removing a pale scarf as she stepped into the room.

"I recognize you," Leah said to Eve. "From the videos."

"I hope the lighting is less disappointing in person."

Leah smiled, but her gaze moved to the empty wall and back to the table.

Eve had promised herself she would not interpret every expression as a review. She welcomed them, checked that everyone could stay until the advertised finish, and began on time.

"The plan is one ordinary situation each, then a chance to compare what has helped or find out where a question belongs. You can pass on speaking. Please don't share another person's story outside the room without asking them. I won't be recording anything."

Ben put his phone in his pocket. Ruth nodded. Leah set hers facedown beside her pen.

"And we're here as owners," Eve added. "This isn't a veterinary or training appointment. I'll keep us away from trying to diagnose anybody's animal."

She gave each of them a card and asked them to write one thing that made an ordinary week of pet care harder than it needed to be.

The silence that followed seemed much longer from the host's chair.

Ruth wrote immediately. Ben tapped his pen against a thumb. Leah studied the heading, then wrote two lines and crossed one out.

Eve resisted explaining the task again.

Finally Ruth looked up.

"Do you mean a problem with the dog or a problem with the people?"

"Either, if it's about the everyday arrangements."

"Excellent. My dog is blameless."

Ben laughed. The room loosened a little.

Ruth's situation involved three adults feeding one dog. They had tried leaving messages, but one person checked messages after leaving for work, one muted the household chat, and Ruth assumed that if the bowl looked clean nobody had used it.

"It is always clean," she said. "He has professional standards."

Eve asked what happened on an ordinary morning, without names or explanations of anyone's character. Ruth described the order in which people left, where the food was kept, and the small scoop that migrated between cupboards.

Ben leaned forward. "We use a board by the kettle."

"For meals?"

"For everything we keep telling each other twice. Not very beautifully."

Leah asked whether people remembered to mark it.

"Usually. We put it where you have to move it to get at the tea."

Eve wrote that suggestion on the shared sheet, identifying it as Ben's household experience rather than a universal solution. Then she asked Ruth what would work with the way her household actually moved.

Ruth thought about the cupboard.

"A card on the food container. Turn it after feeding. Reset it together at night."

"Would everyone know what each side meant?" Eve asked.

Ruth picked up a blank card. "They would if I wrote words instead of drawing a dog, which I admit was my first plan."

They laughed, and Ruth wrote MORNING FED on one side. Eve fetched another pen when the first ran out halfway through the final word.

The broken pen was oddly helpful. This was not a perfect demonstration unfolding for a camera. Four people were finding something simple while an actual pen failed them.

When Ruth finished, she did not announce her household problem solved. She said she would show the card to the others before deciding how to use it.

Eve put that on the next-step sheet.

Ben's card concerned information that disappeared after appointments. He could remember the instruction while standing at reception, then find himself uncertain at home about which document contained it.

"I take things seriously," he said, as though the table might be preparing to disagree.

"What do you currently receive?" Eve asked.

He took a folded paper from his coat and stopped before opening it.

"There are details on this."

"You can keep them private. We only need the format, if you're comfortable describing it."

He put it away again. One clinic used printouts. A previous practice had sent email. His own notes lived in several places, usually written while holding a carrier or trying to pay.

Ruth described an envelope in a kitchen drawer.

"Highly advanced filing system," she said. "Sometimes I write on the envelope."

Leah preferred a folder on her phone. She showed them an empty example rather than her own records.

Eve drew three boxes on a spare sheet: received, questions still to ask, next action. She invited Ben to change them rather than admire them.

"Where does the appointment date go?" he asked.

"Where would you notice it?"

"Calendar. If it's only in a folder, I won't."

They added a line to copy dates into the calendar he already used. No new application, no complicated dashboard requiring a second evening to maintain.

Eve found herself enjoying the work. She could hear which question had become too broad and bring it back. She could separate a useful suggestion from somebody's urge to tell an entire life story. These were skills she used for clients, now sitting at a table with a cabbage beneath it.

At the halfway point she called the short break as advertised.

Ruth went for water. Leah stayed seated, turning her card between two fingers. Ben looked at the sheet they had made.

"I thought we'd be given a lot of general information," he said.

"Would that have been more useful?"

"No. I've already got a lot of that."

Eve let herself smile.

Then Leah said, "Will we be doing the story part after the break?"

"Yes. Your example is next, if you'd like to share it."

"I meant how to make the story work."

Eve looked at her card. She couldn't read it from where she sat, and didn't reach for it.

"For posting?"

"Partly. I wondered how you decide what people will watch."

There it was, a different event sitting at the same table.

Eve could have offered a few quick tips. She knew plenty. The temptation came partly from wanting Leah pleased and partly from relief at a subject where she could perform obvious expertise. But Ruth had paid for one kind of hour and Ben for the same one. Turning the rest into a content workshop would give Leah something while quietly taking it from them.

"I haven't planned content coaching for this session," she said. "We can talk after about whether the description gave the wrong impression. I don't want you waiting for something that isn't coming."

Leah's mouth tightened, not dramatically. "I did see stories."

"I used that word. Let me show you the format again when we finish, and we can sort it out."

Leah nodded. She didn't look happy, and Eve didn't try to make the nod mean she was.

Ruth returned with water and a question about where to put the empty cup. The ordinary interruption helped Eve breathe.

She took a sip of her own water. Three participants had brought three expectations, two of which she seemed to be meeting. The third could not be fixed by pretending she had a different room and another hour.

Ben was still studying his sheet.

"There is something else," he said. "About my cat. It might fit the questions section."

Eve glanced at the clock. They had time for his question before returning to Leah, if she kept the transition clear.

"What do you need to find out?"

He rubbed the edge of the paper between his fingers.

"He's been leaving some of his food. I thought perhaps the appointment notes would explain whether that's expected."

The conversation changed weight so quickly that Eve felt it before anyone moved.

Ruth lowered her cup. Leah stopped turning the card.

Eve put her pen down. On the table, the neat boxes she had drawn remained useful for organizing information. They did not make her qualified to decide what Ben's cat needed.
