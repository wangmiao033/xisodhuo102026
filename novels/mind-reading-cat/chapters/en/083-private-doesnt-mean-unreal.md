# Chapter 083 — Private Doesn't Mean Unreal

Sophie was standing in a changing-room doorway with one arm trapped in a jacket when Eve arrived.

“Don't help yet,” she said. “I need to know whether this is a design problem or a problem with me.”

Eve set her bag down and waited.

Sophie tried again. The sleeve stopped at exactly the same place.

“Design,” Eve said.

“I was hoping you'd reach that conclusion.”

They had arranged to meet on Thursday afternoon because Sophie needed a jacket she could move in at work, and Eve had finished the cut she owed the clinic. Neither had promised a productive outing. The shop offered a sale rail, two mirrors, and lighting that made every person look as if they had recently made a disappointing discovery.

Sophie escaped the jacket and handed it to the assistant.

“I need to reach above my head,” she explained. “Without bringing the rest of the jacket with me.”

The assistant found a different size in another cut. Eve sat on the small bench outside the changing room and tried not to refresh her messages.

Julian had answered that morning. He had said the accidental recording was dealt with and thanked her for not turning his refusal into an argument. She believed him.

Believing him had not stopped her mentally replaying the conversation beside the chair.

“How bad?” Sophie asked from behind the curtain.

“The jacket?”

“The thing you're looking at instead of the jacket.”

Eve locked the phone and put it in her bag.

“I forgot to stop recording yesterday. I caught something private with Julian. He didn't want it used, so I deleted it.”

Sophie emerged in the second jacket and raised both arms.

“And?”

“And I wish I hadn't forgotten.”

“That seems reasonable.”

“It looked very nice.”

Sophie lowered her arms, considering Eve in the mirror rather than staring directly at her.

“Did you tell him that as a reason to keep it?”

“No.”

“Good.”

The assistant returned to ask about the fit. Sophie turned, reached, and bent to pick up her bag. The jacket stayed where clothing ought to stay. Eve checked the back seam when asked and found that it pulled across the shoulders when Sophie carried her usual load.

They tried a third.

The practical interruption was welcome. Eve had a clear job for several minutes: tell the truth about a seam without making it about anybody's worth.

Sophie bought nothing. The only comfortable jacket was more than she wanted to spend, and the sale label did not change that.

Outside, she said, “Tea before I become angry with the whole concept of sleeves?”

“Please.”

They found a small place with a narrow counter and a table just large enough for two cups. Sophie put her work bag beneath her own chair, not in the space Eve needed for her feet.

Eve noticed the consideration and immediately wished she could stop turning every small act into material for a larger conclusion.

“Tell me about your rota,” she said.

Sophie blinked. “Now?”

“Yes. Last time you said there was still a problem.”

“There was. I raised it. They moved one late finish, but the other stays for this month.”

“Is that workable?”

“Less awful. Not ideal. I'm deciding whether the next booking is worth taking.”

She described the actual difficulty: the advertised finish time did not include returning borrowed equipment, and the journey afterward made the next morning unpleasant. She was asking for the return task to be separately allocated, not for a medal for surviving it.

Eve listened until the end. She did not offer herself as free transport before checking whether Sophie even wanted help.

“What would make the next one worth it?” she asked.

“A believable end time and enough pay to stop pretending the journey is a hobby.”

They laughed. Sophie stirred her tea, then caught the string from the bag before it slipped into the cup.

“Your turn,” she said.

Eve told her about the retailer's proposal. She described the fee, the dates requested, and the uncomfortable pleasure of seeing strangers call her and Julian a couple. She didn't show Sophie the deleted footage; there was nothing to show, and Julian's exhausted face did not need a second audience disguised as a trusted friend.

Sophie listened without interrupting.

“I know we aren't obligated to make a program out of it,” Eve said. “That part isn't difficult to explain.”

“What's difficult?”

Eve looked through the window. A person outside was trying to fit a long parcel into a bicycle basket. The parcel was winning.

“When he says he'd rather not put something out, a little part of me hears that he'd rather nobody knew about me.”

“Did he say that?”

“No. He said the opposite, more or less.”

“Then which part are you answering?”

Eve hated the question for approximately three seconds.

Not because Sophie had asked it cruelly. Because Eve knew the answer was not hiding in a missing screenshot.

“I think I want people to see it so it will feel harder to lose,” she said.

The admission sat between the cups, small and unflattering.

Sophie didn't rush to make it beautiful.

“I've had things everybody saw disappear anyway,” she said. “Jobs, mostly. An announcement isn't much help when the actual arrangement is wrong.”

“I know.”

“I don't mean you should never post a photograph with someone. I like photographs. I'd like one of myself in a jacket that doesn't hate me.”

Eve laughed despite herself.

“But if you want to know what he's choosing,” Sophie went on, “does the comment section have the answer?”

“No.”

“Have you asked him the question you actually mean?”

Eve turned her cup.

They had said date. They had arranged another one and kept it. He had asked to hold her hand. She had not invented those things. But there were other questions, larger ones, that she had not yet found a way to ask without turning them into a joke or a contract.

“Some of it,” she said. “Not all.”

“Then maybe don't ask the audience to answer the rest first.”

Outside, the person with the bicycle removed the parcel, rotated it, and discovered that a different angle did not make it shorter. Eve watched with unreasonable sympathy.

“I was hoping you would say I'm very sensible and this is all understandable.”

“You can be understandable without being sensible every minute.”

“An unpleasantly generous position.”

Sophie reached for the little biscuit beside her saucer.

Eve let the conversation quiet. She could feel how tempting it would be to tell the whole story again with better phrasing until Sophie supplied the reassurance she had originally wanted. Instead she asked whether her friend wanted to try another shop.

“One more. Then I accept that my existing jacket has another season of service ahead of it.”

At the second shop, Sophie found a plain jacket with no ambitious buttons. She lifted an empty display basket to imitate carrying equipment. The assistant looked concerned until Sophie explained.

Eve held her bag while she turned and reached. Nothing pulled.

“It looks good,” Eve said.

“It looks as if I can work in it.”

“Both things can be true.”

Sophie checked the price and the care label, then bought it. The decision took less time than the first jacket had taken to release her elbow.

At the counter, the assistant offered to take a photograph of Sophie in the jacket for the shop's customer page. Sophie declined with a pleasant shake of her head. The assistant said no problem and removed the little promotional card from the bag.

Outside, Eve asked whether she wanted a photograph just for herself.

“Not today. I look tired.”

“You look like you've found a jacket.”

“I have. You'll have to take my word for the achievement.”

Eve realized she had started to raise her phone before Sophie finished. She put it away, and they walked to the stop discussing which pocket would acquire the first forgotten receipt. The purchase remained an event in her friend's day without producing an image Eve could keep.

They parted at the bus stop. Eve offered no follow-up consultation about the purchase. Sophie had chosen it, paid for it, and looked quietly pleased.

Walking home, Eve took her phone out, opened Julian's conversation, and typed a sentence about being sorry again.

She deleted it before sending.

He had not asked her to keep performing remorse. She could change what she did and let the apology stand.

Instead she wrote: *Would you like to come by Saturday afternoon? Tea, and I want to show you something that isn't a work proposal. No filming.*

She read the final two words. They sounded almost pointed after yesterday. She changed them to a plainer sentence.

*I'd like an afternoon without work. Would that suit you?*

Then she sent it and continued walking rather than waiting in the middle of the pavement.

At home, Dumpling rose from the cushion and met her near the door. Eve put the closed bag away before crouching. He pushed his head against her hand, then withdrew to investigate a mark on the floor that had not mattered when she left.

“You missed a very important discussion about sleeves,” she told him.

He sniffed the mark.

She sat at the table and opened the private note where she had written about the dates. There was a sentence she had added after holding Julian's hand, a sentence nobody else had been asked to improve.

Reading it made her smile.

The smile was embarrassingly easy. She had been prepared to work much harder for one from a row of strangers.

She did not write a declaration about never needing approval again. Tomorrow she might care about a number under a post. Next week a kind comment might still make her happy. She liked an audience; she worked with audiences. That wasn't the part she wanted to lose.

She wanted not to make them witnesses whose applause could replace a conversation.

Her phone lit with Julian's answer. Saturday afternoon would work. He asked whether there was anything he should bring.

*Nothing necessary,* she wrote. *If you want to bring the book you mentioned, I'd like to see it.*

A moment later she added, *Not because you have to justify coming.*

His reply was quick.

*I understood. I would still like to bring the book.*

Eve laughed, set the phone down, and took out the little stack of photographs she had never properly sorted. Most were of places or accidental angles, not people. There was a picture of Dumpling's ear taking up almost the entire frame and one of a meal she had remembered to photograph only after eating half of it.

She could choose something to keep without deciding it represented the whole of her life.

At the bottom of the stack, an empty paper envelope waited for a label. Eve wrote the date on it, then stopped before adding anything grander.

Saturday would arrive without a title approved by a crowd. She was looking forward to it anyway.
