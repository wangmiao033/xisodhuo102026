# Chapter 146 — The Window Next Door

The notice went up while Eve was trying to persuade a broken umbrella to fit into the center's umbrella stand.

Across the narrow paved strip, a woman smoothed paper against the inside of an empty shop window. TO LET, it said, followed by a telephone number large enough to read without pressing one's nose to the glass.

Eve stopped wrestling the umbrella.

The unit had been a stationery shop before its owner moved to smaller premises. She remembered buying a pack of plain cards there and emerging with three colors she had not needed. Now the shelves were gone. Afternoon light lay on the bare floor all the way to a little partition at the rear.

The center coordinator followed her gaze.

“Finished clearing yesterday,” she said. “The landlord's looking for someone.”

“Do you know the rent?”

“No. But she's there now, if you want to ask.”

Eve looked at her watch. Twenty minutes until she could enter the borrowed room. Her materials were ready; the umbrella was the only failing part of the operation.

“I can ask a question in twenty minutes.”

The coordinator gave the umbrella a thoughtful look. “You could leave that here while you do.”

Outside, the rain had become a fine drifting damp. Eve crossed with her hood up and knocked against the window frame. The woman turned, opened the door, and caught the lower edge before it swung back.

“It sticks if you don't open it properly,” she said. “That's on the repair list.”

A sensible introduction to a potentially romantic place.

Eve explained her current work and asked about the unit. The landlord pointed out the main room, a small rear room with a door, and the sink. The previous tenant's counter had left a pale rectangle on the wall. Beyond the window was a shallow covered strip, wide enough for a person to pause out of the rain without entering the room.

Eve could picture six people around a table. Materials in a cupboard instead of under her desk. A place to leave a half-finished demonstration overnight.

“How often would you open?” the landlord asked.

“I don't know yet.”

Saying it cost her more than she expected. The room seemed to want a confident answer.

She explained the small sessions, the content work that still paid most of her bills, and the need to investigate before promising anything. Animals might occasionally attend activities with their owners, but it would not be boarding or a veterinary practice.

The landlord asked her to put the proposed uses in writing. She gave Eve the asking rent and the usual initial costs, then explained which charges were separate.

Eve wrote everything down. The rent was not impossible. The total made her pencil slow.

“Would you consider something short while I test whether it works?”

“Perhaps. It depends what you mean by test.”

“That's another thing I need to work out.”

The landlord smiled without reducing the price.

Eve took a photograph of the empty main room with permission, checked the time, and returned to her actual booking. She waited out the remaining seven minutes beside her box.

Once her room slot began, she put out chairs with unusual speed.

During the session, the picture kept returning at inconvenient moments: the table could stand near the front; she could make a clear wall for examples; people would not have to carry unfinished work away because a committee wanted the room at eight.

Then Ruth asked whether a document sleeve would fit a folded care note, and Eve came back to the table in front of her.

They tried it. It did not fit.

“Good thing we checked before buying twenty,” Ruth said.

Eve looked at the sleeve, then at the room around them.

“Yes,” she said. “Good thing.”

Afterward, she cleared on time and sent Julian the photograph with a short message asking whether he wanted to walk past after work. He replied that he could meet her for the walk, although he would be late enough that the unit would probably be locked.

She spent the interval drafting the proposed activities. By the time he arrived, her cautious paragraph had become a page.

They stood beneath the shallow cover while the streetlights began to show in the wet paving.

“You can see the back room from here,” Eve said, pointing. “Enough separation for storage, perhaps somewhere quiet when there's an animal visiting. We could put the information table near that wall.”

Julian leaned to look past a reflected streetlamp.

“And you could do the occasional talk there. Not consultations, obviously. But we'd have somewhere people knew to come. We could arrange the week around your clinic days.”

He straightened.

“Eve.”

She heard the change before she looked at him.

“I haven't agreed to run a venue.”

“I know. I'm only imagining.”

“You've put me on the schedule.”

The window gave her a faint double image of them, standing close together in front of a room she had already filled. Heat rose under her scarf.

“I meant if you wanted to.”

“Then ask me that.”

She folded the page until it was too small to read.

“Would you want to?”

“Not as a business partner. I want to keep being a veterinarian.”

“You would still be one.”

“At the clinic. Doing clinical work. I don't want a second job running a shop that people assume is part of the clinic because I'm standing in it.”

A bicycle passed behind them, tires whispering over the pavement. Eve stepped closer to the wall to make room, although there was already plenty.

She had imagined he might worry about the cost or suggest a slower start. She had not imagined that the room itself might simply be hers to want.

“Do you think it's a bad idea?”

“I think I don't know enough to tell you. I can like your idea without wanting that job.”

She looked through the glass again. The pale rectangle on the wall had become an old counter mark rather than the background of an opening photograph.

“I got excited,” she said.

“I could tell.”

“That isn't an excuse.”

“No. It's an explanation.”

His hand was beside hers on the folded umbrella. She wanted to take it and also wanted a moment without anything softening the awkwardness.

“Let's walk,” she said.

They left the window and went toward the shops that were still open. Eve kept noticing possible furniture through other people's glass, a habit her imagination refused to abandon merely because she was embarrassed.

At the crossing, Julian said, “There's something I was going to tell you over dinner.”

She turned too quickly.

“Nothing about this place,” he added. “I've been putting together an application for a short training opportunity out of town.”

The signal changed. They crossed before continuing.

“How far out of town?”

“Far enough that I would stay there for it. I don't have an offer or confirmed dates. There are arrangements to check with the clinic as well.”

“What kind of training?”

He explained the supervised work and the chance to learn with a different team. Eve understood some of the terms and asked about the others. He answered without making it sound like a brochure. One part involved work he did not often get to observe; another sounded difficult enough that he paused before describing it.

“You want to go,” she said.

“Yes. If the place and the arrangements work out.”

She had a brief, unreasonable vision of herself waving him toward a distant train while trying to unlock a shop with both arms full. No shop, no keys, no training offer. Her mind had managed an entire exhausting morning from information that did not yet exist.

“I'm glad you told me,” she said. “I might need to be glad about the rest more slowly.”

“All right.”

“Not because you shouldn't apply.”

“I didn't think that was what you meant.”

They reached a small grocery. Julian needed bread; Eve remembered she had no eggs. Inside, they separated at the narrow aisle and met beside a display of oranges, holding two baskets like people who had failed an elementary teamwork exercise.

“We could have shared one,” Eve said.

“We appear to have survived.”

She laughed, reluctantly at first.

While he checked the bread, she found herself thinking about his application instead of his absence. There would be forms, a selection process, colleagues whose names she did not know. He had work he wanted to become better at, as she did. She wanted to hear him speak about it without measuring every sentence against the floor area of her possible room.

At the till, they paid separately. Outside, she asked him which part he was most interested in learning.

He answered all the way to her building.

She had invited him to dinner before the viewing. That invitation still stood. Upstairs, Dumpling emerged from his bed and inspected Julian's shopping bag with greater immediate concern than he gave either human.

“Bread,” Julian told him.

Dumpling sniffed once, then turned toward Eve.

“Your disappointment has been recorded,” she said.

While the eggs cooked, Eve unfolded the page from her pocket. The creases cut through a sentence assigning occasional evenings to Julian. She crossed out the whole sentence, not merely his name.

He glanced over from the plates.

“You can ask me about a particular talk later,” he said. “When there's something real to discuss.”

“I will. Tonight I want to hear whether the application is horrible.”

“Parts of it are impressively repetitive.”

“Finally, a professional field I understand.”

They ate at her small table, and he described a form that asked for the same experience in three differently sized boxes. Eve told him about Ruth's uncooperative document sleeve. The room next door came up once, when she asked whether he would look at a question about visiting animals after she had a better proposal. He said yes, within what he could sensibly assess.

Later, when he put on his coat to go home, Dumpling lifted his head but did not abandon the folded blanket on the chair.

Eve kissed Julian by the door.

“I'm sorry I put you in the business without asking.”

“Thank you for taking me out again.”

“You remain invited to dinner.”

“That position suits me.”

After he left, she opened the photograph. The empty unit was still attractive. Its window was still better than the borrowed room's overhead lighting, and its small rear room still offered possibilities she wanted to investigate.

She made a new heading above her notes: A place for the work.

Below it she wrote the rent, the separate charges, and the questions she needed answered. Then she added a reminder to ask Julian how the application was going, on a different page.

There was room for both things. They did not need to fit inside the same window.
