# Chapter 193 — How Much Each Pays Is Not an Exam

Eve brought two months of business figures to the kitchen table and immediately wished she had brought something less revealing. A photograph of herself asleep on the bus would have been easier. The figures had no flattering angle.

Julian moved the fruit bowl to make room. It was his kitchen this evening; Dumpling was at home, and the absence of a paw on the paper left Eve without an excuse to delay.

“Tea?” he asked.

“Yes. Then let's do it before I decide the fruit needs rearranging.”

He put the bowl on the counter, safely out of her reach.

They had the proposed rent, ordinary bills from the agent, and their own recent expenses. No one had promised to hold the apartment while they thought. Eve had read that sentence three times before coming over. A home was apparently allowed to disappear while sensible people became sensible enough to rent it.

Julian set down his mug. “If this one goes, we can keep looking.”

“I know.”

“You look as though we're taking an exam that closes at nine.”

“I'm hoping for partial credit for bringing a pen.”

She uncapped it. His own notebook was already open, his usual monthly income written at the top. Eve looked at the number, then at the smaller and less obedient numbers on her page.

She knew he earned more. Seeing the difference beside the price of the same front door made it strangely physical.

“The venue money isn't all available to me,” she said.

“I didn't think it was.”

“Some of it looks like it is, if you only look at what comes in.”

“Show me what you actually take home.”

She drew a line beneath the business costs. Robin's paid hours stayed above it, along with space hire, supplies, and the amount she kept back for quiet weeks. She refused to make their prospective home affordable by quietly making someone else's work free.

Julian leaned closer, following her pen rather than reaching for it.

“This part changes quite a lot,” she said. “This was a good month. This one was a month in which people asked lovely questions and bought fewer things.”

“Which should we use?”

“Neither on its own.”

She turned to the average she had worked out, then the lower figure she felt able to commit to regularly. Saying it aloud required more effort than doing the calculation.

Julian wrote it down without a visible reaction.

Eve waited for one anyway.

He lifted his eyes. “What?”

“Nothing.”

He glanced at her neatly aligned papers. “You can tell me if I'm getting this wrong.”

“You haven't done anything.”

The kettle clicked as it cooled. She wished it would boil again and require attention.

“I hate the idea of moving somewhere I can only stay because you keep choosing to be generous,” she said at last.

His hand stopped beside his mug.

“That's not how I would think of it.”

“I know. It's still how it feels when I see those two numbers.”

Julian considered the page. “I could cover more of the rent.”

“How much more?”

“Enough that you don't have to worry about it.”

Her pen made a small hard mark beside the total.

“There isn't an amount that does that.”

He sat back. For a second his expression closed, and she recognized the familiar difficulty of offering a useful thing and watching it fail to be useful.

“I wasn't trying to buy my way out of the conversation,” he said.

“I don't think you meant to. But we still need the conversation.”

“Yes.” He looked down, then added, “I would quite like a number to solve it. That's probably the problem.”

That admission loosened something. Eve put the pen down rather than pointing it at him.

“I'd like to pay half,” she said. “I also want to keep eating and paying people properly.”

“Both ambitious goals.”

“And I'd rather tell you that now than become increasingly weird about the price of tomatoes.”

“You already have strong tomato opinions.”

“Those are agricultural, not financial.”

He smiled, but neither used it as permission to leave the difficult part behind.

They tried equal shares first. Eve insisted on seeing the result instead of being protected from it. With the realistic income figure, the remaining margin was too thin. One slow month would turn every pleasant purchase into a small private alarm.

She looked at the total for a long time.

“I could take more editing jobs.”

“Do you want to?”

“I want the number to change.”

Julian nodded toward his own notebook. “Then we're doing the same thing from opposite ends.”

They crossed out that version.

The next version divided the ordinary shared costs in proportion to what they could reliably bring home. Julian would pay more. Eve's part remained a real amount she could plan for, rather than a ceremonial contribution he could wave away.

She wrote the amounts herself. Then she worked backward to check what each of them would have left.

“Your personal spending stays yours,” she said.

“So does yours.”

“Even if I buy an extremely unnecessary lamp.”

“Does the lamp require us to move the bookcase?”

“It might.”

“Then we discuss the bookcase. Not your entire moral character.”

Eve laughed into her tea.

They left their existing accounts separate. Ordinary shared bills would have dates and a short record they could both see; each would transfer their agreed share without needing permission for every unrelated purchase. Her business reserve stayed with her business. His professional expenses stayed with him. Neither became a mysterious household debt.

When they reached furniture, Julian looked around his own kitchen.

“I have enough plates.”

“You have five.”

“That's enough for two people.”

“One is a promotional plate with a dog-food logo.”

“Still structurally a plate.”

They made a second list, much shorter than Eve had feared: things they actually needed, things they already owned, things that could wait. Two households did not require a third set of everything simply because the third set matched.

The sofa question took longer. Julian's would fit the plain apartment, according to their measurements, but it would need moving. Eve disliked its sagging left cushion. He disliked spending money to replace a sofa they had not yet tried living with.

“Fine,” she said. “We try it. I'm reserving the right to complain.”

“You've already exercised that right.”

“I believe it renews monthly.”

They added the cost of moving, the deposit, and the possible overlap between their existing tenancies and a new one. The total grew large enough that both stopped joking.

Eve had money put aside. Using some of it was different from pretending it did not exist, but she wanted enough left to leave a bad situation without having to ask the person in it for permission.

The thought made her uncomfortable. Saying it made her palms damp.

Julian listened without interrupting.

“I want that for you too,” he said.

She looked at him.

“Not because I expect us to fail. Because I don't want staying to be something either of us can't afford to stop doing.”

She had imagined having to defend the idea. For a moment, relief was almost embarrassing.

“Thank you.”

“I still hope you stay.”

“I know.”

He reached across the space between their notebooks. She took his hand, briefly, before the tea could cool completely and make this feel like the conclusion.

There was still arithmetic to do.

Halfway through it, Julian got up to make dinner. Eve heard him open the freezer, pause, and shut it again.

“What?”

“I forgot to move the soup down this morning.”

She joined him in front of the frozen container. It was an impressively solid object.

“We could order,” he said.

“Or make the eggs you were standing in front of.”

He stepped aside. “I thought you might be tired of eggs.”

“I'm tired of deciding what eggs say about us.”

That made him laugh properly. She took a pepper from the drawer while he found a pan. They cooked with their papers still covering half the table, and ate on the clear corner without ceremonially dividing the cost of each ingredient.

For several minutes they talked about a customer who had asked Eve whether the venue's smallest chair was antique. It was not. She had watched Robin tighten its screws last month.

The ordinary conversation did something the carefully reassuring sentences had not. Eve could still be funny here. He could still forget his own dinner. Her smaller income had not transformed her into a guest waiting to be treated kindly.

Afterward, she rinsed their plates while he wiped the table. When she returned to her chair, the difficult page was still there, but she could look at it without holding her breath.

They checked the one-time costs against money each could use without emptying those private reserves. They marked which items belonged to one of them already and which would be shared purchases, with receipts kept rather than remembered heroically in a future argument.

Julian suggested buying the larger bookcase himself.

“You already own a large bookcase.”

“I know. This is how I discovered I don't need another.”

She wrote nothing in that box.

The proposed apartment remained possible, though not effortless. They would need to avoid a long, unnecessary overlap. They would also need to accept losing it if the timing or written terms did not work. Neither gave notice on an existing home that evening.

Eve read the final page aloud, slowly enough to catch a bill they had counted twice. Julian found the error and looked so cautiously pleased that she pushed the notebook toward him.

“You're allowed to enjoy being right about subtraction.”

“Thank you. I've been saving it up.”

He corrected the total. The savings were small. Her relief was disproportionate.

They agreed to try a week together at Eve's, with her ordinary work schedule intact and his own home available. She had checked that a short visit was permitted. Dumpling could stay in the place he knew; Julian would bring enough clothes for the week, not arrive carrying a silent decision in a moving box.

The trial would not answer every question. It would answer some questions they could not discover over tea in a tidy kitchen.

Before she left, Eve folded her business pages separately from their shared sheet.

Julian noticed. “Want me to keep a copy of the household one?”

“Yes. Not all the others.”

“Okay.”

No injured silence followed. He photographed the agreed page, checked that the numbers were readable, and handed it back.

At home, Eve found Dumpling asleep on the chair where she normally put her bag. She moved the bag to the floor instead. He opened one eye, found the arrangement acceptable, and closed it.

She placed the folded household sheet beside her keys. It contained two different contributions, an unglamorous allowance for cleaning supplies, and a crossed-out charge for internet they had accidentally counted twice.

Nothing on it measured how much either of them loved the other.

For once, she did not ask it to.
