# Chapter 156 — The Nap Upstairs

The complaint arrived while Eve was congratulating herself on how quiet an empty room could be.

She had placed a felt pad beneath the last chair leg and moved the chair experimentally across the floor. A small, civilized whisper replaced the previous scrape. She was wondering whether the sound deserved its own line in the preparation notes when someone struck the front door three times.

The person outside wore a dark sweatshirt, a coat without its belt, and the expression of somebody who had already had this conversation in their head.

“Are you in charge of the pet place?”

Eve set the chair down.

“Yes. Although we aren't open regularly yet.”

“Could you stop?”

“The chair?”

“The banging. The deliveries. People calling to each other outside.”

Eve felt herself straighten. Behind her, the room was mostly empty. Dumpling was at home. Nobody had barked, yowled, or shaken a wet coat over a stranger all morning.

“We're not going to have groups of animals here every day,” she said.

The person blinked.

“I didn't ask about animals.”

“No, but if that's your concern—”

“My concern is that I was asleep.”

They pointed upward, toward the windows above the neighboring entrance. One curtain was drawn tightly against daylight. Eve had noticed it before and imagined someone protecting a screen from glare.

“It's almost eleven,” she said, and heard the mistake as it left her mouth.

“Yes,” the person said. “I got home at eight.”

A delivery van passed behind them. Its loose cargo rattled over a patched strip in the road. The person closed their eyes until it had gone.

Eve stepped back from the doorway.

“I'm sorry. I assumed something you hadn't said.”

They did not become friendlier simply because she had apologized. They looked past her at the chairs, the measuring tape, and a flat box leaning against the wall.

“How long is this going on?”

“Today's access slot ends at one. The chair collection is booked before then.”

“That's not what I mean.”

She understood. Her two hours and the future of the room were entirely different questions. She knew the first answer precisely because it was hers. The second involved people who had never been given a chance to see her hopeful calendar.

“I don't have the regular timetable settled,” she said. “I can give you the next booked preparation times. And ask before the noisy work is arranged.”

“Ask who?”

“You, if you'd be willing to tell me when it causes the most difficulty. And the landlord.”

The person rubbed the bridge of their nose.

“I'm going back upstairs. Please don't ring the bell now.”

“I won't.”

They left without giving a name. Eve watched them reach the other entrance, then went inside and closed the door gently enough that it failed to latch.

She had to open it again and close it properly.

The second sound seemed extraordinarily loud.

For a moment she stood with her palm on the glass. She had imagined complaints. She had imagined defending the value of a place for owners who needed somewhere modest and useful. In those imagined conversations, she had been patient, articulate, and eventually persuasive.

She had not imagined telling a tired person what time of day it was.

Her phone vibrated. The furniture supplier had sent a message confirming that the collection team was running early. They would arrive in twenty minutes.

Eve called the number. She asked them to telephone her when they arrived rather than shout through the door, and explained where the permitted loading point was. She would have the items ready near the inside wall. Nobody would need to drag them across the room.

The dispatcher said she could add the note but could not guarantee the driver would read it while finding the address.

“Then I'll meet them,” Eve said.

She lifted the two hired chairs one at a time. They were not heavy, but holding the second awkwardly made her shoulder ache. She lowered it, changed her grip, and continued. The first version of her preparation schedule had counted moving furniture as something that happened between actual tasks. The furniture appeared unaware of its minor status.

When the van arrived, she was outside waiting. The collection took six minutes. One worker apologized for a dropped loading strap; Eve told him the apology wasn't necessary and helped keep the entrance clear.

There was no way to make work produce no sound. She could stop adding careless sound to it.

Afterward she called the landlord.

The woman listened, then said, “The upstairs tenant?”

“I think so. I didn't ask their name.”

“Alex. They work shifts. I was going to raise the regular timetable when we met.”

“I should have asked earlier.”

“We both should have.”

Eve appreciated the sentence enough to stop preparing a defense.

The landlord would not give out Alex's phone number. She offered to pass along a short note asking whether they wanted to discuss preparation times. Eve agreed. She wrote the note without mentioning her business concept, rescue animals, or how much the room meant to her.

It said she was sorry for waking them. It listed the next two permitted access windows and identified which activities would involve deliveries. It asked whether a short conversation at a time Alex chose would help.

Before sending it to the landlord, Eve deleted the sentence promising that she would make sure it never happened again.

She could not know that. It sounded generous only because she would not be the person lying awake if she failed.

Alex replied through the landlord that evening. They could speak the following afternoon, after three, for ten minutes.

Eve put the time in her calendar.

At home, Dumpling woke when she opened a cupboard. He stretched, stepped down from the sofa, and approached the sound of a possible meal with no interest in anybody's rotating schedule.

“You're very fortunate,” she told him.

He inspected the cupboard and seemed to disagree.

She fed him at the usual time, then spread her draft timetable on the desk. Four brightly colored blocks filled the first week. Beneath them were delivery times in smaller print and a line reading setup as needed.

That line could contain almost anything.

She crossed it out.

The following afternoon, Eve climbed the neighboring stair with a single sheet of paper and no food gift. She had considered bringing pastries. Then she imagined being woken again by someone who wanted forgiveness to come with a plate.

Alex opened the door wearing ordinary clothes and looking less pale. Behind them, Eve could see a mug, a folded blanket, and several pairs of work shoes near the wall. A small radio played quietly somewhere inside.

“I'm Eve.”

“Alex. You can come in. I have to leave in fifteen minutes.”

“I'll be ten.”

Alex moved a magazine from a kitchen chair. Eve sat and kept the paper folded until they reached for it.

“I wasn't trying to object to your entire business yesterday,” they said.

“I know. I supplied that part myself.”

“I like cats.”

“You don't have to.”

Alex looked at her, then laughed once.

“That is a relief. I thought I might have to produce a photograph.”

Eve smiled, and some of the stiffness left her shoulders.

Alex's shifts rotated. They could not provide the same sleeping hours every week, and they did not want to explain their full work rota to a shop downstairs. What they could identify was the particular difficulty of sudden repeated impact sounds beneath the bedroom after a night shift.

“Ordinary talking isn't the worst of it,” they said. “You hear it and it passes. The chairs kept stopping and starting. Every time I thought you'd finished, something else fell.”

Eve remembered unpacking the chair pads. She had dropped a small metal tin twice while balancing it on a stack.

“Some of that was me.”

“I guessed.”

She unfolded the schedule. Rather than promise silence, she had separated deliveries, furniture work, and quiet preparation. The next week still had three possible daytime slots. The landlord had not agreed to all of them.

Alex touched the earliest one.

“That one is bad for me this week. The later one is fine. Next week I don't know yet.”

“Would knowing the unavoidable noisy times a few days ahead help?”

“Yes. If they remain those times.”

“And if something changes?”

“Tell us before the van is outside.”

Us. Eve looked toward the row of shoes and realized she had been imagining a single person alone upstairs. She knew almost nothing about the household. She did not need to invent it in order to stop waking it.

They discussed an information notice near the shared entrance, where occupants could read planned delivery windows without being asked to join a promotional mailing list. Alex did not offer personal contact details. Eve did not ask again.

Then Alex took her to the small room over the unit's rear wall. The curtain was open now. From the window, the street looked different: the loading space was directly below, and voices carried upward between the buildings.

Eve heard someone on the pavement call a name. The syllables reached the room distinctly.

“Oh,” she said.

“Yes,” Alex replied.

That was the most useful part of the visit. Neither of them needed a larger speech afterward.

Downstairs again, Eve called the supplier of a remaining storage fitting and moved its delivery inquiry to the later window, subject to the landlord's agreement. The available slot cost a little more. She wrote down the difference rather than treating courtesy as free.

She also took the morning rehearsal off the proposed timetable. It could remain an afternoon rehearsal or not happen that week. She did not replace it with an equally noisy task under a softer name.

Julian called while she was waiting for the revised confirmation.

“How did the visit go?”

“I learned where the bedroom is.”

“That sounds useful.”

“It was. I also learned that I can turn a stranger into an opponent in less than a minute if I'm holding a business plan.”

“Did you stop?”

“Eventually.”

He asked whether she still wanted to meet later. She did. She told him she needed another half hour to finish the timetable first, then would put it away.

Before leaving, she placed the revised preparation notice where the landlord had approved. It was plain black text on white paper. No cat face, no cheerful launch slogan, no promise that everyone would soon love the new place.

At the top she wrote the actual dates.

She kept a photograph of the notice with her working schedule, so there would be one version to correct rather than several differently optimistic promises. The next delivery was still an inquiry, and she marked it that way. A passerby stopped to read, discovered nothing was being advertised, and continued without smiling. Eve found that oddly reassuring. The paper could do its small job without making anybody enthusiastic.

Upstairs, the curtain was closed again.

Eve carried her bag away without calling goodbye toward the window.
