# Chapter 160 — After Receiving the Keys

The key stuck on Eve's first attempt.

She looked at the landlord. The landlord looked at the key.

“Lift it a little as you turn.”

Eve tried again. The lock released with a small, undramatic click.

“We should write that down,” she said.

“We already have.” The landlord tapped the handover sheet. “Front lock stiff. Adjustment booked.”

It was there, beneath the rear catch and above the meter reading. Eve had read the sheet twice. Apparently becoming the person holding the keys had temporarily removed her ability to recognize words.

Inside, the room smelled faintly of floor cleaner and cold plaster. Without the borrowed chairs or anyone's coat over a seat, it looked both larger and less promising.

The landlord set her bag on the folding table.

“Shall we do the photographs first?”

“Yes.”

They worked through the room slowly. The mark beside the cupboard, the scuff at the entrance, the repaired catch. Eve photographed the meter display while the landlord read the numbers aloud. Then they compared them with the sheet rather than assuming the camera had caught everything.

A strip of sunlight crossed the floor exactly where she had once wanted the green sofa.

Now she could see how much of the route it would have occupied.

The signed agreement was in her folder. Three months, with the agreed review and a written route out rather than an optimistic promise to work things out later. The hours were narrower than the first proposal. The permitted use and the shared passage arrangements were recorded. Any use of the porch remained subject to the specific permission they had discussed, not something she acquired merely by paying rent.

She had paid for the document check and asked her uncomfortable questions. Some answers had changed what she was willing to sign. Others had simply made the costs harder to avoid looking at.

The landlord hadn't enjoyed every question either. They had still answered them.

“Two sets,” the woman said, putting the second key beside the first. “Keep a record if you lend one out.”

“I will.”

“You're not opening this weekend?”

“No. There's preparation and a small invited rehearsal first. I'll give you the dates.”

“Good.”

The landlord checked the window latches, showed Eve the shutoff she was permitted to use in an emergency, and repeated which building contact to call for faults. Eve wrote the numbers in the premises folder. She didn't rely on remembering the conversation just because it felt important.

At the door, the landlord paused.

“I hope it goes well.”

“So do I.”

“That sounds less confident than your first email.”

Eve smiled. “I know more now.”

The landlord laughed, then left her alone with the keys.

For several seconds Eve didn't move.

She had imagined this moment often enough to have ruined it in advance. There should have been a friend with a camera, a perfectly timed message, a rush of certainty that all the preceding hesitation had been necessary and was now over.

Instead her stomach growled.

She had forgotten lunch.

She took a wrapped sandwich from her bag and sat on the one ordinary chair she had brought that morning. The seat gave a small creak. She froze, then laughed at herself and ate.

Outside, someone wheeled a suitcase past. Upstairs, a tap ran briefly. The building continued being a building around her new beginning.

Her phone lit up with a client asking whether she could change the order of two sections in tomorrow's edit.

She read the request, checked the agreed scope, and replied that the change fitted within it. She would send the revised cut by the arranged time.

For a moment she resented the message. Surely this one afternoon could belong entirely to the keys.

Then she looked at the payment date in her notebook. Tomorrow's work had a very direct relationship with next month's rent.

She finished the sandwich and put the wrapper in the bag she had brought for her rubbish. The room did not yet have all the bins it needed. Ownership of a key had not supplied them.

There were three things she could safely do before leaving: measure the clear storage space again, label the temporary supply box, and take a photograph for herself.

The supply box was an old one with a cracked corner, still strong enough for cloths and paper but too awkward for anything heavy. Eve pressed a label over the name of a former client and wrote the contents beneath it. Halfway through, the pen ran dry. She found another in her bag and finished in a different color.

She nearly peeled the label off to begin again. Then she tested whether it could be read from where the box would stand. It could. She put the remaining labels away and spent the extra minute clearing the route to the cupboard instead.

She did the photograph last.

At first she stood near the entrance, trying to make the room look generous. The wide angle stretched the window and made the folding table seem miles away. She recognized the trick immediately. It was the kind of image she could produce without thinking.

She turned the setting back and took one ordinary photograph at eye level.

An empty room. A chair. The pale rectangle where a previous notice had been removed.

She did not add a caption or upload it.

The picture went into a private folder beside the rejected sofa measurements and the first rough budget. It belonged with the attempts that had brought her here, not in front of a public promise she wasn't ready to make.

A knock sounded on the glass.

Alex stood outside with a grocery bag, eyebrows lifted in a question. Eve opened the door.

“Official?” they asked, looking at the keys in her hand.

“A small trial. Three months.”

“Congratulations.”

“Thank you.”

Alex glanced at the bare room. “Very spacious without people.”

“I'm trying to preserve some of that effect when people arrive.”

They smiled. Eve gave them the preparation dates she actually had, including the invited rehearsal. No early chair collection this time. Any change affecting noise or access would come in a separate message, rather than disappear inside a stream of promotional updates.

“That's helpful,” Alex said. “I may still forget which day it is.”

“I'll put the dates on the inside notice too.”

They went upstairs. Eve watched the shared door close properly, then returned to her labels.

Her next message was to Sophie. Not a request for a free afternoon, just a photograph of the keys and the words: It is real and slightly stiff.

Sophie replied with congratulations and a question mark about the adjective.

Eve explained the lock.

Then she asked about hosting the rehearsal they had discussed, giving the proposed date, hours, and fee. Sophie replied that her new programming project occupied that week. She couldn't do it, but she knew a host who might suit a small paid trial.

For one brief, unreasonable second Eve felt abandoned.

She stared at that feeling until it became recognizable as disappointment rather than evidence against her friend.

Please introduce us, she wrote. And tell me how your project is going when you have a free evening.

Sophie's reply included Robin's name and a promise to check whether they wanted the introduction before passing on details.

Eve put the phone down. The next version of the room would contain people who had not been present for its first version. That was part of making something that could function beyond her closest friendships.

She checked the window latches, turned off the lights, and practiced locking the door from outside.

The key stuck again. She lifted it, turned, and pulled gently on the handle to check.

Then she walked away.

At home Dumpling met her with the loud indignation usually reserved for a dinner that existed only in the future. Eve put the keys on the table and dealt with his actual meal before attempting any ceremony.

He ate. She washed the spoon and looked at the little empty flowerpot Julian had given her.

She had thought she might carry it over immediately. Now she left it on the shelf. The room needed a supply box and a reliable closing routine before it needed to look finished.

When Julian arrived after work, she held the keys out on her palm.

“There.”

He smiled. “There.”

“You could be more eloquent.”

“I was admiring them.”

“They are excellent keys. One requires encouragement.”

He bent to kiss her, then asked how the handover had gone. She told him about the photographs, the sandwich, the lock, and the strangely powerful desire to announce something enormous.

“Did you?”

“No. I answered a client email.”

“That sounds like you have a business.”

She groaned. “Don't say that. I wanted it to sound like a beginning.”

“It can be both.”

They sat on her sofa, with Dumpling attempting to occupy the space between their knees. Eve showed Julian the private photograph.

He looked at it for a long time.

“What?” she asked.

“I can see where you wanted the sofa.”

“You're meant to say it looks wonderful.”

“It looks like a room you can change your mind in.”

She looked at the clear floor again. That was better than wonderful, though she wasn't going to reward him by saying so immediately.

He handed back the phone. “Are you doing anything there tomorrow?”

“An hour after the edit is delivered. Supplies, if the payment arrives as expected. Nothing public.”

“Dinner afterward?”

“Yes. Somewhere I don't have to measure the tables.”

“I'll try to find a place with distracting food.”

She leaned against him. For a few minutes she let the conversation wander into his day, a book she hadn't finished, and whether Dumpling's new preference for a cardboard box represented a rejection of every purchased bed in the flat.

Later, when Julian had gone home, Eve opened the client project.

She had promised herself she would leave it until morning. Then she remembered the two sections that needed moving and decided to set the files in order rather than begin a late-night revision she would regret.

She checked the source clips, marked the requested change, and closed the program. The work would take concentration. It deserved her rested attention, not whatever remained after she had exhausted herself congratulating her own courage.

Beside the computer she placed tomorrow's short list. Deliver the edit. Check payment. Buy the necessary supplies. Stop before the agreed access hours ended.

The keys lay next to it, attached to an old plain ring she had found in a drawer.

She picked them up once more. They felt smaller than the decision they represented and heavier than the photograph had made them look.

Dumpling jumped onto the chair she had just vacated.

“Don't start without me,” she told him.

He tucked his paws beneath his chest, unimpressed by either the venture or its financing.

Eve switched off the desk lamp. Tomorrow already had work in it. Tonight, she could sleep.
