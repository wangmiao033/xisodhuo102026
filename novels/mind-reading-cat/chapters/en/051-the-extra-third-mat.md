# Chapter 051 — The Extra Third Mat

The third mat was the problem.

Eve had put it in the carrying bag after the second, then removed it because there were only two people going to the film. Five minutes later she had put it back. Someone might need one. The grass might be damp. The second mat might suffer a sudden failure of its ability to lie on the ground.

Dumpling climbed into the bag while she was considering these possibilities and flattened the neatly rolled corner beneath his stomach.

“That is not your reservation.”

He closed his eyes.

On the bed lay two jackets, a cardigan, and the shirt she had already decided against twice. Her camera remained on the desk. Julian had been clear about that part of the invitation, and she had no difficulty leaving it. It was the absence of everything the camera normally gave her to do that had caused the trouble.

She could spend an evening deciding where to stand, what to ask, and whether the sound was usable. Sitting beside someone who had asked her simply to come was proving less straightforward.

Her phone lit up.

*I have cups and a flask. See you by the east gate at six forty?*

She wrote *Great, I have mats* and did not add a number.

Dumpling opened one eye when she crouched beside him. Eve offered the little fabric mouse he sometimes liked, dragging it along the floor rather than waving it over the bag. After watching with extravagant doubt, he climbed out and followed it three steps. The moment he stopped, she picked up the bag and put it by the door.

He returned to the bed. She took that as a useful change of location, not permission to go.

Before leaving, she checked his water and the room she had aired earlier. The window was secured, his usual food had been dealt with, and the blanket on the chair still smelled of home rather than outdoor grass. He would spend the evening somewhere familiar. There would be loud speakers at the film and strangers moving in the dark. Taking him along to make the outing less awkward for her would be a poor arrangement for a cat.

She chose the cardigan, changed to the warmer jacket, and left before the bed could suggest another option.

At the east gate, the first person she recognized was a woman she had once interviewed about a water bowl outside her shop. Eve automatically reached toward the empty place on her shoulder where her camera strap usually rested.

The woman waved and continued toward a group carrying folding chairs.

That was all. Nobody asked what she was making. Nobody expected her to preserve the evening.

Eve arrived eight minutes early and occupied herself reading the notice beside the gate. The neighborhood association was showing an old comedy on a temporary screen. Chairs were available toward the back; the front lawn was for mats. The small stall beside the path sold drinks and two kinds of cake. A separate sign requested that people keep the paved route clear.

She read that sign twice, then moved her bag off the route.

Julian appeared with a flask in one hand and a folded dark-blue scarf tucked through the strap of his small shoulder bag. He was wearing neither clinic clothes nor the slightly distracted expression she associated with finding him between appointments.

“Hello,” he said.

“Hello.”

They stood looking at each other for a second too long to be a practical greeting.

“You found the gate,” Eve added.

“It was where they said it would be.”

“Good. I like an event that starts honestly.”

His smile arrived slowly, making her suddenly pleased she had not filled the whole silence.

They walked toward the lawn. A volunteer directed them around a cable cover; Julian thanked her and waited while a couple maneuvered a stroller past. Eve saw three potential improvements to the route and kept them to herself. Nobody had hired her to diagnose the placement of a cake table.

“Near the back of the mats?” he asked. “Or closer?”

“Near the back. I like being able to leave without stepping on a row of strangers.”

“An underrated cinematic feature.”

They chose a place with a clear view between two low groups of chairs. Eve opened the carrying bag, pulled out the first mat, and then the second.

The third rolled out after them.

Julian looked at it.

“I brought a spare,” she said immediately.

“In case one of us becomes taller?”

“For flexibility.”

He picked it up before it could roll down the slight slope.

“Shall we keep it rolled until flexibility becomes necessary?”

“Yes.”

He set it beside the bag without asking whom she had thought might join them. Eve knelt to straighten her mat. It resisted lying flat, curling at the end like an object determined not to appear too willing.

Julian placed the flask on one corner while she smoothed the other. Their hands nearly met; she moved hers to the carrying strap and discovered she had untied the wrong knot.

She had worked complicated events with less difficulty.

“Do you know the film?” she asked.

“Bits of it. I don't think I've seen the whole thing.”

“I read the description.”

“Useful?”

“Apparently a man inherits a house, loses a suitcase, and encounters trouble. It doesn't specify whether the trouble is the house.”

“It often is.”

They settled, shoes still on the edge nearest the path. The grass was dry at the surface but cool underneath. Eve shifted her legs until the mat stopped creaking. Julian unscrewed the flask and poured tea into two cups that fitted inside one another.

“No milk in the flask,” he said. “I wasn't sure. There's a little carton if you'd like some.”

“Tea without is fine.”

He passed her a cup. She held it with both hands and breathed in the mild steam.

“Do you always come this prepared?”

“You have three mats.”

“I'm conducting a survey, not inviting comparisons.”

He sat with one knee bent, studying the gathering crowd. “I used to arrive without anything warm because I didn't want to carry it. Then I'd spend the whole film thinking about my hands.”

“And now?”

“Now I carry things and complain about that.”

Eve laughed. The sound came out louder than she intended, but the people next to them were arguing about whether they had packed forks. Nobody looked over.

She had brought no food beyond a packet of crackers. At home, a sandwich had seemed sensible. Here, she wondered whether she should offer the crackers before he discovered she had prepared seating for an expedition but failed to arrange a meal.

“Have you eaten?” he asked.

“Yes. You?”

“Before leaving.”

A whole imagined set of duties fell away.

She put the crackers between them anyway. “These are recreational.”

“Excellent. I was worried they came with instructions.”

For a while they watched a child attempt to fold a blanket into a smaller rectangle. Each attempt made it longer. His grandfather finally sat on one end, and the dispute resolved itself through the ordinary application of weight.

Eve felt her phone vibrate inside her pocket. Her hand went to it before she remembered that she had finished the day's agreed work. The message could wait until later unless it came from the person whose call she had left enabled. She let her hand rest on the mat instead.

Julian was looking at the screen, where the association's logo had appeared sideways.

“They'll fix that,” Eve said.

“I assumed the film required us to lean.”

The logo turned upright. A small cheer rose from the chairs.

She could have made a little clip of that. The thought arrived automatically, then passed. Instead she asked Julian what kinds of films he actually liked, since old comedies had apparently only been partly covered.

“Things where I can miss five minutes and still know who people are.”

“That rules out most of my mysteries.”

“And radio plays with towns called Tom.”

She remembered the story he had told over tea and smiled into her cup.

He liked travel documentaries in which someone showed the train station instead of pretending every village had materialized untouched by transport. Eve liked old costume dramas partly for the rooms, though she admitted she spent too much time wondering who cleaned the chandeliers.

“Someone outside the story,” Julian said.

“They never get a scene.”

“They'd probably ask why everyone keeps running through the house with candles.”

“That is exactly the scene I want.”

The conversation wandered to poorly designed kitchens, to a holiday apartment where every cupboard had opened into another cupboard door, and then to a board game Eve had once misunderstood so thoroughly that everyone else had let her win rather than explain it again.

“What were you collecting?”

“Roads.”

“Were you supposed to?”

“Not in the quantity I had achieved.”

He laughed, not the brief courteous breath she sometimes heard him give visitors, but a low, startled sound that made him look away as he swallowed his tea.

Eve looked toward the screen before she could start examining what it meant.

Friends could laugh together. Work partners could attend a film. There was no need to choose a title for the evening while it was still happening.

But work partners didn't usually make her forget what she had just been about to say.

The light thinned above the park. A breeze slipped along the lawn, moving the edge of her jacket away from her wrist. She rubbed her hands together once.

“Are you cold?” Julian asked.

“A little at the wrists.”

He nodded toward the bag. “Would the spare mat help underneath? There's still room.”

She looked at the rolled mat, rescued from uselessness without any ceremony.

“Yes, actually.”

They stood long enough to spread it crosswise beneath the other two. She tucked her hands into her sleeves when she sat down again. Julian looped the blue scarf around his own neck and poured a little more tea into her cup after she said she would like it.

He hadn't told her she should have worn something else. He hadn't made taking his scarf the answer to a question she had not asked. He had simply noticed the cold and helped with something she had brought herself.

The volunteer at the front tested the microphone. Everyone heard a single enormous cough, followed by an apology that was much quieter.

Eve rested the warm cup against her knee. Julian leaned back on his hands, shoulders no longer held at the angle of somebody about to get up for work.

On the screen, the first title appeared.

She stopped thinking about the spare mat.

Beside her, he shifted just enough that their jacket sleeves touched when the wind moved them. Neither moved away. Eve kept her eyes on the opening scene, in which a man with an impressive suitcase was already boarding entirely the wrong train.
