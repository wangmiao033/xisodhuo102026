# Chapter 228 — The Curtain in an Old Photograph

Eve was looking for a photograph in which neither of them was holding equipment.

There were fewer than she expected.

On the laptop, Julian appeared beside a folding table, behind a stack of chairs, and at the edge of a frame with half his face obscured by a cardboard sign. Eve appeared as an elbow, a reflection, and the person someone had asked to take the picture.

“We have documented our careers thoroughly,” she said.

Julian leaned over her shoulder. “There was the day without a camera.”

“A strong artistic decision. Poor archival consequences.”

They wanted a few private photographs for themselves, not a public engagement montage. A photographer whose work Eve liked had offered a small, paid portrait session on a Saturday they could both manage. They were considering the booking separately from any wedding coverage. Looking through their own pictures was helping them describe what they liked without presenting a catalogue of poses to reproduce.

Eve closed an image containing somebody else's private exercise sheet. That one wasn't theirs to use just because their faces happened to appear behind it.

Then a notification arrived from Leanne.

The neighborhood center had finally sorted another box of old event photographs.

Eve stopped moving the mouse.

The earlier inquiry had never entirely disappeared. It had become one of those small, unfinished strands she checked when there was something to check, rather than a job she could finish by trying harder. Leanne had sent occasional updates. Most contained no new information. This one included a preview from a charity-sale setup, supplied with permission to compare the location and ask a limited question.

Eve opened the attachment.

There was a table without cups on it yet. Beside it stood a woman carrying a stack of folded fabric. Behind her, a blue door occupied the familiar edge of the image.

And beside the door hung a short curtain with three uneven dark stripes and a pale patch at the lower corner.

Eve leaned closer.

“That's the room.”

Julian looked at the screen.

“The temporary one?”

“I think so. Let me find the other photograph.”

She didn't begin with the cat. She opened the wider original sale image Leanne had supplied before, the one whose border and reverse had finally given them an address and a date. Cups crowded the foreground. At the edge, beyond the blue door, a narrow fold of striped material was visible beside the high window.

Eve had barely noticed it then. The door, the writing, and the orange-and-white shape had mattered more.

Now she put the two images side by side.

The curtain's lower patch appeared in both, partly hidden by a stack of trays in the older scan. The window frame and the wear low on the door matched too. They weren't comparing two blue doors simply because blue was a hopeful color.

Leanne's message said the newly scanned print belonged to the same sale album. The former photographer had allowed the relevant setup image to be shared privately. The center wasn't granting permission to republish a box of strangers' faces.

Eve checked the reverse image Leanne had included. Its date matched the sale leaflet already in her records. The handwriting identified the event, not the cat and not a complete list of everyone who had been there.

“It adds another view,” Julian said.

“Yes.”

Eve enlarged the woman holding the fabric, then stopped before the picture dissolved into squares.

“Not an identity.”

“Not by itself.”

She sat back.

The temptation was already assembling a story. A curtain, folded blankets, a woman standing near the place where a cat had once waited. Eve could imagine someone making a bed behind the door, someone collecting him when the room closed, someone who might explain why luggage still sometimes disturbed him.

The photograph did not show any of that.

It showed fabric held in two arms.

Eve replied to Leanne with the facts she could compare: matching room features, matching event information, and a question about whether the woman could be identified by somebody who actually knew her.

She added that she would like an introduction only if the woman wanted contact.

Then she saved the images privately alongside their source notes, rather than placing them among the couple's portrait examples.

Julian touched her shoulder.

“Do you want to keep looking tonight?”

“I want a person to step out of the picture and tell me everything.”

“That may take longer.”

She laughed softly.

“No. We've sent the question.”

They returned to choosing their own photographs. Eve found one from an ordinary outing, slightly crooked, in which Julian was looking at her instead of the camera. She remembered what he had said just before it was taken. The memory did not require every part of the day to be visible.

They put that one in the small folder for the photographer.

The next morning, she went to You, Me & Paws before opening and checked the display materials with Robin. One reused backing board had a ragged edge where the old scenery had been cut down. Robin suggested covering it with a plain strip rather than replacing the whole piece.

Eve held the strip while they checked the alignment.

“Too high,” Robin said.

She lowered it.

“Now you've covered the instruction.”

“An increasingly abstract service.”

They fixed it on the third attempt.

Work steadied her. People would come that afternoon to improve an ordinary object or explanation. She could help them without resolving every place a cat had slept before meeting her.

At lunch, Leanne replied.

She remembered the woman as Nora, who had helped with short-term animal care during part of the room's use. Leanne was careful about the wording. She had known her through the center, not followed her whole life. The photographer also recognized Nora but couldn't say from the image what duty she had been doing that day.

The stack in her arms was table linen.

Eve stared at that sentence, then laughed at herself in the empty office corner.

She had almost turned a tablecloth into a bed.

Leanne offered to send Nora the brief inquiry through a contact she still used. She would ask first whether the number was current and whether Nora wanted to hear from Eve. She had not handed it over with the photograph.

Eve thanked her and gave permission to share her own contact details for that purpose.

She wrote a concise account of what she knew: the recorded rescue, the clearer images Daniel had supplied, the room-use dates, and the gaps. She did not attach every picture or suggest that Nora ought to remember because Eve had waited a long time.

She included one current photograph showing Dumpling's markings clearly.

Before sending, she reread the sentence I think you looked after my cat.

She changed it to Leanne thinks you may be able to help me check an earlier period of his care.

The second sentence was less intimate. It was also something the evidence could support.

A knock came at the door before she finished filing the message.

A customer had arrived early for the afternoon exchange and wanted to know whether the drawing exercise required artistic skill. Eve put the phone away and showed them the deliberately terrible example of a bag on the sample card.

“That should reassure you,” she said.

“It may reassure me too much.”

They laughed. Eve explained the actual exercise, where to leave a coat, and when the session would begin. The person had come for the service she offered, not for a suspenseful update on her cat.

She didn't check her phone during the session.

Afterwards, while clearing the table, she noticed how much easier it was to imagine kindness in a photograph than to organize its small, repeated tasks. Someone had washed the table linen. Someone had unlocked the room. Someone had taken a turn they could manage, perhaps without knowing where the next turn would happen.

None of that excused a failed handover, if one had happened. It simply made her less eager to find a single person to hold the whole story.

That evening she opened the old room notes at home.

Ada's documents had established the temporary use, the changes of access, and the return of keys. They had never recorded individual animals. Daniel had fed a cat whose photographs and dates fitted Dumpling. He had not claimed ownership. The former coordinator had handled room bookings, not supervised every transfer.

Eve wrote the new information beneath those limits rather than replacing them: setup image from the same sale album; room features consistent; Nora identified by two people who knew her; possible care contact awaiting consent.

She also kept the unaltered scans separately from the enlarged comparison she had made. Cropping helped her see the curtain, but it removed the table and the edge of the room that had allowed the comparison in the first place. She labelled the copy as a close view, not a new photograph. If she needed to ask somebody about it later, they deserved to see the whole modest piece of evidence rather than the conclusion she had framed most attractively.

There was still no line saying first home found.

Dumpling climbed onto the chair beside her and watched her pen. She moved the papers beyond his reach before his curiosity could become a footnote across the original scans.

“Someone may remember you,” she told him.

He looked toward the kitchen.

“Not a request for you to remember them.”

Julian came in carrying two glasses of water.

“Any answer?”

“Not yet. We have a name. And I've learned that fabric isn't automatically a poignant blanket.”

He read the short note she showed him, with the contact details left out of view because he didn't need them to understand the update.

“Nora may remember only a small part,” he said.

“I know.”

Eve put the pen down.

“I hope it's enough to tell us something. I don't want her to feel she's failed if it isn't everything.”

“Then that's worth telling her, if she wants to speak.”

They left the folder on the shelf rather than open on the table all night.

Later, when Eve checked her messages once before bed, Leanne had sent a brief update. Nora had received the inquiry and was willing to consider a call. She wanted to look through her own few pictures first and would reply about a time.

Eve answered with thanks and said there was no need to hurry an answer that depended on old material.

Then she put the phone away before she could begin counting the hours her own sentence had offered.

Across the room, Dumpling settled into the familiar cardboard box. He pushed the blanket with one paw until the fold suited him and lowered his head.

The curtain in the photograph remained in a room they could identify. The person beside it had a name now. Neither fact reached through time to explain the cat asleep in front of her.

For tonight, Eve had somewhere to place the next question, and someone who could choose whether to answer it.
