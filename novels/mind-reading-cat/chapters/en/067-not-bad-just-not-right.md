# Chapter 067 — Not Bad, Just Not Right

Owen gave Eve the corner piece without making a performance of it.

He also noticed that the jug was sitting on the edge of the table and moved it inward before her mother's elbow reached it. These were minor, sensible actions. Eve disliked how grateful she felt for the absence of an obvious reason to dislike him.

“So,” her mother said, “Owen works with buildings.”

“Building services,” he said. “Mostly figuring out why something very expensive won't do what the brochure promised.”

“That sounds familiar,” Eve said.

“Your mother said you worked in advertising.”

“Content planning. There's overlap.”

“Used to,” her mother added, placing vegetables on Eve's plate.

“I still plan content.”

“Independently,” Owen said, as if supplying the word might help them both.

“Yes.”

He asked what sort. Eve described the clinic film, choosing the concrete version: helping an older owner explain how she prepared questions, making sure the resulting story still sounded like her.

Owen listened. He asked whether the camera work was hers too. When she explained Claire's role, he nodded rather than immediately proposing that a phone could do everything now.

“That sounds quite skilled.”

“It is,” Eve said.

Her mother looked at her, and Eve realized she had answered too quickly.

“I mean, Claire's part is. Mine requires other things.”

“Knowing what to ask?”

“Among them.”

For several minutes they talked easily. Owen described a hotel where the guest notices had been translated so badly that visitors were instructed to notify reception if their rooms became dissatisfied. Eve laughed, then suggested the rooms might have legitimate complaints.

Her mother smiled at them over her glass.

The smile made Eve suddenly aware of where she had put her hands.

Owen asked about the neighborhood where she lived. He worked in the same city and knew the difficult crossing near the clinic. He sometimes visited his mother in this area, which explained why accepting lunch had not involved an elaborate trip solely to inspect Eve.

“I thought this was less formal,” he said when her mother went to fetch the bread.

“So did I.”

“I'm sorry.”

“You didn't arrange it.”

“No. But I knew it was an introduction.”

“Thank you for saying that.”

He nodded, looking uncomfortable without trying to make her reassure him.

Her mother returned with the loaf Eve had brought, now cut into slices thick enough to support a small shelf. She put the board between them.

“Owen has a very steady job,” she said.

“So I hear.”

“I like it,” Owen said. “Mostly. The steady part is useful.”

Eve liked that he didn't pretend security was beneath him. She had liked knowing what would arrive in her bank account too. Losing it had not cured her of wanting to pay bills comfortably.

“Do you want to stay in it?” she asked.

“Yes. Perhaps move toward managing projects, but nothing dramatic. I want ordinary evenings.”

“That sounds reasonable.”

“It doesn't make a thrilling conversation.”

“Neither do most thrilling people, after a while.”

He smiled.

Then her mother said, “That would do you good, Eve. Ordinary evenings.”

Eve set down her fork.

“I have some.”

“With the laptop open.”

“Sometimes.”

“And that cat walking over everything.”

“Usually beside everything. He's particular.”

Owen looked interested. “How long have you had him?”

Eve gave the short account of finding Dumpling and taking him for treatment. She left out private guesses about his past and every strange thing she could not explain.

“Your mother showed me a picture,” he said. “He looks comfortable.”

“He has a gift.”

“Do you make money from the account?”

“Some work comes through it. The account itself isn't a reliable wage.”

“That must be stressful.”

“It can be.”

He cut a piece of vegetable, considering his next words.

“At least it's something you can keep doing when you go back to regular work.”

Eve felt the conversation shift by a fraction, like a chair settling onto an uneven tile.

“If I go back,” she said.

“Yes, of course. I meant it's portable.”

“It is.”

Her mother reached for the water. “She gets very defensive about it.”

“I wasn't defending it.”

“You said if.”

“Because I haven't decided.”

The silence that followed was small enough for everyone to pretend it belonged to chewing. Eve took another mouthful and wished the rice were less good. A bad meal would at least have made the discomfort simpler.

After lunch, her mother asked Owen to look at the kitchen tap. Eve nearly intervened on principle, then heard him agree and remembered that adults could choose to examine a tap without a tribunal.

He turned the handle once, listened, and said he could see why it was annoying but wasn't the right person to repair it.

“Buildings don't make me a plumber,” he said.

Her mother looked disappointed.

“I can help you confirm the appointment number,” he offered. “But I wouldn't start taking it apart.”

Eve found herself liking him more.

That was inconvenient, although she wasn't sure why she kept expecting the afternoon to become convenient.

They carried plates into the kitchen. Her mother insisted on washing them while they took tea in the little sitting room. The arrangement was transparent, but refusing tea would not make the introduction unhappen.

Owen chose the armchair. Eve sat at the end of the sofa, leaving her bag by her feet.

“I'm not always this awkward,” he said.

“I wouldn't put that in writing. Somebody might test it.”

He laughed, then looked down at his tea.

“Can I ask something properly?”

“Yes.”

“Are you interested in meeting anyone at the moment? I don't mean me specifically. I don't want to spend an afternoon being polite if you've already said you don't want this.”

Eve thought of the garden wall on Thursday. Julian's surprised face. Her own ridiculous gratitude for the word no.

“I wasn't looking for an introduction,” she said. “That isn't quite the same as not wanting to meet anyone.”

“All right.”

“What are you looking for?”

He took his time answering. Outside, somebody was reversing a car with the window down, receiving contradictory advice from two people on the pavement.

“A settled life,” Owen said. “A home that isn't temporary. Someone who wants to build that fairly soon. Not immediately, obviously.”

“Obviously.”

“I'm not handing out forms.”

Eve smiled. “Good. I left my references at home.”

He smiled back, but continued seriously.

“I like knowing where things are going. Work, money, family. I don't need everything exciting.”

“Neither do I.”

“Then perhaps we aren't very different.”

Eve looked toward the kitchen doorway. Her mother's washing-up had become unusually quiet.

“We might mean different things by settled,” she said.

Owen waited.

“I don't know whether this work will support me long-term. I have to find out without pretending the risk doesn't exist. But I want to give it a real attempt, not keep apologizing for the months before I become sensible again.”

“I didn't mean you're not sensible.”

“I know.”

“I'd worry about relying on uncertain income if we were planning a household.”

“That would be worth discussing. With someone I was actually planning one with.”

His face colored slightly. “I've jumped ahead.”

“A little.”

“Sorry.”

Eve accepted the apology. It did not erase the difference.

Owen glanced at the photograph on the shelf, then back at her.

“I suppose I'd always assumed one of us might need to simplify things when family responsibilities got bigger. Less freelance work, fewer demands at home.”

“What would you simplify?”

“My hours, if I could. I don't want to be away every weekend.”

“And the cat?”

He hesitated.

“I wouldn't ask someone to get rid of a pet.”

“That's good.”

“But I couldn't make every decision around one either. I've never really wanted animals in the house. I could put up with one, I think.”

He said it without contempt. He was trying to be generous about a life he did not particularly want.

Eve pictured Dumpling in his drawer, taking up no more space than one ridiculous cat could occupy, and realized she did not want someone who could just about put up with that life.

She also did not want to promise Owen she would soon become the person who felt relieved to stop making owner films.

“I don't think we're looking for the same things,” she said.

Owen sat back.

“We've only spoken for an hour.”

“I know. But we've said quite a lot.”

“People compromise.”

“They do. I don't think either of us should start by assuming the other's work and home are a stage they'll grow out of.”

He turned the cup in his hands.

“I didn't mean it like that.”

“Then I'm glad. But I still don't feel that this is right for me.”

The words left her with nowhere to hide. She could not blame her mother for that sentence. It belonged to her.

Owen was quiet for a while.

“I can respect that,” he said finally, though he looked disappointed.

Her mother appeared with a plate of sliced fruit at exactly the wrong moment.

“What can you respect?”

“An answer,” Owen said.

Eve looked at him, grateful and sorry, neither feeling strong enough to change it.

They ate a few pieces of fruit. Conversation returned unevenly to the train service and the ridiculous price of replacing a small part on a washing machine. Nobody threw a plate. Eve discovered that an uncomfortable afternoon could end without proving somebody had behaved monstrously.

While Owen washed his hands, her mother touched Eve's sleeve.

“You don't have to decide everything today.”

“I haven't decided everything. I've answered this.”

“He's trying.”

“So am I.”

Her mother looked toward the bathroom, lowering her voice further. “I don't want you to dismiss somebody because he isn't exciting.”

Eve wanted to explain that she had found the practical admission about plumbing more attractive than any promise of excitement. Instead she took her coat from the hook.

“I listened to him,” she said. “Please listen to me too.”

Owen returned before her mother answered.

At the door, her mother packed leftovers despite Eve's insistence that she had enough food at home. Eve took them because the rice was good and she wanted it.

Owen left a few minutes after her. She had almost reached the station when a message arrived from her mother: the gray scarf was still on the hook.

Eve stopped beside the ticket machine.

She could go back, but the next train would then be gone. Before she answered, her mother sent another message saying Owen would be near Eve's neighborhood on Sunday for an errand and had offered to bring it, if she wanted.

Eve asked for his number rather than letting her mother settle the arrangement.

*Only a handover,* she wrote to him. *Around midday would work. If it's inconvenient, please leave it there and I'll collect it another time.*

His reply was brief: *Midday is fine. I'll message before I come. No second lunch attached.*

She thanked him, then sent the building address and the agreed time. There was no reason to make returning a scarf into either a romance or an insult.

On the train, she held the warm container on her knees. She had declined the life Owen described. She could still acknowledge that he had given her the corner piece.
