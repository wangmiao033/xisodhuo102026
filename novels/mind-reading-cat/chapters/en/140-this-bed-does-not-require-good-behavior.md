# Chapter 140 — This Bed Does Not Require Good Behavior

The first usable take ended with tea spreading across the table.

Eve saw Dumpling's white foot, the tipping mug, and the front edge of her paper backdrop lifting beneath him. Then the mug hit the rug with a soft, expensive-sounding thud that proved, when she picked it up, to be neither expensive nor fatal to the mug.

The printed cards were less fortunate.

“Oh, no. No.”

Dumpling sprang onto the chair. His ears went sideways.

Eve stopped reaching across him. She moved the dry camera away, checked that the mug hadn't broken, and gave the cat a clear route to the floor. He took it at once, disappearing around the end of the sofa.

The tea was cold. Nothing electrical was wet. The problem consisted of paper, a stained cloth, and forty minutes she had already spent getting the hand movements right.

It was still a problem.

She stood in the middle of it and said one short word she would not include in the client's captions.

The film demonstrated changing cards inside a simple tabletop holder. No animal had been booked, encouraged, or needed. Eve had chosen her dining table because the daylight was good there, with a clear route left to Dumpling's usual places. He had been asleep when she started.

Then a delivery van had stopped outside. He had climbed down from the window, used the chair to reach the table, and walked directly across her careful arrangement. She had moved the mug out of the camera's frame without moving it out of the cat's possible route.

That distinction now occupied half the tabletop.

Eve fetched towels. The thick printed card drank the liquid faster than she could blot it. One blue line blurred into a pale cloud. She lifted the holder, dried its base, and laid the damaged pieces on the counter.

Dumpling watched from the gap beside the sofa.

She wanted him to look sorry. The wish was so obviously useless that it annoyed her almost as much as the spill.

“You can come out,” she said, more quietly.

He stayed where he was.

She did not crawl after him with an explanation. She cleaned the wet rug, checked the chair and floor for anything he might lick or catch a paw on, and put the dirty cloth where he couldn't reach it. Then she sat down to examine the footage.

The first takes had one flaw each. Her sleeve hid the opening. The card caught at a corner. The focus softened just as the important motion began. The last take had finally shown the process clearly, until a large orange shoulder crossed the frame and the world became tea.

Eve leaned back.

The deadline was tomorrow afternoon. She had enough time to finish, provided she didn't spend the rest of today arguing with the accident.

She checked the spare cards.

Two survived. She needed four.

A call to the print shop established that they could make the replacements from her existing file that afternoon. She booked the small order, added the cost to the job's expenses, and changed the filming plan. The final sequence could be done the next morning. Today's remaining daylight would cover the empty holder and the close details, once the table was dry.

The client didn't need a dramatic report. The agreed delivery time hadn't changed.

Eve carried the mug to the sink. Only then did she notice Dumpling had emerged.

He stood beside the bed she had mended, looking toward her.

She crouched at a little distance and held out her hand. He approached slowly, sniffed her fingers, and pushed his forehead against her knuckles.

“I am annoyed,” she told him. “You're still getting lunch.”

He sneezed.

It was not the profound response the occasion might have received in a better-written household.

She washed her hands, gave him his usual meal, and ate something herself. The two plates of food had nothing to do with how much usable footage existed. Sitting at the dry end of the table, Eve could feel both her irritation and the familiar pleasure of hearing him eat.

After lunch she put the replacement printing order confirmation in the work folder. She didn't turn the spoiled take into a comic post to recover the expense. It stayed with the unused footage, where a practical record of a failed camera setup belonged.

She also moved her drinks to the kitchen counter.

The adjustment lacked glamour, but it would probably help.

At the print shop, the assistant held up the fresh sheet before cutting it.

“Same size as before?”

“Exactly.”

“Usually people say that and then change it.”

“Today I have a very strong commitment to the previous size.”

She checked the color and dimensions, paid, and carried the cards home inside a rigid folder. The errand cost her an hour, including the queue. She had hoped to use that hour on a separate proposal. Now the proposal would wait until the time she had left free the following morning.

There was no clever way to make all the work fit without moving something.

When she returned, Dumpling met her at the door. He sniffed the folder and followed as she placed it in the closed cupboard. The work bag went away too, its handles tucked inside.

She checked his water and finished the shots that didn't require the printed cards. This time the set occupied less of the table. The mug remained in the kitchen, where it performed admirably without appearing in any frame.

By evening the room looked like her flat again.

Eve brought out the fabric mouse and dragged it across the rug. Dumpling watched, waited until her wrist began to ache, and pounced from an entirely different direction than the one she expected.

“Good to know your timing remains selective.”

He carried the mouse toward his bed, dropped it halfway, and returned to see whether she intended to make it move again.

She did.

The following morning, she finished the demonstration without an incident worthy of reporting. Dumpling slept through most of it. At the sound of the camera case closing, he raised his head but didn't get up.

Eve sent the cut on time. The client requested a clearer final caption, which she supplied. Nobody mentioned the damp afternoon that had preceded twenty seconds of competent instruction.

For the next several days, she went out when her work required it and returned with the ordinary evidence of a life outside: printing, groceries, a book she had finally renewed. She kept Dumpling's things in their familiar places. Sometimes he followed her to the door. Once he tried to investigate the wardrobe as she put her bag away. She waited for him to come out before closing it.

There was no steady line of improvement to admire.

On Thursday, Julian came for an early supper before they went for a short walk. Eve had invited him two days earlier, explaining that she wanted to leave the flat after a long editing session. He had suggested a route past the small park. They would eat at hers, walk, then go to their separate homes.

He arrived with bread and a story about buying the wrong replacement light bulb.

“You checked the fitting?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“The brightness?”

“Yes.”

“What went wrong?”

“It doesn't fit inside the shade.”

Eve laughed while making space on the counter. “A dimension they should mention.”

“They did. I chose not to experience it.”

She kissed him, then asked him to slice the bread while she finished the food. Dumpling came to inspect the bag and lost interest when nothing in it was offered for assessment.

Over supper, Eve told Julian about the failed shoot. Not as a confession. She had been annoyed, spent more than planned, and finished the job.

“Did the mug survive?” he asked.

“It has suffered no professional consequences.”

“A fortunate mug.”

“I'm considering putting it on probation.”

Julian glanced toward the kitchen counter. “It appears to have been relocated.”

“Management acted quickly.”

Dumpling climbed into his bed while they cleared the plates. His repaired cover had acquired a shallow crease where he liked to knead it. Eve noticed, with pleasure, that the seam was holding.

She left the dishes to soak and put on her coat.

Julian checked his phone for the time, then put it away. They had about half an hour before he needed to leave for his own flat. The walk would be short enough to enjoy without watching the clock at every crossing.

Eve took her keys from their usual place. Dumpling lifted his head.

She waited for the movement toward the door that usually followed.

He looked at Julian's shoes. Then at her bag, which was small and closed, with nothing dangling from it. His ears turned toward the soft clink of the keys.

He remained in the bed.

Eve's hand stopped on her coat fastening.

Julian looked from her to the cat. She could see him notice too, but neither of them spoke loudly or went closer.

“He's staying there,” she said.

“So far.”

“I know.”

She didn't put the keys down and pick them up again. She didn't ask Julian to open the door twice. They had somewhere to go, and Dumpling had somewhere to lie down.

Eve checked the usual things once: water, clear floor, closed window. Then she said her ordinary goodbye and went to the door.

At the last moment she glanced back.

Dumpling had lowered his chin onto the blanket. His eyes remained open. He was watching them, but his paws stayed tucked beneath him.

She left before the moment became something she needed him to maintain for her.

Outside, the air felt pleasantly cold on her face. Julian waited while she put on her gloves, then offered his hand.

Eve took it.

“I'm trying not to make an announcement,” she said.

“To whom?”

“My entire internal committee.”

“Give them something else to do.”

She looked across the street. “There are three different signs for the same roadworks.”

“A substantial agenda.”

They walked to the park and discovered the nearer gate was shut. The longer route used most of their available time. Eve would once have felt obliged to rescue the outing with a different destination. Instead they looked through the railings at the leaf-covered path, complained mildly about the sign, and turned back.

At the corner where their routes separated, Julian kissed her and reminded himself aloud to take the failed light bulb back tomorrow.

“Bring the shade,” Eve suggested.

“Then I'll have two objects to misunderstand.”

She was still smiling when she reached her building.

Dumpling was in his bed when she opened the flat door. He might have wandered, eaten, watched the window, and returned while she was away. She hadn't seen that part.

She had seen him let them leave.

Eve hung up her coat, put her keys away, and finished washing the dishes. Behind her, he stretched one front leg over the edge of the bed without rising.

Tomorrow she had client messages to answer and another piece of work to plan. Tonight the table was clear, the mug was back on its shelf, and the mended seam held beneath a sleeping cat.
