# Chapter 206 — Thank You Does Not Wash Dishes

The saucepan was still in the sink on Tuesday evening, wearing a ring of dried rice that looked more permanent than anything Eve had managed at work.

She put her bag down beside the table. A page of the contractor's written update protruded from it. The venue's rear wall needed another drying check. The front room was ready for Wednesday's three visitors. The pan, apparently, was ready for a different tenant.

Julian came out of the bedroom carrying an empty mug.

“How was today?”

“Small tasks arranged themselves into one enormous task.”

He nodded and put the mug beside the pan.

Eve looked at it.

He looked at her looking at it.

“I'll wash it,” he said.

“When?”

The question arrived with more edge than she had intended. Julian's shoulders moved almost imperceptibly.

“After I've sat down.”

She had pictured coming home and sitting down too. Somewhere between the doorway and the sink, the picture had changed without her consent.

“There isn't a clean pan for dinner.”

“We can have something else.”

“Which something?”

“I haven't looked.”

That was the sentence that did it. Not the mug, not the rice, not the fact that he had already been home for forty minutes. Eve opened the refrigerator with unnecessary force and saw half an onion, cooked vegetables, and the container they both kept treating as though its contents would announce a plan.

Julian stayed near the table.

“You've held everything together this week,” he said. “I don't know how you do it.”

“I don't want to be good at this.”

He blinked.

“I meant thank you.”

“I know what you meant.”

She shut the refrigerator. A little bottle rattled against another bottle, a ridiculous percussion section for a serious argument.

“You thank me, and then I still have to decide what we're eating. And remember whether we have washing liquid. And check what's going off. And ask when the pan will stop being an archaeological site.”

“I took the clothes off the rack yesterday.”

“Yes. You did.”

“And cooked.”

“We cooked.”

“Fine. We cooked.”

His tone hardened. Eve could hear herself beginning to construct a case, selecting evidence and rejecting anything that weakened it. She knew the feeling from difficult client calls. It was not a method she wanted to bring home. Knowing that didn't immediately stop her.

“I've been handling repairs all day.”

“I've been working too.”

“I didn't say you haven't.”

“You said I've been sitting here while you do everything.”

“No, I didn't.”

They stared at each other, both correct about the exact words and doing very badly with everything around them.

Dumpling appeared in the doorway. His ears turned toward Eve, then Julian. He paused, walked around the table, and disappeared beneath the far chair. Eve lowered her voice. He did not need to become an audience or a judge.

“I don't want to have a competition,” she said.

“Neither do I.”

“I am having one anyway.”

Julian pulled out a chair but didn't sit.

“I came home with a headache,” he said. “I lay down. I wasn't saving the washing-up for you.”

The information shifted something without removing her anger.

“Are you still feeling bad?”

“Less. I should have told you.”

“Yes.”

He sat. Eve remained beside the refrigerator, arms folded, suddenly aware that she had begun the evening by demanding a schedule from a man whose face she hadn't properly looked at.

She unfolded her arms.

“I could have asked how you were.”

“You did ask when I'd wash the mug.”

“That isn't one of my finest expressions of love.”

“No.”

The answer was dry enough to let her breathe, but neither of them laughed yet.

Eve filled two glasses of water. She put one near his hand and took the chair opposite. From there, the pan was hidden by the edge of the counter. The work did not disappear. It merely stopped shining at her while she tried to speak.

“When you say you don't know how I do it,” she said, “I hear that you expect me to keep doing it.”

“I don't.”

“Then something has to move. An entire thing. I can't be the person who knows everything that's waiting and hands out pieces when someone asks.”

Julian looked down at the glass.

“I said I'd clear up last night,” he said. “Then I decided the pan could soak. This morning I saw it and thought I'd deal with it after work.”

“And I thought you had forgotten.”

“I hadn't forgotten. I kept moving it.”

“Without telling me where.”

He nodded.

That was closer to the actual problem than either of their accusations. He had been carrying an intention around all day. She had been looking at the thing the intention hadn't changed.

“When you came home feeling ill, you could have said the plan had failed,” she said. “Then we'd have worked something out. Instead I walked into the same kitchen and thought tomorrow would look exactly like this too.”

“I can see that.”

She rested her hands flat on the table. The need to make him agree with every adjective began to loosen.

They had discussed money, cupboard space, and these ordinary jobs when they moved in. Eve had undertaken food planning and the cat's appointments. Julian had taken laundry and household supplies. The agreement was recent enough that she could remember the cups on the table when they made it. That made the wet towels and empty bottle more frustrating, not less. They had already found sensible words. Now those words needed to survive a difficult week.

She couldn't honestly say Julian did nothing. He bought groceries, cleaned, fed Dumpling, and remembered appointments she forgot. The trouble was less visible than a division into useful and useless people. On busy days, they both retreated to the tasks they knew, leaving the uncertain pieces for whoever reached them first.

Eve usually reached the uncertain pieces while worrying about something else.

But she had helped produce tonight's uncertainty too. Food was her agreed area, and she had left the meal unplanned without saying so. She had hoped he would notice her exhaustion and take over, then resented him for not receiving the request she hadn't made.

“I didn't plan dinner,” she said. “I should have told you earlier I couldn't face it. Instead I came in looking for something to be angry at.”

“The pan was very cooperative.”

“It has a gift.”

He looked toward the sink. “I still left it there.”

“Yes.”

She was relieved he hadn't used her admission to close the subject. She wanted to give him the same room: his headache could be real without making every unfinished job invisible, and her difficult day could matter without becoming proof that his had been easy.

“What is the worst part tonight?” Julian asked.

She nearly said the whole thing.

“Having to invent dinner before I can stop thinking.”

“All right. I'll do dinner.”

“You have a headache.”

“It's eased. I can make something simple. If it gets worse, I'll tell you and we'll change the plan.”

“And the pan?”

“Also mine tonight.”

She nodded, then caught the word tonight sitting between them. A special rescue could get them fed without changing next Tuesday.

“I don't just mean this meal.”

“I know.”

He rubbed his forehead once, thinking.

“The laundry is already mine,” he said. “And the household supplies. I agreed to the whole job. I let the final part collect on that chair, then stopped seeing it.”

“I know you remember the agreement. I need it to happen.”

“Even the thrilling final episode where the towels reach a cupboard.”

She looked toward the chair where two clean towels had been living since Sunday. Another layer in the geological record.

“I can leave them,” she said.

“You can also tell me if I shrink your jumper.”

“I will tell you at length.”

They agreed on no heroic redistribution of every chore that night. Julian would finish the laundry already assigned to him, including the towels on the chair, and check the household supplies he had also undertaken to manage. Eve would handle the next grocery order and meals, as agreed, but choose simple food rather than inventing a nightly audition. They would tell each other before an agreed task failed, rather than silently waiting for the other person to notice.

It sounded unimpressive when spoken aloud. Eve distrusted that a little. Part of her wanted an agreement large enough to justify the argument.

Then Julian stood, emptied the cold soaking water from the pan, and began again with hot water.

She opened her mouth to explain the quickest way to loosen the rice.

Closed it.

He had washed pans before meeting her.

“Is there anything in the refrigerator you need for tomorrow?” he asked.

“The small container is my lunch. Everything else is available.”

“Good.”

She took her bag into the bedroom. On the bed, she spread the contractor's pages, then gathered them up again. There was no repair decision due that evening. Tomorrow's exchange was prepared. The business phone had a clear message and an actual route for urgent building matters. She put the papers in their folder.

From the kitchen came running water, a drawer opening, and Julian saying something quietly to himself about a lid.

Eve lay down on top of the blanket.

Dumpling appeared after a few minutes and climbed onto the lower corner, keeping enough distance to prevent her from claiming his entire body as comfort. She scratched the edge of his cheek when he approached her hand. He leaned in, then went to wash a paw.

She did not ask him what Julian thought.

She had spent the last half hour finding out by the comparatively difficult method of listening.

The smell of warm vegetables reached the bedroom. Her stomach made a noise. She realized she had eaten lunch late and then kept going, calling it efficiency. Hunger hadn't created the problem, but it had contributed several unhelpful adjectives.

When Julian called her, she went to the table. Dinner was eggs, vegetables, and bread warmed too long on one side. The pan was drying. Her lunch container remained untouched.

“Not a restaurant,” he said.

“I have encountered restaurants with considerably worse bread.”

“Please don't name them. I need to retain my ambitions.”

They ate slowly. At first Eve kept noticing other things she could raise: the recycling, the parcel waiting downstairs, the damp coat. She let them wait until she could tell whether she wanted a solution or another point in her favor.

Julian asked about the three participants expected tomorrow. She told him about the revised table placement and Robin's imaginary visitor getting stuck behind a chair.

“Did the imaginary visitor complain?”

“Terrible review. Never coming back.”

“Reasonable.”

After dinner, Eve began stacking their plates.

Julian held out his hand.

“I said dinner.”

She passed him the plates, then hesitated.

“Thank you.”

“Permitted.”

She smiled, tired enough that it felt like something unfolding rather than lighting up.

“I wasn't saying you never do anything.”

“I know. I still heard it that way.”

“And I heard your thank-you as an assignment.”

He nodded. Neither explanation erased the argument, but it made tomorrow less likely to inherit it unchanged.

Eve went to the sofa with a book. At the sink, Julian washed the plates without asking where each should go. Dumpling came out from under the chair and examined the now-empty dinner table, disappointed by its lack of opportunity.

There was still a laundry basket in the bedroom. Still a repair folder in Eve's bag. The evening had not become effortless.

She turned a page while someone else finished the work.
