# Chapter 120 — I'd Like You to Come Tonight

On Thursday afternoon, Eve wrote You can come over if you want and stared at the sentence until it began to annoy her.

Julian had not asked to come over. She wanted to see him. Somehow she had made the invitation sound like a small administrative concession.

She deleted it.

I'd like you to come for dinner tonight. Around seven? You can collect the hat and inspect a terrible drawing.

That was better. It contained a person who wanted something.

She sent it, then returned to the notes for a client call. On the margin of the page, she had written Ask what this version is for. She had learned that lesson several times and still needed the reminder. There were three different screen dimensions in the email, all described as the main one.

Julian replied while she was checking them.

I'd like that, but not tonight. I need an evening at home. Tomorrow?

Eve read the words once, then again.

A tiny part of her wanted to withdraw the invitation altogether. Of course. No problem. Sometime, then. She recognized the impulse by its sudden dignity: she would be so undemanding that he would have to chase her to establish she was still wanted.

She had asked for an evening alone two days earlier. He had given her one without arranging a test at the far end of it.

Tomorrow works. Seven?

Seven. Looking forward to the hat.

The drawing is devastated.

Please tell it I asked after it.

She smiled and put down the phone.

Then she took the salmon she had been about to thaw and left it in the freezer. Tonight she would eat something else. The change cost her a few minutes of deciding, not an evening of being rejected.

At six, Sophie called while Eve was peeling a carrot.

"Have you reached the dog?"

"The dog is the only person I trust."

"That's correct. How's the rest of your week?"

Eve told her about the three main screens, then asked whether the course evaluation was finished. Sophie described a paragraph she had rewritten because every sentence began with I learned, until she sounded like someone trying to convince a teacher she had attended.

"Did you finish it?"

"Yes. I sent it before I could replace learned with discovered in all six places and pretend that was editing."

They talked for twelve minutes. Neither needed to fill the rest of the other's evening. Afterward Eve cooked, watched half a program, and went to bed with the book. Thursday did not become her most remarkable night. She was increasingly pleased by that.

On Friday she finished work before six and took the fish out of the refrigerator, where it had been thawing safely since morning. Julian had offered to bring vegetables. She said she already had them and asked him to bring something he wanted to drink instead.

She considered putting away the drawing supplies. Then she moved them to one end of the table, leaving the unfinished page visible. There was room for two plates without pretending that no one lived here between visits.

At seven, he knocked with a bottle in one hand and a paper bag in the other.

"You brought the entire shop," she said.

"Two rolls. I thought the fish might need structural support."

She laughed and stepped close enough to kiss him before taking the bag. He smelled faintly of soap and outside air. Her hands settled on the front of his coat, and for several seconds she forgot about the pan warming in the kitchen.

Then the pan made a small ticking sound.

"Dinner," she said against his mouth.

"A strong argument."

Dumpling arrived to inspect the bags. Eve closed the door, put the food out of reach, and watched Julian take off his coat. He hung it on the empty hook rather than laying it across the chair.

"Hat?" he asked.

She handed him the paper bag from the shelf.

"There may still be hair. I removed the visible cat."

He unfolded the top and peered inside. "A useful distinction."

"It was in his bed with my slipper."

Julian looked down at Dumpling, who was washing his shoulder with conspicuous disinterest.

"Shared storage?"

"Apparently. I've declined."

He put the hat in his coat pocket and followed her to the kitchen.

Cooking together felt different now that she knew when it would end. Julian was going home after dinner; they had said so in a short message earlier. She did not have to decide whether offering coffee meant offering breakfast. There was enough room in the evening for the fish, the drawing, and anything they actually wanted to say.

"What can I do?" he asked.

"Slice the rolls. The board's beside the sink."

He took it and found the knife. Eve turned the fish, watched one piece break at the edge, and decided the plate would survive looking less impressive than the photograph she had used as a guide.

"How was yesterday?" she asked.

"Very quiet. I ate badly and went to bed early."

"How badly?"

"Toast and something from a tin."

"A complete range of textures."

"I was pleased."

She believed him. It was easier than it would have been a week ago, when she might have treated that meal as proof that she should have fed him.

"Mine involved a carrot and half a television program," she said.

"A full evening."

"I also spoke to Sophie. She finished her evaluation."

She told him the joke about the six uses of learned, then moved on to her own client call. Sophie had not become a report Eve had to deliver in full. Julian told her about a delayed supply delivery at the clinic and the relief of being able to leave the arrangements with the colleague handling the order.

There was no emergency hiding inside either story. Eve set the plates on the table while he poured their drinks.

During dinner, she caught herself watching the way he broke his roll. She had missed a thousand unimportant things about him, all waiting inside one ordinary gesture.

"What?" he asked.

"Nothing. I like having you here."

He put down the bread.

"I like being asked."

"I have asked before."

"I know. Tonight you sounded as if you wanted an answer."

She thought about the message she had deleted.

"I nearly wrote if you want."

"I do want. Just not every night."

"Me too."

They returned to the fish before it cooled. The conversation drifted through a terrible shop display, an umbrella Julian had briefly mistaken for his own, and the dog in Sophie's book. Eve did not summarize what any of this proved. It was enough to laugh with food still in her mouth and have to reach for her napkin.

One of the rolls remained when they finished. Eve held the bag open and asked if he wanted to take it home.

"For breakfast," he said. "Thank you."

She put it inside, then noticed the crumb trail on the table. He brushed the crumbs into his hand while she fetched a cloth. Neither of them hurried to turn the cleared table into a signal that he should leave. They had eaten a meal, and now the table needed wiping.

The simplicity of it pleased her. She could offer him bread without offering a household. He could take it without making himself a resident. She set the bag beside his coat, where it would be remembered.

After dinner he washed while she dried. This time she left him enough space to open the correct cupboard himself. He did, without making a point of it.

At the table he asked to see the drawing.

"You have to guess the subject," she said.

"Is that fair?"

"No."

He leaned over the page. She watched his face with the entirely unreasonable hope that he would recognize the potato and the equally strong wish that he would say something funny.

"A stone," he suggested.

"Edible."

"That rules out my first answer."

"Only for cautious people."

He studied it again. "Potato?"

"Yes."

"I thought the bowl might be for context."

"The bowl is a bowl. The potato is struggling independently."

He laughed, and she was glad she had kept the drawing to herself until now. Showing it felt like bringing him something from an evening he had not occupied, rather than offering evidence that she had managed without him.

"May I try another cup?" he asked.

She gave him loose paper and a pencil. Her yellow handmade notebook stayed on the shelf, its crooked pages reserved for whatever she chose to put there. Julian placed an empty mug in front of them. Eve moved her chair closer.

For ten minutes they drew without talking much. Dumpling slept in his bed. Once Julian held his pencil out at arm's length as the book suggested, and Eve nearly ruined her line laughing at the seriousness of his expression.

"I'm measuring," he said.

"I can see that. The cup appears intimidated."

His second attempt was better. Hers was worse than the one she had made alone. They compared them and agreed that improvement was an unreliable visitor.

At half past nine, Julian put down the pencil.

"I should go."

Eve looked at the clock. There was time for another drink. There was room in the bed. She wanted to kiss him for considerably longer than the time required to put on a coat.

She also wanted him to go home while the evening was still something she had chosen, rather than waiting until exhaustion made the decision for them.

"Okay. Take your cup."

"The actual one?"

"The drawing. I'm keeping the crockery."

He folded the paper carefully and slipped it into his coat pocket. At the door he picked up the bag with his breakfast roll and checked the other pocket for the hat.

"Have you got plans on Sunday?" he asked.

"Some work in the morning. Nothing decided after that."

"Would you like a walk?"

"Yes. Afternoon?"

They chose a time and a place without filling Saturday on the way past it. Eve could hear Dumpling moving behind them. The cat approached, sniffed Julian's shoes, then sat down on the mat.

Julian stepped around him.

Eve followed as far as the doorway, then caught his sleeve.

"I haven't finished saying goodnight."

"My mistake."

She kissed him until his hand came to rest against her waist and the hallway beyond him ceased to be interesting. When they drew apart, she was smiling.

He touched her cheek with the back of his fingers.

"Sunday," he said.

"Sunday."

She watched him take the first few steps, then closed the door before Dumpling could investigate the hall.

The cat looked up at her. Eve crouched, offered her hand, and waited until he rubbed against it. Then he walked away toward his water without making any attempt to retrieve the departing man.

On the table, two drawings lay where she had left them: tonight's uncertain cup and the potato from her evening alone. Julian had taken his own page. His hat was gone. The borrowed charger remained beside her lamp.

Eve made one cup of tea and opened her paperback.

She would see him on Sunday. Tonight had been good. Neither fact required her to do anything more before going to bed.
