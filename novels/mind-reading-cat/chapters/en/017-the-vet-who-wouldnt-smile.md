# Chapter 017 — The Vet Who Wouldn't Smile

Julian looked into the camera as though it had arrived without an appointment and refused to describe its symptoms.

“Before attending the clinic,” he said, “please ensure that you have obtained the relevant information concerning—”

Eve lowered her hand.

He stopped. Behind him, the empty examination room remained reassuringly empty. She had chosen a corner without charts, computer screens, or anything that might accidentally reveal a patient's business. So far, her greatest threat to confidentiality was the expression on his face. It suggested he knew a terrible secret about the entire human race.

“Are we closing?” she asked.

“No.”

“Has the building been condemned?”

“No.”

“Good. I wasn't sure which announcement we were filming.”

His eyes shifted to the sheet beside her phone. “Those are your words.”

They were. In the document on her laptop, surrounded by confident headings, they had looked efficient and reassuring. Coming out of his mouth, they sounded like a letter returning a parcel to its sender.

Eve crossed out the opening line.

The clinic had commissioned one short introduction to preparing for a first visit, plus a brief version she could share on her new account. The scope was small, the fee agreed, and the filming slot was thirty minutes between scheduled work. She had celebrated the arrangement by planning forty minutes of material.

Now seven minutes had passed, and they had successfully recorded the name of the clinic.

“Let's try something more conversational,” she said.

Julian put the page down. “All right.”

“Imagine you're talking to a person.”

“I had been.”

“A person you don't intend to put in an envelope.”

That earned the smallest movement at the corner of his mouth. Eve pressed record too late to catch it.

She had borrowed Sophie's compact microphone for the afternoon. Its little receiver was plugged into her phone, a green light glowing with an authority she wanted badly to deserve. The phone itself stood on a tripod whose shortest leg resisted all attempts at cooperation. She had leveled it with two folded appointment leaflets, then replaced them with blank card so no old information would appear in the frame.

Julian sat very still while she adjusted the angle.

“Is that comfortable?”

“Yes.”

“Because you look like you're waiting for a passport photograph.”

“Should I move?”

“Not until I fix the leg.”

He glanced at the tripod.

“The metal one,” she added.

“I'd worked that out.”

Outside the room, a dog gave one offended bark. Julian turned automatically toward the door, then back when the sound was followed by Tessa's calm voice. Eve watched the change in him. His shoulders had loosened the moment he had something real to pay attention to.

She looked down at her script. It contained useful information, but every sentence had been flattened into the same smooth surface. No wonder neither of them could get a grip on it.

“Wait here,” she said.

“That's the one part I'm doing well.”

At reception, Tessa was replacing the roll in a receipt printer. The new paper escaped her fingers and unfurled across the counter.

“If you want the printer interviewed, it has some very strong opinions,” she said.

“I want three questions people actually ask before their first appointment. Nothing about treatment. Nothing with anyone's details.”

Tessa caught the paper before it reached the floor. “Whether they should arrive early. Whether they need to bring the thing they've written everything down in. Whether we mind if they've written everything down.”

“The last two are different?”

“To the people asking, yes.”

Eve borrowed a pen, then remembered she had three in her bag.

“Anything else?”

“Whether calling because they're running late will make us angry. Also whether they can bring a second person. The answer to that one depends on the appointment and the space, so don't edit me into saying always.”

Eve wrote that down too.

Tessa looked at the crossed-out script in her other hand. “How's he doing?”

“Magnificent. If we ever need someone to announce that a bridge is unsafe, I have footage.”

“Ask him about the woman who apologized for bringing a notebook. Not the woman herself. The notebook part.”

Back in the room, Julian had straightened the tripod's rebellious leg. He hadn't touched her phone.

“It was catching on the locking collar,” he said.

“Excellent. Your second profession is already going better than mine.”

“I think this is still your first profession.”

She busied herself with the microphone cable, unreasonably pleased by the correction.

Instead of asking him to deliver an opening, she sat beside the phone and held up her own notebook. The cover was bent, one corner marked by a coffee cup, the pages crowded with things she had remembered too late.

“If I bring this to an appointment, will you think I'm difficult?”

Julian looked at the notebook, not the lens.

“No.”

“Even if I've made a list?”

“A list is useful.”

“Even if some of the questions are embarrassing?”

“Most people aren't embarrassed about the same things. I'd rather hear the question than have you leave without asking it.”

He paused, and she let the pause stay.

“Sometimes someone apologizes for checking their notes,” he continued. “There's no need. Being in a room you don't know can make ordinary things hard to remember.”

Eve forgot, for a moment, to calculate whether the answer would fit under thirty seconds.

She remembered bracing the damp cardboard box against herself, afraid of letting it shift beneath the injured cat. At the time, she had been determined not to look like a woman who couldn't afford a mistake. That determination had made it difficult to ask almost anything.

“What if the question is about money?” she asked.

He took a little longer to answer this time.

“Then it's still a question you should be able to ask. We need to explain what we're proposing and discuss the circumstances. A short video can't make those decisions for an individual visit.”

She nodded. That was more careful than the reassuring slogan she'd drafted. It was also better.

They moved to practical arrangements. He explained that appointment instructions could differ, and that callers should check what applied to their visit. Eve asked whether someone running late should quietly hope the laws of time would change before they reached reception.

“They should call,” he said.

“What if they're only five minutes late?”

“They can still call.”

“What if they spend four minutes deciding whether to call?”

“Then they'll have had a difficult four minutes.”

The laugh surprised her. It came first from her, then from him, not broad or prolonged, just enough to change his whole face. He looked younger without becoming less himself.

Eve lifted one finger in triumph.

“There. That's the opening.”

“The difficult four minutes?”

“The human being. I knew we had one.”

He shook his head. She almost asked him to laugh again, then heard how dreadful that request would be and swallowed it.

The room's wall clock clicked. They had eleven minutes left.

She listened back to the clip.

The picture was perfect. Julian's mouth moved. Eve's hand entered the corner of the frame. His smile arrived exactly where she remembered it.

The sound was a low, padded scrape.

She turned the volume up.

A longer scrape. One tiny click. Silence.

Her stomach dropped.

“No,” she said.

Julian leaned forward, not close enough to crowd her, but enough to see the screen.

“The microphone?”

She checked the receiver. The light was still green. The cable, tugged when she had moved her chair, sat almost flush in the socket. Almost was apparently a country in which useful audio did not exist.

She pushed it fully in and tapped the microphone.

The little bar on the screen jumped.

For a second she considered saying the recording was fine, then asking him to repeat the last few answers as alternate takes. She could make the mistake disappear into technical language. She had done that at her old job, where mistakes had to travel under false names until somebody senior approved their existence.

“I didn't check after I moved the chair,” she said. “The last section has no usable sound. That's mine.”

“All right.”

“It isn't all right. It's your time, and I—”

“How much time do we have?”

“Ten minutes.”

“Then which answer do you need most?”

She looked at the script and chose three. Not the laugh. Not the perfect accidental exchange. The notebook, the call, the invitation to ask a question before leaving.

They recorded a five-second test first. Eve listened to every one of those seconds through the headphones. Julian watched her with the patient gravity of a man observing a ritual he did not intend to interrupt.

This time she kept the questions simple. His answers were not identical. One was better; another less graceful. When she nearly interrupted to rescue a phrase, he continued and found a plainer version himself.

At the end, she asked him to say his name and what he did.

“I'm Julian. I'm one of the vets here.”

“Lovely. Now pretend you believe people are allowed to survive meeting you.”

He looked directly at her, just beside the lens.

“I'm Julian,” he repeated. “I'm one of the vets here. You don't have to remember everything before you come in.”

She let the recording run a second longer, then stopped it.

Tessa knocked before opening the door. “Time.”

Eve checked the clock. They had used their thirty minutes exactly.

“I've got what we need,” she said.

Julian unclipped the microphone and laid it in her open palm rather than dropping it onto the table. “Send the edit before it's shared.”

“Of course. And if I need more, I'll arrange another slot. I won't ambush you with a camera in the corridor.”

“That seems fair.”

Tessa picked up the abandoned script. “Before attending the clinic, please ensure—oh, this was going to be a hostage video.”

“Thank you,” Eve said. “That's precisely the professional assessment I reached.”

Julian took the page from her, folded it once, and handed it back. “I was following instructions.”

She carried her equipment home in the tired, careful manner of someone transporting both a fragile object and an injured opinion of herself.

Dumpling was asleep in his drawer. He opened his eyes when she came in, looked at the bag, and closed them again. She had left him out of the shoot because a clinic recording did not require a cat, especially one still settling into an unfamiliar life. His complete lack of interest in her crisis was oddly welcome.

At her desk, she made two folders: usable, and sound failure. She resisted deleting the second in a fit of embarrassment. Tomorrow it might remind her to check connections after moving anything.

She played the silent laugh once more. There he was, a man no longer braced for a formal announcement, looking at something beyond the edge of the frame.

It would have made a wonderful opening.

She closed it and began with the notebook instead.

Half an hour later, the rough cut had a beginning, a middle, and no music trying to persuade viewers that asking a question was heroic. She sent it to the clinic for review with a short note about the missing take, then put the borrowed microphone back in its pouch.

Dumpling emerged, stretched, and placed a paw on her notebook.

“Don't worry,” she told him. “You don't have to remember everything either.”

He sat on the entire list.
