# Chapter 137 — A Familiar Tap Against a Bowl

Daniel's directions ended with an instruction to ignore the large painted arrow.

“It points to a business that closed three years ago,” Eve read aloud.

Julian looked across the pavement. The arrow occupied most of a wall and indicated a locked gate.

“Confidently,” he said.

They turned the other way.

Dumpling traveled in his familiar carrier, held level against Eve's side. The short taxi journey had produced two complaints and then watchful silence. She had brought his own mat, water, and a little of his usual food. There would be no additional stops to justify the fare.

The arrangements had taken longer than the journey. Daniel's repair shop had an enclosed office away from tools and customers. He had agreed to clear it and keep the shop closed during their short visit. The current occupier of the rear room had given permission for photographs of its exterior, but wasn't available to let them inside that day.

Eve had also checked the travel plan through Dumpling's usual care channel. Nothing about the visit had to happen if he was unsettled. His history could wait without him sitting in it.

At the shop door, Daniel appeared in an apron with one pocket sagging under the weight of a tape measure. He looked at the carrier, then at Eve.

“That's the right building. The wrong arrow brought you here eventually.”

“We came prepared to distrust it.”

“Good habit around here.”

He led them through a front room smelling of wood and machine oil. The tools were behind a closed inner door. In the small office, a chair had been moved away from the window, leaving a clear patch of floor. Daniel showed Eve the closed window latch and the door to the street before stepping back.

She put the carrier down and waited.

Dumpling turned his head toward a pale square on the wall where a calendar had hung. His ears moved. He did not crouch at the back or try to force the carrier door.

“Would you mind giving him a few minutes?” she asked.

“Certainly. I have tea. Or I have tea I can find.”

“The second sounds promising,” Julian said.

Daniel went to look. Eve sat on the floor beside the carrier while Julian took the chair. For a while the room contained only the small noises of people being somewhere new: a coat settling, a cup placed on a counter, the squeak of Julian's shoe as he moved it out of the way.

Dumpling sniffed through the front.

Once the room was secure and quiet, Eve opened the carrier. She laid his mat just outside and sat back. The door stayed wide enough for him to return without negotiating around her knees.

He remained inside.

She let him.

Daniel returned with three cups on a tray. “No milk, I'm afraid. The refrigerator works. My shopping doesn't.”

“Plain is fine,” Eve said.

He set the tray on the desk. Dumpling leaned forward at the sound, then put one paw on the mat. His nose traveled along its edge before he brought the rest of himself out.

Daniel smiled without approaching.

“Hello again. Maybe.”

The qualification nearly made Eve laugh. Instead she felt a sudden pressure behind her eyes and busied herself with a cup that was too hot to drink.

Daniel showed them the original photographs on his phone. The repair invoice had confirmed the trolley's delivery week, and the old sale leaflet agreed with the printed date. His feeding visits fell within that period, before Eve had found Dumpling. He could not say whether the cat had appeared daily. Daniel had worked elsewhere on some mornings.

“Did you call him anything?” Eve asked.

“Usually 'mind your feet.' He liked that step.”

“That might have been useful to keep.”

“What's he called now?”

“Dumpling.”

Daniel looked at the cat's rounded back. “Reasonable.”

Dumpling had reached the bottom of the desk. He sniffed a leg, turned, and returned to the mat, which he sat on without appearing grateful for the lengthy preparations on its behalf.

Eve took the food container from her bag. It was close to the small meal she would ordinarily offer at home, and they had agreed she would try only if he seemed comfortable. She had brought a dish too, but Daniel saw her searching and opened the cupboard behind the desk.

“I have a clean one. Used to keep several here.”

He brought out a shallow stainless-steel bowl. Eve checked it and asked him to set it on the clear floor near her, away from the carrier entrance. She added the portion herself.

Daniel crouched slowly beside the desk. Without moving toward Dumpling, he touched the outside of the rim with a fingernail.

Two light taps. A pause. One more.

Dumpling's head turned sharply.

He stood, took two quick steps, and stopped with his nose above the bowl. His tail lifted. Eve watched his ears settle forward as he began eating.

She had seen him approach food hundreds of times. The speed of the turn caught her anyway.

Daniel took his hand away.

“I used to do that,” he said. “Sorry. Habit.”

“No, it's all right.” Eve heard the eagerness in her own voice. “That exact rhythm?”

“I think so. My mother did it when I was little. Tap twice, then once. Otherwise I would stand outside saying cat, cat, cat, and feel an idiot.”

Eve looked at Julian.

He was watching Dumpling eat. When he met her eyes, he didn't nod her into certainty.

“Did you see how fast he turned?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“I hadn't seen him do that here before.”

“We've only been here a few minutes.”

She exhaled through her nose. “Yes. True.”

Daniel looked concerned. “Should I not have done it?”

“He seems comfortable,” Eve said. “I'm just trying not to make one moment answer every question I brought.”

“Then you brought better questions than I would.”

She wasn't sure about that. She had already imagined a series of evenings, the same bowl, the same sound, a cat coming home through the blue door. The actual cat was finishing a small familiar meal in a room she had checked for open windows.

She didn't ask Daniel to repeat the rhythm.

Dumpling licked the bowl once, then walked back to the mat and began washing his face. Daniel stayed on his side of the desk. After a minute he straightened with a hand on his knee.

“I should have taken the chair.”

Julian stood. “We can revise the seating.”

They exchanged places, an undignified maneuver involving the tray and Eve's bag. Dumpling watched them as if human knees were a disappointing design.

Eve wrote down the rhythm and the response in her notebook. She recorded the food already present, the new room, and Daniel nearby. She couldn't separate familiarity from hunger, ordinary sound, or anything else Dumpling might have noticed.

His strange ability offered no way around that. Even if he had caught some immediate impression from Daniel, she could not inspect it. Asking the cat to decide which photograph was true would be no better than asking him who owned the roasting tin.

She closed the notebook.

“Could you show me the outside while Julian stays here?”

Daniel nodded. Eve checked with Julian rather than leaving him the carrier by implication. He agreed, and she told him she would be just beyond the shop. Dumpling had returned halfway into the carrier, his front paws still on the mat.

The yard was smaller than it had looked in the photograph.

A shallow drain ran along one wall. The old step was broad enough for two bowls, perhaps three, but Eve refused to improve that estimate into a feeding schedule. A square patch of newer brick marked a former opening. Above it, a short length of unused wire ended against a fixing.

Daniel pointed to the blue door.

“That's the one.”

Its lower edge was worn pale where things had scraped against it. The high glass panel was covered from within. Eve could see no movement behind it.

In the photograph, the door had looked like an entrance into an answer. In front of her, it was painted wood with a stiff handle and a drain that smelled faintly of wet leaves.

“Is this where you put the bowl?” she asked.

“Usually on my side. Under the little overhang if it rained.”

He indicated the step by his back door.

Eve asked permission again before taking photographs, explaining that she wanted records for checking locations, not a public post. Daniel agreed to pictures of the step, the yard, and the blue door. She kept his shop paperwork, adjoining windows, and the building's identifying sign outside the frame. The address went separately into her private notes.

She took one wide view, then a closer image of the door. Nothing in either photograph identified the cat who might once have walked there.

“People came and went,” Daniel said. “I couldn't tell you whose turn it was. I worked Saturdays somewhere else for a while.”

“What did you think the room was for?”

“Keeping supplies. Helping with cats, sometimes. But you should ask someone who actually used it. I was busy convincing a wheel to stay on.”

Eve thanked him for distinguishing the two.

Inside, Julian sat with his hands loose between his knees. Dumpling was fully in the carrier now, upright on his familiar bedding. Eve checked him and decided they had stayed long enough. There was no reason to wait for a second response that could be turned into the first response's witness.

Daniel rinsed the bowl after she packed the unused food away.

“Do you want it?” he asked.

She hesitated. The object was ordinary, shallow, and newer than the old photographs. Daniel said he had bought several together last year; he couldn't say whether this one had ever held Dumpling's food.

“No, thank you. We have his things at home.”

It felt surprisingly difficult to leave it on the draining board.

They thanked him and made no promise to return with the cat. Eve secured the carrier before the outer door opened. The taxi was already waiting, and she carried Dumpling directly to it while Julian checked that her bag hadn't remained beneath the desk.

On the way home, she looked at the pictures without enlarging them.

“I wanted the bowl,” she admitted.

“I noticed.”

“It wasn't even the old bowl.”

“You can want something without it being evidence.”

She locked the phone and rested a hand against the carrier. Dumpling had turned away from the street, his eyes half closed.

At home, he went first to his water, then to the bed he had left that morning. Eve put away the travel supplies before checking her messages.

The current occupier could let them see the rear room the following afternoon. She had found an old folder concerning the building's use. No animal records, she warned. Nothing that would answer who had taken which cat where.

Eve accepted the visit for herself. Dumpling had no further appointment with the past.

From his bed came the faint, rough sound of washing. She looked over, watched him stop halfway through a paw, and waited until his head came down.

Then she sent Julian one of the permitted exterior photographs and put the phone away.
