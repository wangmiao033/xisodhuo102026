# Chapter 058 — No Cat to Choose the Winner

Dumpling was under the bed before Eve had found her second shoe.

A voice rose in the corridor, followed by another. She couldn't make out every word through the closed door, only Nora's sharp “I said eleven” and a man's answer that sounded tired rather than quiet.

The cat's tail disappeared behind the storage box.

Eve put the shoe down. She crouched far enough away to see an orange ear without reaching into his hiding place. Whatever he might have caught from the people nearby, the noise alone was enough to explain retreat. She wasn't going to drag him out and ask him to decide which angry person deserved sympathy.

“You can stay there,” she said.

She checked that the bedroom window was secure, left the route to his water clear, and closed the inner door most of the way to soften the sound. Then she put on both shoes and went into the corridor.

Nora stood near the stairs with her bag held against her chest. Ben was farther down, beside the lift. Neither had come to Eve's door; they had simply met while leaving for the same appointment.

“I thought we were going to the hall,” Eve said.

“We are,” Ben replied.

“Then let's talk there. The coordinator has the room.”

Nora looked as though she might continue. Instead she tightened her grip on the bag and went down the stairs. Ben waited for the lift. Eve followed neither closely enough to make the journey look like an escort.

The side room at the hall smelled faintly of instant coffee. The coordinator had set three chairs around a small table and a fourth near the door. Eve moved that chair to the table before anyone arrived. She wanted the coordinator present as the person responsible for the committee's part, not stationed like security beside an exit.

A clock hung above a cupboard. Its second hand moved with a soft click.

Nora arrived first and chose the chair farthest from the door. Ben took the one opposite. The coordinator offered water, which both declined and then accepted when Eve poured a glass for herself.

“We have half an hour,” Eve said. “If either of you wants to stop, say so. I won't record this. I'll write down only the practical things you agree can be shared afterward.”

“Fine,” Nora said.

Ben unfolded his sheet.

Eve resisted filling the silence with a speech about community. She asked Ben to describe one recent morning and what it had interrupted.

“Tuesday. I got home late. Seven forty, roughly. I lay down at eight. The door started going, then the barking. I was still awake after nine.”

“It wasn't barking for an hour,” Nora said.

“I didn't say it was.”

“You just said—”

“Let him finish this account,” Eve said. “Then you'll have the same time.”

Nora looked away. Ben stared at the sheet for a moment, apparently having lost the next sentence in the interruption.

“I kept starting to drift off,” he said finally. “Then it would start again. That's different from standing in a room listening for an hour, but it still means I didn't sleep.”

Eve wrote *repeated waking after eight* rather than *one hour of barking*.

She turned the notebook so both could see it.

“Does that describe what you mean?”

Ben nodded.

Nora rubbed her thumb along her bag's zip. When her turn came, she described the early clothing collections and the customer who had arrived half an hour before the agreed time, ringing twice because she thought nobody had heard.

“I can't turn customers away after they've taken the trouble,” she said.

“You can if you're turning the rest of us into the waiting room,” Ben replied.

Nora sat back sharply.

Eve put her pen down.

“Ben, what change are you asking for?”

He breathed out through his nose. “No collections outside the time she gives people. No ringing over and over.”

“That I can write down. The other sentence won't help her know what to do.”

His face reddened. For a moment Eve thought he would stand and leave.

Instead he said, “All right.”

Nora looked at the notebook. “I could put the collection time in the message twice. And tell them not to come early.”

“Would you be able to keep to it if they did?” Eve asked.

“That depends.”

“Then don't promise it as if it doesn't.”

The coordinator shifted in her chair. Eve could feel her desire for a tidy first agreement. She wanted one herself. A clear bullet point would look wonderfully competent at the top of the page.

Nora took out her phone and opened her booking messages, keeping the screen facing herself.

“I have two people tomorrow who always arrive early,” she said. “I can call them today. Next week I can move the earliest slot later, but not everything. Some people start work at nine.”

Ben looked down at his hands.

“How much later?”

They worked through the actual hours. The revised slot would help part of his sleep period, not all of it. Nora couldn't remove every morning appointment without losing work. Ben couldn't move his night shift to make the building convenient.

When the gap became obvious, nobody spoke for several seconds.

The clock clicked.

“What else wakes you?” the coordinator asked.

“The door.”

“I've asked the caretaker to check it today. Not disable it. It has to close properly.”

Ben nodded. Nora said Pip often reacted to the sharp sound. Eve recorded that as Nora's observation, not a diagnosis or a guarantee that a quieter closer would solve the barking.

They agreed that complaints would go through the committee's existing contact, with approximate times, rather than anonymous notes on the board. The coordinator would acknowledge them on working days. This wasn't an emergency service and would not replace the normal process if the problem continued.

“Can I have her number?” Ben asked.

Nora's hand closed around her phone.

Eve looked at her, not at him. “Only if you want to share it.”

“I don't,” Nora said. “Not for messages when I'm in the middle of work.”

Ben looked disappointed but didn't insist.

“The committee route works,” he said.

Then Nora said, very quietly, “I don't want people thinking I'm cruel to him.”

Ben lifted his head. “I didn't say you were.”

“The notes—”

“I wrote one. With my name. I don't know who wrote the others.”

Eve could not prove whose pen had made the green sentence. She wasn't going to turn a communication session into an amateur investigation.

“We can record that neither of you is making that accusation here,” she said. “And ask people to stop adding unsigned comments.”

Nora looked at Ben for the first time without immediately preparing an answer.

“I thought the notice meant you wanted him gone.”

“I want to sleep.”

“I know that now.”

It was not an apology, but it changed the room. Nora set her bag on the floor. Ben took a drink of water.

With seven minutes left, Eve read back the small plan. Nora would contact the early customers and try a later collection window for the coming week. The caretaker would inspect the door. Ben would keep a short record of disruptions rather than confront visitors in the corridor. The coordinator would handle further reports. Eve would check back the following Friday as part of the paid scope.

“And the barking?” Ben asked.

Nora's shoulders rose again. “I'm dealing with it.”

“You said that before.”

Eve didn't ask Nora to disclose her appointment or turn private veterinary care into a condition for being heard.

“The plan doesn't promise no barking,” she said. “If the disturbance continues, the committee still has to address the reports. Today we're choosing things people can actually start.”

Ben stared at the paper. “It's not much.”

“No,” Eve said. “It isn't everything.”

That answer seemed to unsettle him more than a confident assurance would have. He looked at Nora, then at the clock.

“I'll try the week.”

“So will I,” Nora said.

They finished at eleven thirty-two, two minutes beyond the intended end because Eve read the wording back slowly. She asked permission before sending the shared summary to each person. Both agreed. Neither wanted a public account of the conversation.

The coordinator would post only the revised contact instructions and a reminder not to leave unsigned allegations. There would be no photograph of two neighbors smiling beside a dog.

At the doorway, Ben stepped back to let Nora pass. Her bag caught the handle of the spare chair. He lifted the chair just enough to free it, then put it down again. Nora thanked him without looking especially grateful. Eve kept her eyes on the notes. She wasn't going to promote an ordinary bit of courtesy into evidence that the problem had ended.

After they left separately, Eve stacked the spare glasses.

The coordinator stood beside her. “That wasn't quite the warm community piece I imagined.”

“No.”

“They didn't shake hands.”

“Would it help them sleep?”

The coordinator gave a reluctant laugh. “Probably not.”

She carried the water jug to the little sink and rinsed it. Eve checked her notes for anything she had written in the heat of the conversation that didn't belong in the shared summary. She removed a description of Nora looking close to tears. It had mattered to Eve in the room, but it wasn't an agreed action.

At home, the flat was quiet. Dumpling had emerged from beneath the bed and was sitting in the patch of sun beside the wardrobe. He looked at Eve, blinked, and resumed washing one shoulder.

She put her bag down without approaching him.

During the meeting she had wanted, just once, an indication that one person's agreement was sincere. Some useful twitch of ears, some impossible fragment that would let her leave with more certainty than a page of modest promises.

The cat had not been there. The people had still spoken.

Eve opened the window a little behind its secure screen and sat on the floor several feet away. Dumpling finished washing. After a while he came over and leaned his side against her shin.

“Terrible attendance,” she told him softly.

He put one paw on her trouser leg.

She stroked his back when he settled, slowly enough that he could move away. Her phone vibrated in her bag. She let it wait until he rose again.

The message was from Nora. One customer had agreed to the later time. The other had cancelled rather than change it.

Eve read the two sentences twice. The plan had begun costing something almost immediately.

*I'm sorry you lost the booking,* she wrote. *I'll keep that in the follow-up, without your customer's details, if you're comfortable with it.*

Nora answered yes.

Eve added the lost booking to the private follow-up notes. Then she sent the agreed summary, exactly as read aloud, without improving the ending.
