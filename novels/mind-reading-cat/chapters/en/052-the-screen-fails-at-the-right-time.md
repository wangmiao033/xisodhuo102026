# Chapter 052 — The Screen Fails at the Right Time

The man with the suitcase had just discovered that he was on the wrong train when the picture froze.

For several seconds, Eve thought the pause belonged to the joke. His face remained stretched between outrage and uncertainty, one finger raised toward a conductor who would never receive the complaint.

Then the speakers made a sharp popping noise.

A child behind them said, with considerable satisfaction, “He's broken it.”

Julian looked toward the equipment tent. “Strong criticism of the performance.”

Eve laughed before the rest of the lawn had quite decided whether to be disappointed.

The screen went blue. At its base, a volunteer bent over a cable, stood up, and bent over a different cable. Someone called that they should switch it off and on again. Someone else announced that his brother knew about projectors, although the brother did not appear to be present.

Eve's hand had already gone to the strap of her bag.

No camera. No spare battery with the wrong connection. No reason why she, specifically, should get to her feet.

The volunteer nearest the aisle was asking people to remain seated while the organizer checked the power. Eve moved her bag out of the aisle instead.

Julian held his cup between both hands. He had not leaped up either.

“Do you know about projectors?” she asked.

“I know they're rarely improved by six strangers leaning over them.”

“That sounds transferable.”

“Most of my useful knowledge is.”

A string of lights above the tea stall still glowed. The failure had left the park looking strangely divided: a dark rectangle in front of them, and, beyond it, people continuing to buy biscuits beneath warm yellow bulbs.

The first family packed up. A boy protested that they could wait until morning, which seemed to him a perfectly reasonable amount of time to allow for repair. His mother put a knitted hat over his objection.

Eve opened her packet of crackers.

“If it comes back,” she said, “we'll have missed nothing.”

“If it doesn't, we've seen a very short film about poor travel preparation.”

“An important subject.”

“Especially for people carrying three mats.”

She offered him a cracker with the solemnity of a peace agreement.

The organizer came to the front of the lawn holding a small battery-powered speaker. She apologized twice before managing to explain that the projector had failed, that they could not safely fix it there, and that they would send a message about another screening. The refreshment stall would stay open for another fifteen minutes. People with folding chairs borrowed from the community center were asked to return them beside the gate, rather than leaving them on the grass.

A woman near the front asked whether they could have the title written down. The organizer spelled it for her, then repeated it more slowly for three people holding their phones. Eve liked that small refusal to let the whole evening disappear. A failed screening could still leave somebody with a film to look for.

A low, disappointed sound passed through the audience. Then blankets rose, coats shook themselves out, and the film became a crowd negotiating its way home.

Eve stood and collected their cups.

“Well,” she said. “That was a very good first seven minutes.”

“Eight. You shouldn't undersell it.”

She tried to roll the spare mat tightly enough to fit beneath its strap. It resisted as though it had acquired a new set of principles while they were sitting on it.

Julian rolled the other two, waiting while she turned hers around.

“Don't,” she warned.

“I haven't said anything.”

“You have a helpful expression.”

He looked deliberately toward the abandoned screen.

That made it worse. She laughed, and the mat finally curled tightly enough to fit beneath the bag's straps.

Near the gate, an older volunteer was collecting chairs from people who seemed to believe that placing one vaguely near him completed the task. Three leaned at awkward angles across the entrance. A woman steering a pushchair stopped short.

Eve moved the nearest chair out of her way, then caught the volunteer's eye.

“Would another pair of hands help for a few minutes?”

“Yes, please. Folded ones against the fence. Don't stack them higher than that pile.”

He pointed. A specific job, a visible end.

She glanced at Julian. “You don't have to.”

“I can manage chairs.” He set their bag beside the fence, away from the moving feet. “As long as nobody requires me to restore the film.”

They worked on opposite sides of the gate. Eve discovered that the metal chairs had a knack to them, and that the knack was not helped by cold fingers. Julian showed her the release catch on the first one, then left her to do the next.

A little girl insisted on carrying a cushion almost as large as her chest. Eve accepted it with thanks instead of taking it away halfway through the journey. The girl returned with a second and an expression of professional determination.

Within ten minutes, the path was clear. The volunteer said they had done enough. Eve gave him the last cushion and retrieved her bag.

It was surprisingly easy to leave once someone had said where the job ended.

Beyond the park, the pavement shone under the streetlights. It had not rained, but the air had turned damp, carrying the smell of grass and hot oil from a shop on the corner. A bus passed with only three people visible upstairs.

Eve checked her phone. No urgent messages. No catastrophe had developed in the time she had spent watching eight minutes of a comedy and arguing with a mat.

“Hungry?” Julian asked.

“I ate before I came.”

“That wasn't quite my question.”

She looked at the small shop with steamed windows. “A little.”

They bought a paper bag of roasted potatoes from the counter and stood outside while the man serving them wiped down a tray. Julian had reached for his wallet first; Eve had already put her card on the reader.

“You brought tea,” she said.

He accepted that without making it a contest.

The potatoes were hotter than they had any right to be. Eve took an ambitious bite and immediately regretted it.

Julian watched her attempt to remain dignified.

“You can spit it out,” he said.

“Absolutely not.”

“A compelling medical argument.”

She managed to swallow, then pointed her wooden fork at him. “We are not at work.”

“No. At work I'd be more diplomatic.”

They walked slowly, letting the food cool. The street offered small, ordinary things to look at: a shopkeeper lowering a shutter, two students trying to fit an oversized plant onto a bicycle, a restaurant window crowded with paper stars left over from an event neither of them could identify.

Eve asked what he would have chosen if the potato shop had been closed.

“Soup.”

“That's too broad.”

“Potato and leek. With bread.”

“You had the chance to choose any food in the world.”

“And I chose something I wanted.”

She considered that. “Fair.”

“What would you choose?”

“The noodles from the place near my old office. But only if the woman who runs the kitchen is there. Her son puts too much vinegar in the sauce.”

“Have you told him?”

“No. I order something else and act as if it was my plan.”

“Very efficient.”

“I contain contradictions.”

She had not thought about those noodles for weeks. Remembering them brought back the narrow pavement, the steam against her face, the ten-minute lunches she had eaten while scrolling messages. For once the memory was not an invitation to examine everything that had gone wrong. She could miss the noodles without missing the job.

Julian asked whether the shop was far away.

“Not really. I keep treating that whole area as if I need special permission to go back.”

“You could go for lunch.”

“I could.” She looked into the paper bag. “And refuse the vinegar.”

“An ambitious afternoon.”

At the next crossing, they stopped beneath a red signal. Eve offered Julian the last potato.

“I was leaving that for you,” he said.

“I thought you wanted it.” She tipped it onto her fork.

“We've constructed a difficult situation.”

She divided it against the side of the bag. One half fell back in. The other remained on the fork, triumphant and uneven.

“Crisis resolved.”

The light changed before he could answer.

On the other side, she asked him for a small embarrassing story. Not a work disaster. Nothing involving patients or someone else's privacy. Something he could afford to lose.

He thought for so long that she suspected he was searching for a polite refusal.

“I once introduced myself to a man twice in the same evening.”

“That isn't very bad.”

“He was wearing a coat the second time. I thought he was someone else.”

“Oh.”

“He said we'd met. I said he must have mistaken me for my brother.”

Eve stopped walking.

“You didn't.”

“I did.”

“Do you have a brother?”

“No.”

Her laughter escaped so suddenly that the woman passing them turned and smiled without knowing why.

Julian was smiling too, although with the resigned patience of someone who had paid for this story before.

“I was seventeen,” he added.

“That doesn't help nearly as much as you think it does.”

“What did you expect me to say?”

“That you once used the wrong spoon.”

“I've done that as well.”

She gave him her own story: a presentation in which she had confidently called a client by the name of his dog because she had spent more time preparing the pet photographs than reading the meeting invitation.

“Did he mind?”

“He said the dog had stronger leadership skills.”

“Did you get the work?”

“Yes. Which was probably bad for my personal development.”

They reached a small square with a closed flower stall. The empty buckets stood upside down in tidy rows. Eve threw the potato bag into a bin and rubbed her cold hands together.

Julian glanced at them, then at a sheltered bench beneath the awning.

“We can stop if you want to finish the tea.”

She did. They sat with the flask between them, its remaining warmth enough for two small cups. She could hear traffic beyond the buildings but could not see it. For a few minutes, neither of them found anything necessary to say.

Eve thought of the frozen man on the screen, stuck forever at the beginning of his mistake.

“I still want to know where that train goes,” she said.

“We could find out another time.”

She looked at him. His cup rested between his hands. He was not watching her with an expression she could name and put away safely.

“Yes,” she said. “We could.”

When they stood, she no longer felt that the evening had been cut short.

The film had stopped. The evening, inconveniently and rather wonderfully, had not.
