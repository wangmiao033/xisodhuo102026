# Chapter 113 — A Weekend Without Cat or Boyfriend

Dumpling watched Eve put on her shoes with the solemn interest usually reserved for a cupboard opening.

"I'm going to a museum," she told him. "There will be no benefit to you."

He stretched one foreleg across the mat.

She checked his water, left his familiar sleeping places undisturbed, and moved the shopping bag he had been investigating into the closed cupboard. She would be back in the afternoon, well before his evening meal. The flat did not need an elaborate farewell ceremony because she intended to leave it for a few hours.

At the door, her phone lit up with a message from Julian.

Have a good trip. Bring back a review of the worst object.

She sent a thumbs-up and left before Dumpling could settle on her remaining shoe.

Sophie was already at the station, holding two coffees in a cardboard carrier.

"One is yours. I can't remember which."

"They're labeled."

"Then one of them knows."

They found seats opposite each other on the train. Sophie put her bag by her feet and immediately took off her scarf. Eve moved the coffee away from the edge of the little table before the train had a chance to test it.

For the first ten minutes, they talked about nothing that needed improving: the man on the platform who had sprinted for a train that wasn't theirs, Sophie's neighbor painting a front door a color she described as indecisive mustard, the strange optimism of the weather forecast.

Eve had brought neither a camera nor her laptop. Her bag felt too light. Twice her hand went toward it, checking for equipment she had deliberately left at home.

Sophie noticed the second time.

"What have you forgotten?"

"Responsibility, apparently. I'm checking the empty space where it usually goes."

"Try putting a pastry there."

They bought one at the museum café before going into the exhibition. It was much larger than expected and shed flakes across both their coats. Sophie brushed at hers, creating a finer, more evenly distributed layer.

The exhibition occupied three rooms above an old riverside storehouse. There were washboards, kettles, a row of alarm clocks, and a chair whose purpose appeared to be discouraging visitors from remaining conscious.

Eve stood in front of an iron with a little opening for hot coals.

"I complain when mine needs filling."

"You're not required to become grateful to it," Sophie said.

"I was going to apologize formally."

A woman nearby laughed, then pointed out a handle on another object. It was a toast rack, although neither Eve nor Sophie would have reached that conclusion without assistance. They thanked her and moved on, quieter now, listening to the creak of the wooden floor.

At a small table, visitors could write the name of an ordinary object they would save and pin the card to a board. Someone had written Dad's chipped mug. Someone else had chosen the television remote, with three underlines.

Sophie picked up a pencil.

"What are you choosing?" Eve asked.

"You first."

Eve thought of Dumpling's first bowl, then her laptop, then the mug Julian always chose when he visited. Her life appeared to be filing itself automatically under work, cat, and boyfriend.

"My blue mixing bowl," she said at last.

Sophie looked up. "The enormous one?"

"It has never once judged the quantity of pancakes I intended to make."

"A professional virtue."

Sophie wrote something and pinned her card near the bottom, where there was room. Eve bent to read it.

The small radio from my kitchen.

"I didn't know you had a radio."

"You can see it from the door."

"That isn't helping my reputation."

"I listen while I cook. Sometimes the reception goes fuzzy if I stand by the fridge. I have to decide whether I want news or milk."

Eve imagined Sophie alone in her kitchen, music breaking up while she reached for breakfast. It was such an ordinary picture that she felt foolish for finding it new.

Downstairs, the rain had begun. Not the dramatic kind that made strangers bond under awnings, just a steady unpleasant drizzle turning the river path gray.

They stood by the door with their coats half-fastened.

"We could go back to the café," Eve said.

"We have just left the café."

"We could return with more experience."

Sophie unfolded a small umbrella. One spoke pointed at an ambitious angle.

"There is a covered arcade five minutes along. The person at the desk told me. Lunch places, secondhand books."

"Did you verify five minutes in normal walking units or local-enthusiast units?"

"We can conduct research."

It took nine. They arrived with damp shoes and one wet shoulder each, having argued good-naturedly about who was failing to stand in the middle. Under the arcade, a shopkeeper was moving a bucket beneath a leak. Sophie stepped around it and admired a display of postcards so faded that every sunset looked like an administrative stain.

Eve picked up a postcard of the storehouse.

"For your course?"

"For my fridge."

"Right."

Sophie glanced at her. "You can ask normal questions. You're acting like every answer contains a trap."

"I'm trying not to turn the trip into a career interview."

"Then don't. But I'm still allowed to have a career while we're eating soup."

They found a small café with steamed windows and three kinds of soup. Sophie ordered tomato. Eve chose lentil and discovered that the bread came in slices thick enough to prop open a door.

At a table facing the river, Sophie took off her damp coat and hung it over the chair back.

"I did talk to someone at the center about programming work," she said. "Not a job offer. Just what the different roles involve."

Eve put down her spoon.

"What did you find out?"

"That the interesting part isn't always the day of the event. There are people who follow a whole season through. They know which activities people come back for, what the building can actually manage. They don't have to meet a new emergency crew every Saturday."

"Would you like that?"

"I think so. I like production. I'm not trying to escape every part of it. But sometimes I'd like to work somewhere long enough to stop introducing myself to the cupboard."

Eve smiled. "You could build your own small agency around that. Venues would—"

Sophie leaned back.

Eve stopped.

The soup between them gave off a last pale curl of steam.

"I've done it again," she said.

"A little."

"I heard a thing you wanted and attached a business plan."

"You attached your business plan. Mine might involve a salary."

There was nothing hostile in the correction, which made it impossible to dismiss as mere irritation. Eve had been imagining Sophie with her own name above a door, freedom to choose clients, a small team. It had felt like praise. Perhaps it was praise for a person Sophie hadn't volunteered to become.

"Do you want a permanent role?"

"Maybe. A good one. Not just any job because it says permanent. I'd like some evenings I don't spend calculating which invoice might clear first. I'd like colleagues who know where things are. I'd like holiday pay to be more than an interesting concept."

"That sounds reasonable."

"It sounds boring when I say it quickly."

"No."

Eve said it too firmly, then softened her voice.

"No. I think I have been using working for myself as evidence that I'm getting somewhere. It doesn't make it the only direction."

Sophie tore a piece of bread in half.

"I don't need you to abandon yours to approve mine."

"Good. I already have a spreadsheet that would be very upset."

They ate for a while. Outside, a cyclist dismounted under the arcade and shook water from a sleeve with an expression of personal betrayal.

"What would you do," Sophie asked, "if you had a day that wasn't waiting for your next client? Not a fantasy month. An actual day."

Eve considered saying this. It was true, but too convenient.

"I'd like to make something I'm bad at. Without needing to post the improvement."

"Pancakes?"

"I am excellent at pancakes. The issue is volume."

"Then come to the bookbinding session. There are still ordinary places available. You could make a terrible notebook."

"Would you want me there?"

"Yes. If you'd enjoy it. Not to photograph my competence or help me in front of the tutor."

"I can be impressively unhelpful."

"I've seen you try. It needs practice."

Sophie told her the date and the booking page, then returned to her soup rather than selling the whole event. Eve made a note to book at home. She did not open the page at the table.

When the bill came, Eve reached for it out of habit. Sophie put a finger on her own half.

"We said we'd each pay."

"I know. I was just—"

"You don't owe me a compensation lunch."

Eve looked at the figures, then took out the right amount. It was unexpectedly pleasant to leave the meal where it belonged, between two people who had wanted soup. Outside, they discovered the rain had eased enough to fold the umbrella. Sophie shook it away from the doorway while Eve held her bookshop bag, and for once neither of them needed to call the small exchange anything at all.

After lunch they wandered through the secondhand bookshop. Sophie bought an old paperback with an alarming cover because she remembered reading another book by the author on a delayed coach. Eve bought a thin book about drawing household objects, opened it at random, and found a patient explanation of how to draw a cup.

"Ambitious," Sophie said.

"I'm starting before handles."

On the return train, they compared their purchases and discovered Sophie's book was the third in a series. She declared herself ready to judge everyone without the benefit of history.

Eve's phone showed a message from Julian asking whether the worst object had been found. She answered with a description of the chair, then put the phone away. Sophie was telling her about a terrible novel they had once passed back and forth, and Eve wanted to remember which of them had written a furious note in the margin.

By the time they reached their stop, both were laughing at a plot twist neither could reconstruct accurately.

At the ticket gates Sophie pushed the paperback into Eve's hands. "You read it first. I want a witness when I start complaining." Eve promised to return it, then they parted, Sophie toward her bus, Eve toward the shops and home. There had been no grand outing photograph. Eve had a damp cuff, a drawing book, and the knowledge that her friend wanted colleagues, not necessarily employees.

At home Dumpling inspected her shoes, then walked toward his bowl with a hopeful glance.

"Not yet," she said. "I checked the time."

She put the drawing book on the table and tried the cup while the kettle boiled. Her first attempt looked like a small dented building. She sent Sophie a photograph.

The foundation is unstable, came the reply.

Eve laughed, turned the page, and tried again.
