# Off the Popular Route

Mia arrived carrying a red pen and looking prepared to use it.

“I've found a mistake,” she said.

Eve had not yet handed her the map.

“In mine or in the world?”

“The phone number on the café's old page. It goes to somebody who sells bathroom tiles.”

They were meeting at the bakery to check the last pieces of information. Mabel was already sitting on the bench, Biscuit resting beside her. Eve had brought large-print copies, a pencil, and no filming grip.

Mia put her bag down carefully. “He was quite nice. He said people keep asking whether dogs can come in.”

“What did he say?” Mabel asked.

“That depends how much tile they're buying.”

Mabel laughed. Biscuit raised his head briefly, saw nothing relevant, and lowered it again.

Eve crossed out the number. They would use only the contact information the café had confirmed directly, or none at all.

Mia uncapped the red pen.

“I'm ready.”

---

Eve had asked for one hour, not an indefinite afternoon of help. Mia had a laundry collection to make afterward. They divided the work so she could leave on time.

Mabel would check whether the notes were readable and whether Eve had understood the places they had walked. Mia would verify two businesses' public details and return with the answers. Eve would sit with Mabel first, then check the final layout.

“Do I take Biscuit?” Mia asked.

“No,” Mabel said. “He has no interest in verifying opening hours.”

Mia looked down at him. “Fair.”

There was a time when Eve might have made the exchange into a small story about Mia becoming ready for an animal of her own. Today Mia had a task and Biscuit had a place to rest. Eve did not need to make either one symbolic.

Mia went to the café with the printed questions. Mabel turned the map sideways.

“This arrow,” she said.

“What about it?”

“It points at a wall.”

Eve leaned over. Mabel was right. Moving the collection-day note had shifted the arrow just enough to make the route look as if it passed through brick.

“That would shorten the walk considerably,” Eve said.

“It would shorten a few other things.”

Eve corrected it.

Mabel tested the fold, found the note about the bins without being prompted, and nodded. She did not mention yesterday's footage. Eve was glad not to be offered another chance to explain it.

---

Mia returned from the café with more information than the questions required, but most of it was useful.

“The chairs are out after they finish cleaning the pavement. Usually by ten. Not a guarantee at ten. I've written that.”

“Good.”

“And he wants us to say the water is available if people ask, not that there's a communal bowl. He doesn't leave one outside.”

Eve checked the wording against the note from yesterday.

Mia pointed to a sentence. “I asked him to read this. He said yes.”

“Thank you.”

“Also, the bathroom-tile man asked if we could fix the old page. We can't.”

“No,” Eve said. “But we can tell the café owner which page is wrong.”

Mia had already done that. She seemed pleased that this counted as finishing something even though the internet had not instantly repaired itself.

They checked the flower shop next. The bench was still being used for plants. The owner had not promised when it would return to sitting duty, so it remained unavailable on the map.

The broken bench had not been repaired. Eve checked the report status and found only an acknowledgment. The bright cross stayed.

The map was acquiring a disappointing number of qualifications. It was also becoming something Eve could stand behind without crossing her fingers.

At the end of Mia's hour, Eve closed the notebook.

“You said you had to leave.”

“I can do one more place.”

“We've done the two we agreed. Go before the laundry becomes tomorrow's problem.”

Mia capped the red pen. “Can I see the final version before it goes up?”

“Yes. I'll send it this afternoon.”

She left with the pen behind her ear, then came back three steps to retrieve it from herself and put it in her bag.

Mabel watched her go.

“Useful young woman,” she said.

Eve smiled. “She is.”

---

Sophie found the privacy problem in less than a minute.

Eve had sent her a preview link after lunch with a request to look only if she had fifteen minutes. Sophie called while standing outside a venue, waiting for someone with a key.

“Your route begins at the wrong place.”

“It's the route we walked.”

“Yes. That's the problem. This first shot, the turn, and that distinctive railing in the next one—does it lead back toward her building?”

Eve opened the preview.

She had never filmed Mabel's door. She had not written an address. Yet the opening sequence began farther along the street than it needed to, and someone familiar with the neighborhood could follow the turns.

“I can start at the public crossing,” she said.

“And remove the timing that says she walks from home for two minutes before reaching it.”

Mabel had mentioned that distance while explaining the route. Eve had treated it as useful context instead of private information.

Eve rubbed her forehead. “I thought leaving the door out was enough.”

“It's a small neighborhood. People can add.”

The venue door opened behind Sophie. Someone called her name.

“That's my fifteen minutes,” she said. “Send it to Mabel again after the change.”

“I will. Thank you.”

Eve rebuilt the opening. The public crossing became the starting point. The distinctive stretch before that public starting point disappeared entirely. She removed a shot with a window reflection that had no practical use and checked the other images at full size for details she had not intended to include.

The film became shorter.

It also stopped pretending that following a particular woman's exact walk was necessary to understand where the benches were.

---

Mabel approved her few spoken lines after seeing the revised cut.

There were no images of her struggling, no close-up of Biscuit's gray muzzle arranged to suggest a tragedy. In the brief piece, Mabel pointed to the unavailable bench and said, “Looking like a place to sit isn't the same as being one.”

The rest was practical: a clear view of the bakery seat, the approach to the café's outside area, the step people might otherwise miss. The information sheet did most of the work.

Eve added captions, checked them twice, and sent the final copy to Mia as promised. Mia caught a missing word. The café owner approved his two lines. Julian's short wording suggestion stayed in the introduction without turning into a claim that he had assessed every route.

Eve exported the video.

For a moment, her finger hovered over the choice of music. Something warm might soften the factual edges. She tried a track and watched the opening.

Mabel sounded as though she was making a brave final journey to buy bread.

Eve removed it.

Street noise, her clear narration, and Mabel's dry sentence worked better.

She posted the video with the dated information sheet, then went to wash the mugs instead of watching the counter change.

---

The counter did change. Slowly.

Nothing burst. There was no sudden flood of strangers declaring the internet restored their faith in humanity. One viewer asked whether the route would suit a particular dog's condition. Eve answered that the sheet described local features, not what any individual dog could safely manage, and directed them to discuss their dog's needs with their vet.

Another viewer reported that the café sometimes shut early on quiet afternoons. Eve checked with the owner before changing the note. He confirmed that the hours could vary and gave her a more accurate way to describe them.

She updated the date and recorded the correction.

Before closing the page, she checked the downloadable copy on her phone. The new note was there, but the old file still appeared in the preview she had saved earlier. She opened the public link afresh, confirmed the revision, and added a plain line explaining what had changed.

It was the sort of work nobody would put in a cheerful montage: checking a link, checking a date, making sure two versions had not begun telling different stories. She fetched the tea she had forgotten, found it cold, and drank it anyway.

When she finally put the phone down, the kitchen clock had advanced much farther than the view count.

Then a message came from a nearby bookshop.

They had a chair near the entrance. People were welcome to ask for a short rest when there was room. Could the shop be included?

Eve wrote back with questions instead of immediately adding a green dot.

The bookshop owner sent a photograph showing a step she had not mentioned. Eve thanked her for the picture and asked which approach it showed.

A second message arrived from someone who had printed the sheet for a relative.

The big letters help. Thank you for not putting everything in tiny pale gray.

Eve read it twice.

Dumpling climbed onto the chair beside her and put his nose against the corner of the laptop. She moved the cable out of reach.

“We've had a typography compliment,” she told him. “This is very serious.”

He turned around and sat facing the wall.

---

Mabel called that evening.

“The bakery man has printed it.”

“He asked for a copy.”

“He's put it by the till. He thinks people might buy more bread.”

“He may be right.”

“He also wants to move the bench a little farther from the bin. I told him not to block the door.”

Eve smiled. “That sounds like a productive conversation.”

“It was. Have you eaten?”

“Not yet.”

“Then I'll make something one evening. For you and the people who helped. Not a ceremony.”

Eve looked around her kitchen, at the small table currently supporting three map drafts and a cat toy.

“You could bring it here, if you'd like. I have chairs. Some of them are available.”

Mabel laughed. They arranged a simple supper. Biscuit would be comfortable at home for Mabel's short visit; she would keep to the routine that suited him. Eve offered to help carry the food rather than turning the invitation into another project.

“Ask the vet if he's free,” Mabel said. “And the others.”

“I will.”

“No cameras.”

“No cameras.”

After the call, Eve sent invitations without describing the meal as a celebration of impact. Sophie already had a job that evening. Mia had a commitment too. Julian said he could come after work, provided the clinic finished on time; he would let her know if that changed.

Eve told Mabel it would be a small table.

Small tables still need feeding, Mabel replied.

Eve moved the drafts from the table and counted the chairs. Dumpling occupied one before she finished.

She counted it anyway.
