# Chapter 095 — You May Say No

“I'd like a couple of days,” Julian said.

Eve had been prepared for a difficult answer. Preparation did not prevent the little drop inside her when the answer acquired a length.

He was still at the door, his coat over one arm. Behind her, the apartment looked exactly as it had before she told him: cups near the sink, notebook on the desk, cat on a chair. She had expected the room to feel different afterward. She had not expected the difference to be mostly in how far away the doorway seemed.

“All right,” she said.

“Sunday afternoon, if that works. We could meet here at three. I don't want to leave you wondering when we'll speak.”

She checked the day in her head. Friday and Saturday between them, not an indefinite sentence. Sunday at three was an actual arrangement rather than the comforting shape of one.

“That works.”

“I may have questions before then, but I don't want to keep having the conversation by message tonight.”

“I won't send you more examples.”

He nodded.

Eve wanted to ask whether he was still her boyfriend. The question sat so close to her tongue that she had to take a breath before deciding whether it was something she genuinely needed clarified or a demand that he remove all discomfort before leaving.

“I know thinking doesn't automatically mean ending things,” she said. “I'm finding it hard not to hear it that way.”

“I haven't ended anything. I do need time without reassuring you through every part of my reaction.”

The honesty hurt less than the kind answer she might otherwise have extracted.

“I understand.”

This time she had waited until he finished.

They confirmed Sunday once more. There was no need for a new rule about how often they could contact each other over ordinary matters. Julian said he would message if the time needed to change. Eve said she would do the same.

At the stairs he turned back.

“Get some sleep.”

“You too.”

It was an ordinary farewell. She let it remain one instead of searching its wording for an extra promise.

When the door closed, she stood with her hand on the latch until the building went quiet. Then she locked it, put away the tea, and noticed the closed notebook still lying on her desk. She remembered exactly which page described the cups.

Dumpling climbed down from the chair.

Eve looked toward him before she could stop herself. He approached her, rubbed against her leg, and continued toward the kitchen. For one unreasonable second she wanted his movement to mean that Julian would return, that the conversation hadn't changed anything essential, that home still contained the same future.

He was a cat walking toward a food bowl.

She checked the bowl. It was not mealtime yet, and there was fresh water. She offered her hand, stroked him briefly when he leaned into it, and let him move away.

“No questions for you,” she said.

The words were for herself. He was busy inspecting a cupboard seam.

That night she slept badly, but she slept. She woke once, remembered Sunday, and didn't reach for her phone. In the morning the arrangement was still two days away, and work was still due before noon.

The edit concerned a small repair business introducing its collection procedure. Eve had already received the approved wording and photographs. Her task was to make them agree and fit the short format, not invent a moving story about people reunited with their possessions.

She opened the project and discovered that yesterday's export had cropped the closing time.

For several minutes she stared at the missing digits as if they were a personal accusation.

Then she enlarged the working view, adjusted the safe area, and checked the other frames. The mistake was ordinary, fixable, and entirely hers. She didn't need a crisis in her relationship to explain it.

The client called at the arranged time. Eve listened to the requested correction, repeated the actual change, and gave a realistic delivery time. When the caller hesitated, she asked whether there was another point to check.

“The collection address stays on screen quite briefly,” the client said.

She played that section again.

“You're right. I can hold it longer without adding another scene.”

They agreed. No hidden meaning had been required. The client knew what worried them and had needed a second to say it.

Eve made the change, watched the export from beginning to end, and sent it. She put the invoice reminder in its proper place rather than opening Julian's conversation as a reward for finishing.

At lunch, Sophie called to ask whether Eve still had the little screwdriver from the sewing tin. The repaired equipment case had developed a loose handle, and Sophie wanted to check the fastening before returning it to the shop.

“I have it,” Eve said. “I can bring it downstairs if you're passing.”

“I am. Ten minutes.”

Eve found the tool among the thread and buttons. She also found an old receipt, a key ring without keys, and a needle she put safely back in its packet. Apparently, the tin continued to resist specialization.

Outside, Sophie stood beside the case with one foot resting against a wheel to stop it escaping down the slope.

“You look tired,” she said.

“I am. I told Julian about what I think happens with Dumpling.”

Sophie's hand paused on the handle.

“How did it go?”

“He believes I'm not making it up deliberately. He doesn't know what explains it. And he's upset about the way I've sometimes looked to the cat instead of taking his answers.”

“That sounds like more than one conversation.”

“It is. We're talking again Sunday.”

Eve handed over the screwdriver, then added, “I mentioned the cups. Only what we did and the uncertain results. None of your private worries.”

Sophie nodded. “All right. Thank you for telling me.”

Eve could have asked her to explain the cups to Julian. Her friend had been present; another witness might make the account seem less lonely. But Julian's chief concern was not whether Sophie could testify about three plastic objects. Bringing her into it would give Eve an ally where she needed to tolerate an answer.

“I'm not asking you to convince him,” she said.

“Good. I'm available for this screw.”

The screw turned but didn't tighten. Sophie tested the handle once and stopped.

“Something behind it isn't holding. Back to the shop.”

“Do you want a hand getting it there?”

“No, I've allowed time. You can walk to the corner if you want.”

Eve did. The case rolled with a rattling complaint, and Sophie described a supplier who had delivered three different shades of white fabric for one display. The story required no moral conclusion. By the time they parted, Eve had laughed at something unrelated to the cat or Julian.

She returned home carrying the screwdriver. The visit had given her company, not a verdict. She put the tool away before losing it again.

In the afternoon, temptation arrived in small, respectable forms. She could send Julian a link about animal perception. She could explain one sentence she thought had sounded too definite. She could say there was no need to reply, a phrase that would not prevent the message from arriving.

Instead she opened a private note and wrote the questions she wanted to ask on Sunday. After the third, she stopped. Half of them were attempts to ask whether he was angry without using the word.

She deleted those and kept one: *What do you need me to understand about the effect this had on you?*

Then she closed the note. Waiting was not a project she could complete early by preparing harder.

Saturday brought laundry, a small grocery shop, and an answer from the repair-business client approving the final file. Eve checked the payment date and filed the approval. She bought only what she needed for the next few meals, leaving the attractive display of kitchen containers alone. Her affordable trial had not ceased to matter because she was anxious.

The grocery bag split just inside her door. An onion rolled beneath the hall chair, followed by a second that stopped against her shoe. Eve put down what remained of the bag and crouched to retrieve them. She had chosen the thinner bag because the thicker one was in a drawer at home, an economy that had saved nothing useful.

She found the reusable bag, put it beside her keys for the next trip, and inspected the groceries. Nothing was damaged. The onions could go into tomorrow's lunch rather than become evidence that her whole weekend had gone wrong.

While she was on the floor, she noticed dust caught around a chair leg and cleaned it. The task was small enough to finish. She had been postponing it for days while finding time to reread the same short conversation on her phone.

Dumpling dragged a soft toy into the hallway and abandoned it where she would step. She moved it to the rug, played with him for a short while, and stopped when he lost interest. He took the toy under the sideboard.

She didn't bring out Julian's coat or anything carrying his scent. She didn't ask whether the cat missed him. The absence was hers to experience without staging it for another creature.

Late that afternoon, a message from Julian arrived.

*Still all right for three tomorrow?*

Eve read it twice, then answered once.

*Yes. Here at three.*

She nearly added a joke about keeping the research institution closed. It might have been funny another day. Today it would make light of the very thing he was trying to consider.

The plain reply remained.

On Sunday morning she cleaned because the floor needed it, not because a spotless apartment could make her easier to forgive. She left the ordinary books on the shelf, including the blue album. She put the observation notebook in the drawer, accessible if they chose to refer to it, but not open as a demand.

At half past two, she made herself eat something. Waiting on an empty stomach was a poor strategy she had already tried often enough.

Dumpling watched from the chair while she finished. Outside, somebody was having an argument with a car door that wouldn't close. The world retained an unreasonable number of concerns unrelated to her afternoon.

Eve washed her plate.

There were things she could offer Julian: an honest answer, a refusal to arrange secret tests, time to speak without being corrected into comfort. There were things she couldn't offer: certainty about an explanation, a promise never to feel frightened, control over whether he needed more time.

She checked the clock once, then moved away from it.

When the knock came, she opened the door without first looking at the cat.
