# Chapter 022 — The Mark Beneath the Fur

Eve noticed it because Dumpling finally allowed her to finish one side of his neck.

He had tolerated three passes of the soft brush, then turned his head with the patient displeasure of someone enduring an unnecessary meeting. She stopped. In the light from the cleared window, the orange-and-white fur lay in slightly different directions, revealing a narrow flattened band.

She leaned closer without holding him still.

It wasn't a collar. There was no collar. But the shape made her think of one.

Dumpling stood, stepped over her wrist, and moved to the other end of the sofa. The appointment was over.

Eve left the brush on the table.

For a few seconds, she could see the whole story with dreadful clarity: a collar removed, a door closed, a cat left in the rain. It arrived complete with a person to blame. She disliked how quickly it arrived. She disliked even more how easily she could imagine turning it into a caption.

She looked at the cat, now folding himself into a white cushion he had never asked permission to use.

A line in the fur was not a closed door.

She took a photograph only when he settled in a position that showed it naturally. Then she sent it to the clinic with a straightforward question: I've noticed this change in how the fur lies. Does it need checking at his planned visit, or sooner?

She did not write, Someone must have hurt him.

The clinic replied with an arrangement to look at it during the already scheduled follow-up, along with a request to contact them if anything about his condition changed. Eve saved the reply and resisted searching through a hundred photographs of cats whose histories she could not verify.

Instead she opened Dumpling's folder.

It contained receipts, care instructions, her original note of where and when she had found him, and the information she had kept from the initial efforts to locate a previous owner. The practical records looked thinner than she expected. She had been so busy learning how to live with him that the question of where he had lived before had slipped quietly into the background.

She had not decided it no longer mattered. She had simply become accustomed to finding him at the end of her bed.

That realization made her uncomfortable.

At the clinic, two days later, Dumpling sat in his carrier watching a patch of sunlight move across the floor. Eve kept the carrier where Tessa directed her, away from the passing traffic. He did not need to meet everyone in order to have a successful morning.

Tessa looked at the folder in Eve's hand.

“Are we expecting an inspection?”

“Only of my filing system. Please be gentle.”

“I don't offer that service.”

When Julian called them through, Eve explained what she had noticed before offering her theory. She was proud of that for approximately three seconds, until he asked her to show him the photograph and she nearly said, It looks like someone took his collar off before abandoning him.

She stopped after collar.

Julian examined Dumpling as part of the follow-up. He handled him without hurry, changing position when the cat shifted away. Eve watched the familiar details—the pause, the waiting, the way he did not make every movement into a contest—and felt her shoulders lower.

“It could be from something that sat against the fur,” he said afterward. “A collar is one possibility. It doesn't tell us when, or what happened afterward.”

“So it isn't proof that somebody threw him out.”

“No.”

“Or proof that somebody is looking for him.”

“No.”

She looked down at her hands. “You have a very efficient way of removing entire plots.”

“I'm looking at a cat's neck.”

“That would explain it.”

Dumpling had found Julian's sleeve. He sniffed it, then leaned forward until his nose touched the fabric near the cuff. Eve could not tell whether he recognized the smell, wanted reassurance, or was inspecting traces of another patient. The small gesture made her want to supply an answer. She left it alone.

They finished discussing the ordinary follow-up details. Nothing in the flattened fur required them to invent a second, dramatic explanation for his past injury. When Eve brought up the gloves and the tote bag, Julian asked what she had actually seen.

She described the carton opening, the missing gloves, the bag dragged across the floor, and the quieter afternoon after the collection.

“Could it mean he's been moved around before?”

“It could mean the changes unsettled him. We can't work backward from one behavior to a complete history.”

“I know. I keep trying to.”

“Notice what happens. Keep his routines as steady as you reasonably can. If you're concerned about changes, talk to us. But you don't have to solve his whole past this morning.”

The last sentence was not a diagnosis. It was, nevertheless, the one she carried out of the room.

At reception, Tessa helped her review which owner-search information was already recorded and which notices could be refreshed. They checked the location and date against Eve's first written note rather than her increasingly polished memory of that afternoon.

“I keep saying it was raining all day,” Eve admitted. “I don't actually know that. I was inside until I left work.”

“Then say it was raining when you found him.”

“Less cinematic.”

“More useful.”

The updated notice would use a clear photograph and the appropriate contact route. Eve would not publish every identifying detail or hand the cat over because someone left a confident comment. Any possible claim needed to be checked through the established process, with information that supported it.

She wrote this down, not because Tessa had made it sound difficult, but because her own feelings were already trying to make it simple.

“What if someone really is looking?” she asked.

Tessa paused with her hand on the keyboard.

“Then finding that out matters.”

Eve nodded. Her throat felt tight in a way she had not prepared for. Until now, searching for Dumpling's past had mostly meant imagining how to protect him from it. She had not spent much time imagining a good person missing him.

Tessa did not tell her she would definitely get to keep him. She also did not tell her that getting attached had been foolish. She printed the updated information and placed it in the folder.

On the bus home, Eve rested one hand against the carrier's side. Dumpling looked out through the opening, eyes half narrowed, his expression offering no assistance with complicated ownership questions.

“You could have left a forwarding address,” she murmured.

A woman across the aisle smiled, then returned to her book. Eve was grateful that no larger conversation followed.

At home, she refreshed the appropriate found-cat notice and checked the contact details before sharing a limited version on the account. She kept the wording plain: found in this area on this date, currently receiving care, information welcome through the listed route.

She did not write rescued from abandonment.

The word rescued would probably have brought more attention. Abandonment would have brought anger. Together they could have built a small army of strangers ready to defend a story she did not know was true.

Instead she posted the facts she had.

A message arrived within an hour: Poor baby. People are monsters. Tell us who dumped him.

Eve's fingers hovered over the keyboard.

She remembered the rain under the awning and the cardboard box against her ribs. The memory still hurt. It was tempting to give the hurt a clear address.

She replied that she did not know what had happened before she found him and was trying to locate reliable information.

Another person suggested a missing-pet group she had not checked recently. Eve thanked them, looked at the group's requirements, and added a notice that followed its process. She did not turn a suggested lead into an announcement that a breakthrough had occurred.

By late afternoon, her eyes ached from comparing orange ears, white paws, dates, streets, and photographs taken from angles no useful comparison could survive.

One image looked close until she opened the full photograph and saw the tail. Another had the right face but came from a city far away, posted long before the date in its caption. She kept notes on what she'd checked so she wouldn't circle back tomorrow and mistake the same resemblance for new evidence.

Before closing the search pages, she checked the notice one last time on her phone. The photograph loaded clearly, and the contact route worked. She had nearly included the building where she lived because it seemed convenient; she was glad she had kept the location to the appropriate area instead. Anyone with useful information could reach the right place without being invited to turn up at her door.

She found no reliable match.

That was a result, though not the kind that came with music.

Sophie arrived to return a charger she'd borrowed. She found Eve sitting on the floor beside an open folder, surrounded by the exhausted neatness of someone trying to organize uncertainty.

“Did you lose a tax year?”

“I may have found evidence that he once wore a collar. Or I may have found fur that lies differently.”

Sophie sat on the edge of the sofa. “And now?”

“Now I want to know everything.”

“Can you?”

“No.”

Dumpling came out from beneath the chair and sniffed Sophie's shoe. He appeared satisfied with her answer to whatever inquiry this represented and moved on to the charger cable, which she promptly put away.

Eve explained the updated search, the lack of a match, and the part she had not expected to feel: fear that the right answer might be someone else's home.

Sophie listened without reaching for the cat.

“Do you want me to say something comforting or help you stop for dinner?”

“Can you do both?”

“I can say you don't have an answer tonight while helping you stop for dinner.”

“That is a very narrow service.”

“I'm specializing.”

They ordered food and put the papers away before it arrived. Eve left the folder where she could find it easily, not buried beneath the laptop and not displayed like a warning on the sofa.

During dinner, Dumpling climbed onto the windowsill and watched a moth make repeated bad decisions about the glass. Eve watched him for a moment. She knew which toy he ignored, where he liked to sleep, how he inspected a new bag. She knew he had been frightened and hurt when she found him.

She did not know the hands that had fed him before hers. She did not know whether the missing part of his story contained cruelty, accident, confusion, or ordinary lives that had become difficult in ways she couldn't see.

For now, there was a notice with accurate information and a cat on the windowsill.

That would have to be where she began.
