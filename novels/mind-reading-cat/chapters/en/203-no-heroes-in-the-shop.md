# Chapter 203 — No Heroes in the Shop

The building manager called on Saturday morning to say there was water beneath the venue's rear window.

Eve sat up so quickly that Dumpling lifted his head from the end of the bed.

“How much?”

“A small area. The contractor's looking at the outside joint. I've kept that recess closed off. Don't move anything there until we've checked it properly.”

“Can I come in?”

“The front room is dry. I'll meet you at the entrance and show you where you can go.”

She asked about the nearby electrical fittings. He said the affected area would remain out of use until the relevant checks were complete. That answer was less comforting than a casual assurance and much more useful.

Eve got dressed.

Julian had already gone to his morning clinic shift. There was bread on the counter and a note about the washing machine, which had finished its cycle. Eve ate standing up, put the note where she would see it later, and checked the transport updates before leaving.

The heaviest rain had eased. Her route was operating normally, though slowly. She travelled with her umbrella folded inside a bag and spent most of the journey calculating the cost of things she hadn't yet seen.

At the venue, the building manager stopped her at the front room.

“This side only for now.”

Beyond the temporary barrier, the bottom edge of a cardboard carton had darkened. A narrow strip of wall beneath the rear window was marked, and water had reached one corner of a low storage panel. It looked distressingly small compared with the enormous cost Eve had invented on the bus.

It was still her carton.

“What's in that one?” he asked.

“Spare printed cards. Some display material.”

“Can you identify it without going over?”

“Yes.”

She took a photograph for the repair record from where she stood. The contractor was tracing the entry point; the landlord had been notified. Nobody asked Eve to climb outside or pull a wet panel away from the wall.

“Monday for the full repair assessment, weather permitting,” the manager said. “We'll tell you what can be used before then. Keep the recess closed.”

She nodded and wrote down his words rather than replacing them with what she wished he had said.

Robin arrived later for the short paid shift they had agreed the previous evening. Eve had confirmed the safe access arrangements before asking them to travel. They took off their wet coat at the front, looked at the barrier, and said, “We are not holding anything in here today.”

“No. The cancellations stand.”

“Good.”

They sat at the front table with the business phone and the booking record. Eve started listing affected stock from the photographs. Robin completed the last contact checks and found that the outstanding refund request had been accepted by the payment service, though the customer's bank still needed time to show it.

Eve copied a quantity from the stock photograph, then wrote the same number on the next line.

“Packs or individual cards?” Robin asked.

She looked down. She had counted the same carton twice.

“Neither. That line shouldn't be there.”

Robin crossed it out while she checked the original record. She had slept badly, waking whenever the rain changed against the bedroom window, and the figures seemed to move when she stopped staring at them. She drank some water and slowed down. The remaining dry stock matched the record once they worked through it together, one shelf at a time.

For half an hour, the work was manageable.

Then the questions began arriving in a different shape.

One person wanted to know whether their dog needed a veterinary appointment after refusing to walk in the rain. Another asked if someone could collect a parcel from a route Eve hadn't checked. A third wondered whether the venue might open for an hour because they were tired of being indoors.

Eve answered each within what she actually knew. She gave the clinic's normal contact details for the medical question, declined the collection, and repeated the closure dates. Nobody shouted. Nobody threatened a terrible consequence. The requests were ordinary enough that saying no began to feel ungenerous anyway.

She looked at the empty chairs.

Perhaps there was something small they could still do.

Julian arrived just after noon carrying a paper bag. He had finished his shift and stopped on his way home, as they had arranged by message. Rain dotted his shoulders. He looked at the closed recess, asked whether the building's checks were underway, and accepted her answer without trying to become the repair authority.

“I brought lunch,” he said.

Eve's stomach responded before she did.

“Thank you. Could I ask you something?”

He set the bag down.

“We're getting questions about pets. Nothing I can answer. Would you be available for an hour this afternoon if I told people they could call you through us?”

His hand stayed on the folded top of the bag.

“No.”

The word landed more sharply than she expected.

“Only an hour,” she said.

“I can't run another phone service after my shift. And the venue isn't set up to receive clinical calls.”

“I know that. I thought you could just tell people where to go.”

“The clinic already has a contact route and cover. They can call it.”

Robin looked down at their screen, giving the conversation what privacy the small room allowed.

Eve felt heat rise into her face. She had asked rather than promised. She had thought that made the idea harmless. Being refused still felt, for a moment, like being left alone in the wet room with all the people she couldn't help.

Julian took his hand off the bag.

“I'm tired,” he said. “I need to go home after we eat.”

“You don't have to make it sound as though I'm trying to use you.”

“I didn't say that.”

His voice had become careful, which made hers sound worse in her own ears.

“I said I can't do it.”

Eve looked toward the rear window. A drop slid down the outside of the glass. She couldn't tell where it would go next, and she was suddenly furious about that too.

“I've been saying no to people since Thursday,” she said.

“I know.”

“It feels as though that's the only thing the place is doing.”

Julian sat down, leaving the chair beside him free.

“You cancelled sessions people couldn't safely attend. You refunded them. That is work.”

“I don't need you to praise it.”

“All right.”

He didn't add another reassuring sentence.

The quiet left Eve with the thing she had actually asked for: his spare energy, as though his day contained a drawer she hadn't checked yet.

She sat opposite him.

“I wanted somebody to give me an answer that wasn't another refusal,” she said.

“I can't be that answer this afternoon.”

She nodded. It took effort, and she wished it hadn't.

“Okay. I won't offer the calls.”

“Thank you.”

He opened the bag. There were sandwiches, one slightly crushed where he had held it too tightly against his coat. He gave Eve the less damaged one without making a point of it.

Robin stood up with their own lunch.

“I'm taking twenty minutes away from this table,” they said. “When I'm back, I can cover messages until my shift ends. You need a break too.”

Eve almost said she would eat while working.

Instead, she closed the stock photograph.

Julian ate beside her. They spoke little. The silence was not comfortable at first, but it became less hostile when she stopped trying to make him withdraw his answer.

“How was your morning?” she asked eventually.

“Busy. Ordinary busy.”

“Do you want to tell me about it?”

“Not yet.”

“All right.”

He finished the sandwich and rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands. She could see his tiredness without needing it to become an emergency before it counted.

When Robin returned, Eve went home with Julian for an hour. The remaining messages had an owner and an end time. The building manager had the landlord's contact details and could reach Eve directly if an actual decision about her tenancy was needed. She did not need to remain beside the damp carton to supervise every drop.

At the apartment, she took off her shoes and sat on the sofa.

Dumpling approached, sniffed her trousers, and climbed into his box instead of onto her lap. Eve was relieved not to be required to interpret anything about it.

Julian disappeared into the bedroom. She heard the curtains move, then the bed creak.

Eve meant to sit for five minutes. She woke forty minutes later with her cheek against the sagging cushion and a crease pressed into her wrist.

The room was quiet. Nothing had collapsed because she had stopped watching.

She washed her face and checked the business phone remotely once at the agreed handover time. Robin had answered three ordinary questions and declined a request to reopen for visitors. Their note was brief: no urgent issues, one email for Monday, shift finished.

Eve thanked them and told them to sign off. She could manage the final half hour of administration from home.

During that half hour, a customer asked whether Eve herself could take over a pet-care visit the following morning. The address was outside the nearby area they had discussed, and no arrangement had been made with her.

Her first response formed automatically: What time?

She erased it.

She had already promised herself a morning to recover and do the household things waiting at home. More importantly, asking the time would invite a negotiation about a service she wasn't available to provide.

She replied that she couldn't take the visit and suggested contacting the customer's existing provider about an alternative. She did not send Julian's name as a substitute.

When the half hour ended, she closed the messages.

The building manager called later with a limited update. The source appeared to be the exterior window joint. A temporary measure had stopped further entry during the lighter rain. The recess would remain isolated pending the checks and Monday's repair plan. The front room could be used for a short staff visit the next day, provided the closed area stayed untouched.

Eve confirmed the instructions and arranged a brief Sunday inventory session with Robin during paid time. They would only handle dry stock in the cleared area.

Then she put the phone down.

Julian came out of the bedroom looking less pale and more rumpled. He stopped when he saw her at the table.

“Still working?”

“Finished. I told someone I couldn't cover a visit tomorrow.”

He nodded.

“And I'm sorry I argued with your no,” she said. “You answered what I asked.”

“Thank you.”

He sat beside her. She rested her head against his shoulder without requiring him to stay upright forever.

Outside, the rain had become a softer, uneven tapping. Eve listened until she could hear the refrigerator beneath it.

There would be repair calls on Monday. There were losses to count and customers who would remain disappointed. Tonight the shop was locked, Robin was off duty, and Julian's hand was warm around hers.

She let the evening end there.
