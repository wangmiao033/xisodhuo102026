# Chapter 106 — Reception Is Not Background

Eve had given reception four seconds.

On her screen, the front door opened, a hand accepted a form, and the film moved on to Julian beside a clean examination table. The cut was smooth. The music made it feel inevitable.

Tessa watched it twice without speaking.

“Can we go back?” she asked.

Eve moved the cursor to the title.

“Not that far. The hand.”

It was Wednesday, two days after Eve had sent the market's corrected report. They were in the clinic's little staff room before the afternoon appointments. Eve had brought a proposal for the visitor-information film Dr. Clark had commissioned after reviewing the old communication cards. The fee and two rounds of revisions were agreed. This was the first rough structure, assembled from permitted empty-room footage and newly recorded staff demonstrations. No visitors appeared in it.

She had thought that made her careful.

On the screen, Tessa's hand accepted a blank form.

“What happens after that?” Tessa asked.

“We introduce the consultation.”

“No. To the form.”

Eve looked from the screen to her.

“You check it?”

“For what?”

A kettle clicked behind them. Tessa did not get up to make tea for either of them.

Eve paused the film. “I don't actually know.”

“I can tell.”

It was said without triumph, which made it harder to answer with a joke. Eve closed the preview window and brought up her notes. They contained three carefully prepared questions for Julian and a reminder to record a welcoming shot of reception.

Tessa looked at that too.

“I'm not asking for a flattering close-up. I would like people to understand why I ask questions, and what I can't decide for them.”

“I've made you the doorway.”

“Doors get fewer complaints.”

Eve laughed once, then stopped when Tessa didn't. Tessa didn't sound angry; she continued before Eve could offer another joke.

“A person tells me something at the desk, then tells the vet they already explained it. Sometimes what they told me was that they were worried. Sometimes they gave me a detail I couldn't assess and I passed it on. They need to know I haven't held a consultation in the waiting room.”

Eve turned her notebook to a fresh page.

“Could I ask about that properly?”

“Now? I have twelve minutes before I cover the desk again.”

“Then twelve minutes. If we need more, I'll book another time with Dr. Clark arranging cover.”

The second sentence cost her the appealing idea of finishing all the interviews today. She wrote the time at the top of the page so she would have something more reliable than her sense that a conversation was going well.

Tessa described the beginning of an ordinary appointment. Confirming who had arrived. Checking contact details privately. Asking the agreed first questions and alerting the clinical team when something required their attention. Explaining where to wait without promising when the room would become free. Finding out whether someone had received an estimate or needed to ask about it.

“Not deciding whether they can afford treatment,” she said. “Not deciding what treatment they should have. Not translating a hurried explanation into a promise I can't keep.”

Eve crossed out the phrase friendly welcome in her notes. It wasn't false. It was inadequate in a way that let the rest of the work vanish.

“What do people think you can do that you can't?”

“Move them ahead without speaking to anyone. Give an exact finish time. Explain a medical result when the clinician hasn't spoken to them yet.” Tessa considered. “And sometimes they think I know why a vet looks tired.”

“That last one would make an unfortunate service.”

“It already is one, unofficially.”

This time Tessa smiled. Eve wrote down none of the identifiable examples Tessa had declined to give. A useful process did not need a particular visitor's worst afternoon attached to it.

When the twelve minutes ended, Eve closed the notebook.

“I'll ask for a second interview.”

“Good. Because I haven't told you what happens after the phone rings while I'm doing all of that.”

Tessa returned to the desk. Eve stayed in the staff room, looking at the four seconds she had assigned her.

The problem wasn't that Eve had forgotten a supporting character. There were no supporting characters at work, arranged according to how well they fitted the central person's story. There were jobs she had looked at and not learned to see.

Julian came in carrying his empty lunch container.

“Finished?” he asked.

“Discovered that I haven't started properly.”

He glanced at the frozen frame. “What's wrong?”

“Reception has four seconds. You have almost a minute.”

“I can speak faster.”

“That would give me a shorter version of the same mistake.”

He set the container beside the sink and listened while she explained. Eve was aware of how easy it would be to make him the person who understood Tessa for her. She stopped before asking what Tessa really wanted.

Instead she asked, “When you're ready to see the next visitor, what do you need from the desk?”

He named the practical handover, including the limits on what reception could know. When he started to describe something that depended on the specific case, Eve asked him to leave that out of the general film.

“I need the route, not a sample patient's day.”

“Then Tessa should help check it.”

“Yes. In paid work time, with room to disagree.”

Julian nodded. He began washing his container. Eve did not ask him to arrange the interview as a favor to his girlfriend. She sent Dr. Clark a short explanation of the revised structure, including the extra staff time it required and the existing production hours she could use differently.

He replied that afternoon. They could arrange half an hour the next morning, with a colleague covering the desk. If more filming proved necessary, he wanted the revised scope before approving it.

That was reasonable. Eve had not discovered an excuse for unlimited work on either side.

At home, she put the rough film on the large screen and watched it without sound.

The hand. The corridor. Julian. Dr. Clark. Equipment. Julian again.

Her old employer would have liked the structure. An attractive expert, a quick reassurance, a clean answer. She had objected to making Julian a romance advertisement and somehow still made him the explanation for a whole building.

Dumpling jumped onto the sofa and settled with his back to the screen. Eve spared him the request to identify the ethical flaw in an edit.

She opened the timeline. Removing a shot left a black gap. She didn't fill it immediately.

The next morning, Tessa brought a stack of blank sample forms, a disconnected demonstration phone, and a chair with a squeak.

“For authenticity?” Eve asked, touching the chair.

“Because the other one is in use.”

They sat at a cleared table, away from the working desk. Tessa showed her how the same question might need repeating because the first answer hadn't supplied the necessary detail. Eve played a fictional visitor, inventing nothing more complicated than an arrival for a booked appointment.

“Has anything changed since you spoke to us?” Tessa asked.

“I already told someone on the phone.”

“That may be in the notes. I'm checking whether there's anything new I should pass to the clinical team.”

Eve stopped herself answering as an actress who wanted the scene to go well. “I'm in a hurry. Can I just go through?”

“I'll let the team know you've arrived. I can't promise that the room is ready.”

“That sounds very simple when nothing else is happening.”

Tessa picked up the disconnected phone and held it beside her ear. With the other hand she pointed toward the door, where an imaginary delivery had arrived.

Eve began to laugh, then saw Tessa's expression and tried the exchange again.

This time she lost track of which question had been answered. Tessa hadn't. She set down the phone, returned to the point where they had paused, and explained the next step without scolding the fictional visitor for interrupting it.

“How much of that would you want in the film?” Eve asked.

“Enough that someone knows waiting isn't always nothing happening.”

“Would you want to say that yourself?”

Tessa took time to consider. “Yes. But not while smiling as if I enjoy being shouted at.”

“I won't ask you to.”

They tried a short spoken explanation. Tessa stumbled over the first sentence, then delivered the next one so briskly that Eve almost missed it. They listened back together. Eve suggested breaking the information into two parts, with a pause for the viewer to take it in.

“I sound impatient.”

“You sound like you're trying to fit it between calls.”

“That is the usual arrangement.”

“We have the room for another fifteen minutes. You don't need to borrow those seconds from anybody.”

The next take was better. Not friendlier, exactly. More like the person Eve had heard when she stopped demanding an attractive welcome and began asking a real question.

Before packing up, Eve showed Tessa the new outline. It began with arriving, asking what needed immediate attention from the clinical team, understanding the wait, and knowing who could answer which question. The clinical section remained, but it no longer made the rest of the clinic appear to exist only until a vet entered the frame.

“Can I see the cut before it goes out?” Tessa asked.

“Yes. For accuracy and how your own contribution appears. If something changes the agreed scope, I'll discuss that separately with Dr. Clark.”

“Not ask Julian whether I'm being difficult?”

Eve looked up.

“No.”

Tessa held her gaze for a moment, then nodded.

They returned the sample forms to the empty folder. At the door, the colleague covering reception asked whether Tessa was ready to take over. Tessa confirmed what still needed doing before changing places. The handover took less than a minute and contained more useful information than Eve's entire original opening.

She watched from the permitted staff area, camera already packed. There was no reason to turn a real exchange into material without asking.

On the bus home, Eve revised the filming schedule rather than sending Tessa another question for her evening. She could collect several neutral shots before opening, using blank demonstration materials. Anything showing a real visitor would require a separate decision, not a notice on the door pretending everyone had agreed. She marked the waiting-room observation as a camera-free visit and sent the plan for approval. The change meant one fewer polished corridor shot and another journey for her. Neither was a catastrophe. Both were costs of discovering what the film needed after she had begun it.

Later, she moved Tessa's interview into the timeline. It occupied the space where the black gap had been. The film grew longer, then shorter again as Eve removed a second attractive shot of Julian saying something the first had already said.

She kept a small silence before Tessa explained the next step. For the first time, reception wasn't somewhere the story passed through. It was work the viewer had time to notice.
