# Chapter 242 — No Need for a Second Branch

Four weeks after the wedding, a woman Eve knew through the neighborhood center offered to show her a room with two sinks.

The second sink was mentioned twice in the message. Eve was used to doing the washing up at the venue without leaving a place to put the clean things. She agreed to look at the room before she knew what the woman, Leena, wanted her to do with it.

The property was three bus stops away, on the main road east of the center. A greengrocer occupied the unit to its left. To its right there was a wide paved passage, which Eve measured with her eyes and briefly imagined as the answer to every question of access she'd ever been asked.

Leena unlocked the door.

The room was brighter than the one Eve rented now. Nothing had been fitted behind the display counter, and a little row of high windows admitted light without exposing the whole rear wall to the street. There was a low recess where a child could stand and see what was happening at the worktable. Eve took out her notebook before remembering she was supposed to be concentrating on what existed.

“The landlord wants an occupant with a use for it during the day,” Leena said. “The last tenant ran a Saturday business and had it dark all week. He isn't asking for your name today. I just thought you might have a use for it.”

“What sort of terms?”

“I have the initial figures. I can ask whether you could speak to him yourself.”

Eve listened. It was a normal lease proposal. There was a deposit, there were fit-out restrictions, and the suggested rent was below what she'd guessed from the street. The transit stop was better placed than the one near the old venue. The main entrance had no awkward turn immediately inside it. Both sinks worked, though one produced a strange knock when she closed the tap.

“That's a relief,” Eve said. “The plumbing has introduced itself.”

Leena laughed. “It gets lonely.”

They walked around the front in both directions before Eve went inside again. She liked the place after the second look too. The room itself did not need to become foolish for her to decide whether it was a good idea.

Leena brought out a floor plan. Two suggested pieces of furniture were drawn to scale; a broad sweep of blank paper between them had been labelled *potential flexible use*. Eve asked what that meant in practice.

“It means the architect didn't know what would go there,” Leena said.

That was a better answer than several she'd heard in her working life.

Eve made notes about the level entrance, possible washing and storage layouts, the lack of a weather-protected waiting spot, and the sound from a delivery cart passing the window. She asked what repairs belonged to the landlord, how long any rent-free fit-out period might be, and whether an inspection could be made before an agreement. Leena said the premises had not been assessed for Eve's exact use. She wouldn't pretend two taps or an old shop sign settled anything.

On the bus back, Eve began sketching a week in two places.

She put Robin at the original venue on Tuesday. Wednesday went in the new room. Before she could start Thursday, she had drawn a line through the Wednesday block to allow for her paid editing appointments. She drew it again on the other side of the page, where it met a public transport timetable she hadn't actually checked.

She could hire. She wanted to be the sort of employer who hired early enough to train people properly. That part of the thought pleased her more than the prospect of two signs on two windows.

The bus stopped. Eve stepped down, realized she was a stop too early, and walked the rest of the way with the open notebook under her arm.

At the venue, Robin was stacking boxes of handout paper on the bottom shelf. Eve caught herself ready to object to a change in the shelf arrangement, then saw Robin had placed the heavier packages lower. The upper shelf now held the things people reached for most often.

“I've seen a possible second room,” she said after they'd put the boxes away.

“Which thing are you trying to do there that we can't do here?”

Eve took off her coat. She hung it on the hook and gave herself until the hanger had stopped moving.

“Hold more sessions, maybe. Find out if there's a local group that uses the bus from the other direction. Pay someone to run part of it.”

“Would you like help costing it?”

“Yes. In your Wednesday planning block, if we could use part of it for that. Please stop when the time's up.”

Robin looked past her at the shelf. “Can I do the numbers before I tell you whether I want the job? Because I don't want a second location by default.”

The notebook still contained their name in the Tuesday slot of Eve's drawing. She thought of the box she had adjusted in her head on the bus without ever considering that her new position might put a person elsewhere than the person wanted to be.

“Of course.” She opened the notebook. “I've already written your name somewhere without asking.”

“Did I look happy in the drawing?”

“You hadn't been given a face yet.”

“I think that places limits on what we can infer.”

Eve laughed and crossed the name out. They agreed Robin would check the implications for the current room and the suggested hours, not design a second full rota for nothing.

Over the next nine days, the figures came in. The informal rent estimate became a written range with the service charges separate. A cleaner gave Eve a quote based on the size and likely use of the room. She found out that the storage shelves alone would not cost much, but the job of having them delivered, fitted and checked on an empty weekday had taken her half a day at the first location. The newer shop had its own questions about heating, furniture and accessible facilities. She kept these as uncertainties instead of asking the building to become whatever she needed its price to represent.

Robin brought out the last three months of records and added a column for what they had actually turned away. Two workshops had filled, but they didn't have a steady waiting list. Four recent inquiries had been for evening appointments rather than another address. Three people had booked private sessions after they understood where they could wait without being in the middle of the room. One person had taken a refund after realizing none of the offered dates worked. Eve called just the people who had consented to a follow-up and asked about times and access separately. A few offered nice things to say about the idea of growth. None made a booking on the strength of a general conversation.

“What happens if we start small over there?” Eve asked during the planning block.

“You can have a smaller program and still pay a whole rent,” Robin said. “Which time would you take from here?”

“I could take the late shift here myself.”

“For how many months?”

It would have been easier to answer a meaner question. Eve looked at the Tuesday column and remembered the ink of a recent invoice which she'd let dry while she took the work phone home. She liked having a proper night in with Julian. She liked being able to go for coffee alone and stay long enough to let it get cold. Running the additional room with herself as the backup for every unmanned block would buy something at the expense of things she had already chosen.

“I don't want to make it a permanent late shift,” she said. “Suppose we could fund one trained person from the outset?”

They calculated one realistic pattern. Existing quiet sessions stayed staffed and Robin kept their agreed hours, with separate leave coverage for the additional hire. The initial budget held long enough to open if reservations came in promptly. A slower quarter would mean dipping well below the reserve they'd just begun to rebuild. Nobody left the table pretending they had solved a risk by writing it in the right column.

“What would make the second room worth it to you?” Robin asked.

Eve looked at the three bus stops on the map she'd printed, then at the list of people who had asked whether there was a weekday late session. “If we knew there was something people actually needed from it. Maybe there is. We wouldn't find out much by putting our whole program there on day one.”

After Robin left, Eve called Leena. She had promised her a decision before the owner started showing the room the following week. She declined the lease discussion and thanked Leena for giving her enough time to do the assessment honestly.

“Is it the money?” Leena asked.

“Partly. It's also not where most of the demand seems to be. What would help right now is one later weekday here and someone trained to share the work we've already got.”

Leena did not argue with her. The other room would be offered to someone else, and Eve would not get the sinks. She missed them for precisely as long as it took to wash the last two mugs in their own.

The decision did not end the questions about widening access. Eve made an estimate for running a once-a-month later session in the current venue, paying whoever covered it and arranging to use their actual agreed entrance hours. She asked Robin to look at which shift it could replace rather than add. They would trial the later time only if the schedules and sign-ups supported it. The second item was to budget practical cover for the work they already did, so a canceled train or a sick day didn't turn Eve into an emergency resource.

It was a less exciting email to write than an announcement about another branch. It also had a person who could answer each of its questions.

She got home with the floor plan still folded inside her notebook. Julian was at the kitchen table eating the cracked halves of biscuits from the bottom of a packet. Dumpling sat underneath, looking up at him without appreciable faith in the arrangement.

“How was the room?” Julian asked.

“Beautiful.”

“Are we getting it?”

“No. Somebody else will be very happy in it, with two sinks.”

He moved the packet toward her.

“Can our sink do anything to help?”

“It's trying a new approach where it leaves me to my own devices.”

He offered her a broken biscuit with chocolate on both pieces. She ate one half and left him the other.

“I wanted it,” she said.

“You can want it and turn it down.”

“I know. I'm going to need a minute about the windows as well.”

He stayed where he was while she took it. Through their own window, she could see a light being switched on in the apartment opposite. Somebody brought a watering can to the glass and tilted it over a plant, very slowly, waiting for the water to reach the soil.

Eve closed the notebook. She had not committed to more weeks than she had, and she hadn't had to make a bad building out of a good one. The attractive lease was somebody else's possibility now. On Wednesday, when she sat down with Robin again, they would have their own room and one actual late opening to work out.
