# Chapter 170 — A Fridge Magnet from Out of Town

The dog had five lumps and no convincing legs.

Eve turned the little magnet over in her hand, hoping the back might offer an explanation.

“It says dog,” Mia told her.

“On the packaging?”

“On the shelf.”

“Was there a potato shelf nearby?”

Mia's smile widened. “I thought you'd appreciate him.”

Eve looked again. Two dark dots served as eyes. The object was brown, roughly oval, and decorated with a cheerful red collar that seemed to be doing most of the work of identifying its species. One ear stood up. The other appeared to have given up halfway through manufacture.

“I appreciate his determination.”

“He was the last one.”

“An exclusive piece.”

“Or the one nobody else bought.”

Mia had returned the previous evening. She had messaged only after arriving home, receiving the handover, and settling back into the routine. Eve knew the trip had happened, that the care arrangement had held, and that the conference room had indeed possessed no windows. The rest Mia had saved for today.

The venue smelled faintly of fresh paper and the rain drying from people's coats. Eve had booked a short final trial session before the formal opening, with the same modest number of places. Its subject was ordinary things that made household arrangements clearer. Two people had come back from the first exchange; another had found the plain booking description through a friend. Mabel was taking off her scarf with the authority of someone prepared to review everybody's labels.

Robin set an empty tray in the middle of the table.

“Objects go here if you want to share them. Nothing private, nothing that somebody else needs to take care of an animal today.”

Mia placed the magnet in the tray.

Robin looked down.

“An excellent beginning. Nobody knows what this is.”

“A dog,” Mia said firmly.

Mabel peered over her glasses. “Poor thing.”

The laughter that followed was immediate and uncoached. Eve felt the familiar temptation to preserve it. She could imagine the clip, the small caption, the easy warmth of people laughing together in a room she was trying to make known.

Her phone stayed by the booking list.

Robin asked who would like to begin. The woman who had made the balcony sign lifted her hand, then lowered it when she realized there was no need to wait for schoolroom permission.

“My partner has actually been checking the latch,” she said. “The drawing helped. The flower apparently looks threatening.”

“Was that intentional?” Eve asked.

“Not initially.”

She had brought a copy of the sign. The flower's petals resembled stern eyebrows around a dark center. Robin leaned it beside the magnet, and the objects looked as if they were holding one another accountable.

Mabel produced one of the loose paper sleeves from her tins. It said HUMAN BISCUITS in large, decisive letters.

“I've kept two labels,” she announced. “The buttons have moved, so I don't need three.”

“Did your neighbor understand the arrangement?” Mia asked.

“After we tried it. They thought the blue bowl meant the pale blue one, which I had forgotten existed.”

Eve laughed. “The sequel.”

“It's now in another cupboard. I have enough blue in my life.”

Mabel had also discovered that the neighbor could not comfortably reach the shelf she originally used. They moved the relevant supplies to an easier place together, then checked that it still worked for Mabel herself. She spoke of it without treating the small alteration as surrendering her kitchen.

“I can find everything,” she said. “Which is more than I can say for my glasses.”

“They're on your face,” Mia told her.

“Today.”

When Mia's turn came, she picked up the magnet, then put it down again.

“I went away for three days,” she said. “For work. I hadn't done that since Toby came.”

The newer guest asked whether the dog had traveled with her.

“No. We arranged care at home with someone he already knew. Different cover from the usual visits. We tried it beforehand.”

“Was he all right?”

“The carer said the routine was mostly familiar. There were a couple of things they checked with me. I came home to a dog who wanted his usual evening, not a ceremony.”

Eve liked the answer. It left room for the actual experience without offering a promise that every dog or every household would manage the same arrangement.

Mia took a folded page from her bag but kept it facedown.

“I brought my own copy to show how much I crossed out. Not the details.”

She held it up from the blank side, letting the group see the thickness of the attached corrections without exposing anyone's contacts or home arrangements.

“The old version assumed Leah would be home. She wasn't. It also assumed that saying the normal food explained anything.”

Mabel made a sympathetic sound.

“The carer asked better questions than I did. And I had to pay for the actual time I needed.”

“Did that make the trip not worth it?” the newer guest asked.

Mia considered the question. Eve was glad Robin didn't rush into the silence.

“It made it more expensive. I decided it was still worth doing. I wouldn't automatically say yes to another one without checking again.”

“What was the work like?” Eve asked.

Mia's face changed. She described arriving with the wrong building entrance on her phone, correcting a presentation detail someone senior had missed, and having lunch beside a woman whose work she had followed for months. The woman had been worrying about spinach in her teeth. Mia had found this unexpectedly comforting.

There had been a difficult question in the afternoon. She had answered it herself.

Eve watched her draw the shape of the conference table in the air, suddenly animated enough to forget the paper in her lap. This was the part that had been hidden behind all the logistics. Someone had wanted to leave home for a reason that mattered, and had done the work required to make leaving possible.

“The chairs were terrible,” Mia concluded. “I thought of yours with great affection.”

“Please put that in a review,” Robin said. “Less terrible than the conference chairs.”

They laughed, and the newer guest asked if the next session might cover making a handover for ordinary household plants as well as pet supplies. Her own trouble involved an aunt who watered everything with equal enthusiasm.

Robin asked what sort of activity she hoped for rather than promising plant expertise. They could compare descriptions and labels, not provide horticultural rescue. Mabel said she could supply a photograph of a plant that had survived despite her directions, which sounded suitably qualified.

Eve wrote the idea down.

Before the exercise ended, Mabel pointed at the next-session card by the entrance.

“Is that a three or an eight?”

“Three,” Eve said.

“Then give it room to be one.”

Eve had squeezed the time beneath a line of welcoming copy. She took the card down, crossed out the unnecessary sentence, and rewrote the time larger. From Mabel's chair, it became legible. The little adjustment spoiled the balance of the design and improved the information.

“Better,” Mabel said.

Eve left the correction visible. She could make a clean version later; nobody needed to wait for her favorite typeface to find out when the door would open.

The group tried a small final exercise with the harmless objects on the table: describe one clearly enough that someone facing away could identify it from a drawing. The balcony flower proved unexpectedly easy. Mabel's label required no drawing at all. The dog magnet produced three versions of a potato and one anxious bean.

Mia held the bean up beside the original.

“This one has captured his spirit.”

“Are you certain the spirit is canine?” Robin asked.

“Completely.”

They finished on time. Eve offered the next published session date without pushing anybody to book immediately. The newer guest wanted to check her work schedule. Mabel said she would decide later. Two of the returning participants booked places, and Mia chose a different date that suited her own calendar.

Eve checked the entries herself. Real bookings, at the stated price. Not a show of hands, not compliments she had quietly converted into expected income.

There were still empty spaces.

She let them remain visible.

After the others left, Mia lingered to give Eve the magnet properly.

“For the room,” she said. “If you want him.”

“I do. What name does he answer to?”

“He doesn't. He has boundaries.”

Eve tried him on the side of the metal cupboard. He stayed. Beneath his peculiar body she tucked the public card showing the next exchange date. No private care notes, no grand inspirational sentence. Just a time people could choose to come back.

Mia stood beside her, studying the result.

“Thank you,” she said.

“You found the carer. You spoke to the housemates. You made the arrangements.”

“I know. That's what I'm thanking you for helping me do.”

Eve turned toward her.

“I thought you might end up offering to take Toby,” Mia continued. “And then I would have said yes because I was worried, and it would have been too much, and I'd have felt awful.”

“I did think about offering more than I could manage.”

“I noticed.”

They smiled at one another with the slightly uncomfortable affection of people who had caught an old habit before it swallowed the afternoon.

Mia went home before the time she had planned to relieve the familiar cover arranged for today's session. She did not stretch it because the conversation was pleasant. Eve saw her glance back through the window at the magnet and wave, apparently to the dog.

Robin counted the remaining supplies while Eve checked the bookings and receipts. They talked briefly about what had worked, including the time allowed for participants to describe something without being interrupted by advice. Then Robin packed their bag and left at the end of the paid clearing period.

The room became quiet.

Eve sat on the plain chair in the empty corner, looking at the cupboard. The magnet was too small to be seen from a good promotional angle. Close up, it was frankly hideous. Yet it held the date card flat with impressive competence.

She took a private photograph and sent it to Julian.

Our first traveling benefactor.

His reply arrived a little later.

Is the potato an animal?

She laughed aloud in the empty room.

The shop said dog, she wrote.

Then I defer to the shop.

Remember the rubbish bin.

I am trying not to.

Eve put the phone away. She still had opening preparations to finish, and the returning bookings did not settle the rent for the next three months. They did tell her something she had wanted to know: people had found enough use in the room to choose another ordinary afternoon there.

Before locking up, she straightened the date card. The magnet leaned slightly to one side, as if listening for a compliment no reasonable person would offer.

“You're staying,” she told it.

Then she switched off the lights and went home to the cat who required no such announcement.
