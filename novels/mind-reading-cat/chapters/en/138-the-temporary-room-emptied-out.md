# Chapter 138 — The Temporary Room, Emptied Out

The blue door opened onto a stack of mounting board.

Eve had to turn sideways while the woman with the key moved a carton away from the entrance. Behind it stood a narrow trolley, several flat parcels, and a stool with one foot resting on a folded piece of card.

“Sorry,” Ada said. “I cleared a path yesterday. Then a delivery came.”

“I know that kind of path.”

Ada ran a framing workshop in the front unit. She had taken the rear room later, for storage, and had inherited a folder of building arrangements she admitted she had never read completely.

“Nothing terribly interesting. Mostly what you can't put down the drain.”

Eve stepped inside.

Without the cups, the table, and the half-visible people, she had trouble recognizing the room from the sale photograph. The ceiling was lower than she expected. A window high on one wall had been covered to protect the stored materials from light. A short sink stood in the corner. Beneath it was an empty gap where something had once been fixed.

She had left Dumpling at home. Julian was working. Coming alone had seemed sensible until the door opened and Eve discovered how much she had wanted somebody beside her to witness whatever this place became.

At present it was becoming a storeroom.

Ada put the folder on a clear end of the trolley.

“I marked the pages that mention the old use. There are other people's details in some of the rest, so I'd rather keep those closed.”

“Of course.”

The first page was a temporary permission to use the rear room. The listed purposes included sorting donated supplies and short-term animal care under the organizers' arrangements. The date preceded the photographs Daniel had provided. Later pages recorded changes in access times and a return of keys.

Eve read the lines twice.

There had been a place here where animals stayed. That much no longer depended on a man remembering bags carried through a door.

“Were cats kept here all the time?” she asked.

“I don't know. I wasn't here then.”

“Sorry. I meant whether the folder says.”

Ada pulled the trolley closer. “This sheet looks like a room calendar.”

It was. Some days had a short note about supplies. Others had initials or an arrow. A block had been crossed out and a different room number written beside it.

Eve touched the edge of the page.

“So they moved.”

“The booking moved,” Ada said. “I can't tell you what was in it.”

Eve took her hand away.

On her own calendar, a changed room meant carrying a box and rewriting the directions. It did not contain the full lives of the people who would arrive. This page couldn't carry the lives of cats merely because she wanted it to.

Ada found an inventory: folding table, storage tubs, cleaning equipment, two low shelves. The animal-care supplies were listed as belonging to the group and were not itemized there. A later receipt recorded the return of the building's tables.

No names of animals. No movements between households. No explanation for an orange-and-white cat beneath a trolley.

Eve checked the room-use dates against the date of the charity sale. They overlapped. Daniel's photographs belonged inside the same period. The agreement could support the place and its purpose, but it could not put Dumpling to bed on any particular night.

“Would you mind if I copied the relevant lines and dates?” she asked.

Ada let her photograph those pages with the unrelated contact details covered. Eve showed her each image before saving it. The current storage arrangements did not need to become part of somebody else's search file.

From the workshop came the sound of a delivery bell.

“I need to answer that,” Ada said. “You can look around from here. Please don't open any boxes.”

“I won't.”

When Ada left, the room became quieter than Eve expected.

She stood beside the trolley and tried to imagine a bed near the sink. Then she tried the opposite corner. Both felt plausible. Neither meant anything.

In the old picture, the cat had looked away from the camera. There could have been food beyond the frame, or a person, or a loose thread worth pursuing. Eve herself had stood behind cameras while somebody waited patiently for her to stop arranging a scene and go home.

She wished she knew who had waited here.

A pale rectangle marked the wall where a shelf had protected the paint. Under the window, two hooks remained. Their purpose was impossible to guess from their emptiness, though she immediately offered herself three options.

She made no photographs of them.

Instead she stood near the doorway and matched the angle of the sale picture as well as the cartons allowed. The sink, door frame, and high window were enough to confirm that this was the room. She asked Ada's permission when she returned, then took a single wider image without the workshop's parcels or labels.

“You look disappointed,” Ada said.

Eve lowered the phone. “I think I expected it to feel like somewhere he belonged.”

“Rooms change.”

“Yes.”

“I mean mine does. Last month you couldn't have stood there. I'd ordered too much backing board.”

Eve smiled. The explanation helped more than a solemn remark about the past would have.

Ada found one further sheet, a notice thanking people for help during the temporary arrangement. It directed future supply deliveries through the neighborhood center. The person who had coordinated the room bookings was named, but the printed number was old.

“I can ask the center whether they'll pass on a message,” Ada said. “I know the present administrator. I can't promise the person still wants inquiries.”

“That would be useful. Only if they're willing.”

Eve wrote a short explanation of what she wanted to check and gave Ada the same contact route she had used with Daniel. She did not ask for a list of former volunteers to work through without warning.

While Ada sent the request, Eve helped hold the outer door for the new delivery. The parcel was wider than it looked. Ada turned it, caught one corner against the jamb, and muttered at the geometry.

“Lower on your side,” Eve said.

They tried again. It passed.

For a few seconds she had a task she understood completely: support one end until the other person had somewhere to put theirs.

They set the parcel safely against the rack. Ada thanked her and locked the blue door behind them.

Eve waited on the pavement for her bus. She had photographs of documents, a room identification, and a possible contact. She should have felt the satisfaction of making progress.

Instead she kept seeing the empty space beneath the sink.

A woman waiting beside her had a paper bag containing bread. Its warm smell reached Eve whenever the wind changed. She realized she had spent the whole visit thinking about where a cat might have eaten while forgetting her own lunch.

She got off one stop early and bought something hot.

At home, Dumpling ignored her return until she opened the refrigerator. Then he arrived at her ankle, bringing an opinion about how much of the day had already been wasted.

“Your lunch was served,” she told him.

He looked at the food in her hand.

“Mine wasn't.”

She ate at the table and worked on a client revision for an hour. A sequence of instructional photographs needed rearranging because the finished item appeared before the step that made it possible. Fixing the order required no knowledge of anyone's childhood. She moved the picture, rewrote a caption, and sent the new proof.

Only then did she organize the room records.

By late afternoon the former bookings coordinator had agreed to a short call. She remembered the temporary care arrangement, but had handled premises and deliveries rather than animals. Eve began with the dates she had, reading them from the pages instead of asking whether they sounded about right.

“Yes, that was the period,” the woman said. “We had those rooms for limited stretches. Sometimes only the supply sorting happened there. The people doing animal care kept their own arrangements.”

“Was there a problem that made everyone leave?”

“Which time?”

Eve went still.

The woman explained. One booking ended when the room returned to another use. Another changed because a person responsible for opening the building was no longer available at those hours. Equipment went to the center. Animals, where any had been staying, were supposed to move under the carers' arrangements. She had not supervised those transfers.

“Do you know whether that happened?” Eve asked.

“Not for an individual cat. I wouldn't want to tell you I checked something I didn't check.”

Eve wrote that down too.

There had been changes of people and rooms. There might have been handovers for Dumpling, if he was among the animals staying there. The records did not say whether he had slept inside, visited from somewhere else, or followed a bowl to Daniel's step.

“Do any care records survive?”

“I don't have them. The center may have old event material. That won't necessarily tell you who cared for a particular animal.”

“Would it be all right to ask whether they can check?”

“Yes. I'll pass your inquiry on. But please don't expect an answer this week. Their old files aren't all cataloged.”

Eve thanked her. She repeated the few points she intended to retain, and the woman corrected one: the keys had been returned on the recorded date, but that did not establish the last day an animal had been present.

Eve changed the note.

Afterward, she looked at the words temporary care until they began to blur.

Temporary had sounded manageable when she first brought Dumpling home. It had given her a way to carry him through the door without imagining every future bill. Somewhere else, the same word might have described the room people could afford, or the time they could offer, or the plan before another plan failed.

She could imagine a cat learning the sounds of different kitchens. She could imagine a bed being lifted and put somewhere new. She could imagine the exhausting effort of watching every bag.

She could not put any of those scenes into his record as fact.

Julian messaged after work, asking how the visit had gone. Eve sent the short version, then added that she felt sadder than she had expected.

He asked whether she wanted a call or company. She looked at the time, the unfinished washing up, and Dumpling now standing with his front paws on the edge of his bed.

“Company tomorrow,” she wrote. “Tonight I think I'd like to stay here quietly.”

He agreed and asked what time would suit.

They settled it without turning tonight into a failure to support each other.

Eve put the papers away. Dumpling circled twice, caught a fold of his blanket with one paw, and pushed it until it suited him. Then he lowered himself into the familiar hollow.

She couldn't tell him who had locked the blue door last.

She turned on the lamp beside his bed and went to wash the dishes in the room where he was sleeping now.
