# Chapter 110 — A Different Way of Being Seen

Tessa was eating an orange when Eve arrived.

Not standing beside the desk with half a segment hidden in her palm. Not apologizing for the smell while reaching for a ringing phone. She sat at the staff-room table with the peel gathered neatly on a napkin and a book open beside her.

Eve stopped in the doorway.

“Am I early?”

“Eight minutes.”

“I can wait.”

“Yes,” Tessa said pleasantly. “You can.”

Eve retreated to the permitted waiting chair outside the room and opened her own book. It was the secondhand one she had bought with Julian. The old shopping list still marked a chapter she hadn't reached.

For eight minutes she read about something entirely unrelated to the clinic.

When Tessa came out, she carried the empty peel toward the bin and asked whether Eve had the final files. The agreed handover was the last part of the commission: copies, captions, transcript, and a short explanation of which version was current. Eve had brought no camera.

They checked the folder together. Dr. Clark joined them, confirmed receipt, and asked about updating a sentence later if arrangements changed.

“A small factual edit can be quoted when you know what you need,” Eve said. “Please don't keep an outdated promise because you're worried changing it requires a whole new film.”

He nodded and put that note beside the file information.

Tessa opened the caption file herself. She found the approved version, then deliberately opened the older review copy that still sat in the clinic's working folder.

“This is how somebody will use the wrong one,” she said.

They moved the obsolete review copy into a clearly marked internal archive while keeping the approved deliverables easy to find. Eve watched Tessa test the folder from the beginning again. The work had a satisfying lack of glamour.

Before leaving, Eve asked whether the cover trial was continuing.

“Yes,” Tessa said. “With changes.”

Dr. Clark excused himself to answer a question from the clinical team. Tessa sat back down, but she didn't open her private notes.

“Some duties have been clarified. The regular extra coordination time is now included in my agreed hours and pay. We have a review date. It isn't every change I asked for.”

Eve wanted to ask which ones were missing. She could hear the question arranging itself into an interview.

Instead she said, “Are you comfortable with what you've agreed for now?”

“For now. And I can reopen it at the review. That's part of the agreement.”

“I'm glad.”

“Me too. Not continuously. On Wednesday I was still annoyed.”

Eve laughed.

Tessa folded the top of her paper lunch bag. “The first time I actually left the desk for the full break, I spent half of it wondering what was happening out there.”

“Did you go back?”

“No. The person covering had the handover and knew how to ask for help. I walked to the corner shop instead.”

“What did you buy?”

“Nothing. I had forgotten why I went.”

There was a quiet comedy in freedom arriving before an errand to use it on. Eve liked the story so much that her mind offered a whole ending for the portfolio piece: a receptionist, a street, a first unhurried breath.

She let the image pass without reaching for her phone.

“Do you want that left out of any public account?” she asked.

“Yes. That's just something I'm telling you.”

“Then it stays here.”

Tessa nodded, not extravagantly grateful. Eve had done no more than respect the distinction she was finally learning to notice.

They walked back toward reception. Another colleague was at the desk, finishing a question before returning responsibility to Tessa. The handover wasn't seamless. A note needed clarifying. Tessa asked, the colleague answered, and neither pretended the answer had already been obvious.

Eve waited until they had finished to say goodbye.

At the entrance, an owner asked whether the film meant all appointments would now run on time. Eve explained that she was the filmmaker, not the person who could discuss the clinic's schedule, and directed the question to the desk. She waited only long enough to see that the visitor had found the right person. The new film could still be misunderstood. That did not make the work pointless, but it did make her grand private sense of a finished lesson rather premature. She added the question to her project notes without the visitor's identity, for the limited follow-up they had already agreed.

Outside, she stood for a moment in the cold sunlight, unsure why the morning had left her so pleased. There had been no rescue, no dramatic reconciliation, and no person weeping because she had finally been understood. The invoice still needed to be paid on the agreed date. The clinic still had delays.

But Tessa had finished an orange.

Eve made herself keep that thought private too.

On the walk home she called the organizer of the market to confirm the next-season inquiry had not become an urgent booking while she was occupied elsewhere. It hadn't. The committee was still considering whether a smaller owners' exchange would be more useful than another full market.

“Would you hold the first Saturday?” the organizer asked.

“I can note your preference, but I can't reserve it without an agreed brief and booking. I can tell you when I need a decision.”

They chose a date for the next conversation. Eve wrote it down after the call, standing safely out of the pedestrian flow rather than trying to type while walking.

The two projects sat beside each other in her calendar. One ending, one still uncertain. Neither had to expand to fill every available evening.

Julian had suggested dinner that night to celebrate the completed film. Eve had said yes, with the condition that neither of them use the meal as an unofficial staff meeting.

He arrived at the restaurant carrying a folded umbrella and a paperback. There was a mark on his coat where rain had caught him anyway.

“Effective equipment,” Eve said.

“I opened it after getting wet. A timing problem.”

“Will you be making an instructional video?”

“I know someone expensive.”

She grinned. The joke no longer had a hidden request inside it.

They sat at a narrow table where both their knees competed with one wooden support. After trying three arrangements, Julian turned his chair slightly and asked whether he was blocking the passage. The server shook her head and brought menus.

“Are we allowed to mention the film once?” he asked.

“Once each.”

“I like that the desk gets to finish a sentence.”

“Thank you. I like that the project has a final invoice.”

“Both moving artistic statements.”

“Now you're out of mentions.”

He closed the menu. “I haven't decided what to eat.”

“Fortunately that remains in scope.”

For several minutes they discussed food with the unreasonable seriousness of people relieved not to discuss anything consequential. Eve chose something spicy and regretted the first bite. Julian offered water without announcing that he'd expected this. She thanked him and continued more slowly.

He told her about the book in his bag. It was not about medicine, though she had assumed it would be. A friend had recommended it, and he disliked the first chapter enough to want to know whether the rest improved.

“Why keep going?” she asked.

“Because my friend said the opening was misleading. I trust her taste more than my first ten pages.”

Eve wondered how many ordinary details of his friendships she still didn't know. She asked about the friend instead of trying to infer a whole history from the pronoun. He told her how they had met and the kind of books they disagreed about. It was pleasantly unthreatening information, supplied because she had asked.

Her phone vibrated inside her bag. She recognized the brief pulse of a message and left it there until they had finished the conversation. When Julian went to wash his hands, she checked: a routine file question from the clinic, no urgent care issue and nothing requiring her attention tonight.

She marked it to answer during work hours the next day.

By the time he returned, she had put the phone away. He did not need a demonstration of how well she had behaved. They ordered dessert and discovered neither had accurately estimated the size of the main course.

“Share one?” she asked.

“Yes. I reserve the right to misjudge that too.”

They chose something warm with two spoons. It was not an anniversary or a milestone anyone else would remember. Eve liked that he had wanted to celebrate a completed ordinary job, and that celebration did not require her to turn the entire dinner into a report of it.

Afterward, they walked toward her building beneath the umbrella, now open at the appropriate time. It was too narrow for both of them to remain entirely dry. They changed sides when the wind turned and laughed at the elaborate choreography of avoiding a puddle neither had seen soon enough.

At her door, he asked if she wanted company upstairs.

“For tea, yes. I need an early night after that.”

“Tea, then home.”

She kissed him before unlocking the door, because the clear answer made it easier to want him close rather than wonder what she was committing to by inviting him in.

Dumpling met them with a stretch and a brief inspection of the damp umbrella. Eve put it somewhere safe to dry, out of his reach, and checked that his familiar sleeping place was undisturbed.

Julian sat on the sofa. Dumpling immediately took the cushion beside him. Eve brought the mugs and found her planned seat occupied by an animal making no apology.

“I see the arrangement has been revised,” she said.

“We can ask him to move.”

“He's allowed that cushion. I'll take this end.”

They sat with the cat between them until he decided their stillness was boring and went elsewhere. Then Eve moved closer of her own accord. No hidden intention needed deciphering, and no paw had authority over where either human slept that night.

Before Julian left, she showed him the passage she had read during Tessa's break. He listened, then offered a different reading of a sentence. They disagreed for the length of the hallway and remained fond of each other throughout.

When the door closed, Eve washed the two mugs. She answered no work messages. The film could do its modest job without her watching the number beneath it every hour.

She thought of asking Sophie to meet the next afternoon. They had talked several times about the market, but there had been less room for anything else lately. Eve opened their conversation, saw her own last three messages began with a practical question, and paused before adding another.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would ask about a time that suited Sophie.

She put down the phone without composing an invitation that assumed the answer.
