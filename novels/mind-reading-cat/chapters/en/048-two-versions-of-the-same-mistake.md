# Chapter 048 — Two Versions of the Same Mistake

On Sunday, Eve sent the message she had promised.

*Thinking of you for tomorrow. I'm here if you want company afterward. No answer needed now.*

She read it once to make sure there was no disguised question about Saturday's staffing, then put the phone beside the sink. Dumpling was investigating the clean dish towel she had dropped over a chair. He tugged a corner, failed to move the whole towel, and pretended the attempt had been an inspection.

Sophie replied after lunch.

*I'm still going to the meeting. Please don't send me a speech. I've already written three and dislike all of them.*

*No speeches,* Eve wrote.

That was the entire check-in. She didn't send an encouraging quotation, a better opening sentence, or an offer to leave her own unfinished work and arrive with pastries.

Later she contacted Julian.

*Could we talk tomorrow? About Friday. I'd rather not keep having the argument by myself.*

His answer came that evening. He could leave the clinic at six, unless an actual appointment ran late. There was a small place around the corner that sold baked potatoes and had tables outside.

Eve said yes.

By Monday afternoon she had made the hall's sample cards, checked the print cost, and removed three questions from the discussion plan. One would have asked people to describe their worst outing. It sounded lively until she imagined spending half a short session helping somebody recover from telling a story they hadn't meant to share with strangers.

She replaced it with a simpler prompt: *What do you take now that you used to forget?*

Then she tried running a round with the kitchen chairs standing in for people. She put a card at each place, set a timer, and spoke the opening aloud.

Four minutes in, she imagined a late arrival asking where to sit. She stopped to find the spare materials, returned to her chair, and discovered she had forgotten the question she was supposed to be listening to.

The exercise felt ridiculous. It was also more convincing than the tidy grid.

Keeping a second person in the revised budget wasn't a way to disappear from the hall. It meant somebody could greet newcomers while she stayed with the discussion. She wrote that distinction beside the duties, then checked whether the quoted fee covered preparation as well as the event itself.

It didn't yet.

She added a paid half hour and subtracted it from her own projected return. The remaining figure would still make the job worthwhile. She left it visible instead of adjusting the hours until the profit looked more flattering.

Dumpling jumped onto an empty chair and knocked a sample card flat. Eve collected the paper before it became his contribution.

At five forty, Julian messaged.

*I'm sorry. I agreed to cover the last hour. Seven rather than six. Does that still work?*

Eve looked at the bag she had already packed.

She could have said no. She checked what the later hour would displace: supper, an hour of reading, nothing urgent. She agreed to seven and ate a slice of toast rather than making hunger a test of patience.

At six fifty-five, the clinic's front room was quiet. Tessa was replacing a stack of service cards beside the desk. She greeted Eve, glanced at the clock, and said Julian was finishing his notes.

“I'll wait outside,” Eve said.

“Thank you. I need to close this entrance.”

Eve didn't take that personally. She stepped back onto the pavement and watched Tessa turn the sign, gather her things, and leave through the staff door several minutes later.

“Good night,” Tessa said. “Please make sure he knows the eight o'clock cover is already arranged.”

“Is he supposed to be doing that too?”

“No. That's why I'm saying it.”

She looked tired rather than amused. Eve decided not to turn the remark into a request for the rest of somebody else's rota.

Julian emerged at seven ten, fastening his jacket.

“Sorry.”

“Is eight o'clock arranged?”

He stopped with one hand at the zip.

“Tessa?”

“Yes. She didn't give me a dossier.”

“I'd said I could stay if they needed me.”

“Do they?”

“No.”

“Then potatoes?”

“Potatoes.”

The shop was nearly empty. They ordered at the counter, carried their trays outside, and chose a table where the chair legs didn't wobble on the paving. Julian tried the nearest one, moved it an inch, tried again, then exchanged it with the next table's spare.

Eve sat down while he was still testing the replacement.

“It doesn't have to support a surgical procedure.”

“It should support my dinner.”

Ordinarily she would have laughed more. Tonight the small joke settled between two things they hadn't said.

She opened her potato with the wooden fork.

“I changed the jobs,” she said. “One onsite event. A separate shoot on Thursday. Later delivery.”

“Does that leave enough preparation time?”

“Yes. Assuming I arrange a second person properly for the hall. I'm confirming that tomorrow.”

“Good.”

She glanced up.

He set down his fork. “That wasn't intended as approval you had to earn.”

“I know. I think.”

She described the discussion with Sophie, including the fee she had omitted. Saying it aloud to Julian was uncomfortable. There was no flattering version in which she had independently discovered the problem before her friend had to point at her own name.

“I was angry with her,” Eve admitted.

“For saying no?”

“For making the no difficult to argue with.”

He nodded slowly.

“And I was angry with you because you chose which job mattered before asking me.”

“I did.”

“I still needed to change something. Both things can apparently happen on the same pavement.”

Julian pushed his plate away just enough to rest his hands beside it.

“I heard the hours and went straight to removing them,” he said. “I didn't ask what either offer meant to you.”

“The shop means somebody came back.”

He looked at her.

“Felix has already seen something go wrong,” she continued. “He still asked me for another project. I know that doesn't mean I should accept everything, but it matters.”

“Yes,” Julian said. “I can see that.”

Eve swallowed a mouthful before it could become difficult to swallow for a less practical reason.

“What was the extra hour tonight?” she asked.

“Cover.”

“I got that from the message.”

He picked up his fork, then put it down again.

“A colleague needed to leave. I was already there.”

“And eight o'clock?”

“I wasn't needed.”

“But you offered.”

“Yes.”

“How often lately?”

“Eve.”

The warning in his voice made her sit straighter.

“I'm not asking why anyone needed help. I'm asking about the time you keep giving away while telling me to cancel work.”

The words came out harder than she intended. He looked toward the street, where a bus had stopped long enough to fill the conversation with its engine.

When it moved on, he said, “Two early starts since last Thursday. Tonight. A possibility for Saturday afternoon that hasn't been decided.”

She had expected something smaller. More irritatingly, she had expected the answer to make her feel vindicated. Instead she pictured him checking the same notes under the same light, one more time because he was already there.

“Did Dr. Clark ask for all of that?”

“No.”

“Did you tell him it replaced your time off?”

“I've moved some things.”

“Where?”

He didn't answer immediately.

Eve looked down at the half-eaten potato. Her old grid had been full of moved things too. She had relocated sleep as though it were a chair that could stand in the hallway for a few days.

“You can't prescribe rest for me from a timetable you won't show anyone,” she said.

“I'm not your doctor.”

“Exactly.”

Neither of them smiled.

The shop owner came out to collect an empty tray from another table. Eve waited until he went inside again. She didn't want to turn a disagreement into a performance for an unwilling audience.

“I don't mean you're forbidden to worry,” she said. “I wanted you to understand what I was risking. You wanted me to stop before I got exhausted. But you acted as if that gave you the deciding vote.”

“It didn't.”

“And discovering your hours doesn't give me one either.”

Julian looked at her for a moment, then picked up his fork.

“I don't know how to leave a gap when I know I could fill it,” he said.

The sentence was quiet enough that she had to lean forward slightly to hear it.

“What happens if you don't volunteer first?”

“We discuss coverage.”

“With people who can actually arrange it?”

“Yes.”

She let him hear his own answer.

A gust rattled the paper napkins beneath her hand. Julian moved the salt container onto the stack. It was an ordinary useful thing, and she let it be useful without attaching an argument to it.

“I'll talk to Dr. Clark tomorrow,” he said. “Before agreeing to Saturday.”

“What will you ask for?”

“The afternoon off that's already on my schedule. And a finish time on Tuesday that doesn't disappear because I'm still in the building.”

“Would it help to write that down?”

“I can remember two sentences.”

“So can I. I still managed to leave Sophie out of an entire budget.”

His mouth moved, almost a smile, then settled again.

“I'll write it down.”

She didn't ask to inspect the note.

They finished eating. The potatoes had cooled at the edges, and Eve had to scrape the last bit from the skin with a fork increasingly unsuited to its responsibilities. Julian broke his own fork trying to do the same.

For several seconds they looked at the two pieces.

Then he went inside for a spoon.

It shouldn't have been funny. Eve still laughed, once, and felt the tightness in her shoulders ease without the conversation being erased.

On the walk back, she checked a message from Sophie.

*Meeting happened. They need to come back on duties and pay. Nothing settled yet. I don't want to rehearse the whole conversation tonight.*

Eve replied that she understood. She didn't tell Julian the details. When he asked whether everything was all right, she said only that her friend had had a difficult work conversation and wasn't ready to discuss it.

He accepted that.

At the crossing, Eve said, “I wanted to show you the new grid so you could see I'd fixed it.”

“You don't have to.”

“I know. But I might want to show you something without it becoming a request for you to fix the rest.”

“Tell me when that's what you want.”

“I'll try.”

He waited until the light changed.

“I might need the same arrangement,” he said.

“I'll try that too.”

They parted where their routes separated. No one offered to complete the other's remaining evening. Eve went home, fed Dumpling, and put the sample cards in a drawer before he could sit on the drying ink.

The night's final task was to remove one extra design she had added to the hall proposal for free. The coordinator hadn't requested it. It would make the display prettier and steal an hour she hadn't included in the quote.

Eve saved the simpler file.

Then she wrote to Sophie about a possible paid role, with duties, hours, and a request for her rate. She said a refusal would be fine, and this time made sure the project could survive one.

Only after sending it did she close the laptop.
