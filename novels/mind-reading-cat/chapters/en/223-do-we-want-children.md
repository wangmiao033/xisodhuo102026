# Chapter 223 — Do We Want Children?

The fox's head came off halfway through the performance.

For a moment the little theater went silent. A hand rose behind the painted wall, caught the fabric head, and lowered it out of sight. The fox's body remained upright, wearing a rather startled human thumb.

A child in the front row gasped.

“It's all right,” the narrator said. “He has temporarily lost his thoughts.”

Eve pressed her lips together. Beside her, Julian made a sound that suggested a cough attempting to become an apology.

They had bought tickets because Eve had seen a poster for the neighborhood puppet matinee and said she missed watching things she hadn't been hired to organize. The listing welcomed all ages. They had taken seats at the back, with no children to justify their presence and no intention of reviewing the event afterwards.

The fox returned with his head attached and continued trying to steal a cabbage.

“Why would a fox want a cabbage?” Julian whispered.

“Ambition isn't always well directed.”

The family ahead of them was managing an entirely different performance. One child wanted to sit on an adult's lap. The other wanted the same lap only after discovering it was occupied. Their mother shifted a coat from one knee to the other while the second adult quietly negotiated an exchange of seats.

Nobody looked transformed by joy. Nobody looked as though love had failed. The show continued around the ordinary work of being there.

Eve watched the stage again.

By the end, the cabbage had become a house, the fox had acquired a job painting doors, and the children in the audience were correcting the narrator's deliberately terrible counting. The applause was enthusiastic and slightly uneven. Julian clapped until the puppeteers came out, then admired the construction of the fox from a respectful distance.

“Don't buy it,” Eve said.

“I wasn't going to.”

“We don't have room for an unemployed cabbage thief.”

“He has a job now.”

They left laughing. Outside, the family from the row ahead had stopped beside a low wall. One child was crying because the afternoon was over. The other was eating the snack they had refused inside. The adults stood close enough to help, looking tired in different directions.

Eve did not decide which of them was doing more. She had seen twenty minutes of strangers' lives, not an answer sheet.

On the way home she bought bread. Julian carried it while she found her keys. Their apartment was quiet, with Dumpling asleep in the place he had occupied before they left. After checking on him, Eve put the kettle on and took two plates from the cupboard.

They had agreed this was an evening for the question they had left unfinished. Now that they were home, she wanted to postpone it by describing every excellent detail of the fox's detachable head.

Julian put the bread down.

“Do you still want to talk?” he asked.

“Yes.”

She turned off the kettle before it had boiled. Neither of them wanted tea yet. She had started it because the switch was easier than the sentence.

“We talked about children when we went to the market,” she said. “I don't want to pretend we've never asked.”

“I remember.”

“And I don't want what I said then turned into a promise now.”

“It wasn't one.”

They sat on the sofa, leaving Dumpling's sleeping corner alone. Eve tucked her feet beneath her and looked at the bread on the table, still in its paper bag.

“I think I might want to try, someday,” she said.

Julian did not immediately smile. He listened, which gave her room to finish.

“That's clearer than it was. It isn't a decision to start. And it isn't something I can guarantee I'll always feel.”

“What makes you think you might?”

She had expected the question to ask when, or how many, or what arrangements would make it sensible. Why was harder.

“I want to know a person from the beginning,” she said slowly. “Not make a small version of myself. I think I'd like to be there while someone becomes completely different from what I expected.”

She laughed, embarrassed.

“That sounds very graceful. I'd probably spend half the time telling them where their other shoe was.”

“Only if you knew.”

“I wouldn't.”

The laughter settled. Julian leaned forward with his elbows on his knees.

“I think I'd like it too. I can picture ordinary parts. Reading a book repeatedly until neither of us can bear another page. Showing someone a place I like, then discovering they prefer the bus journey.”

“You'd explain the route.”

“Probably too thoroughly.”

“And they'd ask for a snack.”

“Yes.”

His smile made something ache gently beneath Eve's ribs. It would have been easy to stop there, with both of them looking toward a life containing only the parts that fit on a postcard.

She made herself keep talking.

“I'm afraid of disappearing into it.”

Julian turned toward her.

“Not because caring for someone would be beneath me. Because I know how quickly I volunteer for everything and then feel ashamed to say I can't manage.”

She pictured her desk. Not its present modest disorder, but an imagined version beneath bags, forms, objects whose purpose she would be expected to remember. A computer closed for so long that opening it felt like asking to become somebody else again.

“I want work,” she said. “Even if I change what work looks like. I want time where nobody needs me to notice the next thing.”

“I'd need to change my work too.”

“How?”

He looked down at his hands.

“I don't know the exact arrangement yet.”

“Good. Please don't tell me you'll be home whenever I'm tired.”

“I can't promise that.”

“I'd rather hear that than build a whole future on a sentence we already know isn't true.”

They spoke about the difference between being willing and being available. Julian could imagine changing hours, taking whatever leave was genuinely available, and refusing some extra commitments. That did not mean a clinic could function on his wishes alone. Eve could imagine adjusting commissions and venue responsibilities, but reducing her work would affect income and the people who depended on agreed shifts.

They would have to price real care, understand actual leave, and ask people what help they wanted to offer rather than assigning it to grandparents in advance.

“My mother would have opinions,” Eve said.

“She already does.”

“Yes. Her supply is healthy.”

They smiled. Eve picked a loose thread from the sofa seam and rolled it between her fingers before putting it in the bin, where Dumpling could not investigate it.

“The thing I liked about that family today,” she said, “was that nobody seemed to be putting on a performance for us. They just had a difficult fifteen minutes.”

“We don't know the rest of their day.”

“I know. I'm trying not to use them as evidence. I just noticed that I didn't want to run away from the idea because someone was crying.”

“That seems worth noticing.”

“What frightens you?”

Julian sat back. For a while he looked toward the window rather than at her.

“Being impatient when I'm tired. Assuming I know the right way because I'm used to people asking me. Thinking I've done my part because I completed something somebody else remembered for me.”

Eve let the last sentence stand without praising him for recognizing it. They had already learned how little praise could wash.

“And wanting it enough that I stop hearing you,” he added.

She reached for his hand.

“I don't want to keep you with a maybe if I know the answer has become no.”

“I don't want a yes said because you're afraid I'd leave.”

Neither offered the comforting fiction that a permanent disagreement would be easy. They had acknowledged that before. Tonight they could say that their present hopes were moving in a similar direction without converting uncertainty into a threat hanging over the relationship.

“If we changed differently,” Eve said, “we'd need to tell each other.”

“Yes.”

“Even if we'd already married.”

“Yes.”

She exhaled slowly. The word had weight because he had not hurried it.

They also spoke about things desire could not promise. Wanting children did not guarantee pregnancy, timing, health, or a particular family shape. Neither needed to solve those possibilities tonight or pledge an unlimited series of efforts in advance. If they chose to try later, each next step would still belong to people allowed to feel differently after learning more.

Eve had been braced for the conversation to become either a cheerful calendar or a catalogue of disaster. Instead it remained two people trying to understand the hope beneath the worry.

“I don't want a child to prove our home worked,” she said.

“Our home needs to work for the people already here.”

“And one extremely entitled cat.”

Julian glanced at the sleeping orange shape. “He considers us included in his arrangement.”

At the sound of their laughter, Dumpling opened his eyes. He looked toward the table, found no reason to move, and put his head down again. Eve did not turn the cat into a rehearsal child or read his indifference as a verdict.

She went to cut the bread. Julian joined her, taking down bowls for the soup they had prepared earlier. There was enough for dinner without a new expedition or a special meal to certify that the conversation had gone well.

As they waited for it to heat, Eve leaned against his shoulder.

“So our actual answer is that we'd both like to keep the possibility open,” she said. “More seriously than before. And we aren't trying now.”

“Yes. We'd choose that separately, when we're ready to talk about the real arrangements.”

“Not because there's a wedding date.”

“We don't have a wedding date.”

“I know. I'm preventing an imaginary committee from getting ahead of us.”

He put an arm around her.

“An active committee.”

“Terrible minutes.”

They ate. The soup was slightly too thick, and Julian added water rather than describing it as rustic. Eve told him that she had once wanted a pet dragon as a child and had considered the lack of garden space the primary obstacle.

“Not fire?”

“I assumed we'd train it.”

“That explains several of your early event plans.”

She laughed so loudly that Dumpling finally came to investigate whether something useful had happened.

After dinner, they returned to the paper in the drawer. Eve wrote their present answer in ordinary language, with no date attached. She did not write a number of children or leave a box waiting to be checked.

Julian read it and nodded.

Then she put it away beside the questions that remained open. They had not built a family by talking about one. They had learned that they could speak about wanting, fear, and the limits of promises without making somebody win.

Before bed, Eve noticed the theater ticket still in her pocket. She showed it to Julian, then placed it in the little collection of ordinary outings they kept for themselves.

“The fox recovered well,” he said.

“He had help.”

“And a spare head, possibly.”

She leaned against him, laughing again.

Tomorrow was a working day. Tonight she wanted this person beside her, with neither of them pretending to know every person they might become.
