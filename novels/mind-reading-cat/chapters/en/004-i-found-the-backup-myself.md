# Chapter 004 — I Found the Backup Myself

Hannah's message arrived while Eve was trying to photograph a cat who regarded cooperation as an unreasonable request.

Not for an audience. For the found notice. Yesterday's picture had mostly captured towel, one eye, and the impression that the photographer was also being rescued.

“Just your left side,” Eve said.

Dumpling turned his right side toward her.

“Thank you. Strong understanding of the brief.”

Her phone buzzed before she could try again.

*Hi! Hope you're settling in. Quick question about the campaign files.*

Settling in where? Unemployment had not offered an orientation package.

Eve put the phone down, waited for Dumpling to move of his own accord, and finally took a clear picture of his orange back and white chest. She kept the less obvious markings out of the public notice, as agreed with the clinic. A genuine claimant could describe those.

Another message arrived.

*We can't find the final working version. Can you send it over? Client review tomorrow and it's all a bit frantic here.*

She knew the version Hannah meant. A small-business campaign that had begun with one short video and expanded, through the optimistic power of the phrase while we're at it, into a week of material.

Eve had done the interviews, shaped the story, revised the captions, and explained twice why the bakery owner's name should not be treated as optional punctuation. The handover folder was already in the company's shared storage.

She typed its location.

Hannah replied almost immediately.

*Found that. But the captions don't match the latest direction. Could you clean them up and resend the editable files? Should be easy for you. You know it better than anyone.*

There it was. A compliment with a handle attached, ready to lift more work onto her desk.

Eve looked at her résumé on the laptop. The recruiter hadn't replied yet. Her phone had no other messages. Here, at least, someone knew exactly what she was good for.

She opened the old project folder.

The new brief was attached to Hannah's next message. Different opening. Shorter interviews. A new closing line. Eve could see how to do it before she'd finished reading.

If she cut the second answer and moved the owner's laugh forward, the rhythm would work. She might find a cleaner transition in the unused footage. It would take an evening, perhaps less if she skipped dinner.

She had no meetings tomorrow. That thought arrived with the sly force of an argument.

*Sure, I can take a look tonight—*

Dumpling landed against her wrist.

Not from a heroic height. He had been beside her chair, then abruptly put his uninjured paw over the hand resting at the edge of the table. His whiskers brushed her knuckles. The touch startled her enough that she lifted her thumb off Send.

The cat held on to her cuff.

“Again?”

He stared toward her lap, ears turned slightly sideways.

Eve looked down. The blue lanyard she'd dropped into her tote was hanging over its edge. Perhaps that was what he wanted. Or perhaps he objected to a neglected breakfast bowl. She couldn't turn a cat touching her wrist into legal advice.

Still, she had stopped.

The unsent sentence remained on the screen.

She read it as if someone else had written it.

*Tonight.*

Two days ago a whole department had assured her that the work could be taken care of without her. Today she was about to prove them right by taking care of it without being paid.

Eve put the phone on the table.

Dumpling released her and investigated the dangling lanyard. She put it away before it became a game, then sat back down.

The cat had not found a hidden folder. He hadn't exposed a lie. He'd touched her hand, and she had finally taken five seconds before answering.

Five seconds turned out to be enough to remember the handover checklist.

She had sent it before leaving the company, before her access was removed. A copy remained in her personal email because she'd been asked to confirm receipt of the departure paperwork there. The checklist listed the shared-folder path and the final revision date. Her own portfolio archive also contained the approved, permitted sample and the correspondence documenting her contribution. She did not need to retrieve company material through access she no longer had.

She opened the email.

There it was: final working files uploaded, version dated, handover acknowledged.

She could almost hear her former self saying, Of course, let me know if you need anything else.

The problem with anything else was that it had no edge.

Hannah called.

Eve considered letting it ring. Then she answered.

“Hi. I found the handover email.”

“Oh, brilliant. Can you just fix the captions before you send—”

“The files are in the location I sent. The dated version is the one that was handed over.”

“I know, but the client changed their mind. You know how they are.”

She did. She knew where the bakery owner looked when he was thinking, which joke would make him relax, and how much of his working day he'd already given to the shoot. None of that made the next revision magically completed.

“The new brief needs new work,” Eve said.

Silence.

Then Hannah laughed lightly. “I wasn't asking for a whole new project. Just a little help. We're all drowning.”

Eve pressed her toes against the floor. This was the place where she normally made a joke, lowered the price to nothing, and promised a first pass before bed.

“I can clarify the handover. I can't take on the revised edit for free.”

The words sounded stiff. Like a borrowed jacket. She resisted apologizing for the fit.

“Oh.” Hannah's voice cooled. “I thought we'd still be able to help each other.”

For an instant Eve wanted to ask when that last happened. She didn't. They could spend the rest of the afternoon trying to settle the emotional accounts, and it would still leave the client needing a revised video.

“If the company wants me to do more work, send the scope and fee arrangement. We can discuss it. Otherwise, I'll resend the checklist so the next person has everything.”

“We don't have time to get that approved.”

“I understand.”

She waited. Understanding didn't have to be followed by surrender. This was a new and distinctly uncomfortable discovery.

Hannah sighed. “Fine. Send the checklist.”

After the call, Eve sat very still.

It hadn't felt victorious. Her heart was going too fast. She could picture Hannah turning to the next desk, telling somebody she'd become difficult the moment she left.

Maybe she had.

She opened a message to Sophie and typed: *I said no to unpaid work and feel like I've stolen a vehicle.*

A response came several minutes later.

*Was it your vehicle?*

Eve laughed once, unexpectedly.

*Apparently.*

*Then drive it somewhere useful. I'm in a briefing. Talk later.*

No inspirational speech. Sophie had work to do.

Eve returned to the email. She forwarded the checklist and the existing confirmation, adding a short note identifying the completed version. She did not attach company assets to a new personal archive or send them to anyone outside the authorized handover. The files were already where they belonged.

Then she saved the correspondence that documented her own work and the delivery dates in her records. Proof didn't need to become a public accusation to be worth keeping.

Her hand hovered over an extra sentence: *I really do want to help.*

She deleted it.

The email was clear without asking to be liked.

She read it once more for the opposite problem: whether she'd made it colder than necessary just to prove she could. There was no reason to punish the next editor for needing to find the right folder. Eve added the name of the reference document and the explanation of an abbreviated filename. Useful information already in her possession, not a promise to spend the evening rebuilding the campaign.

The distinction was small enough that nobody would applaud it. Hannah might not even notice. Eve noticed. She could be decent without becoming endlessly available, and she didn't have to perform cruelty to make a refusal real.

Then she sent it, before she could add another paragraph undoing the first.

Dumpling was asleep in the drawer by the time she pressed Send. Apparently he had resigned from supervising her career.

At noon, Eve went back to the convenience store.

The succulent still occupied the windowsill. It had not experienced a transformative night.

“Alive?” the owner asked when he saw her.

“Which one?”

He pointed at the plant.

“Debatable. The cat's home with me for now.”

He nodded and handed her the pot. A saucer had appeared beneath it.

“That's yours,” she said.

“Bring it back.”

A simple arrangement. No one had to become a lifelong symbol of kindness.

She bought a modest lunch and carried the plant home. In the elevator she checked her messages. Hannah had replied with a thumbs-up and nothing else. No angry paragraph. No public trial.

The world had handled her refusal by continuing. Eve stood with the plant balanced against her hip and watched the elevator numbers change. She had expected a larger sound from a decision that had cost her so much effort.

Back in the apartment, Dumpling was awake and sitting beside his bowl. He looked at her, then at the bowl, which seemed to her an admirably transparent form of communication.

“Very subtle,” she said.

She dealt with his ordinary needs according to the care plan, then ate her own lunch before opening the laptop again. This was so unlike her usual working rhythm that she kept expecting a notification to punish her.

Instead, the recruiter asked for two portfolio samples.

Eve selected work she was entitled to share, checked the credit notes, and wrote concise explanations of what she'd actually contributed. Not *we created* when she meant she had planned the interviews. Not *I created* when a designer had made the visuals. Accurate didn't have to mean small.

For the first time that day, the file she opened belonged to something ahead of her.

A found-pet message arrived later. The sender thought the cat might be one from a nearby street, but the description didn't match. Eve replied politely and recorded the exchange rather than inventing either a happy reunion or a cruel abandonment.

Dumpling watched her from the drawer. The little crease between his ears made him look as though he had questions about her operating procedures.

“Don't look so pleased,” she told him. “I found the backup myself.”

He yawned.

She glanced at the tiny threads pulled loose in her sleeve. Coincidence, probably. A cat, a dangling strap, a distracted person. She had no sensible reason to make it more than that.

Still, when she set her phone down, she did it before her hand began answering the next request on its own.
