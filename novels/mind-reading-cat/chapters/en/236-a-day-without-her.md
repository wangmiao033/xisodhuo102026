# Chapter 236 — A Day Without Her

At eight in the morning Eve found herself standing in the kitchen with her coat on, although she had no reason to leave for another hour.

Dumpling took advantage of the mistake by sitting on the sleeve she had not yet put her arm through.

“You could have waited until I'd taken it off,” she told him.

He put his chin down.

Last week, at the venue table, she and Robin had gone through the rota in pencil. Today was the first time Robin would take both opening and closing responsibility while another paid colleague covered the middle of the day. Eve was not going in. The group bookings were small, the access route had been checked, and the system for a genuine premises problem was unchanged. They had even agreed who could authorize a refund. All Eve had to do now was refrain from replicating the shift on her phone.

She had not expected that to require a coat.

Her printed copy of the rota was still on the kitchen counter. Eve turned it facedown and pulled her arm free, abandoning the coat to the cat.

By nine she was at the library, working through an editing commission she had taken on before the wedding arrangements began. The library's desks were too narrow for her to spread out the way she liked. She regarded this as a structural advantage until a pencil rolled into the hinge of her laptop and briefly made it impossible to close.

At nine seventeen, the shared booking system sent a standard notice. A participant in the late-morning workshop had asked for a seat near the entrance.

Eve opened the request and began to write the directions she normally sent. She stopped when Robin's response appeared beneath it.

*Yes. Tell us at the door and we'll show you the two options.*

Eve's usual reply named the precise chair. She knew which one she would offer. That chair also stood where somebody waiting to sign in could lean over the participant's shoulder.

She deleted her draft.

The commission was for a local repair shop whose owner wanted to explain why a worn zipper need not mean the end of a perfectly good coat. She had two interviews to cut. One contained a wonderful remark about people becoming embarrassed to bring in things they loved; the other had better sound but made repair seem primarily a matter of correct consumer choices.

She played the first recording three times, trying to work around a truck reversing outside the shop. On the fourth pass she heard the owner pause to help a customer lift a shopping bag onto the counter. The following sentence was clear. It would make a better beginning than the line she had already placed at the front.

She moved it.

At ten thirty she went downstairs for water and found herself reading the venue's public listing through the glass door of the library, where the connection was better. The session description now included a sentence about materials already being provided. Somebody had brought an entire picnic basket of old magazines last time. Robin had asked if that had been a requirement. Eve remembered saying no. Evidently Robin had thought the next person might want to know that in time to leave their basket at home.

She checked that the time and price were unchanged and put her phone in her bag. Three minutes later she took it out again so she could see whether she'd received any urgent calls.

She had received a photograph from Julian of a pear with an optimistic amount of green left on it. He asked if it was a lunch pear or a prospective lunch pear.

She wrote, *Put it in a pocket and ask again this afternoon.*

There was nothing from Robin.

Just before twelve Eve did receive a call about the venue, but it was from a regular customer, not the team. The woman was at the door with her grandson. She had seen Robin's sign, which said the workshop would end at twelve twenty, and wondered if there was any point in staying. They had lost time waiting for a bus.

Eve could hear a child complaining that his hands smelled of wet wool.

“Is Robin there?” she asked.

“Yes, but they're clearing one of the tables. I thought I'd try you.”

Eve knew what she could offer: a few minutes at the materials table, perhaps a useful explanation to take home. She also knew that the session might be at the point where introducing two people would make everyone start a demonstration again. She could not see which it was.

“Will you ask Robin directly? They know what the group is doing right now. If there isn't time, they can tell you about the next slot.”

“All right,” the woman said. She sounded mildly surprised, but no more than she might have if Eve had told her that the entrance opened outward. The child began to ask about lunch before they had hung up.

After the call, Eve closed the booking page. She opened it again, found that nothing had changed, then sat with both hands flat on the library desk until the wish to send advance instructions passed.

The pear conversation had not been an invitation to eat together. Julian had his own working day, and Eve wanted lunch anyway. She bought soup from the cafe beside the library and took the last free seat by the window. There was half a lemon floating in the water jug, surrounded by five determined seeds. She used an ordinary glass and had nearly finished her soup before she noticed she had not left a gap in the afternoon for the inevitable repair to somebody else's decisions.

She kept the gap empty.

Back upstairs, she finished the first cut and sent it to the repair shop with two clear questions about wording. Then she listened to the draft through headphones while she made notes for tomorrow. The speeding traffic stayed in the middle of the shop interview, where it belonged to the street instead of spoiling the opening. When she removed a sentence she'd spent twenty minutes rescuing, the end of the piece became stronger.

At four she could have gone by the venue on her way home. Instead she went to buy the abrasive little brush that would get grit out of the seam of her back step. The hardware shop carried eleven sorts. The assistant, entirely untroubled by the abundance, asked how wide the seam was.

Eve hadn't measured it.

She bought the narrow one. It was a small enough gamble to take without producing a table of options.

At home, Dumpling had moved off her coat and onto the arm of the sofa. She changed her shoes, went out to try the brush, and dislodged one regrettable fragment of old leaf at a time. Halfway down the step she found a pale seedling growing out of a split in the mortar. She swept clear around it. By dinner there was a saucer beneath the drip from the roof above it, diverting the water so the small thing would not be flattened every time it rained.

Robin called when the venue was locked. Eve had expected a summary. What she heard first was the sound of a bus and Robin saying, “Hang on, I haven't got a hand left.”

Eve waited through a stretch of ordinary street noise.

“I'm on,” Robin said. “Did you get the note?”

“I didn't look after four.”

“That's fine. One refund. Somebody had booked the first extra seat before I counted the safe scissors. I called and offered our next date; they chose the refund. Then I took the other extra seat off sale. The rest is in the record.”

“The person near the entrance?”

“She chose the chair closer to the window. Easier for her to see us without turning around. We moved a side table out of the way first.”

Eve looked at the brush she had bought without knowing the width of the seam.

“And the late arrivals?”

“They looked at what the group was doing. I had time to show him how the printed guide folded, so they took one and went for lunch. His grandmother said they'd try a whole session next time.”

Eve had imagined a smoother entrance for them than that. She had also imagined it while sitting within ten steps of a library drinking fountain, which had not equipped her with a better view of the room.

“Was there anything you'd do differently next time?” she asked.

“I'd put the guides on the front desk early. I had to find them while answering a question about whether we sell scissors. Somebody thought the ones in the materials basket were part of a shop display.”

“Did you correct them?”

“Eventually. First I had to work out how much they thought we were charging for a pair of children's scissors.”

Eve laughed. Robin kept the call short, as agreed, and got off before their bus reached the next main stop.

The following morning they went through the closing record together. Eve had imagined Robin's shift producing either a list of mistakes or a perfect copy of her own work. Instead there were several choices she couldn't have predicted. The old closing checklist said to return all the furniture to the starting arrangement. Robin had drawn the chair and table positions that had actually worked. The floor was clear and the room could be opened safely the same way again.

Eve walked the turning route with a chair pulled out and pretended to be the woman who had asked for a seat near the entrance. From the place she usually offered, she could see the first people to arrive but very little of the tabletop. From the new spot she could see almost everything.

“Could we keep both choices available before the session starts?” she asked.

“If we leave the side table out,” Robin said.

Eve looked down at its scratched surface. She had bought it in the venue's first month, before she knew how many slippery paper cups could fit on a windowsill. It had been necessary for a while. There were other things it could do.

They moved it into the preparation area and began using it to sort guides. Eve lowered the extra default bookings by one and entered a note to adjust the count only when someone had checked the actual materials.

She still noticed that the chairs were turned at a different angle than she would have chosen. From where she was standing, two of them looked oddly friendly, facing the door instead of the middle of the room. She took a seat in the one nearest her and found Robin could talk to her without raising their voice.

“Would you want another shift like yesterday after the wedding?” she asked.

“Maybe two a month, if we agree the dates in advance.”

Eve made the entry tentative. She had asked because she wanted the answer, including the number that came with it.

Her coat was creased on one sleeve when she got home that evening. She hung it up beside Julian's. On the counter lay the two halves of the pear, saved from a lunch neither of them had remembered to finish. She tasted a slice. It had acquired a little sweetness around the edge without doing much for the rest of itself.

“We could cook it,” she told Julian when he came in.

He looked down at the fruit. “Was that our decision or the pear's?”

“Ours. I think it would have asked for another day.”

He set a small pan on the stove and moved aside so she could get to the drawer. Eve cut the firmer pieces a little thinner. She had never done it that way before, and she could see why it would work.
