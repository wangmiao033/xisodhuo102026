# Chapter 233 — An Old Rhythm, a New Bowl

Dumpling's bowl was not the bowl in any of the photographs.

It had a small mark on the outer edge from an encounter with the sink. Eve knew where the mark was because she had caused it, checked the bowl, and continued using it when it remained sound. Its history was short enough to remember without consulting anybody.

She took it from its usual place and measured the ordinary portion.

Nora stayed at the kitchen entrance, leaving the cat's route clear. Julian cleared the last cups from the table behind them. The visit had not become a party; the afternoon still had an end.

“He used to stand like that,” Nora said.

Dumpling was waiting with his feet neatly together and his gaze fixed above them, on the food rather than the humans.

“Like somebody disappointed by the service?” Eve asked.

“Exactly.”

The shared joke felt good until Eve noticed how quickly she had wanted it to remain hers.

Nora looked down at the bowl.

“May I put it down? Only if you don't mind.”

Eve's first answer was practical: there was no reason to mind. Her second answer took longer and belonged to a less generous place inside her.

She passed over the bowl.

“Here, please. Where he normally eats.”

Nora crouched slowly, keeping enough distance not to crowd him. She set the bowl on the clear mat. As her hand came away, she tapped the outside lightly with a fingernail.

Two taps. A pause. One more.

Dumpling's head turned, although he had already been watching the food. He approached with his tail lifted and began eating.

Eve knew the rhythm.

She felt the old little leap of recognition, the same one she had felt in Daniel's office when a shallow metal bowl had seemed capable of holding an entire lost home.

Nora took her hand away at once.

“Sorry. I used to do that.”

“Daniel does it too.”

“Yes. I picked it up from him at the step. It was better than standing there calling every cat by a description.”

Eve pictured the repair shop, the bowl beneath the overhang, and Daniel admitting that the rhythm came from his mother. Something ordinary had traveled between people, then reached this kitchen years later without bringing an explanation of everything else.

“He did it when we visited,” she said. “Dumpling turned quickly then too.”

Nora watched the cat eat.

“Shall I not do it again?”

“There's no need to repeat it.”

“All right.”

No one asked him to turn a second time. The meal was familiar food, already visible, served at a normal time. He might recognize a sound. He might simply be hungry. Eve could keep the tenderness of the moment without converting it into a trial the cat had to pass.

Nora stood with a hand briefly on the counter for balance. Eve offered the nearest chair, and she accepted with a rueful smile.

“My knees remember more than I do.”

“Mine are developing strong opinions about low storage.”

“Put the heavy things higher before they start writing letters.”

Eve laughed. Julian brought water for himself and sat at the table rather than hovering behind them. Their talk shifted to the everyday inconvenience of places designed for people who did not need to bend.

Nora asked what he liked playing with now. Eve described the familiar wand toy and the expensive track he had declined with remarkable consistency. She caught herself beginning to explain how carefully she checked every attachment, as though she were giving a care assessment rather than answering a friendly question.

“He also steals laundry,” she said instead. “We put it away. He considers that a temporary obstacle.”

Nora laughed. “He took a cleaning cloth off my chair once. I spent ages looking for it.”

“Where was it?”

“On the chair. Under him.”

Eve looked toward the cat, who continued eating with the air of a person entitled to privacy concerning his earlier work.

The story proved nothing beyond a stolen cloth. She liked it for that. There could be small, irritating recollections alongside the uncertain transfers, without requiring either kind of memory to cancel the other.

Dumpling ate through it all.

When he finished, he licked his mouth and approached Nora's chair. She rested one hand beside her knee. He rubbed his cheek against her fingers, then pressed along the side of her hand with more enthusiasm.

Eve felt that little tightening again.

This time she did not pretend it concerned evidence.

Nora looked at her before stroking him. Eve nodded. The cat stayed for several strokes, leaning into the hand while Nora smiled with her eyes lowered.

It was unmistakably affection, whoever had first offered it and whatever he remembered.

It did not have to be exclusive to be real.

The thought sounded simple. Letting it settle in her chest was more difficult than saying it in a caption would have been. Eve wanted him safe. She also wanted the familiar pressure of his cheek to confirm that the safety she had made was unlike any other.

She took a sip of water instead of calling him.

Dumpling moved away when he had finished. Nora let her hand fall to her lap. He crossed the room, stepped into his familiar box, and turned against the blanket until the ridge suited him.

Then he lay down.

Eve's relief was immediate and embarrassing.

She did not announce that he had chosen her.

He had chosen a bed, after a meal, in a room where his things were already arranged. She could love seeing that without making Nora lose something to prove it.

“He's done with us,” Nora said.

“He has a full afternoon.”

“I can see that.”

The clock was close to the time they had agreed. Nora gathered her bag and checked that the prints were back in their envelope. Eve helped find the thin paper that had slipped behind a cushion. It was the shopping list, and for a moment they both laughed at the importance of retrieving old washing powder.

At the door, Nora paused.

“Thank you for letting me see him.”

“Thank you for coming.”

“I don't need you to keep sending pictures. I wanted you to have what I knew.”

Eve nodded. That kindness was easier to accept now that she no longer heard every sentence as a possible demand.

“If another relevant record turns up, you can contact me through the same route,” she said. “But you don't have to spend every evening looking.”

“I won't. If I do find something, I'll tell you what it actually is.”

They smiled at each other.

Nora went downstairs carrying the same bag she had brought. No cat followed. No farewell performance was required from the sleeping figure in the box.

Eve closed the door quietly and leaned against it for a moment.

Julian remained by the table.

“Do you want company or a minute?” he asked.

“Company.”

He came over. She held him around the middle, pressing her face briefly into his shoulder. There had been no catastrophe to recover from. Her body seemed determined to recover anyway.

“I wanted him to come back to me,” she admitted.

“He went to bed.”

“I know. That's part of why I feel ridiculous.”

Julian rested a hand against her back.

“I don't think having the feeling made you do anything cruel.”

“I nearly called him while she was stroking him.”

“You didn't.”

“No.”

She drew back enough to see his face.

“I thought if he liked her too much, I'd discover I was temporary.”

There. The word that had followed her since the first borrowed room finally came out without a sensible question hiding it.

Julian took time before answering.

“You didn't become less present because someone else cared for him before.”

“I know that in the reasonable part.”

“Give the rest a little time.”

She let out a breath against his shirt, half laugh and half irritation that time was again being offered where she would have preferred a quick repair.

They went back to the kitchen. Eve washed the bowl while Julian dried the cups. She checked the small mark on its edge out of habit. The bowl had acquired no visible history from the sound Nora made beside it.

Dumpling's past had acquired a few more pieces.

Those were different things.

After the dishes, Eve saved the agreed copies in the private folder. She kept the source with each image rather than cutting them into a smooth slideshow. Then she closed the laptop. The appointment had ended. She did not need to extend it alone until every emotion had been assigned a proper label.

Julian had a book to return later. Eve had a short grocery list. They decided to go together when she felt ready, not as a test of whether the cat could bear them leaving.

For a while they sat in separate chairs.

The room gradually resumed its familiar scale. Three cups were just three cups again. The table could hold dinner. The cat's box did not look like one endpoint on a diagram of all the places he had been.

Eve watched him twitch an ear in his sleep.

She could remember the fear in the photographs and messages. She could also remember Nora laughing at her own knees, Daniel worrying about a trolley wheel, Ada moving a carton out of a doorway. The people had not become perfect because she could see them more clearly.

They had become harder to reduce to one word.

When Julian stood to find the book, Eve got her coat. Dumpling raised his head at the sound of their movement and watched her take her keys. She checked the ordinary things once, left his bed exactly where it was, and went out.

On the landing she realized she had not looked back a second time.

“Forgot something?” Julian asked when she slowed.

“No. I was noticing I hadn't.”

He waited while she settled her scarf, then they continued downstairs.

At the shop, Eve bought a vegetable neither of them had an immediate plan for. Julian suggested the simplest possible way to cook it. She accepted because dinner did not need to prove her independent creativity tonight.

They returned to find Dumpling at the window, not in his box. He might have spent the whole time there or arrived a minute before them. Nobody had watched, and nothing depended on constructing a beautiful account of his patience.

Eve put the groceries away and went to sit nearby.

He looked over his shoulder. After a while he stepped down and came to her, offering the same firm press of his forehead she knew so well.

She stroked him without comparing it with the gesture he had given Nora.

There was enough affection in this small movement to receive it as itself.

When he walked away, she let him go and joined Julian in the kitchen. Behind them, the new bowl waited in its usual place, ready for the next ordinary meal.
