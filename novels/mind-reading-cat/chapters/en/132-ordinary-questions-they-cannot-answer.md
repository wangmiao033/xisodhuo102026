# Chapter 132 — Ordinary Questions They Cannot Answer

Julian rang the bell at his parents' house and then used his key before anyone answered.

Eve looked at him.

“Habit,” he said.

From inside, his mother called that the door was open. He stood with the key still in the lock, wearing an expression Eve had never seen him use at the clinic.

“I've established that,” he called back.

The exchange should have reassured her. Instead she became acutely aware of the plant she was holding, which had looked modest in the shop and now seemed to have far too many leaves.

They had chosen this Sunday because everyone was free. Julian's parents lived on the other side of the city, close enough for lunch without train tickets or an overnight bag. Eve had asked what they would like her to bring. His mother had said nothing sweet, because she had already made dessert, and mentioned needing something for a bright kitchen windowsill.

The plant was therefore a requested object. Eve reminded herself of this as Julian's mother appeared in the hallway.

“Eve. Lovely to meet you.”

She took the pot with both hands and looked pleased enough that Eve stopped planning an apology for its size.

“Come in. He didn't tell you to dress up, did he?”

Eve glanced down at her blouse.

“No.”

“Good. He used to announce things as though there would be an examination.”

“I'm standing here,” Julian said.

“I know. Put your coat away.”

He did.

His father came from the kitchen carrying a dish towel and greeted Eve with a handshake that became slightly awkward when neither of them knew when to release it. He offered tea, coffee, and a cold drink in rapid succession.

“Water would be lovely,” she said.

“Of course. That's available too.”

In the sitting room, Julian moved a cushion without looking and sat at one end of the sofa. Eve sat beside him. His mother returned, replaced the cushion behind his back, and asked whether he had eaten breakfast.

“Yes.”

“What?”

“Breakfast.”

“Julian.”

“Eggs. Toast. I can give you the sequence.”

Eve laughed. His mother smiled, apparently satisfied, and went to check lunch.

Alone for a moment, Eve leaned toward him.

“Breakfast,” she whispered.

“I heard it as I said it.”

“You sounded sixteen.”

“I was hoping for eighteen.”

He looked toward the kitchen and lowered his voice. “How are you?”

“Fine. The plant has been accepted. My first deliverable.”

He touched her hand, briefly, before his father came back with water.

His father noticed Julian's coat hanging from its usual hook and said the pocket was turning inside out again.

“I know,” Julian said.

“There’s a needle in the drawer.”

“I know where the drawer is.”

Eve studied her water. At her mother's house, she had been the one answering simple offers as though they concealed a verdict. Watching Julian do it was uncomfortable in a new way. She could hear the invitation to mend a pocket. She could also hear everything his short answer seemed to be carrying from earlier visits she knew nothing about.

His father went to bring in the food. Julian rubbed a thumb along the seam of his trousers.

“I'll do it before we leave,” he said, though the person who had asked was already gone.

Lunch began with ordinary questions. Had the journey been busy? Was the neighborhood where Eve lived convenient? Did she cook? She answered yes, mostly, and with uneven results. Julian said she made excellent fish. Eve suspected he was remembering a version that had remained intact on the plate.

His mother asked about Dumpling. His father asked whether an orange-and-white cat counted as a breed. Nobody asked for a performance, a demonstration, or a photograph with a doctor placed romantically in the background.

Eve began to relax.

Then Julian's father said, “What do you actually do for work?”

It was a reasonable question. He asked it while passing the potatoes, with none of the emphasis Eve's mother put on the word actually. Still, Eve felt a familiar impressive description hurry toward her mouth.

“I run an independent content and community engagement...”

She stopped. Julian's father was waiting with the bowl suspended between them.

“Sorry. That explains very little. I plan and make content for clients. Mostly animal-related organizations at the moment. I also host a small paid discussion for pet owners.”

He set the potatoes down.

“What do they discuss?”

“Everyday arrangements. How to make feeding notes clear when several people care for an animal, for instance. How to find information again when you need it.”

“And people pay for that?”

“Some do.”

“How many?”

“The first trial had three. One asked for a refund because she'd expected a different kind of session.”

His mother looked up. “Oh, dear.”

“It was within the terms I'd offered. I changed the description afterward.”

“Has it improved?”

“The later bookings help me test that. It's still small. Client projects cover most of my income.”

She had managed to say refund without pretending it was a prize. That felt useful, even as she wished Julian's parents could have met her during some entirely hypothetical month when all her choices produced obvious success.

His father asked whether she employed anyone.

“Not regularly. I pay people for agreed parts of projects when I need them.”

“So you're working alone.”

“Much of the time.”

“Doesn't that get lonely?”

Eve paused. She had prepared for questions about profit and qualifications. Loneliness had not been on the list.

“Sometimes,” she said. “That's one thing I miss about an office. Being able to complain about a printer to someone who's seen it.”

His father laughed. “Ours used to need a chair pushed against the paper drawer.”

“The chair became staff?”

“The most reliable member.”

For a few minutes, work consisted of unreliable machines and the peculiar noise a colleague once made while trying not to swear. Eve could speak easily about that. She took a second potato and discovered she had been hungry throughout the first half of lunch.

Julian's mother refilled the water glasses.

“Do you know what you'll earn each month?” she asked.

“Not exactly. I know confirmed projects. I keep money aside for quieter periods.”

“How far ahead can you plan?”

“Some work is booked several weeks ahead. Other things aren't. I review what I can afford before taking on new costs.”

“I'd find that difficult.”

“I do find it difficult.”

She saw Julian look at her. It was not alarm, at least not any alarm she recognized. She had told him these things before, but rarely while holding someone else's serving spoon and hoping to be invited back.

“She's careful,” he said.

Eve appreciated the sentence. She also felt the temptation to let him take the rest of the conversation.

His mother turned toward him. “But it means you both work weekends.”

“Sometimes,” he said.

“Do you manage to see each other?”

“Yes,” Eve said. “We arrange it. Some weeks are easier than others.”

“What happens when you're busy at the same time?” his father asked.

“We complain about scheduling,” Julian said. “Then try again.”

His father nodded as though this was a method he recognized.

Dessert required smaller plates and more table space. Eve offered to help. In the kitchen, Julian's mother handed her a stack and thanked her for the plant again. It stood beside the window, its leaves catching the light.

“I bought one like that years ago,” she said. “I kept moving it because I thought it looked unhappy.”

“Did that help?”

“Not at all. I think I exhausted it.”

Eve smiled. “The shop said this one likes being left where the light is good.”

“A useful instruction for several members of this family.”

It was said affectionately. Eve could not tell whether she had been invited into a joke or warned about a difficulty, and decided she did not need to settle that before carrying the plates.

When she returned, Julian was answering his father about a household shelf that needed replacing. His voice had become terse again.

“I said I'd look at it.”

“I know you said that. I was asking when.”

“I don't have the measurements.”

“It's upstairs.”

Eve set down the plates. Julian looked at her and exhaled through his nose.

“After dessert,” he said.

His mother sat down. “This is why I like having people here. Things finally get finished.”

Eve was not sure whether to laugh. She ate a spoonful of the pudding instead. It was good, warm and not too sweet. She said so, and his mother began explaining the small change she made to the recipe.

The afternoon loosened after that. Julian measured the shelf. His father found a second tape measure because the first had a bent hook. Eve looked through a pile of old postcards his mother had been sorting, turning them over only when invited.

There were pictures of places she did not know and one of a familiar beach photographed from an unflattering angle.

“That was meant to be a sunset,” his mother said. “His father bought it because everything else had glitter.”

“A principled purchase.”

“Exactly.”

When the men returned, Julian's mother mentioned a lunch she wanted to arrange the following month. Eve listened while trying to calculate whether the proposed weekend fell before or after a client delivery.

“We could make it the first Sunday,” his mother said. “Then everybody would know where they were.”

“Every month?” Eve asked.

“It would save all these messages. If that suited.”

Eve opened her mouth to say she would need to check the date before agreeing even to the first one.

“We can do first Sundays,” Julian said. “Eve can keep the afternoon clear. We'll come for lunch.”

His mother smiled. “Lovely. I'll put it in the calendar.”

The room had moved on before Eve had found her voice. His father asked whether the shelf should be wood or metal. Julian answered. Eve looked down at the postcard in her hands, suddenly uncertain where to put it.

She could have interrupted. She knew that. The knowledge made her delay worse rather than better.

When his mother rose to fetch her calendar, Eve said, “I haven't checked my work dates yet.”

“We can sort the first one out,” Julian said. “There'll be time.”

There it was again, quieter but still present. An arrangement she had somehow entered without choosing it.

His mother paused at the doorway. “Let me know, then.”

“Thank you,” Eve said.

She put the postcard back in its pile.

They left half an hour later. Nobody had been cruel. His mother wrapped two pieces of dessert. His father asked Eve to explain the information sheets another time because he had a drawer full of things he could never find. Julian promised to send his own shelf measurements once he checked which bracket could be reused.

Eve thanked them for lunch, meant it, and followed Julian through the gate.

The street outside was bright and quiet. Somebody had left a child's scooter leaning against a low wall. Julian reached for her hand.

She let him take it, then stopped walking.

“What?” he asked.

“I need to talk about what you said at the table.”

He glanced back toward the house.

“About your work?”

“About my Sundays.”

His hand shifted in hers. She released it, not to punish him, but because she wanted him to hear the sentence before either of them could turn it into a pleasant walk home.
