# Chapter 013 — The Third Time She Came

Eve was trying to remove a paper dog from a folding table when Mia arrived.

The dog had been attached with an ambitious amount of tape. Its cheerful expression survived every attempt to peel it away, while its body came apart in damp white layers. Eve had already lost one thumbnail to the operation and most of her respect for decorative craft materials.

“You need warm water,” Mia said from the doorway.

Eve straightened so fast her knee struck the table.

“I knew that.”

“You're holding a ruler.”

“It was a preliminary approach.”

Mia carried no new pet supplies. She wore old trainers and a sweatshirt with a bleach mark near the cuff. A cardboard carton balanced against her hip.

“Nora said to bring these back.”

Inside were the borrowed clipboards from the event, neatly stacked, along with a roll of unused raffle tickets someone had apparently acquired from a completely different fundraiser.

“Did you come on the bus with all that?” Eve asked.

“Yes.”

“You could have asked—”

Eve stopped.

Mia looked at her. “Asked what?”

“Where to put them. Over there is better. This table still contains a dog.”

It was a poor recovery, but Mia accepted it. She set the carton down and went to find a cloth.

The volunteer group had borrowed the clinic's small back room for an hour to clean and sort event materials. No animals were being kept there. Stacks of flattened signs leaned against the wall, and someone had written PLEASE COUNT BEFORE REORDERING on a box containing what looked like enough paper cups for a wedding.

Eve was there to photograph neutral details for a possible follow-up piece, with Nora's permission. The original event work was finished and paid. This smaller film was Eve's own trial: a short look at what happened after an adoption afternoon, offered to the group only if they liked it. She had limited herself to one morning, one edit, and no promises about views.

She had not expected Mia.

Their last conversation had ended with a request not to be filmed. Eve turned her camera around so that its lens faced the wall.

“Still no recording you,” she said.

“I remember.”

Mia filled a basin at the utility sink. She laid the wet cloth over the paper dog and moved on to a stack of plastic signs while it softened.

Nora arrived carrying two canvas bags. She greeted Mia without surprise and asked whether she wanted the small brush or the larger one.

“The one that works.”

“Unreasonably high standards. Try this.”

Mia took it.

Eve stepped aside while Nora checked a list clipped to the cupboard. There were no congratulations, no speech about courage, no suggestion that volunteering would improve Mia's application. Perhaps they had discussed all that already. Perhaps Nora knew better than to greet a person doing an ordinary job as if she had crawled out of a moral wilderness.

For ten minutes, the room filled with the scrape of brushes and the faint squeak of a cloth against plastic. Mia released the paper dog in one intact, sodden sheet. It slid into the bin, still smiling.

“I'd like to pretend we worked equally hard on that,” Eve said.

“No.”

“Fair.”

Mia's mouth twitched.

The first bag contained fabric covers from transport crates used for supplies, not animal bedding. The second held clean towels donated by a family moving away. Nora separated the categories, explaining where each belonged. Eve caught herself preparing to film the colorful heap before she understood it.

“Which parts would actually help you explain the job?” she asked.

Nora considered. “The labels. People mean well, then leave us three bags with no indication of what's inside. We spend the first half hour opening everything.”

Labels were not visually exciting. Eve wrote the answer down anyway.

Mia knelt by the towel pile and found a small embroidered crown on one corner.

“Someone had a much grander bathroom than ours.”

“That's for visiting royalty,” Eve said. “We shouldn't use it for folding chairs.”

Mia held it up, studying a worn patch beneath the crown. “Can this one still be used?”

Nora checked it and set it in a separate pile. “I'll ask the foster team. Don't put it in the ready box yet.”

There was no magical transformation in the room. Supplies still needed sorting. Eve still wanted a shot that made the morning feel worth watching. Mia still looked at her phone every time it buzzed, as if she might be receiving a better offer from the rest of her life.

But she stayed.

At the end of the first half hour, Tessa appeared with the clinic's mug collection on a tray.

“Tea. The mugs are on loan, not being donated.”

“Is that a frequent problem?” Eve asked.

“Last year someone packed my lunch into a fundraiser box.”

Mia took the mug with a cartoon duck on it. “Did anyone buy it?”

“Not at the price it deserved.”

They drank standing up because all the chairs had damp seats. Through the open doorway, Eve could see the corridor leading to the waiting room. A visitor passed with a covered carrier. Mia's gaze followed it, and for a moment her face changed.

Eve looked away before the moment became something she could steal.

The old version of her would have recognized an opening. Ask about Socks. Catch the hesitation. A line about wanting to love someone, a close-up of hands wrapped around a mug. It could have been affecting. It could also have made Mia regret coming.

Eve reached for a packet of biscuits instead.

“The best one has broken,” she announced.

“That means it's two biscuits,” Mia said.

“A strong interpretation.”

After tea, they discovered the folding table had lost one of its rubber feet. It wobbled whenever anyone leaned on the edge. Nora said they could mark it for repair, but the foot might be somewhere among the returned materials.

The search took all three of them into the cupboard, around the basin, and under a stack of cardboard signs. Eve found a coin, Mia found a marker, and Nora found the missing volunteer rota from the previous month.

“Historic document,” Eve said.

“Historically annoying,” Nora replied.

The rubber foot finally emerged from inside a paper cup. Mia held it up without triumph and fitted it back into place. The table stood level.

Eve lifted her camera, then lowered it.

“Can I photograph the table? No faces. Just the repaired leg and the supplies.”

Mia shrugged. “It's a table.”

“Yes. A very important table.”

She took a wide shot with everyone out of frame. Then she photographed the empty bowls waiting in a labeled crate, the stack of dry covers, and the volunteer schedule with names and contact details hidden by a blank sheet. The result looked like the back room of a modest operation that depended on people remembering things.

Which, she supposed, it was.

Mia was measuring two stacks of folded towels to make sure both fitted in the storage box.

“You could just push them down,” Eve said.

“Then the lid bulges. Someone has to carry it.”

Eve watched her remove three towels, refold them smaller, and try again.

The lid closed.

She felt a small, disproportionate satisfaction. Not because Mia had proved she deserved Socks. Because someone later would pick up the box and not have to fight it.

Nora checked the wall clock. “We need to be out in fifteen minutes.”

“Where do the wet cloths go?” Mia asked.

“Home with me to be washed. Then back next week.”

“All of them?”

“Unless they've started doing themselves.”

Mia glanced at the bag. “How often does that happen?”

“After events. More during busy periods.”

“Do you take them on the bus too?”

“Sometimes.”

Mia said nothing more, but Eve saw her fold the handles together so they wouldn't drag through the basin water.

They put the room back the way it had been. Eve took down her temporary paper covering the personal information on the rota and checked that the photographs showed no names. Mia returned the mugs to Tessa, including one they'd nearly packed with the towels.

“See?” Tessa said. “It begins innocently.”

Julian passed the doorway as Eve was putting away the camera. He looked into the room, then at the clean table.

“You found the foot.”

“Mia did.”

“It was in a cup,” Mia said.

He nodded as if this were a perfectly ordinary habitat for table parts. “Thank you. It nearly surrendered during the last event.”

“Was that why you kept your hand on it?” Eve asked.

“I was trying to look supportive.”

Mia laughed first. Eve liked him a little for that, then immediately busied herself with a camera strap that did not need adjusting.

Outside, Nora locked the supply cupboard and went back to the clinic to settle the room booking. Mia waited by the pavement with an empty carton flattened under her arm.

“Do you have enough for your film?” she asked.

“Maybe. It might be a very short film about containers.”

“People like organizing videos.”

“Do they?”

“I do.”

Eve looked at her. “What would you want to know?”

“Which bits actually need doing. Not just 'help us.' That's too big. I don't know what to do with it.”

Eve took out her notebook.

Mia raised an eyebrow.

“I'm writing the idea, not quoting you.”

“All right.”

She wrote: Show a task someone can finish.

Mia shifted the cardboard under her arm. “I'm still annoyed about the application.”

“I assumed.”

“I haven't suddenly become wise.”

“That would be a lot to ask of one rubber foot.”

Mia's laugh came out reluctantly but stayed longer this time.

Her bus approached at the corner. She waved once and ran for it, the flattened carton bouncing against her leg. Eve watched her board, then checked the time. The promised morning was almost over. She had not secured a tearful confession, a dramatic turnaround, or an adoption.

At home, she transferred the pictures. Dumpling watched from his usual patch of floor, indifferent to the small triumph of the level table. Eve put the camera away before he could investigate the dangling strap.

On the screen, the empty bowls looked disappointingly empty.

She almost cropped them out.

Then she remembered the full box, the closed lid, and someone who would be able to carry it without stopping on the stairs.

She left the bowls in.

A message from Nora arrived while she was choosing a final frame. The group could use a photograph of the packing arrangement, if she had one, to help the next volunteers put everything back.

Eve exported the plainest picture. No music, no warm filter, no moving caption about kindness. She marked the cupboard shelf on a copy, then checked the original still hid the names on the wall.

Nora replied with a thumbs-up and a request for the box labels too.

For once, the least impressive thing she had photographed was the thing someone needed immediately.
