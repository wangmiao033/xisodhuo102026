# Chapter 101 — No Vet Endorsement Included

The community room had a folding table with one short leg and a photograph of a much larger room on the wall.

Eve put her quotation beside the photograph's handsome imaginary furniture. On her way in, she had counted the actual chairs. There were eighteen, two of which carried handwritten requests not to sit down.

“We usually borrow more,” the organizer said, following her glance.

“For the market, or for this room?”

“For whatever needs them.”

That was an answer Eve knew how to work with. She wrote down who supplied them and who arranged collection, then moved her cup away from the edge of the tilting table.

The invitation had arrived on Saturday. By Tuesday she had received a proposed budget, a rough plan of the courtyard, and a list headed Things People Will Enjoy. The list was enthusiastic. The budget was not.

Still, it was a real inquiry. The community committee wanted a small pet-friendly market in three weeks, with local sellers, information about neighborhood services, and somewhere owners could talk without every conversation beginning with a complaint. They had seen her film about people negotiating noise and routines. They liked that she hadn't made either side ridiculous.

Eve liked being approached for that reason.

She also liked the amount on the planning-fee line she had prepared, and was trying not to apologize for it before anyone spoke.

The organizer unfolded the courtyard plan.

“Here for stalls. Refreshments there. We thought your vet could have this corner.”

The pencil rested in a square Eve hadn't drawn.

“My vet?”

“The one in your earlier videos. Julian, isn't it? Perhaps basic checks, questions, that sort of thing. Nothing elaborate.”

Eve looked at the square. It was about the size of the folding table and apparently large enough to contain an entire second business.

“No veterinary service is included in my proposal.”

“Oh. I assumed you worked together.”

“On particular paid projects. I'm quoting for event planning and content. I can't offer his time or the clinic's services.”

The organizer sat back, disappointed rather than offended.

“We wouldn't expect anything complicated. Owners could just bring their concerns.”

“That still requires someone qualified and available, with proper arrangements. It isn't something I can add because I know him.”

Her mouth wanted to soften the refusal into an offer to ask. She could picture the conversation already: would he mind, only briefly, perhaps because it would help her first independent event? She could make the request sound voluntary while laying out exactly how much would be lost if he declined.

She left the pencil where it was.

The organizer turned over the next page. Someone had sketched a poster. Eve recognized a small printed still from one of the old clinic videos, Julian standing beside a noticeboard.

“We haven't put this anywhere,” the woman said quickly. “It's only an idea.”

“Thank you. Please don't use that image for this. It would make people expect a service we haven't arranged.”

She resisted explaining their relationship. Being his girlfriend neither improved nor weakened the answer.

For a moment the room seemed to contain only costs. A paid planner. Paid additional staff. Chairs that required a van. No attractive doctor emerging for free from a photograph.

“I can see why you liked the idea,” Eve said. “May we look at what you actually want visitors to leave with?”

The organizer considered.

“Knowing who's nearby. We keep hearing that people don't meet anyone unless there's a problem.”

“That doesn't require a consultation corner.”

“No, but it needs a reason to come.”

Eve nodded. That was not a foolish objection, and calling it one would not produce an event.

They took the plan outside.

The courtyard was smaller in person than on paper, partly because the paper did not include a raised planter and a row of refuse bins. A leaf scraped along the paving. Someone had left a child's yellow scooter beside the doorway, exactly where a queue would naturally form.

Eve walked from the street entrance to the room, counting her paces, then stopped pretending paces were measurements and took out a tape measure.

The organizer held the other end.

“Have you always carried that?”

“Since I discovered optimism doesn't fit through doorways.”

They laughed. It was easier to discuss the plan while preventing the metal strip from folding between them.

Eve marked the route that needed to remain clear. She asked about accessible entry, permission for animals in the indoor room, cleaning, toilets, and who had authority to postpone if the weather made the courtyard unsuitable. The organizer knew some answers and wrote down who could supply the others.

There was no sudden moment when Eve became the expert on everything. The venue's caretaker came out, corrected her assumption about a locked side gate, and pointed to the usable entrance from the lane.

“That one stays available,” he said. “Don't stack anything against it.”

Eve crossed out her proposed storage position.

On a low wall near the street, two residents had stopped to talk. One carried a bag of pet food. The other held an empty shopping trolley and was describing a broken fastening on it. Neither had brought an animal.

“People could come without their pets,” Eve said. “That needs to be clear from the first notice. Shops can show supplies. Owners can exchange ordinary practical experience. Nobody has to bring a nervous animal to prove they belong.”

The organizer looked toward the residents.

“A pet event without every pet.”

“A neighborhood event that makes room for them.”

Eve wrote the sentence down, then crossed out the version that sounded like advertising and kept the shorter one: Pets welcome where suitable. People welcome without pets.

Back indoors, she laid out two options. A larger event with more staffing and an independently arranged professional service would cost more and need more lead time. A smaller market could fit the current budget if they dropped the consultation promise, kept the hours limited, and used the courtyard with a clear weather decision.

Her fee remained the same for the planning work described. She had removed an imagined service, not half her actual labor.

The organizer ran a finger down the total.

“If we take the smaller version, could you make the publicity more exciting?”

“I can make it clear and inviting. I can't promise a crowd by implying something we won't provide.”

The woman sighed, but not angrily.

“I need the committee to agree.”

“Of course. This quote is valid until Friday. If the scope changes after that, I'll check the price again.”

She left the paper on the table instead of taking it back to rewrite the amount. Outside, she discovered how tightly she'd been holding her pen. A small crescent remained in her finger.

On the way home she passed a display of bright paper flowers. For a wholly unprofessional second she remembered Saturday's kiss and lost her place in the mental list of unanswered questions.

Then a delivery trolley rattled past, and she stepped aside.

Both parts of her life could exist on the same pavement. That did not mean one had to carry the other.

At home, Dumpling had installed himself on the chair she needed. Eve fetched a different chair rather than conducting a negotiation she would lose. She sent the revised scope and removed the clinic still from the sample-layout attachment before returning it with a note explaining the change.

Then she opened her accounts.

If the committee declined, she would lose several hours of quotation work, not her ability to pay next month's essentials. If it accepted, she needed to reserve time without neglecting the repair-business client and the clinic card still awaiting a decision. She placed provisional blocks in the calendar, clearly marked, and made no promises to stallholders yet.

Julian called that evening at the time they had agreed to speak.

“How was the meeting?”

“They had included you in a square on a plan.”

“A flattering square?”

“Quite small. Apparently you were going to supply unlimited wisdom from it.”

“I'd have needed a chair.”

“Those may also have been imaginary.”

His laugh warmed her more than it should have through a phone. She told him what she had said, briefly and without turning it into a request for praise.

“Thank you for not volunteering me,” he said.

“I didn't want the job on that basis either.”

“Do you still want it?”

“Yes. The version I actually quoted for.”

“Then I hope they choose that one.”

She heard movement at his end, a cupboard closing. He was making dinner. She asked what, and the conversation became ordinary enough for her shoulders to lower.

She did not ask whether refusing the consultation corner had impressed him. She wanted him to be pleased, of course. Wanting was not the same as requiring his approval to validate a sensible decision.

On Thursday, the organizer called while Eve was checking a video export.

“The committee agrees to the smaller plan,” she said. “We've removed the health-check wording. Can you send the final agreement?”

Eve paused the video.

“Yes. I'll include the venue responsibilities we discussed.”

“One thing. Could the publicity say you previously worked with the clinic?”

“Only as a factual credit for work they've permitted me to show, not as endorsement of this event. It may be clearer to use the community project you originally contacted me about.”

The woman considered, then agreed.

After the call, Eve stood up too fast and nearly walked into the chair she had borrowed from the kitchen. Dumpling watched from her original seat. He had supplied no verdict on the client, no useful hint about the negotiation, and no help with furniture.

She was so pleased that she told him anyway.

“They chose my proposal.”

He began washing a paw.

The final agreement took longer than the celebratory sentence. She corrected an address, clarified who would pay the suppliers directly, and specified the approval deadline for promotional copy. The committee would carry the event budget; her agreed fee covered the defined planning and content work, not any shortfall disguised as enthusiasm.

When the signed copy and deposit arrived the next morning, she checked both before marking the booking confirmed.

She could now contact applicants. She could book a site visit with people who might actually work there. She could also make herself lunch before doing either.

Eve returned to the quotation saved in her folder. The name at the top was hers. Beneath it lay a description of work she knew how to do, in a place she had measured, with people who had finally hired that work rather than the person standing beside her in an old video.

She put the deposit receipt beside it and checked that the planning meeting was now in the confirmed calendar, not the provisional one. Then she went to find the lunch she had promised herself. The event could have her afternoon.

She left the document as it was.
