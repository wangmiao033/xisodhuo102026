# Chapter 177 — Do Not Give It Up for Me

Julian's call came while Eve was wrestling a sheet of brown paper into the bin.

It had wrapped the opening cards. She had kept it because it looked useful, then discovered that it occupied the precise shelf space she needed for things that actually were useful. Now it refused to fold along its old creases.

“Tell me you have less complicated news than this paper,” she said.

“What has the paper done?”

“Resisted a reasonable transition.”

He made a noise that might have been a laugh. “Dr. Clark has confirmed the cover. I can accept.”

Eve stopped pressing the paper down with her elbow.

“Good.”

She had meant to sound pleased without qualification. Instead the word emerged with a tiny space after it, as though something had been left off the end.

“Are you at the room?”

“Yes. Alone. Robin isn't due until tomorrow.”

“I can come by after my shift.”

“Come by because you'd like to see me. Don't come by to check whether I've survived the announcement.”

A pause.

“I would like to see you.”

“Then bring yourself. I have enough paper.”

After they hung up, she left the bin half-full and opened her client's file. There was a revision she understood perfectly, a sentence she could improve in less than a minute. She stared at it until the words lost their arrangement.

She wanted him to accept. This was true.

She wanted him in the kitchen that evening and the next and several evenings after that. This was also inconveniently true.

Eve made the edit, sent the file, and then spent too long selecting a full stop instead of an exclamation mark for the accompanying message. Nobody involved in a caption about a laundry basket deserved the burden of her entire emotional life.

By the time Julian arrived, she had swept the floor and put her bag by the door. She had not found a better face to wear.

He looked at her from the threshold. “Walk?”

“Yes.”

They went toward the small park rather than her flat. The air had cooled enough that Eve pulled her sleeves over her fingers. Julian carried a folded sheet of paper in his coat pocket; one corner stuck out whenever he moved his hand.

“What is that?”

“My own reminder of what still needs doing. No patient information.”

“It looks as though it's tried to escape.”

“It disagrees with my pocket.”

She offered to hold it. He took it out, considered it, and folded it again instead.

“I need to do these things myself.”

“I wasn't proposing to run your clinic.”

“I know. I was about to hand you another job because you offered a hand.”

They walked past a boy trying to persuade his scooter to stand upright on its own. His father waited while the experiment failed for the fourth time.

“I could still say no,” Julian said quietly.

Eve turned so quickly that he stopped.

“Do you want to?”

“No.”

“Then don't offer it to me.”

“I wasn't offering—”

“You just did. Like a coat because I'm cold.”

He rubbed his thumb along the edge of the paper in his pocket.

She took a breath. “I'm frightened. I'm allowed to be frightened without it becoming a reason you miss the course.”

“Of course.”

“Not of course. Last night you explained how you avoided telling me because I might be upset. I don't want to spend today performing delighted so you don't decide I need rescuing from next Monday.”

He watched her for a moment. A bicycle bell rang behind them, and they both stepped closer to the hedge.

“That's fair,” he said. “I want to go. I intend to accept it.”

The sentence landed hard and clean.

Eve nodded, then looked at the gravel. “Thank you. That is somehow both better and more upsetting.”

He stood beside her until she started walking again.

At the park kiosk they bought two hot drinks. The lid on hers didn't fit properly, so they sat on a bench while she drank it down far enough to carry safely. Julian took his coat sleeve out of reach of her cup without comment.

“What are you frightened of?” he asked.

She watched a little dog reverse away from a large fallen leaf.

“Being the person who only wants you to have a life if it happens conveniently near mine.”

“That doesn't sound like you.”

“It sounds like a version of me I could grow if I watered it.”

He smiled, then waited.

“And missing ordinary things. Knowing you might stop by. Your terrible opinion about my good scissors. Having somebody to be quiet with without making an appointment.”

“I'll miss those too.”

“You have an opportunity to reconsider the scissors while away.”

“They hurt my thumb.”

“They're not for your enormous veterinary thumb.”

She finally smiled back.

They talked about the week in front of them. He had a final handover block on Saturday, already arranged with the clinic. The covering team would have records and their own responsibilities. He wasn't going to manage ordinary cases remotely from the course, and Eve would not become a messenger between the venue and the clinic.

“Please don't give anyone my number for you,” she said.

“I won't.”

“Tessa would find that offensive anyway. She's much more frightening than I am.”

“She has already asked me whether my handwriting will improve before Saturday.”

“Will it?”

“No.”

“Then she has three weeks of peace to look forward to.”

He drank his tea, looking wounded with great restraint.

Eve took out her calendar. She showed him the existing client deadline, the next venue session, and a dinner with Sophie that had been arranged before either of them knew the departure date. Sunday evening. Sophie had an event-production story that apparently required noodles or pastry and could not be reduced to messages.

“Keep that,” Julian said.

“I am keeping it.”

“Good.”

The first Saturday afternoon of his course might be free, though he would confirm after orientation. Eve could not promise a visit simply because there was an empty square in her diary. Travel cost money, and Dumpling needed proper care if she stayed away.

They looked at the trains for five minutes. Then she closed the page before either of them could turn a possible afternoon into a promise about the whole weekend.

“The second week?” she asked.

“I may be able to come back at the end of it. It depends on the teaching rota. I'll know more once we're there.”

“Tell me when you know. Not after you've solved every connection.”

“I will.”

Her drink was cool enough now. She fitted the uncooperative lid and held the cup very carefully as they left the bench.

At the park gate, his phone rang. He looked at the screen and asked whether she minded if he answered. It was the clinic; she waved him a little ahead so he could hear over the passing traffic.

She couldn't make out the other voice. Julian said that Thursday's appointment needed the covering clinician's review, listened, and thanked them for catching it. He wrote something on the folded paper before ending the call.

“Something forgotten?” she asked.

“Something I thought I'd included, but hadn't.”

“Fixed?”

“Assigned to someone who can fix it. I'll check the record before I leave.”

He put the paper away. Eve was suddenly glad she hadn't taken it earlier. It was crumpled, imperfect, and his. The colleague who called had a question she couldn't have answered, however many carefully ruled lists she offered to make.

She passed him her cup while she pulled a tissue from her bag to wipe the tea from her fingers. He held it without drinking any. That job she could hand over without any confusion at all.

They almost ruined the useful conversation by attempting to schedule daily calls.

Eight suited Eve, except when it didn't. Nine suited Julian, except when it didn't. A long call after dinner sounded pleasant until they began listing dinners, work, study, travel, and the fact that a person sometimes needed to wash their hair without providing evidence of continued affection.

“So we'd both apologize before saying hello,” Eve said.

“It appears so.”

“Terrible format. Poor retention.”

He glanced at her.

“Sorry. Client vocabulary. It leaks.”

They settled on asking for a call when they had time, and saying plainly when they didn't. They would try a longer catch-up on a free evening without treating a delayed reply as a failed examination. Short messages were fine. Actual urgent matters would be named as urgent rather than hidden behind an alarming number of question marks.

“Does this include photographs of the cat?” Julian asked.

“No. Those remain compulsory.”

“Ah.”

“I'm kidding. Mostly.”

Back at Eve's flat, Dumpling was asleep in a position that made his neck look entirely theoretical. Julian washed his hands while she set rice on to cook. They were both hungry enough to become unreasonable, and she refused to have a second difficult conversation beside uncooked food.

He found vegetables in the fridge and asked what needed using. She put him in charge of them, which led to the discovery that her prized scissors were, in fact, uncomfortable for his hand.

“Use the knife,” she said before he could speak.

“I didn't say anything.”

“You thought it loudly.”

Dumpling opened one eye at their voices, found no reason to get involved, and closed it again.

Julian sent his final acceptance after dinner. He sat at the table with his own phone while Eve rinsed the bowls. He read the dates and accommodation details once more, then pressed send.

She hadn't needed to supervise it. Still, hearing him say, “Done,” made her put down the sponge.

“Are you pleased?”

“Yes,” he said. This time there was no crease between his eyebrows. “Very.”

The ache in her chest did not vanish. It moved aside enough for something else.

“Then congratulations again.”

He stood, and she met him halfway across the kitchen. His arms closed around her slowly, leaving her room to decide whether she wanted the hug. She tucked her cold fingers beneath the back of his sweater.

“That,” he said, “feels punitive.”

“It's a celebration.”

“Unusual local tradition.”

She held him a little more tightly.

Later he showed her the size of the suitcase he had been considering buying. She stared at the price, then at him.

“I own a suitcase.”

“You might need it.”

“Not for the next three weeks. It has one wheel that complains, but all four work.”

“I can borrow it?”

“Yes. Please don't interpret the bad wheel as a metaphor.”

He promised with a straight face.

When he left, Eve found a message from Sophie asking whether Sunday still worked. For a moment she was tempted to explain every complication before answering, as if keeping dinner required a supporting document.

She typed, Yes. I may be a bit miserable. Still hungry, though.

Sophie's reply came while Eve was switching off the kitchen light.

Perfect. I have a story involving a missing stage curtain. Misery won't get a monopoly.

Eve laughed and put the phone down beside her keys. Across the room, Dumpling stretched one paw beyond the edge of his bed, occupying a little more of the evening without asking anyone's permission.
