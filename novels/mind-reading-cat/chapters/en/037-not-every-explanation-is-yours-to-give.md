# Chapter 037: Not Every Explanation Is Yours to Give

By Monday morning, Eve had written a sentence so reasonable that she no longer trusted it.

*Staff were responding to complex circumstances not visible in the circulated excerpt.*

There was nothing untrue in it. That was partly the problem. It leaned toward a private story without telling it, inviting readers to imagine that the clinic possessed a decisive secret. She had given the locked door a handsome sign and was calling that restraint.

She printed the draft anyway. Seeing sentences on paper sometimes made their excuses harder to admire.

Dr. Clark had accepted her quote over the weekend. Two hours of drafting and review, followed by one consolidated revision. Any new work would need another agreement. Eve had put a finishing time beside the task in her calendar, then spent Saturday repeatedly wondering whether responsible people checked controversial videos on Saturdays.

She had checked twice.

On Sunday, she had updated two entries in the walking guide, done her washing, and taken Dumpling's paper ball out from behind the fridge. These achievements had not become more glamorous because she had resisted an argument online.

The longer recording was no longer available when she looked on Monday. The short excerpt remained in several places. Absence had immediately acquired a theory: someone must have forced the truth to disappear.

She had no evidence for that theory and no intention of supplying one.

At the clinic, Tessa met her beside the noticeboard. A strip of tape had peeled away from one corner, letting a page curl across the opening hours.

“Can you hold this?” Tessa asked.

Eve held the notice flat while Tessa removed the old tape. It left a gray stripe on the wall.

“The useful side of communications work,” Eve said.

“This side usually stays attached for three days.”

Tessa pressed fresh tape along the top edge, then took Eve to the staff room during the review slot Dr. Clark had arranged. Another staff member covered the desk. It mattered that someone had planned for Tessa to leave it; she hadn't simply vanished from reception and hoped the telephone would develop patience.

Eve spread out the draft.

Tessa read the opening paragraph, tapped *complex circumstances*, and looked up.

“No.”

“I thought that kept the details out.”

“It tells everyone there are details worth asking about.”

Eve had suspected exactly that. Hearing someone else say it still stung.

“What if we say that the short clip doesn't include the full discussion?”

“Does the public need to know that to understand our overnight arrangements? Or do we want them to believe the visitor made an unreasonable request?”

“I don't want to blame the visitor.”

“I believe you.” Tessa set down the paper. “But the sentence is doing work you haven't named.”

Outside, a trolley wheel squeaked over the corridor join. Someone laughed, then apologized to a startled animal in a low voice. Eve stared at the space beneath her polished opening. She had left room for a little heading, perhaps a reassuring blue line. Design had been waiting obediently to make her implication look respectable.

She crossed out the paragraph.

“The full context would help people understand why he refused,” she said.

“It might.”

“And we can't explain it.”

“Not if it means handing out someone's illness or family troubles so people will be nicer to us.”

The bluntness made Eve sit back.

Tessa rubbed a small patch of old adhesive from her thumb.

“I've spent the weekend being asked whether we care about people. I do care. I also want the phone to stop ringing with strangers who have no appointment and a great deal of advice. Those are both true. Neither gives me permission to use somebody else's difficult day.”

Eve looked at the page again. She had been treating privacy as a technical obstacle: an image to blur, a name to cut, a problem good editing ought to solve. Tessa was describing a person who might not want to exist inside the answer at all.

“Has the visitor said what they want?”

“Dr. Clark is asking directly. We don't need to make them responsible for repairing our reputation while they decide.”

Tessa said nothing about what the visitor had told the clinic. Eve did not ask to see the messages.

Instead she turned over the page and drew two columns. The movement was familiar enough to steady her hand, though the headings were different this time.

*Ours to explain.*

*Not ours to publish.*

In the first column she wrote opening hours, staffing, what the clinic could offer after closing, and where visitors could obtain current information about other services. In the second she wrote one word: *Case.*

Tessa pulled the pen gently from Eve's fingers and added beneath it: *People.*

“Otherwise somebody always thinks removing the clinical part makes everything else free material.”

Eve nodded.

They went downstairs to look at the existing information card. It sat in a clear holder behind a display of leaflets, perfectly legible if a visitor already knew to lean around a packet of sample bags and look for it.

Eve crouched to the height of someone seated with a carrier on their knees. From there she could see a smiling dog, two headings about routine care, and the blank back of the card.

“Well,” she said.

“We know.” Tessa moved the sample bags. “It was visible before someone added the display.”

“Was that someone you?”

“Yes. I am capable of creating my own problems.”

They tried the card beside the payment terminal. It obstructed the view of the amount. They tried it at the end of the counter. A man arriving with a broad carrier needed exactly that space to set it down safely.

Eve picked the card up before it could fall.

They settled on the wall beside the waiting chairs, at a readable height, with a second copy Tessa could hand over. Not a dramatic solution. A useful one, provided the wording didn't ask worried people to decode a paragraph of institutional caution.

When the visitor had gone into a consultation room, Eve read the old card aloud to herself. By the third sentence she had lost the subject.

“I do this for a living,” she told Tessa. “I shouldn't need a running start.”

Tessa smiled for the first time that morning.

Dr. Clark joined them in the staff room to confirm the service details himself. The clinic did not provide staffed overnight care. Individual needs required assessment by the appropriate professional; a general notice could not decide where an animal ought to go. Current referral information would be supplied by the clinic, not copied into a permanent post that nobody remembered to update.

Eve wrote carefully. These were operational facts he could verify, not veterinary conclusions she was qualified to reach.

“The card is additional scope,” she said when he finished.

He glanced at the pages. “A small additional item?”

“One layout using this approved wording. I'll quote it separately. We can leave it out today.”

“Send the addition.”

She felt faintly absurd protecting twenty minutes while the internet apparently demanded the defense of an entire profession. Then she looked at Tessa, who had a defined return time to the desk, and decided that twenty minutes was exactly the sort of thing people lost by pretending it didn't count.

Julian came in near the end of the slot. He had been asked to check only the description of his own communication, not approve the clinic's operating arrangements on everyone's behalf.

Eve turned her notebook toward him.

“I think we could make room for the whole—”

“No. We can't publish the conversation.”

She stopped.

For a second she felt the old, unpleasant heat at the back of her neck: the sensation of being publicly corrected for a foolish idea before she had found out whether it was hers.

“The whole question on the card,” she finished. “Instead of a heading that says 'Additional Services.' People may actually ask whether their animal can stay overnight.”

Julian looked at the notebook.

“Right. Yes.”

Dr. Clark leaned closer to read it. The meeting continued, sensibly and usefully, around the little dent that had appeared in Eve's confidence.

She made no joke about needing subtitles. She also didn't say that the interruption had annoyed her. She put a dot beside the proposed heading and concentrated on the job.

At her finishing time, Tessa returned to reception. Dr. Clark left with the single page of questions he needed to confirm. Julian remained long enough to ask when Eve wanted his comments.

“Tomorrow afternoon. Only on what you've been asked to review.”

“Understood.”

She had expected to feel triumphant after assigning such a clean boundary. Instead she felt tired and oddly useless. She was leaving without a compelling story, without a public reversal, and without giving anyone permission to defend Julian in the way she had initially imagined.

Outside, the wind lifted the edge of her scarf. She tucked it into her coat and walked past the tram stop to a bakery with two small tables.

She bought soup and sat facing the window. Her notebook lay closed beside the spoon. A delivery driver came in, stamped rain from his shoes, and was asked to move because he had accidentally blocked the door. He moved. Nobody recorded it. The enormous mercy of that ordinary correction almost made her laugh.

Her phone lit up with Sophie's name.

*How's the clinic thing?*

Eve typed, *Smaller than I wanted. Probably correctly.*

*Sounds like work.*

*It is. How are you?*

The reply came after several minutes.

*Standing beside a loading bay pretending this is lunch. Fine. Will eat properly later.*

Eve started to ask whether later had a specific hour. She stopped. Sophie had answered a brief message between tasks, not invited an audit of her eating schedule.

*Hope you get somewhere with a chair. I'm having soup at an actual table and recommend the technology.*

A laughing face arrived, followed by nothing else.

Eve ate while the soup was hot.

At home, Dumpling followed her from the door to the kitchen, then took up a position between her chair and the cupboard. She stepped around him without appointing his location meaningful. The cupboard held his food. Sometimes an orange-and-white obstacle came with an entirely ordinary explanation.

After feeding him, she worked through the new draft. The dramatic opening went. So did the suggestion that hidden facts would vindicate anyone who waited long enough.

What remained looked almost bare.

A service boundary. A way to ask questions. A commitment to communicate more clearly, still subject to Julian and Dr. Clark agreeing what they could actually stand behind.

She checked the clock and stopped when the agreed drafting time was used. Tomorrow's revision would begin after the clinic answered, not tonight because she disliked unfinished things.

Before closing the file, she moved the question about Julian's wording to the top of the review list.

Not *How can we explain what you meant?*

*Is there anything you think you should have said differently?*

It was less useful for winning an argument.

It might be more useful for doing the job.
