# Chapter 073 — A Corner of an Old Photograph

The cat in the photograph occupied less space than a cardboard box of donated cups.

Eve enlarged the image until the cups became squares and the orange ear became an argument she wanted to win.

Thursday's message had arrived through the contact route on Dumpling's found-cat notice. The sender, Leanne, wrote that she had helped at local charity sales some time ago. An acquaintance sorting old photographs had sent her this one. Was the cat in the corner possibly the one Eve had found?

Possibly was doing an unreasonable amount of work.

Eve put her phone flat on the table. Dumpling slept in his usual bed, with the room arranged almost exactly as it had been before last night's ambitious furniture expedition. There was no reason to wake him and hold a screen beside his face.

She opened the message on her laptop instead.

The photograph showed a long table covered in secondhand crockery. Three people stood behind it, one partly cut off. At the bottom right, an orange-and-white cat looked away from the photographer. A chair leg obscured the front of its body. The image had evidently been photographed from a paper print, because a pale reflection crossed the center.

Eve recognized the unpleasant speed of hope.

A white shoulder. An orange patch near the ear. Something about the thickness of the tail, though the tail was barely in view.

She could have made it into Dumpling before breakfast.

Instead she replied with questions. Did Leanne know when and where the photograph had been taken? Had she seen this cat herself? Could the person who owned the original provide a clearer copy, if they were comfortable doing so?

She did not send every identifying detail from Dumpling's records in exchange for a resemblance.

Then she ate breakfast before the toast went entirely cold.

Leanne called after ten, using the number Eve had agreed she could use for the conversation.

“I should have said I don't know the cat,” she began. “I helped at some sales, but I can't swear I was at this one. My friend thought I might recognize the people.”

“Did you?”

“One, perhaps. We all wore the same dreadful aprons.”

Eve looked at the blurred green cloth behind the cups. “That would make identification harder.”

“It made everything harder. The pockets were on the sides.”

Eve laughed, grateful for a detail too ordinary to be a revelation.

Leanne thought the picture had been taken in the wider neighborhood, but the sales had used several borrowed rooms over the years. Her friend was away until the following week. The date on the message was the date the image had been forwarded, not the date of the event.

“Could it be from before I found him?” Eve asked.

“I think so. But I wouldn't want you putting that down as certain.”

“I won't.”

A small pause followed.

“Are you worried somebody wants him back?” Leanne asked gently.

Eve looked toward the bed.

The honest answer was too untidy to give a stranger in full.

“I want any real information checked properly,” she said. “And I'm attached to him. Those aren't always comfortable together.”

“I understand.”

Leanne offered to ask her friend for the original date and a better image when she returned. Eve thanked her and explained that the picture should not be circulated publicly with a claim that the cat had been identified. Leanne sounded relieved rather than offended.

“I wasn't planning a campaign. I was sorting photographs.”

“Sorry. I spend too much time imagining captions.”

“Cups for sale. Cat not included.”

“That one's better than mine.”

After the call, Eve wrote down the source and the limits. She saved the image unchanged, keeping the message with it. The filename she chose contained no triumphant word like found.

Then she sat very still.

Yesterday, Dumpling had watched them settle on the sofa. This morning, an old image had arrived. Her mind wanted to put those two things together so badly that their proximity began to feel like proof.

A frightened cat. A room full of donated objects. Somebody handing him on.

None of that was visible in the photograph.

Even the frightened part was hers.

She closed the image and opened the caption edit she'd promised to finish by noon. For twenty minutes she made an innocent paragraph worse. She changed helpful to useful, changed it back, and realized she hadn't read the following sentence at all.

She set a timer for one focused pass, put the phone in the drawer, and started again from the actual brief.

The finished version went out at eleven forty-eight. It was not brilliant because her life had briefly acquired a mystery. It was clear because she finally paid attention to the work.

Only after sending it did she contact the clinic through the established owner-search channel. She attached the image and her notes, asking how best to preserve and compare the lead. She did not ask Julian's personal number to decide whether she could keep the cat.

Tessa replied that afternoon. They could add the information to the existing record. Eve could bring the image and the earlier notes when she passed the clinic, without bringing Dumpling merely to compare him with a blurry picture.

At reception, a man was trying to persuade a large dog that the weighing scale was not a bridge over dangerous water. Tessa was helping them, so Eve waited clear of the doorway.

The dog stepped onto the scale, reconsidered, and backed off. Nobody applauded too early.

When they were done, Tessa looked at Eve's folder.

“Is this the same filing-system inspection?”

“The second edition.”

“More tabs?”

“Fewer conclusions.”

“That sounds promising.”

They used a side space away from the waiting clients. Eve laid out the new image beside the clear photographs she had taken for the notice. She had already marked which questions Leanne couldn't answer, but seeing the pages together tempted her into another argument.

“The white comes up here,” she said, pointing. “And the ear—”

“Which ear can you actually see?” Tessa asked.

Eve stopped.

She had been comparing a partly turned head with a straight-on photograph. The shape she wanted to match lay partly behind the chair.

“This one,” she said. “Not enough of the other.”

Tessa nodded. “We can record the resemblance. It doesn't identify him.”

A small orange patch seemed to sit differently. Eve first wanted to explain it away as lighting, then wanted to seize it as proof the cat was someone else's. Both reactions felt selfish when she noticed how quickly one replaced the other.

“We don't have to decide from this copy,” Tessa said.

Eve let her hand fall away from the page.

“No. We don't.”

They checked the found date against the original note, not against the date Eve now told people from memory. The uncertain photograph date could not exclude or establish anything. Leanne's acquaintance had not claimed ownership. If a claim later emerged, it would need the normal supporting information and process.

The clinic printer produced a copy with a dark stripe across the lower corner. Eve stared at the extra mark for a second before Tessa held it beside the screen.

“Printer,” she said.

“I know.”

“You looked as though you were about to take a note.”

“I was.”

They used the digital original for the record instead. Eve crossed out the comparison she'd started on the faulty print and wrote why, then put it in the recycling. It was mortifying how readily a mark made seconds ago had seemed capable of telling her something about the past.

Tessa recorded the contact route with Eve's permission and made clear that nobody would give out Eve's home address to a stranger because they had found a similar cat in a picture.

That was the point where Eve noticed she'd been holding her shoulders near her ears.

“I don't like how relieved I am by that.”

“About not sharing your address?”

“About everything taking longer than one photograph.”

Tessa slid the original note back into the folder.

“Taking care with it isn't the same as hiding it.”

Eve looked at the photograph again. There were people in it too, real people with expressions she couldn't see clearly. She had nearly turned them into suspects before learning their names.

Outside the clinic, Julian was carrying a flattened carton to the recycling area. He stopped when he saw her, then looked down at the carton.

“Not coming to your house,” he said.

“Good. We've filled our quota for cardboard-based drama.”

He put it where it belonged and came back. She told him about the image without handing him a complete theory disguised as a question.

“Can I see?” he asked.

She showed him on her phone.

He looked for a few moments, then handed it back.

“I can see why you thought of him.”

“But?”

“I don't know whether it is him.”

She exhaled. “A disappointing specialty you have.”

“Not knowing?”

“Saying it before I finish building the rest.”

He leaned against the low wall, careful not to block the path.

“Would knowing more change tonight?” he asked.

“It might change something eventually.”

“Yes.”

She appreciated that he didn't argue with the possibility simply to comfort her.

“But tonight,” she said, “he still needs dinner. And I still have a client asking whether a comma makes them sound unfriendly.”

“That sounds urgent.”

“Only to the comma.”

Julian smiled. He had to go back inside. Eve let him, and walked home with the folder beneath her arm instead of another promise she hadn't asked him to make.

Dumpling was at the window when she returned. He stepped down, sniffed her shoes, and stretched with his front paws extended so far that she could see the pale fur between them.

The urge to compare was immediate.

She could take another picture from this angle. She could coax him beside the chair, match the light, and spend the evening producing new reasons to be certain.

Instead she waited until he finished stretching and offered the ordinary greeting he expected.

Later, while he ate, she sent Leanne a short thanks for making the inquiry. There was no request to search every cupboard before her friend came home. Next week remained next week.

She filed the image as a possible lead, with the original message and the unanswered questions. It stayed out of the account's public posts. Her audience didn't need to help her invent a missing life.

At the desk, Eve reopened the image once before closing the folder.

The cups still occupied most of it. The cat still looked away.

She knew no more about what happened after that moment than she had that morning. She did, however, know where the photograph had come from, who might know more, and which parts she had supplied herself.

Behind her, Dumpling knocked his fabric mouse off the chair and looked down in evident surprise at the consequences.

Eve picked it up and tossed it onto the rug.

He followed the thing that was actually there.
