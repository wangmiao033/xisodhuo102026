# Chapter 084 — A Photograph Just for You

The photograph Julian liked best contained neither of their faces.

It showed a low branch crossing the river, with a rubbish bin still visible at the edge. She had taken it during their wandering afternoon, deciding to keep the view without improving every inconvenient part of it.

“That one?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“We aren't even in it.”

“I remember where you were.”

Eve looked at the small image again. The answer made it difficult to criticize the composition.

Saturday's tea had become a modest sorting operation. Julian had brought the boat-restoration book he'd mentioned, with a receipt marking the page he wanted to show her. Eve had produced an envelope of old prints and the photographs on her phone. She had intended to show him two. Somehow they were now looking at a crooked picture of a drain cover she could not remember taking.

“Important work?” he asked.

“Either a texture reference or a very low moment in my career.”

He turned the phone slightly. “It has leaves.”

“Then definitely art.”

Dumpling slept on the cushion behind them. His tail covered his nose, giving the impression that the conversation had disappointed him enough to require a barrier.

The cat was not in most of the photographs. Eve found that unexpectedly pleasing. She loved pictures of him, but the phone also contained evidence that she had looked at other things: a folded paper bird in a shop window, a cake that had slumped before reaching the plate, the ridiculous repair sign Julian had sent her.

She asked whether he minded her keeping that sign in the same private folder.

“No. Though I can't claim authorship.”

“Your credit will say discovery and transport.”

“Accurate.”

They chose six images. Eve rejected the best-lit photograph of an old lunch because it reminded her of stopping a conversation to rearrange a fork. She kept the worse one, with the fork where she'd actually been using it.

Julian chose a blurred view of a bookshop shelf. The title he'd wanted was visible near the edge.

“Did you buy it?” she asked.

“No. I borrowed it later.”

“Was it good?”

“I haven't finished. I'm trying not to judge the book by how easily I fall asleep.”

She laughed, remembering the earlier conversation over soup. This was apparently a continuing professional difficulty with literature.

When she suggested printing a few, he agreed, then asked what she meant by an album.

“A small one. Private. Not a new account category.”

“I'd like that.”

The ease of the answer warmed her. She had approached the word private as if it needed defending; he had heard a practical description.

Eve found a little unused album in the drawer. Its cover was plain blue. It had been part of a stationery purchase she had justified as essential to an old project and then never used. The first sleeve was stuck to the second.

“Luxury edition,” she said, separating them carefully.

Julian held the cover while she worked. Their fingers touched, and neither pretended the transparent plastic required both hands for another full minute.

“Would you like a photograph with us actually in it?” she asked.

“Yes. Would you?”

“Yes.”

She put the phone on its small stand on the table, facing the sofa. This was not the camera, not a recording left running from work. She chose a still photograph with a timer and showed Julian the framing before starting.

He looked at the screen.

“The lamp appears to be growing out of my head.”

“An ambitious new look.”

She moved the stand.

They sat down, leaving room for each other rather than hauling the sleeping cat into the empty space. The first picture caught Eve checking whether the timer had begun. In the second, Julian looked toward her just as she started laughing.

She liked it immediately.

He leaned closer to see.

“Do you want another?” she asked.

“No. I like that.”

“Me too.”

She stopped the timer setting and put the phone away from the edge. Dumpling woke, stretched, and stepped across the sofa between them with the unmistakable entitlement of somebody inspecting furniture he already owned.

Eve moved the album out of his path. He sat on the warm place her hand had occupied, not on the place she would have chosen for a photograph.

Nobody tried again.

Before they went out, Julian opened the book at the passage he had wanted to show her. An amateur boat builder was explaining his measurements with a confidence the following paragraph immediately destroyed. Eve read it, laughed, and turned back a page to find what she had missed.

“I thought you'd like that bit,” he said.

“Because I too can explain a mistake very confidently?”

“Because you notice when the explanation changes.”

She looked at him over the page. It was a small distinction, but one she liked. He had brought a particular thing to show a particular person, not a reliable gift for anybody who happened to occupy her chair.

She asked to borrow the book when he finished. He warned her that this might not be soon. She said the offer could survive his reading speed, and placed the receipt back exactly where it had been.

They finished their tea, then walked together to the nearby photo counter. Dumpling stayed home with his ordinary things where he expected them. Eve checked the selected files before leaving; no client images or private clinic material belonged in the order.

At the counter, the preview cropped off the end of the branch.

The assistant showed her the difference between filling the print and retaining the whole picture with a narrow border. Eve chose the border. Julian stood beside her with the book tucked under his arm, offering no unsolicited defense of professional-looking crops.

They ordered two copies of their shared photograph and one each of the other selected images. The small price was within her ordinary spending money. There was no brand paying for it and no obligation to turn the transaction into a statement against brands.

While the prints were prepared, Julian went to look at the shelf of frames. He returned holding one whose sample photograph showed an implausibly coordinated family at a beach.

“Do you think they had to bring their own trousers?” he asked.

“Those trousers were selected by committee.”

He put the frame back. Neither of them needed a frame today.

Eve noticed a display of very small prints arranged on a card. Each showed a household object from a confusing angle: cupboard hinges, switches, a latch. Somebody had apparently used the service to make instructions rather than decorate a wall.

It reminded her of the notebook-size question beneath her kit video. People kept asking how ordinary things worked once the pretty view ended.

She took out her notebook, then stopped.

“Work thought?” Julian asked.

“Yes. It can wait.”

“You can write one line. I won't become a pumpkin.”

She smiled and wrote: *The small thing that makes the day easier. Show the actual use.*

Then she closed the notebook. One line was a note, not permission to turn their afternoon into unpaid research.

The prints came in a paper envelope. Eve checked the count and the faces, resisting the urge to inspect every color as if a client had commissioned a launch campaign. The branch retained its edges. Julian had no lamp growing from his head.

Outside, he took his copy of the photograph.

“Where will it go?” she asked.

“Probably in the book for now.”

“That's dangerous. You might never get past that page.”

“A real concern.”

He slipped it between the pages with the receipt and closed the book carefully.

They parted at the corner after arranging when they would speak about the retailer's inquiry. Eve walked home carrying an envelope much lighter than the significance she kept trying to assign it.

At the table, she put the prints into the album. The shared photograph went beside the river view. She did not write boyfriend beneath it, not because the word was forbidden but because they had not yet had that conversation clearly enough to let a label do the work for them.

She wrote the place and the day.

The album closed without requiring more.

Later that evening, after feeding Dumpling and herself, Eve opened the one-line note. The idea still interested her, which was a better sign than the burst of excitement at the shop.

She placed her notebook and two pens on the table. Her first possible feature would show a small change that had actually helped her work: keeping the spare pen inside the notebook's loop rather than at the bottom of a bag. It was almost insultingly simple. It was also the answer to a problem she genuinely had.

She tried a shot from above. Her hand blocked the loop. She moved the notebook, tried again, and discovered that the angle she had once corrected in the kit video still mattered. No magical improvement in skill had followed a happy afternoon.

For the second example, she considered the equipment case arrangement inside the cabinet. That could wait until daylight. She would not include her key storage or anything showing the route through her home. Useful did not have to mean complete access.

She wrote three possible questions for viewers: what small thing they kept misplacing, what change they had tried, and what still didn't work. She removed the request for photographs. A first trial could begin with written answers; she did not need people exposing their own homes to prove participation.

The feature would be an ordinary free post, not a new paid service she had somehow launched before testing whether anybody wanted it. There were no sponsors attached. She could make one example within a bounded afternoon and see whether the responses were useful.

Her draft title was terrible.

*Small Systems for Better Everyday Living.*

It sounded like a cabinet selling a course.

She crossed it out and wrote *Where did I put it?*

Better. A question she had asked out loud that morning.

The first short clip showed the pen disappearing beneath three harmless receipts and reappearing where she should have put it. No address, client name, or bank detail was visible. She kept her own exasperated laugh because she had actually made the mistake, not because it would turn another person's tiredness into a charming scene.

Before packing up, she stopped the recording and checked the screen.

Then she checked the captured file, saved it with her work, and left the rest for daylight.

On the chair beside her, the blue album lay closed. She could have opened it, photographed the new page, and made a post that would probably attract more immediate interest than a pen loop.

Instead she moved it to the shelf where she would find it again.

A message from Julian arrived shortly afterward: his photograph was in the book, and he had managed three pages beyond it.

Eve laughed and sent back congratulations on this literary breakthrough.

Then she looked at the shelf once, just because she wanted to.

The picture did not become less real in the dark behind a closed cover.
