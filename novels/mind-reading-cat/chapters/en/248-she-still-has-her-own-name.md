# Chapter 248 — She Still Has Her Own Name

By the time Nell and Theo were five months old, Eve had learned to put her house keys down where she could find them without putting either child down first. On the Thursday she was due back at work, she couldn't find them anyway.

She had just opened the bread bin for the second time when Julian said, “Have you tried the hand holding your bag?”

The keys were looped around one finger. Eve closed the bread bin.

“I was looking for a piece of bread to go with them.”

“Of course.”

Theo laughed from the other side of the room. He'd been laughing all morning at the hem of Julian's sleeve. Nell followed the sleeve with serious attention, as if waiting to see whether it had material besides that one trick.

The first Thursday back was one appointment. A neighborhood repair group was paying Eve to test a draft sign and edit the paragraph people saw before bringing something in. They'd agreed to one meeting, one set of edits and an hour and a half of her time. Robin had offered her a table at You, Me & Paws while the venue’s morning group used the larger table. There wasn't a venue shift attached to the meeting. Eve had had to read the appointment details three times before she stopped trying to find one.

“I'm up to date on the morning,” Julian said. “You don't have to explain the drawer again.”

“What did I explain about the drawer?”

“Some of it was about a different drawer.”

She looked at him. He showed her the burp cloth already tucked through a belt loop and the two clean ones on his shoulder. It was his turn with the twins until noon. He'd blocked the time with the clinic himself; on Sunday Eve would have them from nine to eleven while he went to the early market for an old-record stall he'd been missing. Neither block was a trial of whether one of them could be left with the children. They already did that. They were trying two carved-out pieces of a busy week, long enough to go somewhere without waiting for a text to tell them how much of their morning was left. They hadn't booked another week.

“Right,” Eve said, still standing with her hand on the door.

“Nell ran through the last clean cloth yesterday,” Julian said. “I did the washing while you were out getting milk. These are clean.”

She hadn't been going to tell him about the cloth, precisely. She'd been going to tell him he could call if it was too much. It was too much at intervals even with two of them in the room. Sometimes one of them took a turn standing outside the back door with the recycling and feeling the air against a whole face. Saying he could call, in that tone, would make a morning he'd arranged feel like another thing he was holding for her.

“See you at noon,” she said instead.

From the top of the stairs came a brief scrape. Eve pictured Dumpling turning on the grey blanket on the wide chair. Their bedroom still gave him a quiet place of his own, with water nearby and the babies kept out. Yesterday he'd come down to the sitting room after the adults had put the twins to bed. When nobody called him over, he'd chosen the seat an inch from Eve's elbow and stayed there long after she turned the page. This morning he stayed upstairs. Eve went to the bus stop without stopping to find out what he thought of the arrangement.

At You, Me & Paws the freestanding board that used to face the front window had been turned sideways. From the entrance Eve could read the appointment times before she reached the desk. At first she thought Robin had moved the board to get at a socket. Then she saw an arriving visitor point a friend to the quieter waiting place without having to cross in front of a group that was already working. Robin, putting clean sample pouches on the table, didn't even look up.

Eve stood there long enough for Robin to say, “Are you waiting for a sign?”

“I was trying to decide if I like your new one.”

“Don't tell me yet. I'm betting on this week against last week.”

A woman stopped to ask Robin whether she'd have to take part in a demonstration if she stayed. Robin put down the pouch in their hand and explained how she could watch, and where the extra chair was. They didn't look over at Eve to endorse the answer. Eve took her own coffee to the small table and left them to it. It was a relief, followed so quickly by a small sting that for a moment she couldn't tell them apart.

The repair group arrived with two bolts in an envelope and a sign printed in a notably confident shade of blue. One of the volunteers set it across from Eve and read it aloud.

“‘Bring the broken thing and learn to put it right.’ We thought the right part was encouraging.”

“Do you put everything right?”

“Not everything,” he said. “We had a lamp last time somebody had cut off its plug. We don't handle electrical things.”

His colleague tapped the short paragraph with one nail. “We do say it there.”

They stood the sign up against a water glass and read from the distance of someone approaching a table. Nobody could see the paragraph. Back in her chair, Eve wrote down the things the group could actually take. The pair disagreed about a small folding stool until one went into the envelope for the bolts. The other turned one over in her fingers, recognized the size, and said they'd been remembering two different stools. By then it was clear that a longer list of objects wouldn't solve the problem. The people coming in needed a way to ask before they carried something heavy through the door.

Eve opened a blank document and wrote three versions of a small sentence. The first began with *before*. The second began with *please*. The third looked uncomfortably bare on the screen. She imagined adding two lines under it about the people who fixed things, the reason they tried, the pleasure of going home with something that was usable again. A few months earlier, she'd have used the last twenty minutes to do that for free and then carried the paragraph home to finish.

“Read this one,” she said, turning the screen.

The volunteer who liked the blue read it without having to squint. “‘Ask before you carry it in.’ It does sound a bit like we're cross with them.”

His colleague took the bolts off the table and set the closed envelope on her palm. “Would you want to carry your mother's stool down three flights, then be told you could have sent a description?”

“No.” He left a space. “Can we tell them who to ask?”

Eve put their existing contact beside the line. The volunteer read the two together. He still wanted a little of the blue on the next version. She didn't see any reason he shouldn't have it.

They went through the page once more. One of the volunteers said he'd been hoping to leave with a redesigned sign as well. Eve felt the answer rush up her throat before she'd checked it. Instead she turned her notes back to him. The wording they had just tested was ready to go. A new design would be separate work. If that was what they decided they needed, she could send a separate price after they had a chance to look at the edits.

“Let's print this version in the old layout,” his colleague said, slipping the bolts back into their envelope. “At least somebody can try it.”

Eve emailed the agreed wording and the invoice while they were there to answer one last question. The volunteer asked how she wanted her name shown in their working copy. She said Eve. She'd almost answered the name of the business, which would have been correct for another job. “Just Eve here,” she said again, and put the laptop to sleep when the appointment ended.

Robin was at the front gathering up cards from the finished demonstration. One had been written on with two different pens. Robin held it out to show her. A visitor had found she could read her friend's revision more easily upside down than right way up. Robin had taken that as a better argument for larger handwriting than anything on the handout.

“Do you need another pair of hands to reset?” Eve asked.

“I'm going to eat this first,” Robin said, holding up a cheese sandwich. “The table's not booked till one.”

Eve could see three chairs that ought to go back against the wall. She picked up her bag instead.

There were twenty minutes before the bus she needed. She could have caught an earlier one by going straight to the stop across the road. She sat on the low wall by the row of bicycles instead and pulled out her linen-covered notebook. It still opened at the terrible first drawing of Dumpling if she held it by one corner. She turned to a fresh page. The front wheel of the nearest bicycle was locked at an angle, with its back wheel lifted onto the curb. She tried to draw the space inside the rim rather than the wheel, which worked until she realized she'd made the drawings of both wheels the same width. She turned the pencil over to erase one and found the eraser part had cracked off.

She left the second wheel alone and drew the crooked railing behind it. A person with a bag of oranges came to unlock the bicycle. Eve slipped her feet out of the way. The person balanced the bag on the seat, dropped an orange, and swore briefly when it rolled under the next bicycle. Eve held the bag while the rider reached for it. Then she sat down again and finished the railing after the bicycle was gone. She did not need a second bicycle to get the first one right. The peculiar empty wheel stayed.

She got in at three minutes to noon. One of Julian's socks was on the mat inside the door. She moved it with her toe before finding Julian standing by the sink in two socks of different lengths. He was holding Nell; Theo was on his play mat, absorbed in trying to catch his own foot.

“What happened to the other sock?” Eve asked.

“I'd prefer to deal with the ones that remain.”

She laughed. Julian kissed her when she reached him, then put the last few minutes of the morning into words: who had slept, who had last eaten, and which clean cloth he'd left hanging on the back of the chair because he'd eventually run out of shoulders. He didn't say he'd managed somehow. She didn't ask whether all his time had been taken up.

“Do we keep Thursday?” he asked.

“Maybe. I want to see how Sunday goes for you first.” She rubbed at a mark on her finger and realized it was pencil. “And I don't know whether I'll want to work through the whole next one.”

“You didn't work through this one.”

“No. I drew part of a bicycle.”

“How much of it?”

“An impossible percentage.”

He was waiting, with genuine interest, for her to explain when Theo made a small, outraged noise and lost the foot. Julian set Nell carefully down on the mat beside him. Eve crouched and held one finger out for Theo, who gripped it with more dignity than he'd given his own ankle. Upstairs there was no further sound from the cat's room.

Eve wanted to tell Julian about the line on the sign, and the wrong wheel, and the woman who had known where to wait without either of them going up to her. She wouldn't fit it all in before they ate. She left her notebook open on the hall shelf where she could find it again. The railing looked even less straight than she remembered. She liked it as much as she had outside.
