





After Eve got pregnant, the atmosphere at home changed, extremely obviously.

Julian went chaotic first.

Then Su Li.

Third, Director Chen.

Steadiest of all: Dumpling.

It was almost funny, except it was also her actual life. The humans ran around collecting worries like they were limited-edition. The cat sat in the sun and ran a quieter operation.

Julian's chaos showed up as high sensitivity to anything that "might be a risk."

She reached for a delivery box.

"I've got it."

The box was not heavy. It was a box. He still took it with both hands, as if the cardboard had a personal grudge. She stood there with her arms still out, looking like a person who had been robbed of a chore.

She bent for a spoon that had fallen.

"Don't."

"It's a spoon."

"It's on the floor."

"That's where spoons go when they fall."

He picked it up anyway, washed it anyway, and set it back as if he had defused something. Dumpling watched from the chair, unimpressed by the spoon, impressed by the commitment.

She sneezed one extra time at the door, and he was already there with warm water, a jacket, vitamins, and the hospital appointment slip. Four objects. Two hands. She didn't ask how. Some questions ruined the magic, or the illness, depending how you looked at it.

She didn't know whether to laugh or cry.

"Julian."

"Mm?"

"If you keep this up, I'm going to think I'm not pregnant. I'm already sitting the month."

He looked at her, very serious.

"We can practice early."

She opened her mouth. Closed it. Looked at the vitamins. Looked at him.

She'd noticed: once he entered expecting-father mode, his logic became extremely autocratic, and internally consistent. You could not argue with a man who had already decided gravity was a personal attack.

Su Li's chaos was that she'd already given the two kids eight hundred milk names.

She came over with fruit and a notebook. The notebook was new. That was never a good sign. She sat on the sofa, flipped a page, and began as if this were a meeting with minutes.

"Boy can be Bao, girl Nuo."

"Or boy A-Ju, girl Xiao Li."

"No, no, more literary. Boy Star, girl Moon."

Eve sat on the sofa eating fruit, a black line down her face. The fruit was cut too small. Someone in this house had opinions about choking hazards. She ate the pieces with a fork like a person in a hospital drama.

"Your naming is at the same level as the dog-matchmaking auntie's dog resumes."

"You don't get it." Su Li, offended. "The more countryside the milk name, the easier they are to raise."

"That's not science."

"It's folk science."

"That's worse."

Director Chen wasn't better.

Now, first thing when he came to the shop wasn't revenue.

It was squatting in front of Eve to study her belly.

He arrived with the thermos. He set the thermos down. He asked about the morning numbers with his mouth and asked about the belly with his entire face. Then he gave up on the numbers.

"Did it get bigger than yesterday?"

"Director, children are not balloons. They don't inflate by the day."

"You never know." He rubbed his chin. "Twins. High efficiency."

The phrase high efficiency stunned her into silence for a while. She pictured a quarterly report. She wanted it out of her head.

Julian came out from the back with hot milk, and corrected him, calm.

"Director. Don't talk nonsense."

"How is that nonsense?" Innocent. "This is simple life observation."

"It's not observation. It's a performance review."

Director Chen looked wounded, then proud, then wounded again. He drank from the thermos. "I'm just saying the shop is expanding. In more than one way."

Eve felt, her entire pregnancy, the main prenatal education might be learning to stay calm inside a group of unhinged adults. The twins were going to come out already tired.

Dumpling's steadiness showed up as a new, very conscious role: household inspector.

He used to jump on the sofa via her stomach. He completely stopped.

The first time he changed the route, she almost said thank you, then didn't, because thanking a cat out loud in the shop would give Su Li new material. He paused at the edge of the cushion, looked at her middle, and took the long way around.

When she sat, he'd circle first, then carefully tuck in by her leg or her feet, like he'd suddenly understood two people already lived there. No commentary. Just a new route, filed.

A few times her leg cramped at night, sharp enough to make her hiss. Dumpling was first off the bed, sitting at the edge, meowing toward the hall.

Not a cute meow. A report.

Julian rushed in, about to ask, and Dumpling looked back at him, serious as a duty nurse.

Meaning extremely clear.

Don't stand there. Save her.

Julian's later review: "He reacts faster than a lot of interns."

Eve took the calcium he handed her, laughing.

"Are you going to give him a bonus?"

"We can do cans."

"The expensive ones."

"Okay."

Dumpling licked his fur to the side, ears flicking.

Not bad.

This house still had some conscience.

After six months, she got obviously slower.

She used to spin around the shop like a little top.

Now he'd issued a strict ban.

"No long squatting, no long standing, no lifting, no staying up for signup numbers."

She listened, face sincere.

"Should I just stand at the desk as a mascot then."

"Not impossible."

"Julian."

"Mm?"

"You really do sound like you're keeping a premium pregnant woman."

He tucked a cushion behind her waist, the motion practiced like he'd done it eight hundred times. He had. She'd counted. She was keeping a private score of how annoying it was, and how much she still leaned back. The cushion smelled like the shop, a little sun, a little disinfectant, a little cat.

"I am."

"What's premium about it?"

"My wife. Twins. Temper not entirely stable." Completely serious. "Already premium."

She'd been about to roll her eyes. "My wife" still did something stupid to her face. In the end she only hummed.

"Your service attitude is acceptable."

"It can be better."

"For example?"

He held a piece of cut apple to her mouth, naturally.

"For example, continued feeding."

When she bit it, she thought marriage was a strange thing.

It could be a certificate, a ceremony. It could also just be someone, when you didn't want to move, quietly cutting an apple into small pieces and holding it out. No speech. Juice on his thumb. She pretended not to notice he'd cut off the peel because she was being picky this week.

The shop had fully settled by then.

You, Me & Paws had gone from a niche trial to its own word of mouth. People brought pets from the other side of the city, some for consults, some for events, some just to sit. The sofa had a dent that belonged to a regular who never booked, only drank water and watched other people's dogs. Late afternoons smelled like wet fur and tea. The bell over the door had a tired, familiar sound.

Eve had turned the whole cork wall by the desk into a note wall.

Photos, stickies, little cards.

Thank you for making our cat not scared of people anymore.

I sat here a whole afternoon after a breakup. I didn't want to cry as much on the way home.

The dog didn't get a match, but I met a pretty good friend.

Bean is gone. Thank you for sitting with me while I learned how to say goodbye.

Every time she looked, her throat did that annoying tight thing. She didn't stand there and have a moment. She straightened a crooked photo. That was as much ceremony as she could do without getting embarrassing.

One evening she leaned behind the desk, a hand on her belly, staring at the wall. The shop was closing. Light gone honey-colored on the floor. One of the twins kicked, unimpressed with her feelings. The kick was small and rude. She pressed back, lightly, like answering a message.

He came over, followed her gaze for a few seconds.

Dumpling was on the chair already, loaf-shaped, eyes on the apple plate as if it were a second note wall with worse handwriting.

"What are you thinking?"

"That this shop really did save a lot of people."

He looked down, and covered the back of her hand, lightly.

His palm was warm. The kick happened again, as if the twins wanted in on the meeting. He felt it too. She watched his face change by a millimeter.

"It saved me too."

She looked up.

He was looking at the wall. Quiet. Not performing the line. Just saying it at the cork, like the cork had asked.

"If it weren't for you, I'd probably still be clinic and home, thinking being a good doctor was enough," he said. "You made me see there's a lot we can do besides treating the sick."

She waited a beat. If she answered too fast she'd get syrupy, and she refused.

"Do you regret it?"

"Regret what?"

"Getting dragged into a shop by me, plus a marriage, plus twins."

He smiled, and flicked her forehead, lightly.

"Eve."

"Mm?"

"I came out ahead."

Her nose went itchy, immediately. She blamed the apples. She did not have a defense against this kind of line, which was rude of him, because he barely used extra words and somehow still won.

Not earth-shaking.

Just ordinary, certain:

Being with you was a win.

She ate another piece of apple so she wouldn't have to answer right away. He didn't push. Dumpling, on the chair, watched the apple like it might become policy.

When she finally spoke, it was only, "Your feeding service can stay."

"I was going to stay anyway."

"I know."

She leaned back into the cushion he had put there, premium and annoyed about it, and let the wall keep being a wall.
