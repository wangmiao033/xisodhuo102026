After the shop opened, Eve started noticing: they weren't only catching cats and dogs. They were catching little feelings people had nowhere to put.

Some people brought a pet because they were happy, and wanted a birthday.

Some brought one because they were anxious, scared they'd done something wrong.

Some brought one because they couldn't let go.

That Thursday afternoon was beautiful. Sun through the glass, even the wood floor warm, the kind of warm that made you forget the mop was still in the back with last night's water. The auntie who pushed the door open had eyes that were terribly red.

In her arms was a very, very old Chinese village dog, fur more than half white, head still trying to rest in the crook of her arm, like he wanted his person not to be sad. He was doing a job. The job was too big. He was still doing it.

She started, and her voice broke.

"The doctor said he doesn't have much time."

The shop went quiet. Even Dumpling, on the platform, stopped licking a paw. He was not a sentimental cat. He still knew that tone.

Eve took her into the little consult room, asked a few gentle questions, and learned the old dog was Bean. Twelve years. The auntie's husband had died early, kids out of town. The one who talked to her most, usually, was Bean.

He was very sick now. The hospital had already given a plan: if they went conservative, it was mostly about keeping him comfortable.

She still couldn't accept it.

"Every time I wake up, I'm scared he's not breathing." She wiped her face with the back of her hand, then looked at the hand like it had betrayed her. "I can't stand him hurting, and I can't stand him leaving. I don't even know what I'm supposed to do."

Eve's throat tightened, lightly, the way it did when a number on a bill was worse than expected and there was nobody to argue with.

She used to think goodbye was a big movie plot. Rain. Music. Someone running.

Later she learned real goodbye was often too quiet. It was an old dog's fur going white. An old woman's shaking hands. A sentence that was just I don't know what to do. No soundtrack. Just the consult-room clock, cheap, doing its job.

Julian sat beside them, gentler than usual. The clinic voice, without the clinic hurry.

"You've already done so well," he said. "Right now the important thing isn't how long you keep him. It's making him more comfortable, and leaving yourself fewer regrets."

She cried harder. Not louder. Harder.

Eve passed tissues, poured warm water, and stayed with her while she talked it out. She didn't rush. Rushing felt like a kind of cruelty she had learned at the old company, and she was trying not to import it.

In the end they helped her make a very small "goodbye prep" list.

Not to force anyone to accept it.

Just so the words that might not get said in time had a chance to come out early.

Take more photos.

Record the sound of him sleeping, that huff.

Make one meal he loves, if the doctor allows a little.

Tell him, these years, how much he mattered.

At the door, holding Bean, the auntie stood for a long time. Then she looked back. "Miss, why do you do this here?"

Eve paused, then smiled. It wasn't a shop smile. It was smaller.

"Because somebody should sit with people."

The auntie nodded. Tears again.

"Then you're doing it really well."

After she left, the shop was quiet for a while. Sun still there. The warmth of it suddenly felt like an accident.

Eve crouched to pick up the little blanket Bean had lain on, slow. There was white fur on it. She didn't shake it off right away.

Julian came over and folded it with her. His corners were neater. She let him have the corners.

"Did that hurt?"

"A little," she said, quiet. "I used to think we were starting a business. Now it feels like more than that. Which is inconvenient. Business is supposed to have a lane."

He looked at her.

"Regret it?"

"No." She shook her head, eyes a little red, still smiling, because the alternative was sitting on the floor, and the floor had just had a dog on it, and she still had to open tomorrow. "I just suddenly thought being a doctor must have been hard for you, for a long time."

He paused.

"It's alright."

"It's not alright." She held the blanket. "You look at this every day. Other people's panic, sadness, not-letting-go. You have to catch some of it. It doesn't go in the bin with the gloves. Don't tell me it does."

He didn't speak.

Understanding from the outside was one thing. Being held in someone's actual care was another. She could see him notice the difference. He didn't name it. He just stood there, holding half a blanket, like half a blanket was a medical instrument.

She looked up, the blanket in her arms.

"Julian."

"Mm?"

"If you're sad later, you can come find me too."

Too natural.

Natural like she'd already put herself in an important place. Natural like keys, and who locked the back, and who remembered the extra tissues.

He looked at her. The look stayed. Not decorated. Just stayed.

"Okay."

Then he reached up and tucked a bit of hair behind her ear.

Very light.

Light enough that she felt it in her ear, then in her throat, then in the stupid urge to laugh, because her body could not hold two temperatures at once.

She'd still been in the heavy feeling. After that, the sadness didn't vanish. It just had to share the room with her pulse, slamming around like it had missed a meeting.

She was so useless.

One second, life and farewell. The next, staring at a man's hand, thinking about whether her hair had been messy, which was an insult to Bean and also extremely her.

Dumpling sat to the side, shaking his head.

Humans were complicated.

Crying and falling in love at the same time, and somehow it didn't conflict. He would not be filling out a form about this. He would, however, sit on the folded blanket later, because the blanket was warm, and grief had left a good seat.

***

Bean's auntie left a little leftover warmth in the shop.

That night after close, Eve and Julian didn't talk much. They just put things away. Bowls. Signs. The list, folded, into a drawer, like it might be needed again, because it would.

As they were leaving, she heard an argument outside.

Not loud. A familiar, irritating sharpness in it. The kind of sharpness that came with good clothes and a plan.

She opened the door, and her face changed.

Cheng Yin.

Standing with a man in a suit: the older brother who'd supposedly "introduce investors." Family friend's daughter, plus family friend's son's pitch deck. A matching set. Eve had not ordered this set.

Cheng Yin clearly hadn't expected her. A blink, then that soft look again, the one that always arrived a half-second late, like makeup.

"Eve, sorry. We're here to talk to Julian."

Eve felt off, for no good reason she wanted to say out loud.

Not jealousy. Jealousy she had already practiced, badly, with a man and a watch.

Alert.

The shop was still behind her, lights half off, smelling like disinfectant and the afternoon. She didn't like people bringing "all at once" to a door that had just held Bean.

She hadn't spoken yet. He was already behind her. Close. Not touching. There.

Cheng Yin's brother went straight at it. "Julian, Yin-yin said this shop of yours is doing alright. I have a brand looking for a pet-lane partner. If you want, I can package you up and make you big. Funding, chains, account matrix, all at once."

It sounded tempting.

Tempting past the point of comfort. Tempting like a job posting that used the word family.

Eve stood there, already tight. Her hand at her side wanted to close. She let it.

It wasn't that she didn't want to grow.

She knew too well what "all at once" sat on.

Process. KPIs. Return for capital. Content compromises. Copy-paste stores...

The things that had made this shop land in people might get sanded off overnight. The list in the drawer. The auntie with Joy. Teacher Eve, which she still wasn't. All of it, sanded, until it looked like a slide.

Cheng Yin saw he wasn't answering, and added, soft.

"It's a good chance. You're still small. Someone leading means fewer mistakes."

Eve's hand at her side slowly closed. She didn't have the right to decide for him.

She still didn't like it.

At all.

She was about to speak — about staying true, about not getting dragged, a whole speech she had written in her head during worse jobs — when Dumpling walked out from behind the door, unhurried.

He looked at Cheng Yin, then her brother, then flicked his tail, walked to the welcome mat, and sat.

The not-welcome was extremely clear. He did not hiss. He did not need to. He sat like a bouncer who had already checked the guest list and found nobody.

The brother's face stiffened. "What's with the cat?"

Eve, blank: "Maybe he also doesn't like 'all at once.' He's in operations. He's seen a pitch."

Julian couldn't help it. His mouth moved.

Then he looked up, even.

"Thanks. We don't need it."

Cheng Yin stared. "Why not?"

"Because this shop isn't a project for copying," he said. "At least not now."

The brother frowned. "That's not how you do business. Too much feeling."

"Mm." Julian nodded. "Then we'll do feeling first."

When he said it, his eyes landed on Eve, naturally.

Short.

Short enough to make her forget the speech. Short enough that she had to look at Dumpling's back so she wouldn't do something unprofessional with her face.

Cheng Yin clearly got it.

The softness on her face finally cracked. Not ugly. Just real, which was rarer.

"Julian, are you..."

"Cheng Yin." He cut in, still not heavy, very clear. "You don't need to arrange this kind of thing for me anymore."

Quiet.

She looked at him, then at Eve, said nothing else, managed a smile, and left.

Her brother didn't look happy. He followed. The suit went with him. The pitch went with the suit.

When they were gone, there was only night wind at the door. A scooter somewhere. Someone's TV, too loud, through an open window.

Eve stood there, pulse still going.

She'd prepared a whole speech about staying true, about not getting dragged by capital.

He hadn't given her the chance.

He'd even, in front of other people, very obviously stood on her side. Not a speech. A refusal. She liked the refusal more than she would have liked a speech.

"Julian."

"Mm?"

"Just now, were you..."

"Was I what?"

"A little hot."

He looked down at her, and smiled. Small. The one that meant he had heard the joke and was going to make it worse.

"Only a little?"

She looked away, then back, ears burning, the urge to win already late.

This man was getting better at it.

Behind them, Dumpling stood up from the mat, shook one paw, and walked inside first.

The night air was fine.

The mat was his.

If people wanted to do business at the door, they could make an appointment, and the appointment would be declined.
