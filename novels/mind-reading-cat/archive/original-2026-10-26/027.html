The First Time She Sees Him Really Down

Dating can give you a fake idea.

Like: if you like someone, you only ever see the gentle side, the solid side, the side that catches you. The man who brings egg tarts. The man who dries your hair. The man who says "slow is fine" like he has all the time in the world.

Until that night, when Eve saw Julian actually low for the first time.

The shop wasn't busy. He'd gone back to the clinic midday for an emergency surgery fill-in. She'd texted him a joke: Julian, clock out on time or the family will complain. Added a sticker. Felt cute about it.

The sticker was a cat with a clock. Dumpling, on the counter, had watched her send it and then closed his eyes, as if secondhand embarrassment were a real medical condition.

10:30 p.m. Still nothing.

A bad feeling started. Not drama. Just the quiet, ugly kind, the kind you get when someone who always answers... doesn't.

The shop lights felt too bright. The signup sheet looked unfinished on purpose. Eve checked her phone, put it face down, picked it up, checked again. The last message was still her joke, sitting there like it had walked into the wrong room.

She was about to call when Director Chen rang first.

His voice was heavier. No thermos joke. No "Eve, your cat is bullying the intern."

"Eve, Julian just finished. The surgery went fine. The puppy still didn't make it. The owner was pretty intense, said some ugly things. He's sitting in the back by himself. Do you want to... go see him?"

Her stomach turned over, once, hard.

The words arrived in order. She still had to put them down twice. Surgery fine. Puppy didn't make it. Ugly things. Sitting in the back.

She shut the shop almost immediately. Lights, register, the half-written signup sheet. Grabbed Dumpling because she didn't want to walk into that hallway empty-handed, and because Dumpling, for all his nonsense, had sat through worse nights than this.

Dumpling did not love the carrier at this hour. He went in anyway, after one look at her face. No protest meow. Just a heavy, warm body accepting a night shift.

The clinic at night was a little hollow.

Cold white hall lights. Disinfectant more obvious than usual, like the building had given up pretending it was anything else. A drip stand in the corner. Someone's shoes squeaking far away, then nothing.

The floor shone in a cheap, tired way. Eve's own footsteps sounded too loud. Dumpling's carrier handle cut into her fingers. She did not switch hands. Switching would have felt like stalling.

When she pushed open the break-room door, he was by the window, white coat still on, elbows on his knees, looking at the floor. Not at his phone. Not at a file. The floor.

The window showed mostly his own reflection and a dark parking lot. The milk in her bag was still warm. The bread had already lost its shape.

He heard her, and looked up.

Still a calm face.

Under it, a heavy tiredness. The kind that sat in the shoulders. She knew that look on other people. She hadn't seen it on him, not like this, not with the coat still on like he'd forgotten he could take it off.

She walked over, didn't ask anything, just set the warm milk and bread beside him. The bread was squashed from the bag. She hadn't thought about presentation. She'd thought: food. Something warm. Don't make him talk first.

The plastic bag crackled. Too loud for the room. She left it anyway.

"I'm here."

Three simple words. They sounded dumb in the quiet. She left them anyway.

He looked at her. His throat moved.

"Why aren't you asleep?"

"Boyfriend worked late. Girlfriend came to check."

She made it light on purpose. If she went gentle, he might shut the door again. She'd seen him do that with other people. Polite. Finished. Alone.

He didn't laugh.

A long quiet, then, low: "The dog was already in bad shape when it came in. We did what we could. We still couldn't keep him."

She listened. Didn't fill. The milk steamed a little and then didn't.

Somewhere down the hall, a machine beeped once and stopped. Dumpling shifted in the carrier, then went still again, as if even he understood this was not a snack night.

"The owner said if we hadn't wasted time, it wouldn't have died." He looked at his palms, voice down. There was still iodine stain in the crease of one thumb. "I know that's not true. Sometimes, though..."

He stopped.

The iodine was brown-yellow. Ordinary clinic color. It still made her angry, not at him, at the sentence that had been thrown at those hands.

She crouched, looking up at him. The floor was cold through her jeans.

"Sometimes you still feel terrible."

He didn't speak.

Which was a yes.

Her eyes burned. She blinked it back, annoyed at herself, more annoyed at the owner she hadn't met.

She used to think he was so steady.

Steady like nothing could knock him over. Like vets came factory-sealed.

Now she got it. It wasn't that he didn't feel it. He was just used to handling it alone. Coat on. Window. Floor. Wait until the feeling got bored and left.

"Julian," she said, quiet. "You're not a god."

"I know."

"You also can't save every animal every time."

"I know."

"Did you blame yourself today?"

A few seconds. Then a low mm.

The mm was small. It still filled the room.

She stood, and hugged him.

Not hard.

Steady, though. One hand on the back of his coat, which still smelled like disinfectant and soap. She could feel him breathe, too shallow.

The coat was slightly stiff. Clinic laundry. His shoulder was warm under it. She stayed.

"Then blame yourself for a minute." She set her chin on his shoulder, also quiet. "When you're done, come back and eat the bread. It's ugly, but I paid for it."

His body went a little stiff.

Like he wasn't used to being held in this kind of feeling. Like the hug was a procedure he hadn't studied.

She didn't let go.

After a while she felt him lift his arms, slowly, and hold her back.

Not tight. It still had a tiredness that had been sitting there a long time. His hand stayed on her back, not moving, like if he adjusted it the whole thing might become a conversation he couldn't do.

Dumpling sat in the doorway, and for once didn't make trouble.

She had opened the carrier without thinking. He had come out without making a speech. He just sat, tail around his paws, like he was on door duty. No slipper. No fake injury. A fat cat doing a night shift.

His eyes were half-closed. Not sleeping. Guarding. The hall light made a thin line on his back.

A long time later, Julian said, by her ear, "Eve."

"Mm?"

"Thank you."

"For what?"

"For coming."

She loosened a little, and looked up at him. His eyes were dry. That almost made it worse.

The break-room clock was ugly. She looked at it anyway, so she wouldn't look too hard at his face.

"Didn't you say? If you're sad later, you can come find me too."

His gaze shifted, very lightly. Not a speech. Just the sentence landing, late, like a message that had been sitting on unread.

He bent and touched his forehead to hers. His skin was cool. Hers wasn't.

"I'll find you. From now on."

She thought, stupidly, about the joke sticker she'd sent at lunch. Clock out on time. Family will complain. She wanted to take it back and also didn't. The joke was still true. She was still here.

Compared with all the soft, silly little days, this was the part dating didn't put on the brochure.

Not only coming close when it was happy.

When the other person didn't look good, when they were down, when they were doubting themselves: still walking over, and standing there. Even if the bread was squashed. Even if you didn't know the right line.

She took his hand and stuffed the bread at him. His fingers were cold.

"Okay. Then we execute rule one."

"Which is?"

"Julian. Eat."

He finally smiled.

Faint.

A lot more real. Crumbs on his coat. Dumpling still in the doorway, pretending he wasn't watching.

Eve sat on the extra chair and drank half the milk herself, because someone had to, and because if she kept looking at him she might start giving speeches, and neither of them needed that.

The milk was still a little warm. Not good. Fine. She drank it like a job.

Julian ate the ugly bread. Slow. The way a person eats when they have remembered they have a body. Dumpling came over at last, sniffed the empty bag, found nothing, and sat on Eve's foot instead.

Weight. Warm. Ordinary.

She scratched his ear once. He allowed it.

Outside the break room, the clinic kept being a clinic. Inside, for a few minutes, it was just three of them, and a rule that was only one word long.

Eat.

Julian did.

Eve watched the crumbs, and did not say I was scared when you didn't answer, because he already knew, and because the bread was doing the speaking she couldn't make pretty.

Dumpling, on her foot, closed his eyes for real this time.

Night shift complete.

The humans could lock up themselves.

Eve waited by the sink while he washed his hands twice, the way he did after a bad night even when the soap had already done the job. She didn't make a joke. She held the carrier. The corridor lights were the same ugly white they always were.

"Next time you text first," she said. "Even if it's only three words."

"Which three."

"I'm still here. I'll come."

He nodded once. That was the rule, written down in the air between them, not on any clinic form. Dumpling bumped the plastic, once: home. They went, the bread gone, the coat over his arm, the night a little less hollow than when she'd walked in.
