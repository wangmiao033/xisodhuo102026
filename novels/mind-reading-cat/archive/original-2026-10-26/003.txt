Eve went home and took the adoption-event gig, mostly to see if it was real.

The pay wasn't great. She did the math on the bus, twice. Two weekend days covered Dumpling's medicine and part of his food, if she didn't get ambitious about her own dinners. More important than the money, though: she finally had something specific to do. Unemployment had a way of turning hours into rooms. You sat in them. You stared. Eventually one of you blinked.

Work, even small work, gave the day an edge.

She pulled an all-nighter. Theme, poster copy, short-video scripts. She made three versions of the headline and hated all of them until 4 a.m., when the fourth one was just okay, which in this industry counted as a miracle. Dumpling slept on the chair across from her, one bandaged paw hanging off, like a supervisor who'd given up on the night shift.

When she sent the pack to Julian the next day, she was ready to get picked apart. She had a whole inner speech prepared: it's a draft, we can change the tone, I know the logo placement is—

He replied with two words.

Looks good.

Half a minute later, another line.

A lot more professional than the last person we hired.

Eve stared at the screen. Then she smiled, for no respectable reason, the kind of smile you make when you're alone and therefore allowed to be pathetic.

Dumpling was on the sofa back, head tilted.

"What?" She flipped the phone face down, as if that would hide the crime. "An adult gets a compliment from the client, she smiles. That's normal. That's capitalism."

His tail flicked. He did not buy it. He had a very specific look for when he did not buy it.

Saturday's event was in the little plaza outside the clinic. The weather, for once, decided not to be a villain.

Eve got there early: backdrop, banners, product table. When she was busy, she was good. She knew this about herself. It was the sitting still that ruined her. Fast hands, faster mouth. Even the receptionist whispered to Tang, not quietly enough, "So Eve isn't just here to argue with Julian. She's actually scary when she works."

Tang had the face of someone already shipping them and not even pretending to be professional about it. "I said it. People who fight the second they meet? That's a couple. That's a couple with homework."

Julian was checking a border collie. He looked up, just once.

Both girls scattered and became extremely interested in a bag of cat food that did not need to be moved.

The event started at ten.

Plenty of people. Some actually wanted to adopt. Some just wanted photos for their moments, the kind with a sad-cute caption and no follow-through. Eve moved through the crowd: guiding, explaining, filling forms, too busy to drink water, which felt familiar in a way she refused to miss.

She'd brought Dumpling too, parked in a display pen with a little sign she'd handwritten: Visiting. Not available. Please do not tap the glass of his soul.

The original idea was fresh air and extra cuteness. Once the fat cat sat down, he looked more like security. A round, orange security guard with a bandage and opinions.

If someone actually liked animals — the quiet ones, the ones who crouched instead of squealing — he'd reach out a paw. Casual. Like a handshake.

If someone shoved a phone in his face, said "so cute" in that high voice, while their whole body said this is dirty, he pulled his tail in and looked disgusted on purpose. Eve watched it happen three times and started trusting the disgust.

The weirdest part was lunch.

A polished young man came over, talking about how he'd always wanted to adopt, he had so much love to give, animals had saved him, really. He sounded so sincere a few girls nearby almost teared up. Eve had written copy like that. She knew how it was built.

The second he got close to the pen, Dumpling exploded.

Not regular wariness. Not the I-don't-know-you fluff.

The whole cat went tight. Ears flat. A low sound in his throat that Eve had not heard yet, and did not like.

She went alert immediately. The clipboard in her hand suddenly felt like it should be a weapon.

The man was still smiling. Nice teeth. "Is he just shy with strangers?"

Eve looked at him. She thought of the badge on the floor. The backup folder. The trash-can tap that hadn't happened yet, but felt, somehow, like a category.

She heard herself ask, "Have you had a pet before?"

A pause. Small. Then: "I did. I rehomed it later."

"Why?"

"Work got busy. No time."

Clean answer. Natural face. The kind of answer you could take to HR.

Dumpling's reaction got worse. He smacked over the water bowl. Water everywhere. A few people laughed, nervous. Dumpling did not laugh.

Julian came over at the sound. He didn't rush. He never rushed, which was its own kind of pressure. His eyes sat on the man's face for two seconds. Then, mild as tea:

"If you have a history of abandoning animals, we don't approve applications here."

The man's expression changed. The sincerity peeled. "What's that supposed to mean?"

"Nothing." Calm. "Just fill out the form honestly. We do follow-ups. We actually do them."

The man forced a smile, the kind that showed he'd been caught and wanted to call it a misunderstanding. He said nothing else, and left. He didn't take a brochure.

When he was gone, Eve let out a breath. "You thought he was off too?"

"Old cat-scratch scars on his wrist," Julian said. He said it like he was noting a limp. "When he talked about the pet he used to have, his eyes didn't move at all. People who actually kept one, actually liked one, don't look that empty. They remember something. Even if it's small."

Eve glanced at the man's retreating back, then down at Dumpling.

He was still offended at the edge of the pen, fur not quite settled, like he'd just finished a security sweep of the entire event and found the event wanting.

A chill went up her spine. Not magic-movie. More like: if I had been tired, if I had been polite, if I had wanted the numbers to look good...

If Dumpling hadn't reacted that hard, she might not have asked.

Julian followed her gaze and scratched Dumpling's head. Dumpling allowed it, which was becoming a pattern. "He's smart."

"I don't think smart covers it," she said, quiet enough that the plaza noise almost ate it.

"Hm?"

"Nothing." She course-corrected immediately, because you did not stand at an adoption event and say my cat might be haunted. "I'm complimenting your clinic's feng shui. The cats come out psychic. Very on-brand. Very... beige walls."

He looked at her like he'd heard the lie. He didn't push. He just said, "Don't let people stick their hands in if he does that again," and went back to the collie, as if this were all still a regular Saturday.

By the end of the afternoon they'd placed three cats and a little mixed-breed dog, better than expected. Eve's voice was hoarse. Her feet hurt. She felt, dangerously, useful.

Director Chen looked at the sign-up sheet and couldn't stop smiling. He had the joy of a man whose clinic had, for one afternoon, been the kind of place he told himself it was. He waved a hand: hotpot tonight, on him.

Eve tried to decline. It was automatic. Declining was how she stayed un-owed.

Su Li blocked her with one sentence, without looking up from stealing a leftover balloon string.

"You're unemployed. Turning down free food is a professional failure. I'm writing you up."

So she went.

The restaurant was all steam and orange light and the particular noise of people who had done a good thing and wanted to eat about it. Everyone packed around a split-pot. Dumpling got passed between the front-desk girls for photos and received the highest-tier hospitality of the night, including a freeze-dried treat he accepted like a celebrity accepting a gift bag.

Eve was dunking tripe, finally hungry, when a plate of just-cooked beef appeared in front of her. Still pink in the middle. The way she'd said she liked it, two hours ago, to nobody in particular.

She looked up. Julian was picking food like nothing had happened. Like plates moved by themselves in this city.

"Didn't you say you like it a little rarer?"

She blinked. "You heard that?"

"You weren't quiet."

Which was fair. She was never quiet when she was working. Su Li was drinking her soda, fighting a smile and losing so badly it was a whole performance.

Heat crawled up Eve's neck. She buried herself in the meat. It was good. That made it worse.

After a while the steam made everyone looser. Conversation got easier. People talked about the collie who'd peed on the banner, and the auntie who'd cried when she got approved, and Tang's theory that Dumpling should be on payroll.

Tang asked, chopsticks waving, "Eve, want to work with us long-term? The events got classier the second you showed up. No offense to last year's posters. Full offense to last year's posters."

Eve laughed. "Let me see if I get another job first. I have a very competitive relationship with my rent."

Director Chen jumped in, delighted. "Don't rush if you don't. Our temple's small, but there's always somewhere you can be useful. We have a lot of animals and not enough people who can spell."

"Director," Julian said mildly. "Don't poach people to their faces."

"This isn't poaching," Director Chen protested. "This is providing reemployment opportunities for the youth. It's patriotic."

Everybody laughed.

Eve did too. It surprised her, a little, how easy it was. How her face still knew how.

For a second she realized she hadn't sat at a table like this in a long time.

No endless overtime hanging over dessert. Nobody pretending to care while they fished for who was getting cut next. No compliments with a hook in them.

Just food. Talk. Someone sliding her a piece of beef like it was nothing, which meant it wasn't nothing.

On the way back, the wind was cool. The sycamore leaves made that dry, late sound.

Eve held Dumpling, walking beside Julian down the road by her neighborhood gate. Dumpling was a warm, heavy weight, already half asleep, trusting her not to drop him, which was a lot of faith for a Saturday.

Su Li had been summoned away by her boss, because of course she had. That left one man, one woman, one cat, and a silence that was a little too much. Not bad. Just... noticeable. The kind you wanted to fill, and also didn't.

"Thanks for today," Eve said first, because someone had to. "The gig, and..."

She paused. Looked at the trees. Looked at his shoulder, not his face.

"And making me feel like I'm not actually that much of a disaster. Which, considering the week I've had, is a high bar. You cleared it. Don't let it go to your head."

Julian's step hitched. Small. She would've missed it if she hadn't been walking right there.

Under the streetlight she could see her own hair moving in the night wind, which was a dumb thing to notice. She kept talking anyway, quieter.

He was looking ahead. The backs of his ears were red again. She was starting to collect that, which was a problem.

"You weren't a disaster," he said.

Just that. No speech.

Then, after a second, still not looking at her: "The last place just wasn't right for you."

A very ordinary sentence. The kind you could put on a mug and ruin.

It still landed. Not like fireworks. More like walking into a room and realizing someone had already turned the lamp on, and you'd been standing in the hall telling yourself you didn't need it.

"That's a nice way of saying they fired me."

"That's a nice way of saying they were wrong."

She didn't have a joke for that. She let it sit.

Dumpling cracked one eye in her arms.

He looked at Eve, then at Julian, then closed his eyes again, satisfied, and started a very quiet purr. The rumble went through her sleeve.

If he could talk, she thought, he'd say something rude.

Something like: finally. One of you has taste.

She scratched under his chin and whispered, "Don't start."

He purred louder.
