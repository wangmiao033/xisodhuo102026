Eve found out she'd been laid off at 3:07 on a Wednesday afternoon.

She'd just finished rewriting a pitch deck and was heading to the break room for instant coffee, the kind that tasted like burnt plastic and still somehow kept her alive. HR was already standing by her desk. The girl had that look companies train into people: I don't want to be here either, but this is my job. Her voice came out soft, like she was afraid of waking a baby.

"Eve? Mr. Zhao wants you in the meeting room."

Eve's fingers tightened around her mug. For a stupid second she thought, maybe it's a bonus. Then the rest of her brain, the part that had survived three years of this place, said: don't be an idiot.

If a boss called you into a meeting room alone, it was almost never good news. Good news went in the group chat, with a little firework emoji, so everyone could pretend they were a family. Bad news needed a closed door. That way you could fall apart without ruining the company's brand image.

Inside, Mr. Zhao and HR sat side by side with hot tea. There was even a printed document waiting on the table, squared to the edge like someone had practiced this.

It looked exactly like she was about to sign some huge partnership deal.

Shame the other party was unemployment.

"Eve," Mr. Zhao started, gentle enough to be setting her up on a blind date. "The company's been doing some restructuring. You know how the industry is right now..."

Eve silently filled in the rest of the script for him. She'd heard it in other people's stories. She'd even written versions of it, back when she still thought she was safe.

You're very talented.
It's just that the company's current direction doesn't quite match your growth path.
This isn't about your performance. It's a role adjustment.
We're very sorry.

"After a comprehensive review," Mr. Zhao slid the papers forward, "we've decided to terminate your contract."

There it was.

A buzzing started in her ears, like someone had stuck a broken fan inside her skull. She looked down at the line that said Notice of Termination of Employment. She knew every word. Put together, they were extremely rude.

Mr. Zhao kept going. He had the tone of a man who believed he was being kind.

"Don't take it personally. Your severance will be handled by the book. Honestly, we've always recognized your ability. It's just... how do I put this. On projects, you could've shown a little more drive. A little more initiative when it comes to fighting for resources."

Eve looked up and smiled. It felt strange on her face, like it belonged to someone else.

"When you say 'fighting for resources,' do you mean taking the fall for Supervisor He, or answering client WeChats at 2 a.m.?"

The room went quiet for three seconds. She could hear the AC. She could hear her own pulse, which was embarrassing.

Mr. Zhao's kindly expression froze, then recovered into that mature-manager tolerance, the kind that said: see, this is why.

"Young people shouldn't talk like that. Nobody has it easy at work."

She nodded. "True. Especially me. It's about to get a lot less easy."

She hadn't planned on crying.

Then she remembered she'd just renewed her lease last month, sent money home this month, and her credit card bill was sitting in her phone like it knew. Her eyes heated up anyway.

She held it in. Blinked once, hard. Swallowed.

Her best talent, if she had to name one, was staying a little bit proud even when she was being humiliated. It wasn't a useful talent. It just kept her from making a scene, which companies loved.

Sign. Handover. Pack.

The office was too quiet when she went back to her desk. Her team pretended to be drowning in work. Keyboards clattered like a rainstorm. Eyes kept sliding her way, then snapping back to screens, as if looking too long might be contagious.

Some of it was sympathy. Some of it was awkwardness. Some of it, she could tell, was relief. Better you than me. That one sat in the air whether anyone said it or not.

Especially He Qian.

He Qian walked over with a water cup, voice low, face so sincere she could've been running for Coworker of the Year. "Eve, don't be too sad. The market's like this. You're so good. You'll land somewhere better in no time."

Eve looked at her and thought of last week's client-exploded proposal. He Qian had changed it on a whim, then smiled in the meeting like she'd been the reasonable one. Then she'd dropped the whole mess on Eve's head, and Eve had stayed late to unexplode it.

"Thanks." Eve's smile was just as sincere. "You too. After this, nobody's around to clean up after you. Take care of yourself."

He Qian's smile cracked. Just a little. Enough.

Eve walked out with a cardboard box under a whole gallery of complicated looks. The box was lighter than it should've been, for three years of a life. A mug. A charger. A dying succulent she hadn't successfully kept alive either. A few printouts she didn't need.

When the elevator doors shut, she let out a long breath and watched her own face in the steel. She looked fine. That was the worst part. She looked like a person who could still commute home and pretend this was a regular Wednesday.

She was unemployed.

Twenty-seven. Not much in savings. No job. Her ex had ghosted six months ago with a "we're just not a good fit," which was a polite way of saying he'd already started fitting with someone else. Even the pothos she'd kept alive for three years had died last month after she overwatered it, because apparently she couldn't even do "put water on a plant" correctly.

Her life felt like a pot of noodles cooked past saving. Soft. Sticky. Impossible to fish out of the water.

She stood at the building entrance. The sky hung low, the color of wet concrete. The wind already smelled like rain.

Her phone buzzed twice. The landlord.

Eve, rent might go up two hundred next month. See if you can still stay, yeah?

Eve stared at the message until the words stopped looking like language. Then she looked up at the sky.

"Go on," she said, very quietly. "Strike me."

The sky, to its credit, was very cooperative.

Thunder cracked so close it went through her teeth. Rain the size of beans slammed down on her head, her box, her last remaining dignity.

She stood there for one stunned second, dripping, and thought: okay. Okay, I walked into that.

Then she ran.

She ran for the subway with the box in her arms, cursing herself for jinxing it, cursing the landlord, cursing Mr. Zhao's tea, cursing the fact that her socks were wet and there was nothing more miserable on earth than wet socks. By the time she reached her neighborhood gate, she was half soaked. Hair stuck to her cheek. Mascara somewhere it shouldn't be. She looked like life had shoved her into the mud, pulled her back out, and given her a shake for good measure.

That was when she heard it.

A thin little meow, coming from beside the recycling bins.

"Meow."

It was so faint it felt like someone had drawn a piece of thread across the inside of her ribs. Not dramatic. Just... there. Hard to ignore once you'd heard it.

She'd almost made it to the stairwell. Her keys were already in her hand. A hot shower was four minutes away. The smart, adult, newly unemployed thing to do was keep walking.

Her feet stopped anyway.

She turned around, annoyed at herself before she even saw him.

Under the rain shelter were two cardboard boxes that had given up on being boxes. An orange-and-white kitten was curled in the corner, soaked through, one front leg tucked in. Half-lidded eyes. Wary. Pitiful. The combination was unfair.

He was fat.

Not a frail little stray you saw in sad videos. Round. Plush. Like a dumpling that had taken a wrong turn in life and was now pretending this was still a plan.

Eve crouched down. Water ran off her hair onto her knees. She met his eyes.

"With a body like that," she said softly, "you don't look like you've been struggling."

The kitten flicked an ear, as if to say: don't make this weird. Then he meowed again.

Even weaker this time.

Something in her gave. Not the pretty kind. The humiliating kind, where you know you're about to do the expensive, stupid thing, and you're going to do it anyway.

She could barely afford herself. Rent was going up. Her bank app was not in a generous mood. The correct move was to pretend she hadn't seen him, go home, take a shower, open the job apps, and keep fighting.

The cat looked up at her. His wet eyes were black and bright, and for a second she had the unhinged thought that he was waiting to see what kind of person she was.

She could've sworn she read a very specific sentence in them.

Help me.

Eve swore under her breath, stripped off her jacket — which was already ruined, so fine — wrapped him up, and ran for the street.

"Fine. Today's already a disaster." She was panting, the box under one arm, a whole cat under the other. "You better be worth the vet bill."

The rain got worse, like it had been waiting for her to commit.

She burst into the twenty-four-hour pet clinic on the corner. The door chime went ding, cheerful, unhelpful. The girl at the front desk had barely looked up before Eve nearly slipped on the floor and announced herself like a person with no indoor voice left.

"Doctor! Please!"

A man came out of the exam room. White coat. Dark hair. Tall in that unfair way. A pair of thin silver glasses on his nose, like the universe had said, let's make this worse.

Very good-looking.

A little cold, though. The kind of face that did not look prepared for a drowned woman and a fat cat at 6 p.m.

His eyes went to the cat first. He was already walking over. "On the table. Let me see."

Eve set the cat down. The second her hands loosened, he clamped onto her sleeve like if she left, he'd file a complaint. Claws. Eyes. The whole tragic performance.

The man frowned. "You're holding him wrong. His front leg is hurt. You've been putting pressure on it."

Eve was still dripping. Her mood was already in the gutter. That was enough.

"What was I supposed to do, have him call a cab?"

He glanced at her. Clocked, probably, that she was one comment away from crying or biting someone. He dropped it. He just took the kitten from her, steady and careful, like he'd done this a thousand times and still wasn't going to rush it.

His hands were light. Long, clean fingers. No drama. The way he held the cat, it was like he was holding something that might tear if he got careless.

The little round cat who'd been clinging to her went quiet at once. Traitor. Complete traitor.

Eve stood there with one wet shoulder and felt, unreasonably, offended.

Excuse her?

She'd found him. She'd carried him the whole way, in the rain, with a cardboard box, like an idiot. He clawed at her and went meek for a stranger in glasses.

The receptionist already had tissues ready. Professional. This had clearly happened before. "Miss, wipe off first. Julian will check him. It shouldn't be too serious."

Julian.

Eve repeated it in her head, because her brain, even now, was collecting details like this might matter later.

It suited him, unfortunately.

Fifteen minutes later, the results were in. She had used eight tissues. Her hair was doing something criminal.

"Minor fracture. Malnutrition isn't obvious. Someone probably kept him before, or he got fed a lot. Old scars on him, though. He's been on the street a while." The man set the x-ray aside, voice even, like he was reading a weather report. "We'll treat the wound, splint the leg, and watch him for a week."

Eve let out a breath she hadn't noticed she was holding. "As long as he lives."

"He wasn't going to die."

She stared at him.

He looked that gentle. Why did he talk like that? Like she'd run in here screaming over a paper cut.

He seemed to catch the look, and pushed his glasses up. "I'm being honest."

"Cool." She nodded, slow. "Thanks for the honesty. How much is this whole thing going to cost? I just got laid off. If it's too expensive, I might have to negotiate a payment plan with him."

He actually looked at her then. Properly. Not at the cat, not at the chart. At her.

She knew what he was seeing. Pale. Still cracking jokes. The edges of her cardboard box already going soft in the rain. She didn't look like a pet owner. She looked like life had grabbed a random extra and thrown her into a suffering simulator, then forgotten to give her a costume change.

"We'll bill it as basic treatment," he said, and named a number.

Eve winced before she could stop herself. The number wasn't ruinous. It was just... not a number a person wanted to meet on the same day she'd been told she no longer had a salary.

The cat was bundled in a towel. He meowed at her, short, like a reminder.

She looked down. "You better make this worth it. At least live long enough to earn the medicine money back."

The cat stared at her. His expression was, somehow, extremely complicated. Not cute. Not grateful. More like: this is the human I got?

Like he was looking at an idiot.

Julian lowered his eyes and swallowed a smile that almost made it out. Eve saw the almost, which was annoying, because it meant he had a sense of humor and was choosing not to share it.

"You're keeping him?"

"What else?" She heard how tragic she sounded and leaned into it. "I already paid. I'm not putting him back out there to hustle."

"A cat isn't a mood." He handed her a list of care notes. The paper was still warm from the printer. "Deworming, vaccines, checkups. Food, litter, nutritional gel. All of it. If this is just today's feelings, think it through first."

She almost said, then let me think. It would've been the responsible answer. The adult answer. The answer a person with 3,600-kuai rent hanging over her head should give.

Then she looked down.

The little round cat was tucked in the towel, one paw already bandaged, the tip of his tail brushing her hand like he was checking she was still there.

She thought about walking away. She really did. For about one second.

"I thought it through," she said. "I'm keeping him."

He didn't argue. He just wrote a few words in the chart. No lecture. No you're so kind. Just... okay.

Nice handwriting. Clean. Sharp. The kind of handwriting that probably never sent a voice note at 2 a.m. saying "quick change, sorry."

"Name."

"Whose?"

"The cat's."

Eve looked at the round orange-and-white lump. He blinked at her, slow, like he already had opinions about this.

"Dumpling," she said.

"Fits," the man said.

"Thanks."

"I meant the cat."

She opened her mouth, then closed it. Of course he did.

Great. Day one of unemployment, and she'd already met a veterinarian who committed face crimes and then insulted people for free.

***

By the time she finished the paperwork, the rain had eased into that annoying drizzle that still got in your collar.

Eve went home carrying a freshly treated, immediately-in-debt Dumpling, doing mental math the whole way. Severance, minus this bill, minus next month's rent if it jumped, minus cat food, minus the fact that she still had to eat too. The more she counted, the more her scalp went numb.

"Listen up." She addressed the carrier like this was a board meeting and he was the new hire. "From today on, you and I are broke on the same leash. No major illnesses. No picky eating. No 3 a.m. parkour. And you are not peeing on my bed. Let's just survive this month, okay?"

Dumpling blinked, slow.

He did not look like a cat.

He looked like a manager listening to a report, then nodding, barely, as if to say: fine. For now. We'll revisit in Q3.

Her place was a forty-square-meter one-bedroom. Small, and missing several essential organs. Fine for one person who ate standing up. With a cat, the living room suddenly felt busy and cramped, like she'd invited a roommate without asking the furniture.

She dug out an old washbasin, lined it with a towel that had seen better years, then ran downstairs for the cheapest cat food and litter the convenience store had. The clerk looked at her wet hair and didn't ask. Bless him.

When she came back, Dumpling had already climbed out of the carrier. He was on the sofa arm, calmly inspecting his new territory, as if he'd leased the place and she was the one on probation.

"You settle in fast." She set the bags down. "Unlike me. Society just retrained me from scratch today."

She crouched to open the food. Her hands still smelled like rain and clinic disinfectant. Dumpling hopped down, limped over, and bumped his head against her calf. Not a big bump. Just... there.

Eve froze with the bag half open.

"Are you... comforting me?"

He looked up. His pupils were round, like two wet black grapes, and he had the nerve to look patient about it.

She sat down on the floor. Just for a second. The linoleum was cold through her jeans.

Fine.

She was unemployed. Her socks were still wet. At least she'd picked up a cat who knew how to work the room.

At eleven, she showered until the water ran warm enough to pretend she was a person with a plan, wrapped herself in a towel, and sat on the bed rewriting her resume.

The job apps made her eyes blur. Every posting wanted five years of experience, a "passion for hustle," and a salary that was "negotiable," which had the same energy as: you already know you're not worth that much, right?

She sent out a dozen applications. Changed one bullet point. Changed it back. Then she texted her best friend, Su Li, because if she didn't say it out loud to someone, it might turn into a secret, and secrets like this got heavier overnight.

I got laid off.

Su Li replied instantly, which meant she'd been on her phone, which meant she was probably also not okay, just in a different department.

Who died?

Me.

Don't die yet. I'm taking tomorrow off and coming over.

Eve was about to type don't, because she was still in the phase where needing people felt like failing, when a thud came from the living room.

Something had fallen off the sofa.

She went out with the towel still on her head. Dumpling was on the coffee table, one paw on the work badge she'd tossed there and meant to throw away later, when she was braver.

More precisely, on the photo of her and He Qian, the two of them smiling at some team dinner like they were friends.

Eve frowned. "You hold grudges too?"

Dumpling slapped the photo onto the floor. Not a cute tap. A decision.

Her phone lit up at the same time.

The old work group chat, which she had not had the energy to mute yet.

He Qian had @'ed her, polite and casual, like Eve still owed her a favor for existing.

Eve, the client suddenly wants the original draft from that project. Can you send it? I looked everywhere. Thank you so much!

Eve stared at the message. Then she smiled, the cold kind, the kind that didn't reach anywhere useful.

Of course. The body wasn't even cold. The work, apparently, still needed her.

She hadn't even started typing when Dumpling jumped down, limped to her laptop, and started smacking the keyboard.

Tap. Tap. Tap.

Complete chaos. A cat with a mission and no concept of files.

She scooped him off. "Sir. This laptop costs more than you. And that's not a high bar today, so don't test me."

Then she looked at the screen, and stopped.

Dumpling had opened a backup folder she hadn't touched in ages. The kind you make at 1 a.m. because you don't trust people, then forget because trusting people is exhausting.

Inside it, sitting there like a receipt from the universe, was an earlier draft. Timestamped. Edit history intact. Send time still attached. Her name all over it, boring and official and, tonight, beautiful.

If He Qian wanted to dump blame on her again, or blur who did the work now that Eve was gone, this was enough to shut her up.

Eve turned her head, slowly, toward the cat now licking his fur on the sofa like he had not just committed IT.

"Coincidence, right?"

Dumpling paused mid-lick and looked up.

Same expression as at the clinic.

Still looking at an idiot.

Eve sat with that for a few seconds. Then she laughed once, under her breath, and replied to He Qian with the politeness of a person who had learned, finally, to cc people.

I have the original, with full edit history. If you want it, I can cc Mr. Zhao and the client so we don't have another 'who did this' situation. Thoughts?

Three seconds later.

He Qian: No need! I just found it.

Eve: Okay then.

She put the phone down. Her mood had improved, illegally fast, like she'd stolen it from someone.

Dumpling hopped off the sofa, walked to his bowl on his bandaged little leg, and started eating like a gentleman who had concluded the evening's business.

Eve watched him for a long time. Then she leaned her elbow on the coffee table and laughed, for real this time, the leftover-rain, leftover-job kind of laugh that made her eyes water and didn't hurt as much as it should have.

"Fine. You get credit for today." She tapped his forehead. He allowed it, barely. "Want a side gig as my workplace guardian angel? Benefits are bad. The roommate's worse."

Dumpling lifted his head. His whiskers twitched.

"Meow."

It sounded a lot like yes.

Or like: we'll see. Don't get comfortable.
