


Turns out Dumpling's reads were always accurate.

The next morning, the second Eve walked into the clinic, the air felt wrong.

Subtle.

Very subtle.

The kind of subtle that had already had a group chat.

Tang saw her, eyes bright, then tried extremely hard to look like nothing had happened, and mashed the calculator like she was computing her own workers' comp. The calculator beeped. Tang looked at the beep as if the beep might keep a secret.

Director Chen was worse.

He came down the hall with a thermos, coughed once as he passed her, coughed again, then patted her shoulder with the weight of a father who had walked in on something and was now going to live forever.

"Young people. Mind the setting. Doors exist. Locks exist. I am old, but I am not furniture."

Eve's brain buzzed. She opened her mouth, closed it, opened it again, because this was not a sentence you could leave on the floor.

"Director, let me explain."

"I don't want to hear it." He waved, full I-already-know. "Explanation is cover. Cover is fact. I have run a clinic for twenty years. I know a cover when I see cat ears."

"It's not fact!"

"Fine. Not fact." He nodded, generous. "Then the cat ears on Julian's head grew there by accident. Very rare condition. I should write a paper."

She looked at the thermos. The thermos looked back, unhelpful.

Every public death of her life, stacked, still lost to last night's "cat ears, almost kissed." She thought about the shop. The shop was two streets away and still somehow in this hallway.

Tang finally cracked and pounced, whispering, which in Tang's body was still not quiet.

"Eve, is it true? Did you two almost..."

"No." Absolute. "We bumped heads."

"And after that?"

"There is no after that."

"That's still romantic." Tang looked dreamy, calculator forgotten, career forgotten. "New shop, lights, boxes everywhere, cat-ear vet and pretty founder, foreheads together... I could sell that as a short video. I won't. I could."

"Stop consuming garbage."

"I didn't want to. The picture walked into my head by itself. I am a victim."

Eve put a hand to her forehead. The forehead was still a little tender. Traitor.

She'd known. The second Director Chen opened his mouth last night, this was never going to stay at "someone almost almost-something."

By now it had probably evolved into:

Julian passionately confessed in the new shop at midnight, Eve shyly threw herself at him, and a spiritual cat witnessed true love.

Unhinged, and weirdly complete. She almost respected the narrative structure. She did not respect the workplace.

She was still hurting when Julian came out of an exam room.

Their eyes met. The air went unnatural again, immediately, like the hallway had been waiting for the second lead.

Last night's knock hadn't even been that hard.

The aftertaste was huge.

Huge enough that looking at him now autoplayed cat ears, lights, breath, and the thought that she'd been one inch from actually kissing him in front of a box of cat toys. She looked at the wall chart of dental hygiene for dogs. The teeth did not help.

He was not much calmer.

His step hitched, then he came over and set breakfast on her desk. Soy milk. Rice ball. The motion of a man pretending this was Tuesday.

"You haven't eaten?"

"...No."

"Soy milk's warm."

"Oh."

"And the rice ball you like."

"Oh."

Tang looked at one, then the other, and suddenly felt extra.

Not a little.

Like a construction spotlight that had wandered onto someone else's drama set and was now legally liable.

She backed up two steps, and dragged Director Chen with her. Director Chen allowed himself to be dragged, which was its own kind of blessing.

Front desk: Eve, Julian, and Dumpling on the counter, watching, tail ticking like a clock that knew gossip.

A few seconds of silence. She could hear the fish tank filter. She had never noticed the fish tank filter. She noticed it now, personally.

She went first.

"About last night..."

"Mm."

"Did the director get the wrong idea?"

He thought about it, very objective, like he was looking at an x-ray. "It looks like it."

"So what do we do?"

"Let him."

"Let him?" Her eyes went wide. She almost laughed, then didn't, because laughing would sound like agreement. "How can we let him? The way he looks at me now, I'm already a daughter-in-law. I haven't even met your parents. I haven't even finished the signage invoice."

His throat moved. Low: "It might not be the wrong idea."

She forgot how to hold chopsticks she wasn't holding.

"What?"

He seemed to realize he'd gone too fast, and looked away, toward the window, toward anything that was not her face.

"Nothing," he said. "Eat. Don't do it on an empty stomach."

She watched him for two seconds. The urge to win the sentence rose, then sat down again, because winning would mean asking him to repeat it, and she was not that brave before soy milk.

This man was good.

He never seemed to say anything. Every sentence still sped her up. She looked at the rice ball like the rice ball might translate.

Dumpling flicked his tail.

Another almost. Adults, doing adult things, which was mostly standing still and not finishing words.

He yawned. The yawn was not respectful.

Public death or not, the shop still had to open.

A week later, You, Me & Paws did its soft opening.

The day the sign went up, Eve stood outside and stared at those warm white letters for a full half minute. Her throat went tight. She blinked too hard, then blamed the sun, which was convenient of the sun.

She really hadn't thought, a few months after getting laid off, that she'd be here.

From a rain night, a hurt fat cat, a sprint into a clinic, to this: a small bright shop, a business slowly taking shape, and...

She glanced at Julian, helping a worker carry the last box of litter. The box said 10 kg like a threat. He lifted it like it was a suggestion.

And a person she wanted to walk with for a very, very long time. She did not say that. She looked at the sign again. The sign did not tell on her.

Su Li had taken the day off to help. She walked in and couldn't help it.

"Not bad, Eve. This has that... how do I put it. Thirty-eight kuai drink, cry a little, make peace with your life energy. I would post this. I would also sit here and not post, which is how you know it's good."

Eve squinted at her. "Why does there have to be crying?"

"Because this shop looks built for 'pet a cat and forgive existence.'"

Director Chen, from behind: "Thirty-eight is too cheap. We have professional services. Forgiveness is not a loss leader."

Tang, immediately: "Fifty-eight then. Pet a cat, forgive existence, plus a signed Dumpling photo."

Eve laughed. "Dumpling can write?"

"Paw prints, then." Su Li, commercial. "Limited edition. Worth more. I'll do the copy. Don't let Director Chen do the copy. He'll put 'organic traffic' on a sticker."

Everybody talked over everybody. Warm noise. Someone's dog sneezed in the back and nobody even panicked. That, to Eve, was already a KPI.

Dumpling lay on the little wooden platform they'd built him at the desk, a tag around his neck.

Chief of Hospitality.

He accepted the title well.

Especially when the first guests came in. He sat, tail curled, eyes reserved and expensive, like he'd personally acquired the whole place and was allowing humans to visit during business hours.

Soft opening day, more people than expected.

Some from the account. Some from events. Some just because of the sign, which meant the sign had done its job, and she could stop rewriting the sign in her head at 2 a.m. Maybe. Probably not.

The first thing to overflow was the "first cat, don't panic" corner.

Eve sat there while a guy in glasses asked, wrecked, "Why does my cat start parkour at 2 a.m. on the dot? Does he have a side gig delivering food? I checked. There are no bags. There is only chaos."

She held in a laugh. "No. He just thinks you need cardio. He's a coach. You're the client. The client is not thriving."

Another girl scribbled in a notebook, very serious, like this was class.

"He always sits on my chest. I feel like he's committing murder."

Julian, very calm: "He trusts you. Also that spot is soft."

The girl clutched her heart, pained. "But I don't even have an A-cup!"

The room died laughing. Eve had to look at the floor. The floor was shaking. The floor was people.

Dumpling licked a paw at the desk, slow.

He used to not get why humans needed to discuss such obvious things.

He got it now.

Most of being human was just checking that you weren't the only idiot. A shop could charge for that. He respected the model.

At the lunch rush, an auntie walked in with a bichon in a little floral dress. First sentence: "Can you set my dog up with someone?"

Eve had been drinking water. She nearly sprayed it. She swallowed, eyes watering, professional.

"Auntie, you mean... like a match?"

"Yes." Completely righteous. The bichon looked righteous too. "Joy is three. She hasn't liked a single boy in the neighborhood. Her standards are high. I think I raised her too well. You do pet social, right? Find her someone suitable. House-trained. Not too barky. Family has to be decent."

For the first time at work, Eve felt a huge, sincere blankness. The kind of blankness that used to show up in performance reviews, except this one had a bow on its head.

She looked at Julian.

He was silent for two seconds, then actually caught it.

"We can start with social-adjustment sessions."

The auntie beamed. "I knew you were professionals!"

Eve almost laughed, and quietly wrote a new line on the sheet.

Pet matchmaking.

Who knew this had a market too. She should have known. People would pay for anything that sounded like hope and came with a treat bag.

Around three, the shop went briefly quiet. That dangerous afternoon quiet, when the light got long and you could hear the fridge again.

Eve leaned on the desk with today's booking sheet, a little color in her face from being busy. A good tired. The kind that made rent look less like a monster and more like a number you could chase.

"Tired?" He handed her warm water.

"Tired," she said, honest. "Also really good."

She looked at the grid full of names and needs, and let out a breath.

"Julian. I think we actually built this."

He followed her gaze. He didn't smile big. He almost never smiled big. The look just stayed, longer than a look about a booking sheet needed to stay.

"Mm."

"It's still small. There'll be a lot of problems later."

"Mm."

"But right now I think..." She looked up. "That rain on the day I got fired doesn't feel as bad."

He watched her. Quiet.

"Because you found Dumpling after."

"And because I met you after."

The sentence came out, and even she paused.

She'd just followed her mouth. The mouth had been waiting. The mouth did not respect brand guidelines.

Then she heard how direct it was.

Her ears burned. She was about to add I mean as a business partner when he spoke first.

"Same."

She looked up.

"What?"

"Meeting you," he said. "Also good."

The desk went quiet. Water in her hands, going lukewarm, doing nothing wrong.

Su Li had been organizing shelves in the back. She heard that, grabbed Tang, and dragged her toward the storage room with excellent survival instincts.

"Come on. We're counting litter."

"We counted this morning."

"Count again. In case it reproduced. Litter does that if you look away. Science."

They vanished. A bag rustled, theatrical.

Director Chen glanced over from a distance, also tactful, and turned away with Dumpling.

"Let's go inspect the territory, Grandpa's taking you. The plants need a senior."

Dumpling hadn't wanted to leave.

Then he looked at the two adults at the desk, faces doing that I have something to say and I currently cannot say it thing, and went, reluctantly, because the director had freeze-dried in his pocket. Dumpling could smell it. He was Chief of Hospitality, not Chief of Charity.

He would give them the hallway.

Not because they had earned privacy.

Because the pocket was crinkling, and he had a title to uphold, and titles came with snacks.
