At the start of the month, the landlord came for the rent hike, as promised.

Eve was boiling instant noodles when her phone buzzed. Considering the neighborhood market, let's do 3,600 next month. The egg almost went straight into the pot. She caught it with two fingers, burned herself a little, swore at the egg like it had written the message.

"Did the neighborhood market consider whether I live or die?"

She leaned on the fridge, staring at the screen. The fridge hummed. Dumpling blinked from the table. 3,600. Plus cat food. Plus the leftover clinic bill that still sat in her notes app like a polite ghost. After getting laid off, the thing that scared her wasn't not finding a job. Job apps were annoying. Job apps didn't show up at your door.

It was that every bill showed up on time. On time to the point of cruelty. Rent, utilities, the credit card, her mother's "just a little if you can." The universe could fire you. The universe would not, under any circumstances, delay a payment.

Dumpling sat on the dining table watching her, ears flicking. She was about to type something she would regret — a paragraph, probably, with market rates and a joke that wasn't a joke — when the phone buzzed again.

Not the landlord. Director Chen.

Eve, that weekend event did great. A client saw the videos and wants someone long-term for the account. Budget's limited, but it should get you some money first. Interested?

Her eyes lit up. The noodles were forgotten. Steam fogged the edge of the phone. She typed immediately, thumbs faster than dignity.

Yes!

He came back fast.

Then come by tomorrow afternoon and we'll talk.

Eve held her phone for two seconds, then turned and kissed Dumpling hard on the head. He tasted faintly of cat food. She did not care.

"You hear that? We have a way out!"

Dumpling looked disgusted, wriggled free, and flicked his tail. A noodle-steam kiss was, apparently, beneath him.

The body language was: look at you. This is all it takes. One WeChat and you're doing a victory lap in the kitchen.

The next afternoon she put on light makeup, a clean shirt and jeans, and arrived twenty minutes early. The shirt was the one that still looked like she had a job. She'd checked it twice for cat hair and still found some on the bus.

She used to think she was good at her job. That a comeback was inevitable, like a plot point. Standing in front of the clinic elevator, she had to admit the first step of a "comeback" was giving herself a five-minute pep talk in the mirror for a not-even-official little collaboration. The mirror in the elevator was slightly warped. She looked tired and hopeful, which was a stupid combination.

"You can do this," she told her reflection. "You're worth money. You're reliable. You can save an account other people already wrecked. Also please don't trip."

By the time the doors opened, the brainwashing had mostly taken. She walked out with her files under her arm like a person who belonged in meeting rooms.

Then she walked into the actual meeting room and nearly tripped over reality again.

Besides Director Chen and Julian, there was a man in a plaid suit, early thirties, big smile, hair combed like it had never known chaos. He had the energy of someone who thought he was a walking networking event. Name card energy. The kind of person who said "let's circle back" and meant it.

Director Chen was warm. "This is my nephew, Xu Fang. He does new media. He's here to help look at the account plan."

Xu Fang stood and shook her hand. His palm was dry. His smile was not. "Heard a lot. Sounds like you saved the last event."

Eve was modest, because that was the script, and also because she still didn't trust compliments from men in plaid. "That's overselling it. Everyone just worked well together."

His smile got more sincere. "People who say that are usually the ones actually doing the work."

First impression: not bad. She filed it under we'll see, which was how she filed most people now.

Ten minutes later, it started collapsing.

Xu Fang knew ops, and his mouth was fast, but he drifted. He talked like a slideshow. Emotional value. Persona. Conversion. He used "story" the way some people used "leverage."

"Pet content has to hit emotional value."

"The account needs a clear persona. A fixed CP line would be ideal."

"Julian handles expertise and looks. You handle lifestyle and conversion. As for story, we can manufacture a little."

Eve's brow creased. "Manufacture?"

"Content design." He spread his hands, pleased with himself. "Film some tearjerker rescue beats on purpose. Add a little tension when you two interact. That's what people watch. A little push-and-pull. Audiences eat it."

Director Chen looked interested. Eve could see the numbers landing in his eyes. She didn't blame him. Clinics needed money. She still felt itchy in a bad way, like a tag in the back of a shirt.

She didn't hate making content.

She hated taking something honest and turning it into a skit for views. She'd done versions of that for brands that sold vitamin drinks. The animals here couldn't sign off on it. Neither could the owners, not really, not if you dressed it up as "design."

She was about to say it nicely — she had a nice version, she'd practiced nice versions — when the door nudged open.

Dumpling had somehow snuck in. She hadn't brought him on purpose. He'd been in the lobby, then he wasn't. He walked over, four-square and unhurried, and stopped at Xu Fang's feet. Looked up. Looked at the plaid.

Xu Fang's eyes lit up. "Aiyo, this is the orange internet-face, right? Come here, Uncle'll hold you."

He bent down. Hands out. The uncle voice.

Dumpling jerked back. Even his whiskers said no.

Then he circled Xu Fang once, sat his whole ass on the trash can by the man's shoe, and patted the lid.

One clean motion. Deeply insulting. The lid made a cheap little plastic sound, which made it worse.

Silence.

Director Chen froze, then went red holding in a laugh. His shoulders did a small, illegal dance.

Julian dropped his eyes to his water. His shoulder shook, barely. Eve saw it and had to look at the table, because if she looked at him she would lose it.

Xu Fang stared at the cat, then at her. "...What's that supposed to mean?"

Eve thought about it. She could have said cats are weird. She could have said sorry. She did not.

"Maybe he thinks you're recyclable."

Director Chen finally lost it. A snort, then a full laugh he tried to turn into a cough and failed. Tang's voice, faintly, from somewhere outside: "What happened?"

Xu Fang stood up, furious. "I'm sorry, is this cat personally attacking me?"

Eve had only meant to defuse it. The more she thought, the more it felt like Dumpling was warning her. Same trash-can tap as the startup call. Same look. She glanced at Julian. There was a considering look on his face too, not quite a smile, like he'd filed the moment under data.

When the meeting restarted, she didn't pick a fight. Fighting made you look emotional, and emotional was how men in plaid won. She just put her plan down more clearly, papers squared, voice even.

"I get the traffic logic," she said. "But we're a clinic and an adoption program, not a short drama. If the account starts on staged emotion, you burn trust later. Real people. Real pets. That's what lasts. You can still edit. You can still hook. You don't have to fake a rescue for the algorithm."

Not loud. Steady. Her hands wanted to shake. They didn't. Unemployment had at least given her that: the ability to talk while her bank app screamed in the background.

Xu Fang opened his mouth. Julian got there first. "I agree with her."

Xu Fang blinked. "You don't even do ops."

"I know what we shouldn't mess with." Julian looked up, even. "A hospital isn't a set. Sick animals aren't props."

The room went quiet. Even Dumpling stopped licking a paw.

Director Chen looked left, then right, and slammed it. "Fine. We do it Eve's way. Honest, useful, a little lifestyle on the side. Xiao Xu, stop chasing tricks."

Xu Fang had nothing. His smile had nowhere to go. On the way out he still had to get a last word in, because some people would rather leave a comment than leave.

"I reserve my opinion."

Eve smiled. "Reserve it. We're not using it."

When he was gone, it was just her and Julian. The plaid energy drained out of the air. Director Chen had been pulled away by a phone call, still chuckling.

Dumpling had jumped onto a chair at some point, curled into a ball, like a shareholder who'd enjoyed the corporate warfare and was now taking a nap on company time.

Eve packed her files, suddenly self-conscious. The high of winning a sentence always dropped you somewhere awkward. "Was I too direct just now?"

"No," Julian said. "You were right."

She looked up.

"And," he added, after a beat, "you were pretty good in there."

Second time he'd praised her professionally. She counted. She shouldn't count. She counted anyway.

She'd noticed: this man didn't pile on pretty words. His face barely changed. That was why every sentence landed true. No firework emoji. No "you're amazing." Just: you were right. You were pretty good.

The self-doubt unemployment had grown in her got pressed flat at one corner. Not gone. Just... sat on.

"Then..." She cleared her throat. The files were already in her bag. She still needed something to do with her hands. "Here's to working together?"

He put out his hand.

"Here's to working together."

Long fingers. Warm palm. The pressure exactly right. Not a networking handshake. Not a limp one either. She was about to pull back — three seconds was a handshake, four was a problem — when Dumpling jumped down and slapped a paw onto the back of their joined hands.

Woman, cat, vet. All still.

"What's he doing now?" she asked, and heard her own voice go thin.

Julian looked at Dumpling. A thread of helplessness, quickly hidden.

"Maybe telling us to get to work."

Dumpling meowed. Then he took his paw back, hopped to the floor, and went to sit by Eve's bag like the meeting had run long and someone still owed him lunch.

That did not sound like get to work.

It sounded a lot more like: you two can keep talking. I'm hungry. Pay me in cans.
