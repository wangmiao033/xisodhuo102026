


People really shouldn't get too pleased with themselves.

One second Eve was melting over a slice of cake. The next, after nights of proposals, shop runs, and watching the renovation like the renovation might run away, she successfully gave herself a fever.

At first she wouldn't admit it.

A little dizzy. A little sore throat. A little empty, like her body had sent a meeting invite and she had declined it without reading.

Adults were great at summarizing all discomfort as I'm fine. I'm fine was a complete medical system. It had never been peer-reviewed. It still ran her life.

Then, 9 p.m., she was in the kitchen making instant noodles. The kind with too much seasoning, because she had skipped lunch and told herself the cake from yesterday still counted as a meal. She lifted the lid. The world went black, polite about it, like a power cut. She nearly seasoned her life with herself and the pot. The stove clicked. Her knee hit a cabinet. Very glamorous founder behavior.

"Shit..."

She held the stove, head swimming, reached for her phone, and her first thought wasn't Su Li, wasn't delivery.

It was a text to Julian.

I might be dying.

She stared at it after she sent it. The noodles kept bubbling, unbothered, like rent. She thought, briefly, that if she died here, the last message in his chat would be unhinged, and she would not even get to edit it.

The next second, he called.

"What happened?"

That usual, tightly packed calm in his voice had gone taut. Not loud. Taut, like a line pulled too straight.

"It's nothing big." Still performing. Sitting on the floor counted as performing. "I might have a little fever."

"Did you take your temperature?"

"No."

"Medicine?"

"No."

"Eve."

"Mm?"

"Go lie down. I'm coming."

She wanted to say don't. The apartment was a mess. There was a sock on a chair that had no excuse. She hadn't opened her mouth. He'd already hung up.

Twenty minutes later, the doorbell.

He stood there with medicine, fever patches, a thermometer, and a bag of plain porridge and sides. He looked worse than the patient. Hair a little off. Jacket like he'd grabbed the first one on the hook. A man who had not stopped to decide whether this was too much.

Dumpling was in the entryway. He saw who it was and immediately made space, solemn as a cat greeting the imperial physician. He even sat. He did not usually sit for guests. He sat for this one.

"How did you get like this?"

Frowning the second he was inside. Shoes off. Bag on the table. Already in the room like the room had been expecting him.

Eve sat on the sofa in a blanket, face flushed, still trying. "Maybe the entrepreneurial fire in my body is burning too well. Very dedicated. HR would be proud."

"Stop talking."

"Oh."

He took her temperature first. No argument. The thermometer beeped like it also worked for him.

38.7.

"This is a 'little' fever?"

"In my language system, under 39 still counts as a little. Over 39 is a meeting. This is a calendar hold."

He looked at her. Helplessness, and obvious worry, stacked in the same face, which was rude. She wanted to win the sentence. She also wanted to put her forehead on his sleeve, which was not a sentence.

"Medicine first."

"Is it bitter?"

"How old are you?"

"Twenty-seven."

"Then you can decide."

She took the pills, scolded into obedience. They were bitter. She still swallowed, because he was watching, and also because Dumpling was watching, and losing to a cat on medication compliance would follow her into the afterlife.

Dumpling sat on the coffee table the whole time, staring.

When she swallowed, he even leaned in, like he was afraid she'd spit them out. Warm cat breath. No sympathy.

"Can we have a little mother-son feeling?"

He meowed.

Translation, probably:

Less talking. Swallow. I have already moved for the doctor. Do not waste my seating chart.

After the medicine, Julian went to heat porridge.

She leaned on the sofa, watching him in her kitchen, finding bowls, lighting the stove, testing the temperature with the inside of his wrist like the porridge was a living thing. He opened the wrong cupboard first. Then the right one. He did not comment on the cupboard that was only sauce packets and a bag of Dumpling's freeze-dried. The rental had always been a little messy, a little small, a little held together by one person and a lot of bags from the convenience store. With him in it, the mess didn't look braver. It just looked like a kitchen someone else knew how to use. That was worse. That was better. She thought about the rent, because the rent was her name on a contract, and he was still in her kitchen, heating porridge like that was a normal Tuesday.

Fever made her mouth stupid.

"Julian."

"Mm."

"You kind of seem like my husband right now."

Two seconds of silence from the kitchen. The stove made a small sound, like it wanted out of the conversation.

His back was to her. The tips of his ears went visibly red. She saw it from the sofa. She filed it. She was ill, not blind.

"Eve."

"Yeah?"

"Fever is fever. Don't talk nonsense."

"I'm not." She squinted, righteous, blanket up to her chin. "I'm making an analogy. Analogies are for work. We are a startup. This is branding."

"No analogies."

"Why not?"

"Because..."

He stopped.

The word was too dangerous.

Dangerous enough that if he followed it, tonight would stop being about staying over and feeding her medicine. She could hear the almost. It sat in the kitchen with the porridge. She looked at the sock on the chair and decided not to help him.

He brought the porridge, crouched in front of her, held out the spoon.

"Eat first."

She was flushed, brain hot, looking at that contained, helpless face, and found him a little cute. Not painting-cute. Annoyed-cute. The kind of cute that made her want to poke, which was unwise, and still her hobby.

"Are you shy?"

"No."

"Your ears are red."

"It's warm."

"You just came out of the kitchen. Warm would be your face, not your ears. I know how rooms work. I pay rent here."

He inhaled.

"Eve."

"Mm?"

"If you keep talking, the porridge gets cold."

She finally quieted, and drank. The porridge was bland, which meant he had done it right. Fever food was not supposed to have a personality.

Dumpling patrolled: sofa to check on her, floor to check on him, busy as a night-shift charge nurse. At one point he sat on Julian's foot, which was not medically indicated, and still happened.

After ten, the medicine kicked in. The fever eased a little. She curled in bed, half asleep, the blanket too heavy and also not enough.

He didn't leave. She waited for the sentence where he would say call me if it gets worse, which was the polite version, the version that left her with the sock on the chair and the noodles going cold. It didn't come. He sat on the little chair by the bed, watching the time, ready to check her temperature again later. Phone face-down. Jacket on the back of the chair. Like a person who had decided the night was a shift.

A warm yellow lamp. Soft light. The kind of light that made even a cheap nightstand look like it had a secret.

When she blinked her eyes open, he was writing in the startup notebook she'd tossed on the nightstand. Her notebook. Her ugly handwriting. His pen.

"What are you doing?"

"Fixing your budget sheet."

"You're pulling an all-nighter and also overtime? That's two jobs. We can't afford two of you."

"Mm."

"What do you get out of this?"

He looked up.

Her eyes were watery from the fever, hair stuck to her face, all the usual sharp edges gone. Nothing like the Eve who always had a comeback. She knew how she looked. She still asked, because if she didn't ask, the quiet would do something worse.

His voice went quieter too.

"I get you not working yourself into the ground again after you get better."

Her throat went tight. She stared at the notebook, then at his hand, then at a water ring on the nightstand that had been there for weeks. A few seconds of quiet. Then, small: "Julian."

"Mm?"

"If you're this good to me, I really am going to get the wrong idea."

She'd said it once before.

Last time it had been a test. A poke. A joke with a hook in it.

This time it sounded a little hurt, a little soft, a little scared, like she had put the sentence down without a backup plan.

He looked at her. His throat moved.

"Then don't get the wrong idea yet."

She went still.

The yet landed late. First she heard don't. Then she heard yet. The first one made her want to pull the blanket over her head and become furniture. The second one made her ears go hot for a completely different reason.

She said oh, and pulled the blanket higher anyway, because she was not going to let him see her face do math. Don't, then yet. She could live on a yet. She had lived on worse. Still, for one ugly second she had heard only the first half, and her ears had gone cold, and she had thought: fine, shop first, I'm a partner, I know how this goes. Then the second half arrived, and she felt stupid for the first half, and also a little grateful, which was annoying. Grateful was not a look she wanted to wear with a fever.

Dumpling had been licking his fur at the foot of the bed. He stopped, lifted his head slowly, and looked at Julian with four words in his eyes.

What are you doing.

Two seconds of silence. Julian finished the second half, low, like he had heard the cat, or like he had heard himself.

"Wait a little longer."

She watched him over the blanket. Only her eyes. It felt dramatic. She was too tired to stop being dramatic.

He looked down. Voice even lower.

"At least until you're better. Until the shop is a little more stable." He said, "I don't want you looking back later and thinking I moved in on you when everything was a mess."

The room went still. The fridge, somewhere, hummed like it was trying to be professional.

She stared at him. The urge to win the sentence came back, slower, stupid, fond. She blinked. Her eyes were wet from fever and also not only fever. She was not going to name the other part. Naming it would make it a slide in a deck.

"You..."

"Mm?"

"You're really unfair when you talk."

He didn't take that. He just tucked the blanket in, corners neat, as if blankets had a correct position and she had been violating policy.

"Sleep."

When she closed her eyes, her mouth curved, slowly. She could still hear the pen. She could still hear Dumpling settling, a small thump, then quiet.

Dumpling lay back down at the foot of the bed, flicked his tail once, and was, barely, satisfied.

The man had almost fumbled the night shift.

Then he had finished the sentence.

Dumpling would allow this. For now. In the morning there would be freeze-dried, and if there wasn't, the review would be public.
