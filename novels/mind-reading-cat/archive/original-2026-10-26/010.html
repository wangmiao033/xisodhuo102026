The clinic account took off on a very ordinary Friday night.

Eve had just finished a new video: "The kinds of people and animals a pet hospital meets in a day." No weepy copy. Just Julian covering a needle-shy puppy's eyes, Tang crouching on the floor talking a cat down, Director Chen yelling at someone and then secretly adding a can for a stray. Little pieces, strung together. She'd argued with herself about the Director Chen bit for twenty minutes. Left it in. People liked a man who yelled and then folded.

The apartment smelled like instant noodles and laptop heat. Her desk lamp made a small island of light. Outside, someone was dragging a trash bin. Ordinary. That was the joke. Viral things were supposed to arrive with thunder. This one arrived with a progress bar and a cat who had already claimed the warm spot on the windowsill.

The ending was Dumpling on the front desk, surveying the lobby like a general. One slow blink. The caption was simple.

This place treats the sick every day. It also gets healed every day.

Two hours in, likes passed five thousand. She refreshed like a person who did not trust joy. At 1 a.m. she was still watching the backend, comments and shares climbing, her whole body awake. The apartment was dark except for the laptop. Dumpling had already chosen sleep. She had not.

She whispered numbers at the screen like they might spook if she said them too loud. Five thousand. Then more. Comments about the puppy. Comments about Tang's voice. Comments about Dumpling's slow blink, as if a cat had invented customer service. She screenshot one that said "I want to be treated like that stray can," then felt insane, then saved it anyway.

"Dumpling!" She scooped the fat cat off the windowsill. "We're going viral!"

He could barely keep his eyes open. She shook him. He offered a symbolic meow, the kind that meant: put me down or pay overtime.

She put him down. He immediately returned to the windowsill, as if fame were a rumor he had already declined. She sat back in the chair and grinned at an empty room, which was embarrassing, and then she did it again.

The next morning, Director Chen dropped three red packets in the work chat. Eve claimed one before she finished brushing her teeth. Foam. Phone. The WeChat sound was indecently cheerful.

"Eve, legend!"
"Dumpling, legend!"
"This clinic is about to get rich!"

She laughed with toothpaste still in her mouth and had to spit before she could type anything civilized.

She'd just claimed another when a new message popped.

He Qian. They hadn't talked in ages. The name sat on the screen like a leftover from a life she'd already packed into a cardboard box.

Eve, you okay lately? I saw your pet account. You're amazing!

Eve looked at that familiar fake-smile voice, and her mouth tugged. Amazing. He Qian had also thought she was amazing when there was a draft to steal.

The toothpaste taste went metallic. She wiped her phone on a towel, like the message had left a print. It hadn't. It just had that old-company shine: concern first, ask later.

He Qian followed up.

I've been looking at new opportunities too. Our company's short-video side is shifting. If you're free, we should catch up. Maybe we can collaborate later.

Eve hadn't replied yet. Dumpling hopped onto the table and put a paw on the phone.

Then he looked at her, extremely impatient. Not cute-impatient. The other kind. The kind that had slapped a badge off a coffee table.

She laughed. It came out meaner than she meant, and she did not correct it.

"I know. Don't pick up trash."

She sent two words.

No thanks.

He Qian probably hadn't expected her to be this direct. After a while, an awkward sticker. The sticker was trying. The sticker failed.

Eve's mood soared. She sent the screenshot to Su Li, because victories like this needed a witness or they turned back into overthinking.

Su Li, instantly:

Who's thriving? Me.
You finally evolved from office victim to someone who says no.

Eve cackled into her phone for a while. Dumpling watched her from the table like a manager reviewing a performance he had already approved. She scratched his cheek. He allowed three seconds. Then he went back to sitting on the charging cable, which was his other job.

By afternoon, she wasn't laughing.

Because after an account blows up, the trouble comes with it.

People in the comments calling it trauma-marketing. Stolen clips, cut to make Julian look cold to owners. Self-proclaimed "industry accounts" saying they were just using strays for traffic. The comments went rancid. One of them used the word "performative" three times, which Eve would have respected as a bit if it hadn't been aimed at a puppy getting a shot.

Eve's brow locked.

The backend, which had felt like a slot machine at 1 a.m., now felt like a kitchen fire. Same numbers. Different smell. She scrolled until her thumb ached. Someone had cut Julian mid-sentence so he looked like he was dismissing an owner. She knew the original clip. She had exported it. She had watched him cover that puppy's eyes with two fingers, careful, like the world was too bright.

She'd been in this long enough to know traffic is never free. When the numbers go up, people who like you multiply. People who don't like you pop like mushrooms after rain. She'd written crisis docs for brands that sold yogurt. Yogurt was easier. Yogurt didn't have a face.

Director Chen sent a panicked voice note in the group: "What do we do? Should we delete it?" His panic had typos even in audio. She could hear them.

"Don't," she typed, then sent a follow-up because Director Chen would delete things with his whole chest. "Deleting now is admitting guilt. Leave it. I'm coming over."

Then she grabbed a jacket, grabbed Dumpling, and ran to the clinic. The bus felt slow on purpose. Dumpling complained once, then stared out the window like a commuter with tenure. The carrier handle cut into her palm. She kept checking her phone. More comments. More of the same accusation in different fonts.

Julian had just finished an emergency, mask off, obviously tired. There was a line at his jaw from the mask. She noticed it and then told herself to notice the comments instead.

"You saw the comments?"

"I saw them." She put her laptop down. The table was already a mess of printouts. "I'm here to deal with it."

"Is it going to be messy?"

"Yes," she said, honest. "But we can fight it. Fighting is the part I'm good at. Unfortunately."

He nodded once. Didn't tell her to calm down. Didn't tell her it wasn't that serious. The clinic AC hummed too cold. Printouts stuck to her wrist when she leaned.

When she got busy, the whole air around her changed. She knew that about herself. It was the sitting still that ruined her. Flagging weird accounts, tracing the stolen clips, sorting the repeated accusations, drafting a response. She even had Director Chen scheduled down to the minute, which he accepted with the face of a man in timeout.

"Director, do not go fight people in the comments."

"Why? I'm good at fighting."

"Because when you get mad, you typo. And your typos become screenshots. And screenshots become the next video."

Director Chen opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked betrayed by literacy.

Tang, beside them, nearly crumpled the printouts trying not to laugh. "He's going to file a complaint against the alphabet," she whispered. Eve did not look up. If she looked up she would laugh, and laughing would feel like leaking.

Julian stood behind Eve, watching her type and talk at the same time, and felt something strange.

Like as long as she was here, a mess could slowly turn into an order. Not magic. Process. A person who had been yelled at by clients at 1 a.m. and had learned to put the yelling in a folder.

"Eve."

"Mm?"

"Thank you. This is a lot."

Her hands didn't stop. "Don't thank me. I bill by the hour. Write it down. I'm serious. Unemployment has made me mercenary."

A smile showed in his eyes. He didn't argue about the invoice. She filed that under later.

At nine they posted the statement.

No victim act. No yelling. Just the facts and the clinic's daily rules, laid out clean, plus the stolen clip next to the full original. She'd made Director Chen read it twice. She'd made herself read it three times, looking for anything that sounded like she was begging. Nothing. Good.

The comments flipped fast.

"Lmao the rumor people went quiet."
"The full video is literally a normal doctor."
"This ops person is good. Calm and sharp."

Eve watched the backend and finally exhaled. Her shoulders hurt. Her jaw hurt. She hadn't noticed she'd been clenching.

She was about to stretch when something landed on her shoulders.

A jacket.

Julian had put his own coat on her, voice mild.

"AC's too low. Don't get sick."

When his fingers brushed her shoulder, her ears went warm. She sat there in a jacket that still had his warmth, sleeves too long, and it took her a while to say "oh." The "oh" was not a sentence. It was a stall.

The coat smelled like laundry soap and clinic. She pulled the sleeves over her hands, then pretended she had not. Tang, somewhere behind a filing cabinet, made a noise that wanted to be a sentence and became a cough.

Dumpling sat on the table, watching one of them go red for no reason, and the other one pretend to be calm with red ears anyway.

He yawned. Then he walked across the keyboard, sat on the warm laptop, and stared at Eve like a creature who had been used as a viral ending and had not been paid in extra dinner.

A key mashed under his belly. The statement page jumped. She moved him. He moved back, heavier, as if weight were an argument.

She got the message at home. An extra can. He ate it without comment, which, for him, was a five-star review.
