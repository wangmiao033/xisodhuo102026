After that dinner, Su Li clocked it immediately: Eve was off.

Specifically, she had started zoning out at her phone. Used to be, Eve looked at her phone to curse at job apps. Now her mouth would lift on its own, then she'd mash it back down, then pretend nothing had happened, which was the least convincing performance Su Li had seen since last year's annual party.

Su Li sat cross-legged on the sofa with a pillow, interrogating. The pillow was a prop. The eyes were not.

"Talk. How far along are you and Julian?"

"What far along." Eve combed Dumpling, aiming for breezy, getting somewhere around mildly guilty. "It's a normal collaboration. We make videos. We talk about cats. Very professional. Extremely boring."

"Normal collaborations text at eleven p.m.?"

"That's work."

"Work that goes 'remember to eat' and 'sleep early'?"

Eve's hand stopped. A knot of orange fur stayed between the comb's teeth.

"You read my phone?"

"The screen was that bright. I was assaulted by it." Su Li sniffed. "And, babe, a man who cares about your slides and whether you've eaten? That's not pure anymore. That's leftover soup. That's intimacy."

Eve went stubborn, because if she didn't go stubborn she would have to admit Su Li was good at this. "Could also just be basic professional courtesy between partners."

Su Li looked maternal. The knit set helped. "If you're happy, I'm happy. I'm also nosy. Those can coexist."

Eve ignored her and kept combing. Dumpling's eyes were slits of pleasure. Then he put a paw on her phone, casual as a person opening someone else's mail.

The screen lit on Julian's chat.

Newest message, half an hour ago:

There's a very shy golden shaded coming in tomorrow. If you're free, bring Dumpling. He's good at settling other cats.

Su Li read it upside down, which was a talent, and clicked her tongue.

"Look at that. He's manufacturing reasons to see you."

Eve flipped the phone. The screen went dark, thank god. "Don't be ridiculous. He invited Dumpling, not me. I'm the chauffeur. I'm the bag."

Su Li, mild: "Then don't go."

Eve opened her mouth. Closed it. Looked at Dumpling, who was no help.

Su Li cackled into the sofa. The pillow took the impact. "That's what I thought. Academic interest. Very scientific. Wear something that isn't a hoodie, for science."

The next afternoon, of course she went.

Her reasons were extremely legitimate, and she listed them to herself on the bus so they would sound like a memo.

One: helping.
Two: Dumpling's socialization.
Three: she needed to see, academically, how shy this golden shaded actually was.

She did not add four. Four was that "remember to eat" was still sitting in her chat like a small, unprofessional stone.

Julian was already in the exam room. The cat was a puffed-up ball in the far corner of the crate. Even the owner made it worse, hovering, talking too fast, which Eve understood. Panic was contagious. Cats knew.

The woman was nearly in tears. "He's fine at home. The second we go out, this. Doctor, is it psychological? Did I do something? I bought the expensive carrier. I played the rainforest sounds."

"Just stress," Julian said. "Don't panic yet. He can hear you panicking."

Then he looked at Dumpling in Eve's arms. Dumpling looked back, already bored with the rainforest.

"Can we try?"

She set him down. The tile was cool. Dumpling had presence today, which was annoying, because she had brushed him and he was taking credit. He walked to the crate like he had an appointment, didn't crowd it, sat a little away, licked a paw twice, then looked at the cat inside.

Weirdly, the shaking kitten actually started to settle.

They stared through the door, quiet, like they were having a conversation humans were not invited to. Eve found herself holding her breath, which was stupid. She was not the one in the crate.

A few minutes later the golden shaded inched forward and touched the crate with his nose.

The owner was stunned. Her hands went to her mouth.

"Oh my god. He ignores everyone. He ignored my mother. My mother is loud."

Eve was a little stunned too.

She looked down. Dumpling was still sitting like a professional. See? This is why you hire experts. Don't clap. Don't squeal. Let him work.

A smile showed in Julian's eyes. Not much. She caught it anyway.

"He's better than I thought."

Eve felt an unearned pride, the kind you get when your roommate does well and you pretend it was your syllabus. "Obviously. Look who raised him."

He looked at her.

"Mm. You too."

The compliment landed without a runway. Why had this man started complimenting people with no warning. There should be a form. A lead-in. A "this may sound strange." She looked at the crate instead of him, because the crate was not going to make her ears hot.

After the exam, the owner thanked them into the ground, then asked Eve, "Could your cat come play sometime? I'd pay. I mean it. I'll Venmo. I'll WeChat. I'll bring fruit."

Eve blinked.

"Play?"

"He seems really good at calming other cats. Cream — that's his name — he hasn't looked this normal in weeks."

When the woman was gone, Eve stood there, thought of something, and her eyes lit bit by bit. The idea arrived the way good ideas did: messy, then suddenly a string.

"Julian."

"Mm?"

"What if we did pet companionship? Do you think people would pay?"

He raised a brow. "Like?"

"In-home help for first-time cat people. Social support for anxious pets. Training for shy cats. Even, like... emotional first aid for the humans." She sped up, scattered ideas suddenly clicking. Her mouth got ahead of her notes. "A lot of people don't just need a vet. They need everyday backup. Especially people who live alone. One cat, one person, and they panic the second something's off. They don't need a hospital. They need someone to say, he's not dying, he's just dramatic, here's what you do."

He watched her.

When she talked about work, her eyes went bright and her mouth got faster, like someone had plugged her in. He used to think she was just fun, and wouldn't let anyone have the last word. Now he could see it more clearly. She was actually built for this.

He'd watched her clock the panic under the owner's questions, then turn a fuzzy need into a price and a time slot. That was the part most kind people never got to.

"Possible," he said. "And not a small possible."

Eve got more excited, scooped Dumpling, and kissed his head. "Son, you're about to get rich!"

Dumpling meowed, full of protest, and tried to twist out of the kiss. The meow had the energy of a creature who had not consented to a business model.

Julian watched them, the smile in his eyes getting harder to hide.

He thought, suddenly, that if they actually built something together, that might not be bad. The thought was practical. It was also not only practical. He put it down before it grew a second sentence.

The thought had barely formed when Eve's phone rang.

Unknown number.

A woman's voice, too enthusiastic. The kind of enthusiasm that came with a script.

"Hello, is this Ms. Eve? This is Yunqi Media. We saw your recent pet-account work. We think you'd be a great fit for our content director role. Can we set up an interview?"

Eve's eyes brightened.

Content director. That sounded like it might pay more. It sounded like a title you could send to your mother. She was about to say yes — yes was a reflex, yes was rent — when Dumpling launched out of her arms, went for the phone, and slapped it.

The call died.

She stared at the dark screen. Julian stared at the cat. The room made a very stupid silence.

She looked down. Dumpling looked up, serious as a cat who'd just stopped an international scam.

Two seconds later the number called back.

Dumpling lifted a paw again.

Eve grabbed him, but she already had a read. Same trash-can energy. Same "don't."

She picked up, asked a few more questions, and sure enough: "content director" meant find creators across the internet, run their accounts, also do business development and livestreams, all stitched into one Frankenstein job. The pay structure was "the dream is plump, reality is weather-dependent." Base plus commission plus "exposure." She knew exposure. Exposure did not pay 3,600.

After she hung up, she sighed. Put the phone face down. Dumpling sat on it, which was not necessary and felt personal.

"Okay. I'm starting to believe it."

Julian looked at her. "Believe what?"

"That he might actually read minds."

The room went still for a second.

His face didn't change much, like he was chewing on a sentence that clearly broke several laws of nature. Then he looked at Dumpling, sitting on the table like a security officer, and said, calmly, "If it's true, at least he hasn't used it to do anything bad."

She'd been braced for him to tell her she was being ridiculous. That made her pause. Most people laughed first. Laughing was safer.

"That's your reaction?"

"What else?" He handed Dumpling a tube of nutritional gel. Dumpling, generously, licked it, then licked it again, then committed. "You picked him up in a rainstorm. A cat having a little extra going on isn't that wild. I've seen weirder. A parrot that hated men in hats. A turtle that only ate if you played the news."

She looked at him, and the argument she'd been saving — the one about coincidence, about sleep deprivation, about not telling anyone — went unused.

Most people she'd met, faced with something outside their map, either questioned it first or laughed. Julian just... stood on her side. Even when it sounded absurd. Even when the evidence was a fat cat and a hung-up HR call.

"Julian."

"Mm?"

"You're actually a really good person."

His hands paused. The gel tube crinkled.

Dumpling looked up from the snack, a smear of paste on his mouth, and gave Julian a long look. Not cupid. Not matchmaking. More like: she said a nice thing. You can say thank you. Also this gel is mine. Don't get ideas.

Julian lowered his eyes. Quiet: "I know."

Then, after a second, still looking at the cat: "Don't tell him I said that. He'll raise his rates."
