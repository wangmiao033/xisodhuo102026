Once the collaboration started for real, Eve's life filled up overnight.

Days were resumes and account content. Nights were editing, scripts, comments. She was a workhorse again, just newly employed by herself. The difference sat in her hands: old overtime paid for a boss's year-end bonus. This overtime, she could actually see the result. A caption that worked. A comment that wasn't "when is the discount." A video that didn't make her want to delete her own account.

The first video she made for the clinic was "Day seven after a street dog got adopted."

No forced pathos. No engineered crying. Just a shy little dog learning, bit by bit, to wag, to crawl into someone's arms. She cut it three times. The third time she left in a second of the owner just sitting there, not saying anything, which felt illegal in short video and also correct.

At the end, the owner stroked his head and said, "You don't have to be good. You can take your time."

Comments blew up that night. Eve watched them on her sofa with Dumpling using her thigh as furniture.

"I cried. How is this puppy so gentle."
"Help. 'You can take your time' felt like it was for me."
"Pet accounts don't have to be loud. This is actually healing."

The numbers beat everything the clinic had ever posted. She refreshed twice to make sure it wasn't a glitch. It wasn't. Director Chen sent her eight voice notes in one night. She played them on low. The thesis was one sentence, shouted in eight different volumes.

"Eve, you are a money god!"

Eve didn't know whether to laugh or cry. She screenshotted the stats and sent them to Julian, because he was the other person who would care about the dog more than the numbers, and also because she wanted someone to see it who wasn't yelling.

A few minutes later:

Very good.

Night shift. Just finished.

Dinner tomorrow. To celebrate.

She stared at the last line. Her thumb hovered over a demure reply. That's too much. You've been working. I can just eat instant noodles and feel successful privately.

Then Dumpling appeared on her lap, walked across the phone like a person with no concept of personal space, and slapped near the send key.

The message became:

Sure.

She stared at the screen. Then at the cat.

"You—" She stopped. There was no point. He'd already settled, calm, and even shook his whiskers, like a man who'd filed the paperwork and was waiting for the stamp.

Julian sent a time and place almost immediately. No "haha." No sticker. Just the address of a little noodle place near the clinic, and a time that assumed she would show up.

Eve held the phone until her ears went slowly, traitorously red. She put it face down. Put it face up. Put it in a drawer, which was insane, then took it back out because she needed the alarm.

The next night he took her to that little place near the clinic.

Small, clean, steam on the windows. The owner knew him. Wiped his hands on his apron and lit up like a relative at a wedding.

"Xiao Zhou! Bringing your girlfriend today?"

Eve's feet stopped. One shoe on the threshold, one shoe not. Very elegant. Very her.

Julian stopped too. Not dramatic. Just... stopped.

The owner slapped his own forehead. "Aiyo, sorry. Not yet?"

The air died on the spot. A radio in the back was doing something cheerful, which felt rude.

Eve wanted to vanish. Into the floor. Into a takeaway bag. Into a version of this evening where she had worn a mask.

Julian, somehow, stayed even. "Two house noodles. And the sweet-and-sour ribs."

The owner wrote it down, smiling like he understood a secret and would take it to the grave, or at least to the next table. "Got it. Friends. Friends."

When he was gone, Eve found her voice. It took a sip of water first. "Your neighborhood's... warm."

"He's like that with everyone."

"Then I'm relieved."

"What were you worried about?"

She opened her mouth. Closed it. Looked at the chopsticks. She'd figured it out. Sometimes Julian wasn't trying to trap people. He just asked a calm question and backed you into a wall, then stood there like the wall was a coincidence.

She drank more water and refused the bait. The water was not that interesting. She treated it like it was.

He watched her a moment, mouth curving, and let it go. Small mercy. She filed it under he can be trained, which was not a thought she needed.

Halfway through dinner, her phone rang.

Mom.

Her stomach tightened. The noodles suddenly looked like a witness. "Mom?"

"Eve," Lin's mother said, all softness. "Have you eaten?"

That opening never led anywhere good. Eve could set a clock by it. Next line, right on cue.

"Your aunt found someone for you. Works at a bank, very decent. When are you free to meet?"

Eve's scalp prickled. "Mom, I cannot think about this right now."

"Why not? Work's unstable, love life's empty, you're out there alone, of course I'm anxious."

She glanced at Julian. He was eating like a person who could not hear, which meant he could hear everything. She lowered her voice. "I've been busy."

"Busy with what? Busy wearing yourself down? A woman your age..."

She could recite the rest. Biological clock. Cousin's wedding. The neighbor's daughter who already had a second child, as if children were a group project Eve was failing.

She shut her eyes, bracing, when someone tapped the table.

She looked up.

Julian had pushed his phone over. One line already typed. The font was default. The offer was not.

If you need, I can pretend you're in a work meeting.

She stared. Then, somehow, she wanted to laugh. The irritation from the setup talk drained by half. Not because her mother was wrong about the facts — twenty-seven, unsteady work, single, yes, the slide deck of her life was not pretty — but because someone had just handed her an exit that wasn't a fight.

"Mom, I really am busy. I'm in a project meeting with a partner. Later, okay?"

The word partner softened her mother immediately. Eve could hear it, the little shift, like a lock clicking. "Fine, go work. But don't keep putting this off."

The call ended.

Eve exhaled and set the phone down. The table felt solid. Good. She needed solid.

"Thanks."

"No problem." He took his phone back. "They push hard?"

"Mm." She tugged a smile. "In my mom's eyes, twenty-seven, unemployed, single, I'm basically a high-risk population. Should be wearing a warning label. Do not approach without a hukou and a stable salary."

He looked at her, then said, "You're not."

"Not what?"

"High-risk." Completely flat. "You're just not on land yet."

She snorted. Water nearly went up her nose, which would have completed the evening.

"Julian, your comforting style is unique."

"I don't comfort people much."

"I can tell."

"So you can take it as an actual assessment."

The noodles suddenly felt hotter than usual. She blew on them, unnecessarily. Looked at his hands on the chopsticks instead of his face. He meant it. That was the problem. He didn't say things to be kind. He said them like charts.

Outside, the night wind was good. Not movie-night. Just cool enough that her ears calmed down.

They walked slowly. Nobody rushed. Eve talked about old content disasters, useless overtime, pointless reports, last-minute requests that never ended. Halfway through, she started laughing first, because if you didn't laugh at "make the blue breathe," you would have to scream, and screaming on a sidewalk was a different kind of unemployed.

"You know the worst one? A client at 1 a.m. told me to make the blue in the key visual 'breathe more.'"

Julian frowned. "How does blue breathe?"

"No idea." She spread her hands. "Maybe it has to come alive first. Maybe it needed a lung. I sent three versions. They picked the first one. The 1 a.m. was for sport."

He laughed, low.

Like wind through leaves. Light, and unfairly nice. She looked at him and thought: this man always looked so contained. When he actually smiled, he was gentle. Much gentler than the first night she'd met him, when she'd been dripping on his floor and he'd told her the cat wasn't going to die, as if that were the insult.

A flower shop up ahead was closing, discounted mint by the door. Little pots in a crate. Someone had handwritten 5 kuai on cardboard.

She looked twice. Not because she wanted a plant. Because the green was stupidly alive, and her windowsill at home still had the dead pothos pot, empty, which she had not thrown away, because throwing it away felt like admitting something.

"You like it?"

"Not really." Honest. "I killed a pothos. Plants still make me feel guilty. Like they know."

He stopped, went in.

Came back with a tiny pot of mint. The leaves were a little dusty. It smelled like toothpaste and kitchens.

She stared. "What are you doing?"

"For you."

"Why?"

"Because the pothos is over." He held out the pot. "People can go from killing pothos to keeping mint alive."

She took it. The ceramic was warm from his hand. She looked at that little bit of green for a long time, then at the sidewalk, then at him, and didn't trust a long sentence.

"Julian."

"Mm?"

"You're actually pretty good at this."

Two seconds of silence. A scooter went past. Too loud.

"I'm not comforting you."

"Then what are you doing?"

"Hoping you're a little happier."

After that, she had nothing. Streetlight. Night wind. A pot of mint in her arms, leaves brushing her wrist. She used to think liking someone had to be huge. Movie-big. Flowers. Confessions. Manufactured surprises, the kind Xu Fang would have storyboarded.

Turns out it could also just be this.

When she was at her most wrecked, most out of confidence, someone stood next to her, quietly, and told her, for real:

The pothos died. That's fine.

You can still keep something else alive.

She walked the rest of the way with the pot held a little too carefully, like it might notice if she got weird about it. At the corner she said goodnight too fast. He hummed, like that was enough.

Dumpling was waiting on the sofa when she got home. He sniffed the mint, decided it was not food, and went back to sleep. She put the pot on the windowsill where the pothos used to fail, washed her hands, and stood there in the dark kitchen longer than a plant required.
