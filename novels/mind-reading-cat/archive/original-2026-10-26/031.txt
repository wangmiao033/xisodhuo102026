





Married life was not what Eve had pictured.

She used to think getting married was turning a new page.

It should be serious. Dignified. A little brand-new ceremony in it. Maybe a new mug set. Maybe people who suddenly knew how to fold fitted sheets. She had, embarrassingly, imagined mornings that looked like an ad: two cups, one window, no cat sitting on the important mail.

Reality: three days after the wedding, she and Julian had their first official married debate, topic: "Is Dumpling allowed on the bed."

It started in the middle of the night. She was sleeping well. Then something heavy landed on her chest.

She blinked, fuzzy, and there was Dumpling on the blanket, peaceful as a night auditor. Full weight. One paw on her collarbone, like a stamp. His whiskers tickled her chin. He smelled like the expensive can he was not supposed to have gotten a second of. His eyes were half-closed. He was not asking. He was clocking in.

"Why is he on the bed?"

Julian woke too, looked down. Hair smashed. Voice still half in a dream.

"He's always been like this."

"He has a bed."

"He has several beds."

"That's my point."

Dumpling, very naturally, lay down between them, spreading himself into a warm fur blanket. He took the exact middle. Not a guest. A border. His tail landed on Julian's wrist. He purred once, short, like a stamp on a form.

Eve, quiet protest: "This isn't okay. He has his own bed."

Julian, objective: "When you lived alone, he did this a lot too."

"I had a twin. We have a queen now."

"So he thinks there's more room."

She stared at the ceiling. The logic was so stupid it circled back around to reasonable. She wanted to argue. The cat was already purring, which felt like cheating. The purr filled the small space between them like he'd brought his own heater and his own policy.

The logic made her laugh, angry. She reached for the cat.

Dumpling rolled toward Julian, practiced, tail pinning his wrist, clearly picking a side. He even put his head on Julian's arm, slow, as if this had been negotiated in a meeting Eve had not been invited to.

She was shocked.

"Since when are you two this close?"

He coughed, lightly. "Maybe because I'm an official household member now."

It sounded reasonable.

She still felt this cat's palace-intrigue route was getting more complicated. First the slippers. Now the bed. Next he'd start forwarding her calendar. She poked Dumpling's ear. Dumpling did not move. Julian, traitor, scratched the ear for him.

"You're encouraging him."

"I'm being used."

"That's what encouragement looks like from this angle."

Dumpling's eyes opened a slit, gold in the dark, then closed again. Message received. Debate adjourned.

She lay there another minute, listening to two kinds of breathing and one kind of purr. Married life, she thought, was less like a new page and more like the same book with an extra bookmark that had claws.

Besides the bed war, married life had a lot of very small, very real sweetness. Not the greeting-card kind. The kind where you argued about whether the soy milk was still good, then both drank it anyway.

They started actually keeping house together.

Not in a montage. In pieces. A hook by the door that finally got used. A second toothbrush that stopped looking like a guest. His charger on her side of the nightstand because her side had the only outlet that didn't make the plug wiggle. She complained about it. She did not move it.

Grocery runs: he read ingredient lists and dates, she snuck snacks into the cart. He would find them at checkout, look at her, and still pay. She called it marriage. He called it "you already put them in." Once she hid a bag of freeze-dried chicken under the greens. He found that too. He held it up between two fingers like evidence.

"This isn't for us."

"It's for the household."

"The household has a name."

"The household has a union."

He paid. Dumpling, at home, accepted the chicken as if he'd placed the order. He sat by the bag, waited, and only meowed once, which Eve translated as invoice, not request.

Cooking: she washed and chopped and created a disaster zone, he cleaned the disaster zone and finished dinner. Once she tried to fry something ambitious and set off the alarm. The alarm was loud and ungenerous. Dumpling left the room at a walk that said he would not die for cuisine. Julian opened a window, no lecture, then made noodles like the kitchen had not attempted a crime. She ate the noodles standing up, because sitting felt like admitting defeat.

"They're good," she said.

"They're noodles."

"That's what I said."

Coming home: who showered first, who fed the cat, who got the packages, all of it could turn into some weird, interesting little scene. She learned he folded plastic bags. He learned she left one sock on the sofa like a flag. Neither of them fixed the other person. They just started stepping around the flags.

One evening she won the shower by claiming cat-hair on her clothes, which was always true. He fed Dumpling and brought the packages in. When she came out, Dumpling was already sitting on the largest box, as if delivery were a tribute system. Julian stood there with scissors, waiting for the cat to finish inspecting.

"He has to approve it first," Eve said.

"I noticed."

One night they just got back. The shop day was still stuck to her skin, voices and fur and sanitizer. She collapsed on the sofa, too tired to take off her shoes. One heel still hooked. She didn't care.

He went to heat milk. Dumpling walked over, sat by her leg, and looked at her, very serious. He sniffed the air, then her knee, then the bag she'd dumped on the floor.

"What?"

He patted her bag.

Not a cute pat. A professional one. Two taps. Repeat.

"Something in there?"

She opened it. Half a piece of cake she'd stuffed in during the day, meaning to eat at home. The frosting had glued itself to a receipt. Very gourmet. The cake had suffered. It still smelled like sugar and a little like her keys.

She stared, then laughed.

"How is your nose that good."

He meowed, reserved. Not begging. Billing.

Julian came out with the milk and caught it. Two mugs. He set them down and looked at the cake like it was a small animal that had gotten lost.

"Is he negotiating benefits with you again?"

"Mm." She held up the cake. "Overtime perk. Chief of Hospitality."

"The frosting is on the receipt."

"That's the garnish."

He sat beside her, took her bag, naturally, and tucked her loose hair behind her ear. The milk went on the table. He pulled her shoes off, one then the other, like it was just part of coming home.

Dumpling accepted a corner of cake. He ate with the dignity of someone who had not just audited a handbag.

"You were tired today."

"A little."

"Go in later tomorrow morning."

"I can't. I have to watch the new event signups."

"I'll watch them."

She turned her head.

After the wedding, these "I'll watch it," "you rest," "I'll do it" moments seemed to multiply.

Multiply into a habit.

Not one person giving in.

Just both of them, on instinct, putting the other person into their own rhythm. She still made fun of it in her head. Old-couple speedrun. Pathetic. Also she was not going to stop him from taking the signups, because her eyes were already closing. The milk was exactly the temperature she liked, which meant he had stood there waiting for it, which she was not going to acknowledge out loud.

"Julian."

"Mm?"

"Have you noticed we already look like an old married couple."

He thought about it.

He looked at the shoes on the floor, the cat on the rug, the two mugs, her on the sofa with her makeup half gone. He did not rush it.

"I have."

"Is it too fast?"

"No," he said. "I like it."

No extra. No "this is what I always wanted." Just I like it, said like a fact on a chart.

She moved closer, and put her head on his shoulder. His shirt smelled like outside and a little like cat. She thought: if Su Li could see this she'd send a voice note with no words, only screaming. Then she thought: don't tell Su Li. Let her find out from the leftover cake in the group chat photo.

"I like it too."

The apartment was quiet in the after-work way. A neighbor's TV through the wall. The fridge doing its one loud thought. Eve could still feel the ring when her hand shifted against his shirt. New enough to notice. Old enough already that she forgot it in the shop and then remembered it when she washed her hands.

"If he gets on the bed again tonight," she murmured, "I'm putting him on your side."

"He already picked my side."

"That's what I said."

Dumpling sat on the rug, looking at the clingy humans on the sofa, then at the half piece of cake by his paw, and decided not to comment.

Fine.

As long as there was food.

He ate the frosting off the receipt first. Professional.

Eve watched him, then looked at Julian's profile in the lamp, the same face that had said I like it like a fact. Married three days, arguing about a cat on a bed, stealing cake from a bag. She had pictured something more ceremonial. This was better. This had crumbs.

"If we fight about the bed again tomorrow," she said, "I'm still going to lose."

"Probably."

"Don't agree so fast."

He kissed her hair, which was cheating. Dumpling finished the frosting and sat on the receipt like a stamp. Meeting adjourned. The queen-size bed, down the hall, already knew how this would go.
