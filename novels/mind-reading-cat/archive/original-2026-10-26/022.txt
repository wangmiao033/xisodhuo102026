


Julian had said "once the opening rush is over." Eve thought that meant at least another ten days, maybe two weeks.




She didn't think he'd cash it in after three.




Weekend. They'd just finished a "bring-your-pet mixer."




The floor had looked like a giant parent-teacher conference.




A corgi chasing a cold silver shaded in circles. A woman introducing her poodle, serious: "That Pomeranian's family situation is actually pretty good." The bichon-matchmaking auntie with a little notebook, asking:




"Mom and dad's weight? Shedding? Temper stable?"




Eve stood to the side, laughing until she couldn't straighten. Joy sat in her floral dress like a girl at a blind date she had not agreed to, which, to be fair, she had not.




She finally got why so many comedies were about the marriage market.




People or dogs, the second you entered "match" language, everything got weird and also made sense. Housing. Temper. Whether the other party barked at delivery. She could have run a whole company on this and still not understood her own group chat.




Guests filtered out. Treat bags. Leashes. Someone saying "text me" about a golden who had not consented to be networked.




Su Li, leaving, gave Eve a look full of meaning. The look had homework in it.




"I'm not coming over tonight."




"You don't come over every night anyway."




"Tonight I especially don't." She patted Eve's shoulder. "Good luck. Drink water. Don't say anything insane. Or do. I'm not your lawyer."




Eve hadn't processed what she was supposed to be lucky about. Su Li was already clicking away in her heels, a person exiting a theater before the credits.




Director Chen was worse. After packing the last box of freeze-dried treats, he actually took Dumpling too.




"Grandpa's taking you to the clinic tonight. We have a night, we have a sofa, we have no young people making faces."




Dumpling struggled. Clearly not a fan. His back feet did a small, insulted dance.




Director Chen dropped his voice. "Be good. Give your mom and dad some space."




Eve's mouth opened. The "mom and dad" made her ears hot, and in the end she didn't get a full sentence out. She got a sound. The sound was not professional.




When everyone was gone, the shop went fully quiet.




Wind through the half-open window, a little night-cool. The sign outside hummed, or maybe that was just her.




She was sorting signup sheets when he spoke. Her name, not the work version.




"Eve."




"Mm?"




"Come out for a second."




"Where?"




"The door."




Her pulse jumped, for no reason she was willing to write down. She put the papers down and followed him. The papers would wait. Papers were good at waiting. She was not.




The path light was on. Warm yellow in the glass. The sign looked especially gentle at night, You, Me & Paws, like it had always been there and the street had simply caught up.




And beside the step, sometime without her noticing, there was a small plant stand.




Three pots.




Mint. Cat grass. And a pothos, newly growing, a little shy, a little like it knew its reputation.




She stared.




"This is..."




"An upgrade on your old mint," he said. "Cat grass for Dumpling. The pothos... a retrial for someone who used to kill them."




She looked at that pothos and wanted to laugh, then didn't, because laughing would come out wet. She remembered, a long time ago, a night when he'd handed her a little mint and said people could go from killing pothos to keeping mint alive.




Some lines, he'd been remembering too. Not the pretty ones. The plant ones. The ones that had dirt on them.




"You said you had something to say to me properly."




He stood in front of her. Night wind moved the hair on his forehead. He didn't fix it. He looked like he had forgotten how to fix small things.




He looked at her, and for once the nerves were obvious. Not a lot. Enough. A man who could set a bone and still not know where to put his hands.




"Mm."




She got nervous with him.




He was usually so steady.




Steady enough that she'd almost never seen him like this.




That was how she knew the next part mattered. She thought about the shop, the rush, the man with the watch, the fever, the cake. She thought about rent, because her brain was a coward and rent was a hiding place. Then she stopped hiding, because he had already said come out.




"When I asked you to wait," he said, slow, "it wasn't because I wasn't sure."




She listened. She did not help. Helping would ruin it. A scooter passed on the street. She hated the scooter. She also could not have asked it to wait.




"It was because I was too sure." He looked at her, voice low, every word clear, like he was reading a chart he had already checked twice. "Sure that I like you. Sure that I want to run this shop with you. Sure I want a lot of years after this, with you in them."




Her eyes stung at once. She blinked, hard, like blinking was a skill.




"I know how hard it was when you first got laid off. Then the shop, and we never stopped being busy." He kept going. Not a speech. A list. He was good at lists. "I didn't want to add weight when everything was already a mess, so I kept thinking, wait. Wait until you're lighter. Wait until it's more stable."




"Then I realized liking someone is hard to time perfectly."




"When you laugh, I want to look a little longer. When you're not well, I want to get to you first. If someone asks for your number, I mind. If someone makes you unhappy, I'm unhappy too."




"Eve."




The way he said her name always had a seriousness, and a gentleness, she couldn't file. Not a nickname. Not a title. Just Eve, like saying it was a decision.




"I like you."




"Not because we opened a shop together, not because you happened to be next to me," he said. "Because that person is you. So I keep liking you more."




The tears went immediately, which was rude of them. They did not ask. They just showed up, like Dumpling on a keyboard.




She wasn't even that much of a crier.




Some words, waited for too long, thought about too long: when you actually heard them, you had no moves left. No joke. No invoice. No winning the sentence. Just a face doing too much in front of a plant stand.




She was crying and also wanting to laugh, which probably looked a little stupid. She could live with stupid. Stupid was honest.




He clearly panicked. A vet, panicked at water. She would remember this. She would be kind about it later. Not now.




"Don't cry."




"I'm not crying." She wiped her face. Her sleeve was not a tissue. She used it anyway. "I'm just... emotionally well-hydrated. It's a condition. Very common in founders."




That got a laugh out of him too. Relief, mostly. She heard the relief. She liked it.




"Then, Eve, are we done being well-hydrated?"




"Not yet."




"Can I keep going?"




"Keep going."




He looked at her. The laugh stayed in his mouth, quieter.




"Will you be my girlfriend?"




She sniffed, and a lot of pictures came up, not in order, not cinematic, just stacked.




A rain night, herself crashing into a clinic with a cat, a complete wreck.




The first time she saw him, his hands wrapping Dumpling's leg.




The mint. The breakfasts. The cake. The fever he sat through. Every time she was about to fold, the way he just... stood there. Not a speech. A person, in the room.




She looked at this person and thought: some laters really weren't empty.




They were walked out, step by step, with someone beside you, and also with a cat who billed in freeze-dried.




"I will."




Her voice still caught. The smile was already winning. She let it. She was tired of competing with her own face.




"But I have a condition."




He blinked. "What condition?"




"You have to keep making it up to me." Completely serious. "I look tough. I'm actually very easy to soothe. Cake. Neck. Don't let strange men add me. The usual benefits package."




He laughed.




"Okay."




"Also."




"Also?"




"The cake can't stop."




"Okay."




"Also..."




He took a step in.




"Eve."




"Mm?"




"If there are more alsos, I might miss now."




She forgot the next also. Completely. It left. It could file a complaint later.




The next second he bent, and kissed her, lightly.




The kiss was light.




Like a test. Like a wish that had been held for a long time, finally setting down, careful of her teeth, careful of the night. He tasted like the tea from closing, which was not romantic, and still the only thing she could think besides don't miss this, don't go stiff, don't make a joke yet.




She went stiff for a second. Mouth, brain, both lagging.




Then she loosened, slowly, and caught the edge of his shirt. The shirt was ordinary. The catching was not.




Night wind, light. Streetlight, warm. The pots by the door moving a little, mint and cat grass and a pothos on probation.




Somewhere toward the clinic, somebody's dog barked.




Background audio for a confession, apparently. She would not put it on the account. She would think about it every time a dog barked at night, which was going to be a problem.




At the same time, behind the glass, Dumpling — who was supposed to have been taken away by Director Chen — had snuck back.




Of course he had. There was cat grass. There was also unfinished business. He was not a cat who left a ticket open.




He sat inside, staring through the door at the two adults who had, finally, kissed.




He did not clap.




He did, however, look at the cat grass, then at them, then at the cat grass again, as if to say:




Fine.




You may have the door.




The grass is still mine.




And if you knock the pots over with this, I will personally review the renovation.
