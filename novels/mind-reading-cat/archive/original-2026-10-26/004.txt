Once you lose a job, time gets weird.

Eve used to count down to clock-out, convinced twenty-four hours were never enough. Now the same twenty-four hours stretched like cheap gum. Nine in the morning she sat in front of her laptop sending resumes, copying the same three bullet points into slightly different boxes, telling herself this one might be the one. By eleven she was reading her own summary and wondering who that competent person was supposed to be. At two she was sneering at job apps. The ones that wanted five years of experience, a "passion for hustle," and a salary that was "negotiable," which always meant: you already know you're going to lose this fight. At night she rewrote the resume again, changed one verb, changed it back, and comforted herself with the oldest unemployed lie in the book: it's fine. Heaven is preparing you for great things. Heaven, in her experience, was mostly preparing people for more rent.

Without Dumpling, the days would've been worse. Not dramatic-worse. Just quieter. The kind of quiet that made you check your bank app for no reason.

Since the adoption event, he'd clocked in properly. Duties, even. She hadn't assigned them. He'd taken them, the way a cat takes a sofa: without asking, and with the attitude of someone who had always lived there.

Like making sure she didn't stay up.

One night she was still editing a proposal at 1 a.m. The kitchen light was on. Her neck hurt. Dumpling sat by the laptop for ten minutes, watching the cursor blink, then put a paw on her mouse.

"Let go." She tried reason, which was already a sign she was losing. "Mom... roommate needs to make money. The roommate would also like to keep the lights on."

He did not let go.

He even meowed, very softly, with the cold eyes of a creature who profoundly disagreed with human sleep schedules. The bandage on his leg made it worse. You could not win an argument with a cat who was injured and morally certain.

They stalemated. She lasted maybe another two minutes, mostly out of pride. Then she saved the file, shut the laptop, and muttered something unkind about performance reviews. He hopped down like a supervisor who'd closed the meeting.

Like screening jobs.

One afternoon she got an interview call. She put on her work voice before she even stood up, which was muscle memory and a little sad. The company opened with, "We're a young, energetic startup. Compensation is still being optimized, but the growth potential is huge." Her forehead twitched. Optimized. She'd written that sentence for other people. She knew what it meant. It meant: we will pay you in adjectives.

She was going to hear them out. That was the unemployed disease. You told yourself listening was free. Then she put it on speaker, because her hands were doing something with a mug, and Dumpling stood up in his bed.

Ears flat. He circled the living room once, like he was checking the exits, and stopped by the trash can. One paw tapped the lid.

The meaning was not subtle.

Garbage.

Eve was quiet for two seconds. She looked at the trash can. She looked at the cat. She looked at her own resume sitting open on the screen, the word "driven" still in there, embarrassing her.

Then she said, politely, into the phone, "Thanks. I'm not considering unpaid-plus-full-attendance 'growth opportunities' right now."

She hung up and opened a can for him. The cheap kind. He did not complain. He had standards, but they were targeted.

Dumpling ate in peace.

The kind of peace that said: this is my consulting fee, obviously. Don't make it weird.

Or fraud prevention.

Monday morning, a man calling himself the head of a "well-known MCN" wanted to talk collaboration. He'd seen her ops skills. He'd give her platform resources. She just had to do three months of "trial content" first. Then they'd really invest in her. The word invest arrived with a little sparkle on it, like a filter.

She'd already been about to decline. She had a sentence ready. Then he sent a long string of voice notes, painting a future so fragrant it almost made her hungry. Studios. Traffic. A team. The kind of life where you said "my assistant" without laughing.

Dumpling listened for ten seconds. Yawned huge. Then turned his ass to the phone.

The contempt was complete. Even the tail tip spelled two words.

Get lost.

Eve laughed out loud. It came out uglier than a polite laugh, which was better.

She'd noticed: since Dumpling arrived, she was still broke, still had no idea where she was going, still refreshing her bank app like it might grow a conscience. But there was suddenly something to laugh at. A fat cat with opinions. A trash can used as a performance review. An MCN pitch getting mooned.

That was probably the point of a cat.

He couldn't make you money. He couldn't pay your mortgage. He couldn't make Mr. Zhao take back the papers.

He could roll his eyes for you, in advance, when you wanted to curse someone out and still had to sound professional.

That was already useful. More useful than half the people she'd cc'd in the last three years.

A week later, Dumpling's injury was doing well.

When Eve brought him in to remove part of the splint, the receptionist waved the second she saw her. "Eve! Julian's inside."

"I know," Eve said. "Generally, as long as I still owe money, Julian is in."

The girl almost snorted. Then remembered she was at work, and turned it into a cough. Eve appreciated the effort.

Julian was on the floor trimming a corgi's nails when she pushed the door open.

No white coat today. Light gray knit, work pants, sleeves rolled to the forearm, a clean line of wrist showing. The knit looked soft. She did not need to think about whether it was soft. The corgi had been bouncing like a nightclub loaf of bread. In his hands it went still, which was either skill or witchcraft, and she was not in a position to investigate.

The corgi barked twice at Eve.

Dumpling answered from the carrier.

Cat and dog yelling at each other across the room. The clinic got loud immediately. Someone in the hallway said "aiyo." Eve set her bag down and thought, not for the first time, that animals had no indoor voice and somehow still got more respect than she had in meetings.

Julian looked up. "He's in a good mood today."

"Maybe he knows he's almost better." She set the carrier down. The plastic clicked on the tile. "Or maybe it's because he stole half a ham sausage this morning."

Julian's brow jumped. "Cats shouldn't eat ham sausage."

"I know. That's why he only got half."

He looked at her like he wanted to say something, then let it out as a very quiet sigh. The kind of sigh that had treated too many animals owned by people who meant well and read labels never.

"Eve."

"Mm?"

"If you're going to keep a cat, maybe try a little harder."

She wanted to snap back that she already was. That she'd bought the right food. That she'd set alarms for medicine. That she had not, despite several opportunities, put him back by the recycling bins. Then she saw the way he lifted Dumpling out, that serious face, hands careful around the bad leg, and lost her nerve. Winning the sentence suddenly felt cheap.

Dumpling went on the table, rubbed against her first out of habit, then went still the second Julian's fingers touched the back of his neck.

Eve's mouth tightened.

"Does he like you more?"

"No." He checked the wound, voice even. "He just knows I'm helping him right now."

She paused.

It sounded like he was talking about the cat.

It also didn't, entirely. She looked at his hands instead of his face. Long fingers. No rush. The splint coming off in small, boring steps, which was how real care looked when it wasn't content.

She was still zoning out when the door opened. A polished young woman in heels walked in, a ragdoll in her arms, tone familiar enough to suggest she'd known Julian for years. Perfume, light. The kind that said I have a skincare routine and also opinions.

"Julian, look at Lucky. He didn't want to eat again yesterday."

Eve looked up on instinct. Lucky was beautiful, which felt slightly unfair. Dumpling was fat and bandaged and currently her entire household.

Julian straightened. His voice cooled a notch. "Did you check in?"

The woman blinked. "I have to check in?"

"Everyone checks in."

She looked a little embarrassed. Then her eyes found Eve, and a measuring look slid in. Eve knew that look. It was the look people used in offices when they were trying to place you: intern, assistant, threat, nobody.

She smiled. "Sorry. I thought if we knew each other, it might be easier."

Eve hadn't spoken yet. Dumpling went off first.

Not at the person.

At the ragdoll.

More precisely: after the word "knew each other," Dumpling's fur puffed, his tail went stick-straight, and his eyes went sharp. Eve's throat tightened. She knew this routine. Every time he did this, something ugly was coming, and she was going to have to stand there in her unpaid-gig clothes and deal with it.

Sure enough, the woman looked back at Eve, smile still soft, words not quite.

"Are you the new assistant? I haven't seen you before."

Eve's mouth tugged. Rent. Wet socks. The urge to win the sentence, just once, without being polite about it.

"No. I'm a creditor."

The woman clearly didn't get it. Julian, very quietly, laughed.

Not much. Enough to improve Eve's entire mood. She felt her shoulders drop, which was embarrassing, because a laugh shouldn't do that. It did anyway.

The woman's face stiffened a little.

Standing there with her cat, a flash of displeasure went through her eyes, then she was smooth again. "I'll go check in then. Oh, and Auntie asked yesterday when you're coming home for dinner."

She glanced at Eve like it was an accident, and left. Heels on tile. Lucky looking mildly confused, which Eve related to.

The door shut. Two seconds of quiet. The corgi, forgotten in the corner, sneezed.

Eve folded her arms. "You get a lot of that."

Julian was unwrapping Dumpling, didn't look up. "It's not that."

"Then what is it?"

"My mom's friend's daughter."

"Ah."

"She has a... misunderstanding."

Eve couldn't help laughing. It came out before she could sand it down. "That's a very academic way to put it. Misunderstanding as in, she thinks you'll marry her?"

His hands paused.

"I won't."

Steady. No drama. Weirdly serious. Like he'd already had this conversation, maybe with himself, maybe with Auntie, and had filed it under closed.

She'd only been teasing. After that, her ears went hot for no good reason. She looked down at Dumpling fast, because looking at Julian felt like volunteering for something.

"Done? If you're done, we're leaving."

He hummed and wrote out new notes for her. Nice handwriting. Same as last time. She watched the pen instead of his mouth.

At the door, holding Dumpling, she turned back. Dumpling's head bumped her chin, impatient. He had the energy of a creature who had endured enough social events for one morning.

"Hey." She wagged the chart. "Thanks for the gig last time. Director Chen said they want to do another event next month. If you don't mind, I can keep going."

He looked at her. "We don't mind."

A beat.

"I don't mind either."

Her ears got worse. She said "oh" like a coward, and walked out fast with the cat, nearly clipping the doorframe, which was not the exit of a professional.

Dumpling poked his head out of her arms and looked back at Julian. Not long. Just enough to check the man was still standing there, still holding a pen, still not chasing after anyone with a ragdoll.

Then he yawned, huge, and put his face into Eve's sleeve, done with the morning.

Julian lifted a hand and rubbed his brow.

The chart was still warm. He had, suddenly, the distinct feeling of having been approved by a cat, and not having any idea what the criteria were.
