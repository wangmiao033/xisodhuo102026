


Sometimes the only thing between a person and a turning point is one socially anxious cat.

Eve used to not believe that.

She thought turning points should be dramatic. Winning the lottery. Getting promoted. An old boss finding his conscience and begging her to come back, preferably in writing, cc'ing HR. Turns out real life corners are quieter than that. Quiet enough to be a weekday afternoon and a golden-shaded owner texting:

Eve, is your cat free this weekend?

Eve stared at the line and felt a strange, floating absurdity.

She was twenty-seven. Laid off. Content planner. Current career path: booking gigs for her own cat. The resume, if she updated it honestly, would get her laughed out of every job app in the city. She screenshotted it anyway, because Su Li would want evidence.

Su Li slapped the table laughing. Soy milk nearly died.

"New vertical," she said, wiping tears. "Other people raise kids. You're raising a cat-prodigy. Is Dumpling going to buy a house before you? Be honest. I'll help him with the down payment if he lets me list him as a dependent."

"If he can get rich off his face and mind-reading, I could live with that." Eve was dead serious. The soy milk was sweet. The joke wasn't only a joke. "Someone in this household has to amount to something. I've had a week. He can take a shift."

Then she laughed too. It came out easier than it used to.

Joke or not, she was taking it seriously. She opened a notes app. She made a list. She hated that she made a list, then kept making it.

The owner, Ms. Xu, was a designer in a high-rise, living alone with her cat, busy, wound tight. The little boy cat, Cream, had always been timid. After a move and too many environment changes, he hadn't touched his regular food in days. Strangers made him shake. The message had a photo attached: two round eyes under a cabinet, like a hostage situation.

Eve forwarded it to Julian. Added a question mark, then deleted it, then sent the question anyway.

"Think we can take it?"

He read the chat, thought a moment. She could see the three dots, then nothing, then a proper sentence. He did not do voice notes. She was starting to like that.

"Try once. The point isn't for Dumpling to 'cure' him. It's to see if he settles faster."

"How much do we charge?"

"You decide."

She'd asked it casually. She hadn't expected him to hand her the decision that naturally, like she wasn't a person who still flinched at "budget's limited."

She blinked, then started calculating. Travel time, session time, pre-talk, follow-up, plus a trial price that shouldn't scare people off. Too high and you looked like a scam. Too low and you looked like a hobby. She'd priced campaigns for brands that sold water. This should not have been harder. It was harder.

"Three-eighty for two hours, plus basic advice. What do you think?"

He looked at her. They were in the clinic hallway, between a bag of litter and a poster about dental week. "You're better at this than I am."

"Obviously." She lifted her chin. "You save lives. I make money. Division of labor. Very modern."

His mouth curved.

"Alright, Eve."

Her ears went hot. She was noticing it more and more: Julian didn't always need a proper "you're amazing." Sometimes just tossing out Eve like that made her want to win the next sentence and also sit down. She muttered "don't call me that" and did not mean it enough to repeat it.

Saturday, she brushed Dumpling and put him in a light blue harness printed with little fish.

He looked down at himself, expression complicated. The fish were cute. He did not want to be cute. He wanted to be respected.

She clipped the buckle and lectured. "You're going out to work today, not to rob anyone. Image matters. Understood? No stealing ham. No sitting on other people's trash cans. We are professionals now. We have a rate."

He meowed, and endured it. The meow said: this is a hostage situation. The fish are a war crime.

Ms. Xu's apartment was even nicer than Eve expected. White walls, wood floors, half a city in the window, a thin thread of diffuser in the air. The nicer the place, the more pitiful Cream looked, mashed under a cabinet.

He was almost flat to the floor. Only a pair of round eyes showing, like the world owed him eight million and had paid in silence.

Ms. Xu looked worried. She talked with her hands. "He's been crying at night too. I'm scared the downstairs neighbors will complain. I already left them fruit. I don't know what else you leave people. I left fruit."

Eve nodded, crouched, and asked about routines, food, stress points. All questions Julian had told her to ask. Don't rush the cat. Learn the room and the rhythm first. She wrote them on her phone like a person who still believed in process.

While she asked, she thought: so this job isn't just shots and pills. A lot of the time, vets are translating the hurt that animals can't say. Owners too. Ms. Xu kept apologizing for the cabinet, for the crying, for existing at a volume Cream didn't like. Eve recognized the tone. She'd used it on clients.

She set Dumpling not far from the cabinet. Unclipped the leash. Left the fish harness on, because she didn't trust him with a white sofa.

He didn't patrol like usual. He lay down slowly, tail sweeping, loose, like he was at home. Like the high-rise was a sun patch he'd ordered.

Cream watched him for a long time. Slowly, his ears stopped pinning so hard.

Ms. Xu barely breathed. Eve almost told her to breathe, then didn't, because whispering "breathe" at a stranger was a different kind of weird.

Twenty minutes later Cream actually inched out. Sniffed the air, then the floor, then crept toward Dumpling and stopped half a meter away.

Dumpling didn't move.

He even yawned.

The meaning was extremely clear.

Relax. Big bro's here. Also this apartment has better heating than ours.

Eve's throat went tight, in a good way. She suddenly understood why people said pets had their own magnetic field. Some feelings didn't need language. Just one calm body nearby, and another life started to settle. She didn't need to put it in a caption. She still kind of wanted to. Occupational disease.

Near the end, Cream managed a few bites of freeze-dried treats by Ms. Xu's feet. Small bites. Real ones.

Ms. Xu was so moved she almost cried on Eve's hand. Eve let her. The hand would survive.

"I thought you were just trying. I didn't think it would actually work."

"Mostly Dumpling." Eve scratched her fat cat's head. He allowed it, barely. "I'm just his agent. I take a cut. He does the talent."

Ms. Xu laughed, transferred the money on the spot, and even offered the story as an account case, as long as they kept faces out. Eve said yes before she could overthink the ethics, then overthought them on the elevator anyway, and decided faces-out was the line, and the line was fine.

On the way back, Eve held Dumpling and stared at the payment notification. Those digits looked cuter than any old paycheck. Three-eighty. Not a lot. A client used to spend that on a lunch that wasn't even good.

It was the first money she and Dumpling had made with their own skills. Not a boss's leftover. Not severance. Work.

"Son," she said, emotional, and then hated the word, and used it anyway. "We made it. Don't let it go to your head. We still have rent."

Dumpling was warm from the sun in her arms, and couldn't be bothered to correct the title. The fish harness jingled. He gave it a look of deep, personal betrayal.

Back at the clinic, Julian had just finished a small surgery, hands still not fully washed. She burst in, eyes bright. His step caught. Soap on his wrists. A mask pushed down.

"How was it?"

She shoved the phone at him. The screen was still on the transfer.

"Paid."

He looked, and couldn't help smiling. Small. Real.

"Congratulations."

"Julian." She was solemn, because if she wasn't solemn she might bounce, and bouncing in a clinic felt illegal. "As of today, are we officially in startup pre-season?"

He looked at her lit-up eyes. The tired from the surgery sat down a little.

"We are."

She leaned in a little, voice down, like someone might overhear. The hallway smelled like disinfectant and cat food. "If we get rich later, can the clinic save an honorary shareholder seat for me and Dumpling? We don't need a plaque. He can't read. I can read. I'll take a plaque."

He leaned in a little too.

"We can."

They were suddenly close.

Close enough that she could smell the faint disinfectant on him, cool and clean, and somehow calming. Her breath snagged. She stepped back half a pace on instinct, nearly hit a rolling cart, caught herself, and became extremely interested in a poster about fleas.

Dumpling, still in the fish harness, let out a short, offended sound.

Not about them. About the buckle still sitting under his armpit.

Eve got the hint on the bus. She took the harness off. He spent the rest of the ride facing the window, recovering his dignity, and did not forgive the fish until dinner.
