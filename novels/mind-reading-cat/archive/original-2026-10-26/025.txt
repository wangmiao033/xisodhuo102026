She Makes the Money. He Spoils Her Rotten.

One of the most obvious changes after they started dating: Julian began playing favorites in very small places.

Eve used to think he was gentle, sure, but evenly gentle. Gentle with sick animals, with guests, patient enough with colleagues who asked the same question three times. The kind of gentle that looked like a factory setting.

Now she could see it wasn't even.

He just hadn't opened the special lane before.

Special lane open, the difference was criminal. She kept catching herself staring at some tiny thing he'd done and thinking, wait, this is for me? Like a person who'd been using the public bathroom and suddenly got handed a keycard.

It made her self-conscious in a way that was almost funny. She would be mid-sentence with a guest and feel the warmer in her pocket and lose the next word. She would open the fridge and see the yogurt and have to close it again, just to breathe.

Cold days, the shop full, she'd get too busy to think about herself. Hands red from washing. Smile stuck on. A bichon on the floor who would not hold still.

The floor was always a little cold near the door. Her knees knew it. The bichon knew nothing except that tape was a game.

He'd slip a hand warmer into her hands from behind. Already cracked, already hot.

"Take it."

His voice was close. Not a performance. Just a delivery.

"Where did this come from?"

"Grabbed it."

"When did you grab it?"

"When you were on the floor measuring a bichon's neck."

She looked down. The warmer had a cartoon cat on it. Of course it did. She wanted to ask if he'd bought it today or if he'd been carrying it around like a man preparing for winter in July. She didn't. She put it between her palms and went back to the bichon, who immediately tried to eat the measuring tape.

The heat climbed into her fingers slowly. Dumpling, from the cat tree, watched the whole transfer like a security camera. He did not need a warmer. He had fur. He still looked offended that the gift was not fish.

Or a new batch of sample cat cans came in, and she only said, "This packaging is cute." She meant the color. Pale pink, stupid little ribbon. The next day her fridge had a row of the yogurt she liked. Same color. Lined up like he'd measured the shelf.

The fridge light was too bright. The yogurts looked like a small, ridiculous army. She stood there in socks and felt, very clearly, that she was being dated on purpose.

Reason:

"I thought it matched the color."

She stared at the yogurt. "Julian, did you secretly take a class on how to date?"

"No."

"Then how are you this good at it?"

"Maybe because it's you."

She lost, on the spot. No comeback. She stood in front of the open fridge with cold air on her knees and thought, that's illegal, you can't just say that while I'm holding a spoon.

Dumpling jumped onto the counter behind them, inspected a yogurt lid, and decided humans were collecting the wrong kind of cans. He sat down anyway. If there was going to be a scene, he would supervise.

She used to think sweet talk was optional. Have it, fine. Don't, also fine. People who collected lines made her tired.

Now she knew it wasn't that sweet talk was powerful.

It was a man who usually sounded like he was reading a chart, suddenly meaning it when he flirted. That was lethal. No extra words. No "baby." Just the facts, aimed.

She ate the yogurt standing up, because sitting would have given the sentence more room to live.

Of course she wasn't just being spoiled.

On the making-money side, she'd unlocked something. After the shop opened, she turned the scattered services into clearer packages and a booking flow, and built a few small events people actually loved. Not because she was a genius. Because she kept watching who lingered, who asked twice, who left looking like they'd put something down.

Bring-Your-Pet Icebreakers.

First Cat Survival Class.

Pet Family Emotional Care Day.

And one Su Li named, as a friend:

"You Can Be Heartbroken, Just Don't Scare the Cat" recovery salon.

Director Chen went silent first. He held his thermos with both hands, like the name might splash.

"Is that a little too direct?"

Eve looked at the signup count, delighted. The sheet was already a mess of handwriting.

"Direct is good. Direct works."

Day one, full.

Almost all women, twenty-five to thirty-five. Some with cats, some alone, some saying "I've moved on" with red eyes and a laugh that didn't stick. One woman introduced her cat as "the only man who didn't leave," then immediately apologized to the cat.

The shop smelled like tea and someone else's shampoo. Chairs scraped. A carrier zipper kept getting stuck. Dumpling made a slow circuit of the room, offering himself as a prop and a judge. He sat on one empty chair until a woman asked, politely, if she could have it. He left. He did not hurry.

Standing in front of them, Eve suddenly understood why she liked this more and more.

A lot of the time, people didn't come for a standard answer. They didn't want a five-step plan. They wanted one night, in a room of people, to be understood a little. To say the ugly sentence out loud and have someone nod instead of handing them a tissue and a lecture.

She talked less than she thought she would. She asked more. The room did the rest. Someone laughed at the wrong moment and then apologized, and three other people said no, that was the right moment, and Eve felt the shop do the thing it was built for.

After, a girl with red eyes came and hugged her. Cheap perfume. A little shake in the arms.

"Thank you," she said, quiet. "Today was the first time I felt like I'm not actually that much of a mess."

Eve hugged her. The back of her throat went tight, which was annoying. She hated crying at work. She also wasn't going to shove this girl off.

"You were never a mess."

The girl's cat, in a carrier by their feet, meowed once, like agreement, or like a request to go home. Dumpling flicked an ear from the desk. Peer review complete.

When everyone had gone, the shop smelled like tea and cat litter and someone else's leftover tissues. Julian stood in the doorway looking at her. Not the clinic look. The looking-at-her look.

The leftover chairs were still in a crooked circle. Eve's voice was a little gone. Her feet hurt in a specific, event-day way.

"What?" She walked over. Her feet hurt. "You're staring."

"Nothing," he said. "Just thinking my girlfriend is really good at this."

She'd still been in the leftover feeling from the hug. "My girlfriend" landed somewhere stupid, behind her teeth. She had to swallow before she could talk.

The door chime was quiet. The street outside was ordinary. She was not.

"Julian."

"Mm?"

"You've been really into that title lately."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because I couldn't say it out loud before."

Heat climbed her neck. She pushed his shoulder, not hard.

"When did you get so good at saying this stuff?"

He caught her wrist, lightly, and drew her in a little. The shop was empty. The door chime was quiet. She could hear the fridge.

His fingers were warm. The warmer in her pocket had gone cool hours ago. This was better. Worse. Both.

"I'm not good at it."

"Then what is it?"

"I can finally say it."

She looked up at him and thought the shop lights were a crime. Cheap warm bulbs. They still made him look like that. Gentle in a way that wasn't a performance. She wanted to make a joke. The joke got stuck.

Dumpling jumped onto the desk, sat, and stared at them with the patience of a person waiting for a meeting to end so he could go home. He licked one shoulder. Very loud. Nobody asked him to stop.

She leaned in anyway. "Julian."

"Mm?"

"If you spoil me like this, is it because you want me stuck with you later?"

He looked at her, half a second of quiet, then, low:

"Yes."

No smile. No follow-up. Just yes, like a diagnosis.

This man...

Used to be shut like a clam. Now every sentence was target-fed. She was about to tell him that was unfair, that she needed a minute, that her brain was still trying to catch up to her mouth—

The door chime rang.

A young guy burst in with a cat carrier, face full of terror. Hair wrecked. Shoes on the wrong urgency setting.

"Help! My cat stole my proposal ring!"

Two seconds of quiet in the shop.

Eve and Julian looked at each other, and laughed at the same time. Not polite shop-laugh. The ugly kind, the kind you do when the universe refuses to let a moment finish.

She wiped her eye with the back of her wrist, still laughing.

Love and work both thriving, and the cursed-client stories had never clocked out. Of course they hadn't. This shop did not do quiet endings.

Dumpling stood up on the desk, stretched, and looked at the carrier with professional interest.

A colleague had arrived.

With drama.

And, apparently, jewelry.

He jumped down to meet it. Chief of Hospitality, back on the clock, already judging the human's panic and the cat's likely filing system.

Eve still had Julian's "yes" sitting in her ears. She put it away, carefully, like a thing she would open later, after the ring, after the laugh, after the shop remembered it was a shop.
