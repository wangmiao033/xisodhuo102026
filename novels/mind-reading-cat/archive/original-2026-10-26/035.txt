


On the twins' first birthday, they closed the shop early on purpose.

The bell over the door got a rest. Eve taped a handwritten note to the glass. Closed for a family thing. See you tomorrow. Someone still tried the handle anyway, then laughed, then left. That felt right. The shop had taught people to show up. Today the showing up was at home.

Eve and Julian didn't throw a big party. Just the closest people, a table of food at home, a few balloons, a soft mat in the living room. One balloon was already dying in the corner. She left it. Honest decor.

The apartment smelled like cake and fruit and the wet-wipe industrial complex that now lived under the sink. Sun came in sideways, catching dust and frosting. Director Chen's thermos had claimed a coaster. Tang's phone was already out, then put away, then out again because Su Li said if you miss the smash-cake you will never be forgiven.

The brother was already crawling everywhere, trying to put whatever he grabbed in his mouth.

A napkin. A block. Someone's slipper, which Eve found personally offensive for reasons that were old now. Julian kept intercepting with the calm of a man who had spent a year on duty and still had not processed the happiness, only learned its logistics.

The sister was quieter, sitting on the mat with Dumpling's tail in her arms, studying it with great seriousness. Like a report. Like the tail had failed an audit.

Dumpling had long since been promoted from Chief of Hospitality to Family Elder.

A little fatter. More opinions.

The extra weight looked like retirement that had refused to retire. He sat where he could see the door, the cake, and the two small humans, which was three jobs, which he considered the correct number.

Weirdly, he had extra patience for the two kids.

The brother pulled his whiskers; he only made a face.

The face was tragic. It was also permission. Eve had seen him walk away from adult hands for less.

The sister yanked his tail; he only looked back, like a helpless, kind old cadre. Eve had seen that look on Director Chen. She did not say so.

"He's going to bill us for this later," Eve said.

"He already does," Julian said. "The expensive cans."

"That's the pension."

Su Li sat on the sofa, eating cake, sighing.

"These two really did pick the best features."

Director Chen, immediately: "Right. The boy has a little of Julian, the girl has more of Eve."

He said it like a quarterly comparison. Then he looked at the brother, who was trying to eat a balloon ribbon, and added, "The energy, though. That's not Julian."

"That's Eve," Su Li said.

"Hey," Eve said.

Tang added: "But the way the girl looks at people is especially Dumpling."

Everybody paused, then laughed.

The sister, as if on cue, gave Dumpling's tail one more official tug and stared at Tang, unblinking. Tang held up her hands. "See."

Eve came over with a fruit plate, about to say don't talk nonsense, and saw the sister let go of Dumpling's tail and turn toward the balcony.

A sparrow had landed on the rail, chirping twice.

Ordinary bird. Ordinary rail. Ordinary late sun on the neighbor's drying shirts. The chirp was small, busy, the kind of sound you stopped hearing if you lived in the city long enough.

The sister blinked, then opened her mouth, milky:

"It said Daddy's dumb."

The living room went still.

Everybody froze. A fork stopped. A balloon made a tiny rubber sound, which felt rude.

Julian had been bringing soup. His hands stopped.

The soup did not spill. He was still a person who did not spill, even now. His face, though, did the rare thing of going completely blank.

"What?"

She sat on the mat, little face up, and repeated it, very serious.

Not performing. Not looking for a laugh. The same seriousness she used on Dumpling's tail.

"The bird said Daddy's so dumb, he almost dropped the spoon."

Right after, the sparrow chirped twice more on the balcony.

The sister nodded, naturally, like she was answering it.

Eve went rigid.

She turned, slowly, toward Dumpling.

He was sitting at the edge of the rug, tail quiet around his paws.

He was not surprised.

There was even a that's-finally-here calm in those round eyes.

Like he'd always known this day would come. Not magical. Administrative. A meeting that had been on the calendar. He blinked once. The sparrow, outside, fluffed itself and looked unrepentant.

A lot of leftover pictures flipped up out of memory.

A rain night, a cat looking up at her.

The way he kept clearing mines, screening people, tapping her on the shoulder.

The sign at the wedding. The bed in the middle of the night. The socks circled by a tail. The circling of the door while she was in the hospital. Like he knew a lot, and never said it.

And now her daughter was on the mat, earnestly repeating a sparrow's review of her father, as natural as if she'd heard the most ordinary sentence in the world.

Su Li got there first. Her fork almost hit the floor.

"Eve..."

"I know."

Eve's voice was a little floaty. She heard it. She hated it. She also couldn't make it land normal.

Director Chen's eyes went huge. "This, this kid..."

He looked at the sparrow, then at the sister, then at the thermos, as if one of the three might issue an official statement.

Tang already had a hand over her mouth, about to bounce off the sofa.

"I told you," Tang whispered. "The looking. I told you the looking."

Julian, after the first blank second, was actually the first to settle.

He set the soup down, walked over, crouched in front of the sister. Same crouch as the bathroom with the test, she thought, and then told herself to shut up. His voice came out careful, not scared. Curious, even.

"You can hear it talk?"

She nodded, milky: "Yeah."

"What else did it say?"

She tipped her head, thought, and pointed at Dumpling.

The pointing was very accurate. Dumpling did not dodge.

"The kitty said, you finally noticed."

The room went fully quiet.

Even the brother stopped crawling, as if the air had changed classification. The sparrow chirped once more, then left, work done.

The next second Eve laughed until she cried.

Laughing, covering her face, shoulders shaking. Not a speech. Just the laugh that comes when a joke you've been living inside finally gets a punchline, and the punchline is your one-year-old. It came out of her in waves, ugly and happy, the kind of laugh that had rain in it, and a shop in it, and a wooden sign that said officiating cat.

"I knew it." She looked at Dumpling, voice catching. "I knew there was something wrong with this cat."

He blinked, slow.

Same expression as always.

Like he was looking at a human who had, at last, caught up.

Julian laughed too. Quiet. He didn't try to explain it. He picked the sister up, then scooped the brother, who was still trying to crawl toward the cake, and finally looked at Eve.

Warm living-room light. Balloons moving. Friends still stuck in the shock. Su Li had not put the fork down. Director Chen had both hands on his thermos, as if the thermos could explain birds.

The sister, from his arm, looked at Dumpling again, then at the empty rail, then patted her father's cheek with a sticky hand, as if to say the review had been fair.

The smile in his eyes was deep, and quiet.

"Eve."

"Mm?"

"Looks like this house is going to get even louder."

She looked at him, at the two kids, at the fat cat sitting to the side like he'd seen every dynasty.

She'd had a terrible stretch too.

Laid off, rain, a cat in her arms, life feeling like it had caved in on one side.

She wasn't going to make a thesis out of it. The thesis would sound fake. The actual thing was messier: a kid translating a sparrow, cake on a plate, her husband holding too many people at once, Dumpling looking bored on purpose.

A mind-reading cat. A little girl who can hear animals. Fine. Add it to the household rules.

Eve walked over, kissed the sister, and rubbed Dumpling's head.

His skull was warm. He allowed it.

"Alright," she said, smiling. "From now on, this house is in the hands of you two intelligence officers."

The sister didn't really get it, and clapped anyway.

Dumpling flicked his tail, noble.

Like he was, reluctantly, accepting the new family post. Or like he'd been in the post the whole time, and the humans were late to the org chart.

Night outside, gentle. Lights on, bright.

The brother finally got a bit of cake, cream all over his face, laughing.

The sister sat in her dad's arms, answering the birds on the balcony now and then. Small nods. Very busy.

Eve leaned on Julian's shoulder, and said, quiet:

"Look."

"Mm?"

"I think we actually ended up in a fairy tale."

He bent and kissed her temple.

"Not a fairy tale."

"Then what is it?"

"It's our days."

She smiled. Her eyes curved with it.

Yeah.

Not a fairy tale.

Just the days they'd yelled through, laughed through, and lived, seriously, one day at a time. Plus a sparrow with opinions, which she was going to have to get used to.

And days like that, there would be a lot more years of.

She stole a bit of cake off the brother's plate. He yelled. Dumpling watched the cake change hands, filed a complaint with his eyes, and did not move. Elder status. He had people for that now.
