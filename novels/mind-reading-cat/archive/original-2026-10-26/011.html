After the storm passed, the account actually grew.

DMs piled up.

How do I help a shy cat. How do I deal with a dog dying. Can we book that "companionship" thing you did with Cream.

Eve put the questions into a spreadsheet and felt her pulse pick up the longer she looked. Rows. Tags. Repeat questions in different wording, which was how you knew a market wasn't a mood.

The market was real.

The need was real.

Not a fever dream she'd talked herself into on a night of instant noodles and too much pride.

She read one DM twice, then a third time, because the person had typed with shaking punctuation. A shy cat under a bed. An owner who had already watched three videos and still didn't know whether to wait or crawl in. Eve knew that feeling. She had been that person with Dumpling, except her version had involved a mint pot and a lot of pretending she was fine.

She color-coded until the colors looked like a mood board for a person who was trying not to hope. Shy-cat questions in one column. Grief in another. Booking requests in a third, which she kept renaming because "money" felt too loud and "help" felt too soft. Dumpling walked across the keyboard twice. She undid both times. He sat on her notes after that, which was harder to undo.

Saturday night the clinic closed early. Director Chen got dragged out for drinks by an old classmate. The front-desk girls clocked out on time, which almost never happened, and they celebrated it like a holiday. The lobby was rarely this quiet. Warm light. The fish tank humming. Everything soft around the edges, like someone had turned the volume down on the whole building.

Eve sat behind the desk with her laptop. Dumpling was already there, occupying a patch of counter that was not a bed and had become one. His fur caught the lamp. He looked expensive in a way that had nothing to do with money and everything to do with taking up space on purpose.

Julian was brushing him beside her. Long strokes. Dumpling had gone boneless, which was a professional courtesy he did not extend to everyone.

The brush made a small, steady sound. Plastic on fur. Somewhere a fridge kicked on. Eve could hear herself thinking, which was rude of her brain. She watched Julian's wrist, then the spreadsheet, then the fish, then back, like a person switching tabs in real life.

It was so quiet it almost looked like a preview of married life.

The thought jumped into her head and scared her. Married. The word didn't even belong in a clinic lobby. They were two people and a cat and a laptop that needed charging. She told herself that. Her brain, unhelpfully, supplied a grocery list anyway. She drank some water, fast. The water went down wrong. She coughed, which was not mysterious, just embarrassing.

Dumpling opened one eye, offended by the noise, then closed it again. The brush did not stop. Julian's other hand steadied Dumpling's hip like the cat might slide off the counter from sheer luxury.

"What are you thinking about?"

His voice, from the side. Not looking at her. Still brushing.

"I'm thinking..." She paused, then told the truth, because lying would have required a better brain than she had after a week of comment moderation. "Whether we should actually do this. For real."

"Do what?"

"Pet companionship. Owner support. Emotional care. Maybe light social stuff later." She turned the laptop. The screen glow hit his glasses. "Look. A lot of the DMs this week are the same kind of thing, and a lot of it doesn't belong in a regular vet visit. People aren't asking for surgery. They're asking what to do at 2 a.m. when the cat hides and their brain goes to the worst video they ever watched."

He looked at the table.

She'd split it fine.

First-time pet guidance. Shy-cat settling. Family-and-pet dynamics. Grief support. Pre-adoption consults. Trial social events...

This was not a whim.

She was already building a frame. Color-coded, even, which she would not admit was for comfort.

"I've been thinking," she said, quieter. "A lot of people don't keep animals as animals. They're family. Company. An outlet. And when something goes wrong, help is limited. A clinic can treat the body. It can't treat all the panic. I know that sounds like a pitch. It's also just... true. I was the panic, a month ago. I still am, some mornings."

She said it to the screen. Not loud.

Like she was talking about a market.

Like she was talking about herself.

After she got fired, she hadn't known where to put her feet. Dumpling, these people, Julian: they'd brought her back down to the ground, little by little. Wet socks. A mint pot. A 380-kuai transfer. None of it was a five-year plan. All of it was a floor.

She picked at a corner of a sticky note. The adhesive had given up. "I keep thinking if we don't name it, it'll stay a cute idea. Cute ideas don't survive rent. Cute ideas become content, and then people get tired, and then it's just another account that used to be nice."

"I know it's not small." She pressed her lips together. "We might not make money at first. We might even lose it. The shop isn't a thing yet. I'm not saying we rent a place tomorrow and hang a sign. But if we do it, I want to do it properly. Not as content. As a real thing people can book."

A few seconds of quiet in the lobby. The tank bubbled. Dumpling's purr skipped, then came back.

He set the brush down.

"Eve."

"Mm?"

"I want to too."

She froze. Finger on the trackpad. The spreadsheet waited, rude. Want. He said it like it was simple. Like he hadn't just lined it up next to her spreadsheet and made it real. She could hear the fish tank. She could hear her own swallow. She could not, for a second, remember what cell she had been about to click.

He looked at her, steady.

"Not because the account blew up, and not because it looks like an opportunity now," he said. "I've always thought people and their pets shouldn't have to wait until somebody's sick to get seen. The sick part is my job. The rest of it just... sits there. Until it becomes the sick part."

She watched him. They kept landing in the same place without having to explain it. That was rarer than chemistry. Chemistry she didn't trust. This she almost did.

The fluorescent light over the waiting chairs buzzed once and settled. She wanted to make a joke. The joke didn't show up. Her mouth felt unused to agreeing with a future that had a door.

"Then..." She sat up slowly. The chair squeaked. "We could start with a trial?"

"We could."

"Low cost first. Weekend bookings, small salons, sharing sessions..."

"We could."

"If it works, look for a small storefront. No need to go huge overnight. No investors with brothers. Just... a door, maybe. Later."

"Also fine."

Halfway through, she laughed first. It came out of her nose, inelegant.

"Julian, why is everything 'we could'?"

"Because you're making sense."

"What if I stop making sense later?"

He glanced at her.

"Then I'll tell you."

"How?"

"The same way I tell you not to feed the cat ham sausage."

She couldn't help it. She laughed until she was on the desk, forehead almost on the laptop, the kind of laugh that had been waiting since the rumor week and had not been invited to the statement post.

"That's not a system," she got out. "That's nagging."

"It's a protocol," he said, very serious, which made it worse.

Dumpling looked between them, confused, then jumped onto the counter and lay down right in the middle, like a professional. Full stretch. One paw on the keyboard. The spreadsheet gained a new character that was not a business term.

Eve scratched his belly.

Julian smoothed his back.

Their hands met in the fur. Very light.

It still went up her fingers like static. She pulled her hand back and pretended to be very serious about a knot behind Dumpling's ear that did not exist. He didn't speak either. His breathing might have skipped. She did not check. Checking would have been a choice. She counted to three in her head, the way she used to count before sending a client email she hated. One. Two. Three. The static did not take the hint. Dumpling's ear flicked, bored of being a cover story.

The fur was warm. The knot was fake. She kept her eyes on Dumpling's ear like it had a diagnosis. Somewhere in the tank, a fish turned, unimpressed.

Dumpling squinted, then bit the brush, gently, and dragged it off the counter.

If they wanted to use him as a table, they could pick the brush up themselves. He had already been brushed. The next item on his list was dinner, and nobody in this lobby was moving fast enough.

Eve picked the brush up. Julian took it from her, automatically, the way he took things that were about to fall. Their fingers touched again. Shorter this time. She still felt it.

"Saturday," she said, to the laptop, not to him. "We try Saturday."

"Saturday," he said.

She made a note in the spreadsheet that was not really a note: trial, six people, no cameras, Dumpling not for rent. Then she saved the file twice, because once didn't feel like enough for a future.

On the bus home, Dumpling heavy in the carrier, the city lights smeared past the window like someone had dragged a wet thumb across the night. She kept seeing the lobby. Warm light. A brush on the floor. A man saying I want to too, without making it a speech.

She whispered, into the carrier, "We're actually doing this."

Dumpling did not comment. Doing this, in his view, still started with dinner.
