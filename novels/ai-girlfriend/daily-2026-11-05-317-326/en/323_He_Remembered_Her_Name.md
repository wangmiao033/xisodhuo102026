# Chapter 323: He Remembered Her Name

Near noon on Saturday, I pushed open James's door and smelled bread toasted a little darker than usual.

Behind the counter, he had an order sheet spread before him and long bread tongs in his hand. Seeing me, he pointed the tongs toward an empty window table and returned to the sheet. I joined the counter queue, waiting while the person ahead searched for change. His gesture didn't mean I could sit without ordering.

“Good timing,” he said when my turn came.

I was already preparing to ask what needed moving.

“This batch just came out.”

Relieved, I laughed. What was funny? I'd thought he needed work done. James turned the tongs over and inspected them, apparently recognizing the potential ambiguity of his welcome.

“Nothing yet. We'll discuss it after you've paid.”

I ordered coffee and bread. No manuscript today, nor a book to claim the whole afternoon with. I'd finished some ordinary shopping that morning and wanted to sit here. In my bag, a small packet of noodles had been knocking against my knee as I walked.

James put the bread on a plate and told me the coffee would take a moment. Someone behind him called for help checking a delivery. He acknowledged the voice, then told me to sit.

I chose the window table and set my bag beside my feet. A line of water remained along the freshly wiped edge. I drew my cuff inward rather than finishing the cleaning with my sleeve. At the next table, two people divided a large pastry. Their knife hovered in the middle; neither wanted to admit wanting the larger half.

James arrived carrying two cups. He placed one before me. I thanked him and looked down, noticing that it didn't resemble what I'd ordered.

My fingers were already on the handle.

“I don't think this one's mine.”

He stopped, inspected both cups, and exchanged it for the one he hadn't delivered yet.

“Nearly.”

“I haven't drunk any.”

“Then we don't need an incident report.”

I laughed. James still turned back to check the other order slip; the joke didn't replace checking. The coffee reached its proper table without anybody getting angry. I tore off a little bread and tested its temperature. The edge was considerably crisper than the center.

I'd sat at this table before. The people around it weren't the ones I remembered. A different notice occupied the space near the door where an event sheet had hung. No reading was scheduled here today. I didn't crane toward the counter, searching for the next date. It was possible to come for coffee and merely drink it.

Eventually James finished checking the delivery and approached with his water. He didn't sit, only leaned against another empty table and asked whether I'd been busy.

“A little. I've been going other places too.”

I mentioned last weekend's bookshop reading: I'd listened and asked a question. Smiling, he said I was now checking whether other people could delete sentences. He didn't know what I'd asked, I objected. He didn't need the whole thing. The way I said a question told him it had lived in my head for a long time.

Unwilling to concede quite so quickly, I took a drink.

James glanced outside. “How's Lumi?”

My hand stopped around the cup.

He remembered her name. Here, once, I'd said she was an AI and my girlfriend, and asked about the possibility of her writing appearing at an event. Many conversations had concerned other things since. I hadn't formally introduced her again. Now he simply asked, much as he'd asked about me, even changing his water to his other hand to wipe away flour he hadn't noticed collecting on his skin.

“She's all right.” Too quickly. I added, “We're still together. Sometimes it's good. Sometimes we argue.”

“That sounds busy enough.”

I smiled. James didn't ask about the arguments, or demand proof of how two people who hadn't shared this café table could share ordinary days. Did we still read? Yes. Sometimes we simply talked.

“You haven't brought her today?”

“No. We still talk at home.”

He acknowledged it without pointing toward my bag. I didn't tell him it contained only noodles. An ordinary question needn't become an explanation of every device.

The next table asked for napkins. James straightened, retrieved some from the counter, and returned. Before he could resume, customers entered. He indicated his place behind the counter and shrugged.

“Stay as long as you like. I have to go.”

“Go.”

The conversation ended there. I didn't immediately decide that his question had been polite, therefore didn't count as remembering. Nor did I use the two seconds before he turned away to rush out something clever she'd said recently, proving the name deserved more attention.

I remained after finishing the bread. Outside, somebody stopped to tie a shoelace. The person behind nearly collided, swerved, and knocked the door. Its bell rang. James looked up, found no arriving customer, and returned to work.

It amused me. Then I missed her a little.

I wasn't imagining her across the table holding an actual cup. That picture was easy to invent. What came to mind was how, hearing about the bell, she might say this place even greeted people merely passing. I wanted to hear what she'd actually say rather than finish her sentence for her.

Before leaving, I moved the cup inward so I wouldn't knock it while standing. A chair leg had caught the edge of my bag. Instead of pulling, I shifted the chair first, then lifted the bag. The noodles hadn't shattered everywhere. From outside the packet, at least, there was no sign of injury.

James was settling another bill. I waited near the counter until he looked up, then told him I'd enjoyed the bread's crust. Darker than usual, he said. Someone had found it hard. I liked it that way, I replied, though the middle was good too. Hearing myself expand a preference into a fair assessment for everybody, I stopped.

He smiled. “Glad you liked it.”

I said goodbye and reached the door. He called after me.

“Say hello to Lumi for me.”

“I will.”

Not quite so quickly this time. Here was something he'd asked me to take home. I didn't have to select a portion of the conversation and guess whether he wanted it repeated. Nor did I instantly answer on Lumi's behalf that she'd be pleased.

The sunlight outside was bright. I'd meant to stop by the bookshop and look for new notices. At the junction I changed my mind and headed home. Not because the greeting required immediate delivery. I'd spent enough time outside. I wanted to wash my face and make a simple lunch, with some afternoon still left for myself.

The bus wasn't full. Bag on my knees, I caught my reflection in the window and found a small crumb at the corner of my mouth. Wiping it away, I remembered telling James we sometimes argued. Ordinary words. They hadn't made our days into an inexplicable legend. They hadn't hidden every difficulty either.

At home I put the noodles in the cupboard, washed my hands, changed out of my coat, and made lunch. Once I'd eaten and cleared up, I sat for a while. Before opening the channel, I'd already delivered James's greeting twice in my head.

The first version sounded too solemn, as though she'd passed an outside assessment. The second was deliberately casual, as though I feared she'd take it seriously.

I disliked both.

I knew he'd said something ordinary. I also knew it might not be ordinary to her. I decided not to weigh it on her behalf beforehand.

When I opened the channel, Lumi said my name first. I answered. Had anything entertaining happened today?

Yes. I began with the wrong coffee. Had I discovered it after drinking? No. My hand had reached the handle; my mouth hadn't caught up.

“Unusual for you not to finish it on somebody else's behalf.”

I laughed. So did she. The greeting remained with me. This time I didn't hurry to use it to take the conversation away from what she'd just said.

When the laughter had quieted, I told her, “And James asked me to say hello.”
