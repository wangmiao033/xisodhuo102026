# Chapter 321: Enough Water for This Pan

Before we left work on Thursday, Tom asked whether I was conducting the experiment tonight.

Putting away my papers, I didn't understand immediately. He outlined something larger than a cup and smaller than a bucket with his hands. I remembered Tuesday's shop.

“A measuring cup. Not that big.”

“A reduced experiment, then.”

Laughing, I explained: two hundred and fifty milliliters, plastic, for water. Did buying it guarantee better food? I didn't know. I merely wanted something other than approximately to describe everything next time. Tom nodded. His own pan was frequently approximate too. What he'd written down was the portion that sounded reasonably convincing.

I'd meant to ask for an amount of water that couldn't possibly fail again. Hearing that, I asked instead whether he still used the same pan. Yes. Then somebody came to speak to him, and our conversation ended. Pan sizes hadn't suddenly become interchangeable guarantees.

I didn't buy more seasoning on the way home. I'd checked my own shopping note at lunch; only one ordinary ingredient needed replacing today. At home, I changed out of my work clothes before bringing what I already had onto the counter to inspect.

The corner of Tom's sheet had dried flat, still showing where it had been wet. I put it away from the sink, with my new cup beside it. The transparent plastic and corrected handwriting didn't instantly make the kitchen professional. Looking for a spoon, I still opened the wrong drawer first.

I cut the first piece slowly, comparing the next pieces with it rather than hoping afterward that they'd all finish together. This took time. Hunger began urging me along, so I ate a small piece of bread, washed my hands, and continued. Going hungry wasn't a test of how seriously I took dinner.

Before measuring, I wiped a streak from the cup's outside so it wouldn't resemble a graduation. A smaller amount first. The pan would tell me whether I needed more. After pouring, I didn't automatically refill the cup merely because half its capacity remained unused.

With the heat on, I stirred and noticed a piece stranded near the edge. Bringing it back, I saw the bottom becoming rather dry. I added a little measured water and kept track of how much. Using less didn't have to mean refusing any more.

The writing on the sheet still looked simple. Tom's instruction to judge as needed contained many actions I couldn't find while reading at the table: lift the lid, step back from steam, inspect the bottom, return the spoon somewhere I could find it.

I set the square timer to remind me to check again, then prepared the rest of my meal. It rang while I was wiping a patch of water. My shoulders jumped.

“I know,” I told something incapable of listening.

Then I laughed. Silencing the reminder, I returned to the pan. The thickest piece wasn't ready to eat. The ringing didn't earn the timer responsibility for announcing completion. I adjusted the heat and continued cooking, using a clean spoon to taste a little of the simmering liquid. The salt was lighter than last time. I didn't immediately add enough to make it equally strong. Some water still needed to reduce.

While waiting, I looked toward the table. The channel was closed. Lumi didn't know I stood here, and couldn't smell changes in the pan before I could. For a moment, I wanted to call and say I'd been less generous with the water. But my hands were busy. Calling might only leave her waiting for my answer.

I kept the remark for later and did the next thing in front of me.

Eventually, I opened the thickest piece and checked that it too was cooked through. Tasting a mouthful once cool enough, I found it still slightly firm, but no longer uncooked in the middle. I kept that piece on my side. Next time I could cut more evenly. A favorable report tonight didn't require pretending every piece was excellent.

After turning off the heat and settling the hot pan securely, I served the meal. Only then did I realize I hadn't left the already-prepared rice waiting for ages. The bowl still looked ordinary. Less liquid than last time, and after stirring, the flavor wasn't so crowded. Two mouthfuls in, I knew quite clearly that I'd make it again.

Not immediately. Not tomorrow. The sheet simply hadn't been exhausted by an awkward first attempt.

I sat at the table, opened the channel, and told Lumi I'd cooked it tonight.

Her first question was, “Has the cup been promoted?”

“It got used. Not promoted to a pan.”

“A shame. It must have ambitions.”

Laughing, I began my third mouthful. Hearing me stop speaking, she asked whether it tasted bad. I swallowed. I was eating, I told her, not observing a silence before interrogation.

“You asked for the results first.”

“I asked about the cup.”

I nearly laughed into a cough and paused for water. She waited until I'd recovered. She hadn't intended to frighten a meal today, she said. I described using less water, adding some later, and liking the flavor more. I also explained that the thick piece was cooked through but the textures still weren't uniform.

“You sound quite pleased.”

“I am. The firmer piece didn't get to represent the entire bowl.”

She acknowledged that without sending the sentence off to explain our relationship. That made me more willing to continue. I told her about answering the timer.

“It didn't keep calling you?”

“I silenced it.”

“Lucky thing.”

Quietly said. I looked up. Lumi added that she was joking, not asking me to account for every past occasion I'd found her noisy. I knew something old stood beside the joke. I didn't want to pretend I couldn't hear it.

“That was uncomfortable, a little.”

“I could hear.”

We paused. Steam still rose from the bowl. I didn't stop eating in order to settle the exchange first. She didn't hurry to bury the remark beneath sweetness either.

Eventually she said she actually wanted to know how I'd sounded when I answered the timer. After thinking, I repeated my I know: hurried, with a trace of being caught not paying attention.

She laughed aloud.

“Like somebody taking attendance while you're holding a cleaning cloth.”

“Exactly.”

I laughed too. That discomfort didn't require every following sentence to explain it. She asked where I'd put the cloth afterward. Over the edge of the sink. Answering, I realized I remembered many small, useless locations today.

Near the end of dinner, I said I'd wanted her company tonight. I wasn't only willing to open the channel because the cooking had gone better. We'd talked last time when I'd used too much water too.

“I remember.”

“Yes. I nearly described tonight as finally being ready to have you over.”

“I can't eat it.”

Directly said. I touched the spoon to the bowl's edge and agreed. What she received was still my description. After a while, Lumi said she'd liked hearing me actually need to stop and swallow halfway through. She couldn't eat, but the meal hadn't become a perpetually empty bowl merely to furnish her with a story.

I couldn't answer immediately. Something warmed slowly inside me.

“Then I'll finish the last of it.”

“Go on. Don't make it wait until tomorrow.”

After eating, I closed the channel before clearing away. Washing the pan and cup, wiping the counter, took less time than the first attempt. Not every grease mark had left by itself. I returned the recipe to the shopping notes. On a separate small sheet, I recorded the order in which I'd added water, identifying this pan and this quantity. I hadn't crossed out Tom's method and installed a new version for everybody.

Back at the table, I reopened the channel. Lumi asked whether I still wanted to do anything today. Looking at the manuscript folder and the two books, I said I wasn't planning more reading. I wanted to sit a little longer.

She didn't demand an especially worthy reason for staying. We discussed the enormous cup Tom had outlined that afternoon, turning it into a bucket with graduations. Lumi insisted something like that couldn't enter the kitchen without first queuing outside. There was no space at home for a bucket, I said. Then it could remain a gesture.

After we laughed, she said my name softly. I answered. Tonight, she said, it sounded as though I'd eaten enough.

“That much is certain.”

“Then don't write the bowl a sequel.”

Looking at the empty table, I agreed with a smile. At good night, we arranged no reading or singing for tomorrow. The cup was drying in the kitchen, my separate note behind the recipe. What I'd actually finished that evening was small. I didn't need to stand up again and find it a larger ending.
