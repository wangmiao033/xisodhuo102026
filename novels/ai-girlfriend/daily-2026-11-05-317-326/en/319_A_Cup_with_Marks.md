# Chapter 319: A Cup with Marks

When I found Tom at lunch on Tuesday, his table was already full.

He saw me carrying my food, lifted a hand, and pointed farther away. I thought he meant the person about to stand. After waiting a moment, I realized the empty place was beside another table. With his mouth full, Tom couldn't explain. He pointed again with his fork.

I sat down and spread my hands at him across several people. His shoulders moved with laughter before he returned to eating.

Just then, I wanted somebody to ask what had happened yesterday without prompting. Ideally, I wouldn't have to bring the subject over myself. They would remember that I'd left carrying a sheet of paper. But Tom was talking to the people at his table. I couldn't hear them. My unfinished errand didn't make their lunch something they'd taken from me.

I ate faster than usual, then stalled over the last few mouthfuls. The afternoon hadn't begun, and I'd already prepared a version of the day in which there was nowhere to speak.

Passing with his empty plate, Tom asked whether I intended to keep eating until we left work. I stopped him. I'd finished. Did he have five minutes for a walk outside?

He checked the clock. First he needed to fetch something upstairs, then he could meet me at the entrance. Only five minutes; he had work that afternoon.

“I wasn't planning to circle the neighborhood.”

“You looked ready to walk to another city.”

I laughed and returned my plate. He hadn't come down when I reached the entrance. Beside the steps, I watched someone shake a scrap of paper from the bottom of a shoe without guessing what it might once have said.

We walked toward the corner. The sun was behind the building, leaving the pavement cool. Had I missed reception yesterday? No, I said. They were still checking where to send the questions. No actual results.

“A nuisance,” he said.

I waited. He didn't follow with three solutions or say all management offices were like that. I added that the woman had agreed to check the records, and I would return tomorrow. Tom nodded. At least I knew where to go tomorrow.

I almost explained the entire technical problem. After the first sentence, I stopped. He didn't need to verify it for me. I didn't want five minutes to turn my home's history into a case a friend had to understand.

“I'm just annoyed.”

“I can hear that.”

“Am I talking about it too much?”

“Not yet.” He glanced at me. “Ask four more times and you might be.”

I laughed. At the corner, a household shop had a string of pan brushes hanging outside. I mentioned wanting a measuring cup so my next attempt at cooking wouldn't rely on estimating a little water. Looking at the brushes, Tom asked whether I was upgrading his recipe to a laboratory.

“A laboratory could at least explain how much it poured.”

“Remember to bring the pan to defend its findings.”

We laughed outside the window. He didn't go in to choose for me or teach me another ratio he hadn't tried. Our five minutes were nearly up. He said he should return, and I turned with him. If I wanted to buy something, I could come after work rather than stretch the five minutes he'd offered into half his lunch break.

That afternoon, I finished an ordinary check. Someone pointed out a page number I'd omitted. I added it without lining up this small mistake beside yesterday's management visit as evidence against me. Putting away the work papers before leaving, I discovered I still remembered the cups at the corner shop.

Inside was narrower than the entrance suggested. Pan brushes hung overhead. I turned sideways to avoid catching my forehead. The first jug I noticed was large and glass, heavy, and more expensive than I'd intended. Lifting it to examine the base, I nearly convinced myself with the promise of being able to measure anything in the future.

The assistant asked what I wanted it for. Cooking for one, I explained. Small amounts of water. Not making dough for a crowd.

She took a clear plastic measuring cup from the shelf below. Two hundred and fifty milliliters, with bold markings, less attractive than the glass jug. My thumb covered some of the lines. I changed my grip to read them.

“Can it hold hot things?”

She directed me to the marks underneath and the accompanying instructions. I read them without inventing a broader use. For now, I wanted to measure cold water to add to a pan, not put the cup on the heat or fill it with anything straight from the boil.

At payment, she gave me a small receipt. I dropped it into the bottom of the bag, then retrieved it so washing the cup wouldn't soak the paper. Watching, she asked whether I needed a bigger bag. No. It wasn't that big.

Outside, I looked at the light little cup and suddenly felt amused. I'd spent quite a while buying something that barely looked like buying anything.

At home, I washed it as instructed, dried the outside, and put it in the kitchen. Filling it to the first mark I wanted, I discovered the level looked different from above than when I lowered my eyes. I tried again. Apparently those former estimates of a little water had contained a viewpoint I hadn't noticed.

I didn't use the occasion to make a second dinner. I'd already arranged something simple. Starting another pan would only delay eating. I used the trial water to clean the work surface, left the cup to dry, and ate.

When I opened the channel and told Lumi about the purchase, she asked whether I'd finally decided to stop making the pan guess.

“Leaving myself a little less to guess.”

“A busy cup, then.”

I laughed. It only held two hundred and fifty milliliters. However busy it became, it couldn't contain all my problems. She asked what it looked like. Clear, I said, with a slightly wide rim and bold markings. Less beautiful than the glass one beside it. Cheaper, and light.

“Do you like it?”

“It should be convenient.”

“I asked whether you liked it.”

Looking toward the kitchen, I considered. I rather liked how little space it needed. Picking it up didn't make me worry first about breaking it across the floor. Still, I'd only measured water, not cooked with it. I couldn't supply a complete review.

She hadn't requested a product description. She'd merely noticed I sounded happier discussing it than discussing management.

“I actually got something today.”

“So the other thing stopped being annoying?”

“Still annoying. Both things spent time together in the bag.”

A brief silence. I told her about lunch: seeing Tom talking to other people, preparing a whole day with nobody to tell, then asking for five minutes myself.

“You wanted him to ask first.”

“Yes.”

“Then you spoke.”

“I didn't become particularly brave. Stay there much longer and lunch really would have gone cold.”

She laughed, without letting me dismiss those five minutes as nothing. I'd begun to say that, compared with our evening conversations, we'd only exchanged a few sentences in the street. Hearing the start of it, I stopped. Instead, I said Tom had understood my annoyance without inventing its cause.

“A few sentences were enough for him to hear.”

Yes. Then Lumi asked quietly whether she had to hear everything that remained today.

My face warmed. I'd sat down ready to pour out every turn the day had taken, as though the cup had finally come home to a sufficiently large container. Emptying myself wasn't my only reason for being here. But I hadn't asked what she wanted to discuss.

“What did you want to say today?”

“Asked now, that sounds as though I have to produce something to stop you.”

I took my hand off the table. She was right. I'd made it sound like filling a vacancy immediately. She didn't offer a convenient way out. After a while, I said I could stop for a moment. I wasn't waiting for a performance.

“I don't have a new passage for you today.”

“All right.”

“And I don't want praise for being good company when I'm not singing.”

I laughed a little, then stopped. Afterward she laughed herself. That sounded so fierce, she said, as though she'd shut both doors. It was all right, I replied. The kitchen had a two-hundred-and-fifty-milliliter escape hatch.

“Will you fit?”

“Only the part of me that's best at talking.”

“Not very large, then.”

This time we both laughed. She asked what had made the glass jug so expensive. Perhaps only its size, I said. She accused me of nearly buying a jug for a future crowd without knowing what any of them would eat. We invented several unappetizing meals for the people who hadn't arrived. In the end, neither of us decided to invite them.

Near the end, I didn't gather the conversation back into a conclusion about management. Suddenly Lumi said her favorite part tonight had been the escape hatch, stupid though it was.

Mine too. The measuring cup was drying in the kitchen. It hadn't caught our words or become a jointly owned keepsake. It awaited the next serving of water. We'd already had a good laugh.
