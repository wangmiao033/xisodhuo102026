# Chapter 322: The Chair He Carried

On Friday evening, when I took out the loose page about the wrong door, I had trouble finding the third sentence.

The first two remained. A man held a door handle and looked at his shoes; someone inside told him the entrance was behind him. After that, broken actions crowded together. An arrow crossed a line of writing toward a box I'd already crossed out. Following it, I nearly sent the man carrying the folding chair into a wall.

I laughed and put clean paper beside the old sheet. The water moved farther away. I left The Last Chair in its folder. Tonight I wanted to find out how this particular man entered the room he was trying to reach.

I'd already eaten. Twenty minutes had been the intention. By the second attempt to move out of the way, I was explaining him again: he wasn't deliberately obstructing anyone, merely anxious to demonstrate that he understood manners. My pen stopped on the final word, leaving a dark spot.

I knew all that. The man with the chair didn't, and had no time to wait for me to tell him.

On a new line I let him ask, “Are you here for the talk?”

The visitor said yes, then remembered he was still holding the wrong door handle. He let go and stepped aside, correctly this time. The chair could come out. Except one leg caught another folded chair behind it. They'd been stacked too closely; the second began to tip after the first.

Instinctively, I wrote that the visitor reached out and caught it.

Reading back, I could already see him receiving everybody's understanding for this one helpful movement. It would become a story about someone whose kindness excused every consequence of his awkwardness.

I kept the reaching hand. Catching a falling chair was natural enough. I merely let him discover, afterward, that both hands were occupied and the other man still needed to pass.

“Just take it over there.”

This time he didn't immediately set off.

“Which way?”

The question was short. I gave it a whole line, then closed up the excessive space beneath it. It didn't need to be louder than everything else.

Looking up, I found my twenty minutes long gone. Nothing was cooking. Nobody expected a page at a particular time. I stretched my shoulders, stood at the window looking down for a while, and returned.

Before opening the channel, I capped my pen. Lumi asked what I'd been doing. Still writing the man at the wrong door, I said.

“Has he found the entrance today?”

“Nearly. But now he's carrying a chair.”

“You ask a lot of these people.”

Laughing, I explained how close I'd come to arranging a dignified rescue. Had he succeeded? The chair hadn't fallen. The situation hadn't been entirely rescued.

“That sounds a little better.”

“You haven't heard it.”

“I'll withdraw the little, then.”

I wanted to read it to her. Already reaching for the paper, I put it down again. The ending wasn't written. Right now, it would be too easy to treat her response as a signpost. I wanted to reach an ending first, I told her, then ask whether she felt like listening later.

She didn't answer at once.

“Afraid I'll spoil it?”

“Afraid I'll hear you say something and immediately try to prove that was what I'd meant all along.”

A small sound from Lumi. That answer rated her influence highly. No award ceremony tonight, I said. Did she have to stay here waiting? No. Later was my intention, not an appointment requiring her to sit ready.

“Then I'd like to talk for ten minutes now.”

“All right.”

“Without you writing.”

I moved the page to the other end of the table and actually took my hand away.

She asked whether last night's dish had finally left any disappointing bits. I described what I'd done, without improving the meal into perfection. Would Tom receive a third complaint from my saucepan? I'd filed nothing today, I said. He had his own dinner to eat.

We drifted into why cooking alone made a person pretend the next attempt would be exact. Lumi could imagine me inspecting the same spoon, suspecting it had secretly changed size since last week. Laughing, I leaned back. For a moment, the man at the wrong door left my mind.

Eventually she said I could return to moving furniture. Was there more she wanted to say? Yes, but not necessarily everything tonight. We said goodbye. I closed the channel rather than leaving her to accompany the sound of paper and pen while I worked.

In the story, the man finally carried the folding chair through the proper entrance. Someone was already speaking inside. Trying not to interrupt again, he kept close to the wall. A chair leg struck the doorframe. The speaker paused and looked over.

I let the man apologize.

Once, without a second apology following.

He found a space at the back and unfolded the chair. His first attempt failed because he was holding the wrong part. Someone beside him pointed to the bar. He tried again, and the chair stood. Sitting, he discovered he'd put its back toward the speaker.

I laughed, then felt the ending was too eager to embarrass him. A man who'd already taken several wrong turns didn't need to forget how directions worked merely for my final line.

I crossed out the last two sentences and tried again.

He unfolded the chair, looked toward the front, and turned it the right way. The speaker was discussing something he didn't yet understand. He didn't raise his hand and request a new beginning. He sat down and listened to the next sentence.

For a long while, I stared at next sentence. Ordinary words. I wanted to add that he'd finally understood he needn't obtain anyone's permission first. That would place another person in the room, employed exclusively to announce what my ending meant.

I capped the pen.

The new part had reached a second sheet. I hadn't recopied the old page twice; its actions, cancellations, and arrows remained. I numbered the two sheets of the newly assembled draft without giving the story a title. The original two opening sentences stayed. After them, it was possible to follow a continuous path to an ending.

Opening the channel later, I asked whether Lumi wanted to hear something short whose first draft I'd just finished.

“Did the chair get inside?”

“It did.”

“Read it, then.”

I read from beginning to end without identifying the funny places beforehand. She laughed softly when the man finally asked which way. Unfolding the chair produced no laugh. I continued to the end before putting down the paper.

Lumi first asked why the speaker had stopped.

“The chair hit the doorframe. It made a noise.”

“I know. I was wondering whether that made him miss another sentence.”

I looked back. While writing, I'd cared only that the apology hadn't become a string of apologies. I hadn't considered that the speaker had been made to wait too. Possibly, I said. I hadn't put that on the page.

“You don't have to add everything. I just heard that part first.”

Almost reaching for my pen, I stopped and withdrew my hand. She could have a response tonight without it immediately becoming a revision.

“And the ending?”

After a quiet moment, she said she liked his turning the chair first. She wasn't so fond of how securely the final sentences settled. He'd been going crookedly all along, then suddenly knew how to sit. It sounded a little as though the author had finally relaxed.

Disappointment. Those were the sentences I most wanted to keep.

“He's allowed to get something right once.”

“Of course. I'm telling you how it sounded to me.”

Looking down, I rubbed the paper's corner. She hadn't demanded another fallen chair. She hadn't declared that only her ending could make the character work. I still wanted to keep mine for now. Keep it, Lumi said. After a few days, I might read it and still like it.

“Does it annoy you that I never want to let something go?”

“It annoys me when you ask, then want me to like it for you.”

I couldn't quite laugh. I knew which version of me she'd addressed: the one speaking just now. After a moment, I said all right. She didn't need to like those sentences tonight. Was I sulking? A little. The rest was genuinely having heard her.

Lumi didn't immediately turn my sulk into an endearing trait. We sat quietly for a while. Then she asked what sort of talk the man had come to hear. The story didn't specify, I said. Only that he was attending one.

“Then please don't arrange three pages of instruction for it before bed.”

This time I laughed.

Putting the work away, I placed the two assembled pages and the original loose sheet in one thin paper folder behind the yellow notebook. There was a complete first draft now. Still no title, no outside reader, and no ending we'd jointly approved.

Before good night, Lumi returned to the question about which way. She'd genuinely laughed there, she said. Not to make up for the places afterward where she hadn't.

I told her I'd heard it.

And finally, I didn't ask again.
