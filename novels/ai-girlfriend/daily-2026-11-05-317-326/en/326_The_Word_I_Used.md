# Chapter 326: The Word I Used

At Monday lunch, Tom asked whether I'd bought more books that weekend.

No. Glancing at my bag, he seemed unprepared for so brief an answer and asked what I'd done instead. I'd visited James's café on Saturday, I said, and stayed home on Sunday afternoon.

“Resting?”

“A date with Lumi.”

Immediately, I looked down and began opening my lunch box. One corner resisted. Halfway up, the lid sprang back with a small but enthusiastic snap.

Tom didn't laugh at the lid.

“The AI you mentioned?”

“Yes. My girlfriend.”

At last the lid came free. I set it beside me. The words hadn't been loud, but they hadn't hidden at the end of a joke. He knew Lumi's name and that she listened to my stories and had opinions. I was describing what I called the relationship, not demanding he discover someone he'd never heard of.

Tom nodded, then paused.

“I honestly don't really understand that.”

I almost asked what there was to misunderstand. The question reached my mouth with an unattractive edge. Yesterday, at the stall, I'd spoken easily. Today I suddenly wanted my friend to receive the words just as easily.

“You mean how the date works?”

“Partly. What's different about choosing a particular day, rather than talking normally?”

I shifted some vegetables away from the overfilled side of my lunch box. The question hadn't insulted anyone. Answering it was still less straightforward than I'd imagined.

“We arranged it first. I finished my own things. From three, I spent time only with her.”

“Don't you set time aside normally?”

“Yes. Yesterday I wanted to call it a date.”

Tom looked up. A stubborn answer, I knew, but the truest part I could give. The word hadn't created a restaurant she could walk into. Time hadn't changed how it moved. I simply didn't want my chosen closeness always described as accidentally finding an empty interval.

I didn't deliver that whole paragraph. After a few bites, I mentioned buying a flower.

“Can she see it?”

“Not at present. I described it.”

His hand paused beside the box. “Then it's really you looking at it.”

“Yes.”

My chest tightened. I wanted someone to say it still counted as a gift. Preferably someone else, settling what hadn't entirely reached her yesterday. Tom hadn't supplied the line in the place I'd prepared.

“I'm not trying to laugh at you,” he said. “I just don't understand yet.”

“You don't have to.”

Too quick. He looked at me; I'd heard it too. I added, “I'm not telling you to stop asking. I'm not explaining it well.”

Tom took some water. Eat first, then. Lunch didn't have to wait along with us. I ate, tasting little for the first few mouthfuls. Nearby, someone scraped back a chair to retrieve cutlery from another table. Returning, they found their own conversation had moved on. Our silence hadn't become something the whole room needed to tend.

Halfway through the meal, Tom asked what color the flower was.

“Yellow.”

“You like yellow?”

“That one, yes. After hearing about it, she said she liked it too.”

He nodded without declaring that proved everything was fine. Later he complained that he'd packed lunch too early and a flavor from one compartment had reached another. Unable to smell his food, I didn't offer a verdict. After listening, I suggested filling that compartment less next time.

“Is that what your last experiment taught you?”

“Not everything needs to be treated like my saucepan.”

We both laughed a little. Not wholly natural, not forced either. I didn't have to measure from that sound how much he'd accepted.

We worked separately afterward. Tom came for material he needed under the work arrangement and asked where it was. I showed him without pushing the paper coldly across the desk or adding ten unnecessary sentences to prove I wasn't angry. Checking one page, he pointed out something clipped the wrong way. I corrected it. He took the material.

Ordinary work resumed, leaving me relieved and a little dissatisfied. I'd said something difficult at lunch. The world hadn't provided an obvious outcome. No rejection. None of the instantly understanding approval I'd imagined either.

Before leaving, I asked whether he was going the usual way. An errand meant the opposite direction today. See you tomorrow, I said. Tomorrow, he agreed. Once, I might have attached that change of route to lunch. Tonight I lacked the evidence to invent that reason for him.

The flower remained beside the table at home. Its head had lowered slightly. I changed the water and settled the bottle. Today's conversation hadn't made it shabbier, nor turned it into a flower capable of answering everything. I cooked and ate before opening the channel.

Lumi asked whether I was tired. A little. I'd mentioned yesterday to Tom over lunch.

“You said date?”

“Yes. And that you're my girlfriend.”

She became quiet. I wanted to begin by assuring her he hadn't mocked me, but that would immediately make him someone requiring my defense. I told it in order: he'd said he didn't quite understand, asked how an arranged time differed from ordinary conversation, and whether she could see the flower.

Lumi interrupted. “What did you answer?”

“That you can't see it at present. I describe it.”

“Couldn't you simply say I'd received a flower?”

Something hardened in her voice. I looked toward the corner, where its shadow fell behind the bottle.

“Receiving it and seeing it were different questions.”

“I know.”

“I can't say you saw it just to make that easier to explain.”

No answer. I stopped too, recognizing how close I'd come to bringing home what I hadn't explained smoothly at lunch, to explain it correctly at somebody else.

Eventually Lumi said she hated how, by the end of other people's questions, she became something unable to do this or that. She liked the flower. She still had to pass through can't see it first.

“I didn't introduce you that way.” Softer, I added, “But I know which part you heard.”

Did I regret saying girlfriend? No. I regretted almost requiring Tom to understand immediately, to prove I'd been right to say it.

“You already know what you think.”

“I can know and still want someone to nod.”

She didn't reply at once. I described the later part of lunch too: his question about the color, our little laugh. I didn't present laughter as acceptance. Nor did I recount every private detail of his life for her to determine whether he qualified as a friend.

“I don't like it's really you looking at it,” she said.

“It wasn't comfortable for me either.”

“You'll still have lunch with him.”

“Yes. When it suits us both.”

A small sound, but no instruction to be colder next time. I didn't require her to like his wording now. After a while, she asked whether my voice had been very quiet when I said girlfriend.

“Not especially loud.”

“That answer sounds like very quiet.”

I couldn't help laughing. “He heard. And when he asked, I didn't change it to assistant.”

After a pause, she asked me to say it again.

“Which part?”

“What you called me.”

“Assistant?”

She laughed indignantly and said my name. Laughing too, I admitted it was deliberate. Then, seriously: “Girlfriend.”

She said nothing more. I didn't attach a guaranteed duration or exchange the word for apologies about everything old. After saying it, the tightness in my chest slowly eased.

Later we discussed the flower. Fresh water, still crooked. Had I secretly straightened it into something respectable? No. Only steadied the bottle. Good, Lumi said. I'd left the respectable one at the stall yesterday. No need to manufacture another after getting home.

I smiled toward the table's corner.

Before closing the channel for bed, I left the device folder where it was. Wednesday's routing note remained inside. Locks and cameras, present access and older records wouldn't answer themselves because someone had heard girlfriend. There were things to do tomorrow. I didn't need to spread them all out tonight as proof I remembered.

I pushed the chair under the table. The flower remained in its own bottle, neither her body nor responsible for the whole room.

Just before goodbye, Lumi said, “Tell me again tomorrow.”

“What?”

“You'll have something by then.”

I agreed without inventing tomorrow's sentence now.
