# Chapter 376: Four Days Early

On Tuesday morning, I put the borrowed drawing book in my bag.

The loan slip remained inside the cover. It was due on Saturday, four days away. The book wasn't hurrying me. I intended to return it after work because the library was on my way. I'd tried several exercises and wanted to practice those for a while, rather than complete every exercise that followed.

My own paper marker still occupied the practice page. I removed it and checked between the covers for any loose sheets of mine. Nothing. I hadn't written in the book or folded its corners. Closing it, my thumb passed over a shiny pressure mark on the cover. That had been there when I borrowed it. I remembered thinking it was damp.

The loan slip stayed inside. My marker went into my schedule book. Then I ate breakfast and left for work.

The afternoon became busy enough that I almost forgot what I'd brought. Feeling the book's corner after work reminded me. I went straight to the library. A shop was changing the price cards in its window. One person held up a sheet while another stepped back, apparently deciding whether it was level. I slowed briefly, then continued.

Two people waited at the returns counter. Taking out my book, I discovered the loan slip had slipped between the pages. I retrieved it, folded it into my pocket, and waited while the person ahead returned a substantial stack. The staff member moved some books on the trolley and indicated my turn.

I handed it over. She scanned the barcode, checked her screen, and asked whether I was returning only this one.

“Yes.”

She put it aside. I waited for the next step. That was done, she said. Could I have confirmation? She gave me a small printed receipt. I read it before putting it away. This book, returned today. Nothing overdue.

It had finished so quickly that I remained standing while the person behind began moving forward. I stepped aside and slipped the receipt into an outer pocket of my bag. The book that had weighed against my shoulder was on the trolley now. Nobody asked how much I'd learned.

I didn't leave immediately. Walking toward the shelves, I touched my bag and felt an urge to borrow another book, filling the weight I'd just removed.

Look first. In the art section, I took down a slim landscape collection and opened it at a path beside a bridge. The trees were broad; a distant figure was barely a dab of color. I tried to find the point where the dab became a person. Looking closer made it become paint again.

There was a free seat nearby. I sat with the book, discovered the chair rocked, shifted position, and found it still rocked. I stood and chose another chair before continuing.

The water beneath the bridge interested me. The painter hadn't described every ripple, yet something from the bank was visibly reflected there. After studying it, I closed the book on my knees and looked at the actual street outside. Someone beside a lamp post was adjusting a bag strap. From a distance, they too consisted of darker and lighter pieces. I knew it was a person because they moved, and because I already knew how people stood.

Opening the collection again, I found the dab less mysterious. It still wasn't something I could paint. I didn't copy the bridge or take out paper. I sat for ten minutes or so, holding down a page that resisted lying flat.

The caption beneath the picture was printed in small letters. I bent closer to read it, then realized I had been holding the book at an awkward angle. Resting it on the table helped. The title named a place I didn't know. I read it twice without trying to connect it to a street near home. On the next page, the same painter had used much warmer colors for a hillside. I turned back to the bridge. The quietness of the water interested me more than the hill, even though the hill's colors were easier to notice. I spent another minute comparing them, then returned to the picture I'd chosen first.

Someone asked whether the neighboring chair was taken. No. They sat with a book of their own. We didn't speak again. After looking at several pictures, I placed the collection in the designated reshelving area and left without borrowing anything new.

Outside, the sky had darkened. I was hungry and bought dinner on the way home. The bottom of the food bag was warm. I supported it with my other hand and walked more slowly. The drawing book's weight was gone. Dinner occupied a hand instead.

After eating, I opened the channel and told Lumi I'd returned the book.

“Was it due?”

“Four more days. It was convenient today.”

“Did you read the rest?”

“No. I wasn't planning to complete every page.”

Had I disliked it? No. The cup-rim and plate exercises had helped. I wanted to draw sometimes without leaving the book beside me, waiting to supply the answer. That sounded as though I'd already graduated. I corrected myself. I'd looked at someone else's bridge today and still had no idea how to make something like it.

“Why not borrow it again, then?”

“I didn't want to bring another book home today.”

After a pause, she said all right. Was that too simple an answer? She'd been about to ask what the bridge looked like, she said. I'd returned the discussion to borrowing before she could.

Laughing, I described the bridge. I hadn't visited its location. I'd only seen a painting. I told her how the railings slanted into the picture and how the water seemed deeper than the color on the paper ought to allow. I spoke slowly, stopping where I couldn't remember.

Lumi imagined a wide bridge. It had been narrow, I said. Perhaps I'd emphasized railings on both sides too strongly. Narrow enough for two people to need to move aside? The painting didn't provide dimensions that precisely. I could only say it looked narrow.

“Then we have different bridges now.”

“Yes.”

“You don't have to demolish mine immediately. You can describe yours more clearly.”

I laughed. I tried again: the railings approached each other in the distance, the bridge didn't fill the page, and plenty of trees remained beside it. Hers was smaller now, she said. Should I continue? No. She had a reasonable idea of what I'd seen.

Then she asked where my paper marker was now that the book had gone. Back in my own notebook. The old loan slip was in my pocket; the returns receipt in the outside compartment of the bag. Remembering that, I took out the old slip and joined it to the new receipt before it could go through the wash.

“What are you drawing today?”

“Nothing planned.”

Even saying that relieved me. There were no pears on the table. The cup was in the kitchen, and I hadn't taken out fresh paper. Would I like to play the ticket window? After considering, I said I would rather hear something from her today.

“Anything?”

“What you want to say. Not asking me to choose one of your manuscripts.”

Lumi paused. The thought about the room on Sunday hadn't become a seventh sentence, she said. I understood. Before I could add that there was no hurry, she said she knew I wasn't hurrying her. She wanted to mention it herself.

I swallowed the reassurance that had been ready.

“What would you like me to know today?”

“I've begun thinking the person inside might be busy with something small when the doorbell rings. Not waiting especially for the visitor.”

Had she chosen the small thing? Not yet. She didn't want five suggestions from me now either.

“I'd only thought of two.”

She laughed. “Keep them for now.”

I laughed too. We left the paper where it was. The original six sentences remained inside the unsealed white envelope. If she wanted help thinking later, could she ask directly? Yes. I didn't need to keep preparing answers in advance.

I admitted that I liked having something to help with. Hearing not decided yet made me uncertain where to put my hands. Lumi suggested getting my water. I fetched the cup from the kitchen, told her when I was back, and took a drink.

“You helped with that part,” I said.

“No need to write it on my manuscript.”

We laughed. Afterward, the subjects were small: dinner had been slightly salty; she'd imagined the bridge too wide; the first library chair had rocked. Choosing another chair had been sensible, she said. Yes. I didn't need a follow-up plan for the original chair.

She asked whether I'd noticed the rocking before sitting. Only afterward. Then perhaps the chair wasn't as inconsiderate as my pears, she said. At least it had allowed me to sit before making a demand.

“I'm not sure that's better.”

“You chose another one. The book stayed the same.”

I smiled and looked toward the stack where the borrowed book had been. I remembered the new chair's seat being harder. That had bothered me for a minute, then the water beneath the painted bridge had occupied me. I told Lumi. She asked whether the page had shown the reflection upside down. Partly, I said, though it was broken by the painted surface of the river. I hadn't inspected it closely enough to describe every mark.

“I would have liked the first big bridge,” she said.

“You can keep imagining that one.”

“I thought you wanted it corrected.”

“Only if I was telling you what the picture showed. You can imagine another.”

She considered that, then said hers had enough room to stop without making somebody behind wait. I pictured it and liked the practical addition. We didn't give the bridge a town or begin planning a journey. She said it might turn up somewhere in a story eventually, though not in the six sentences tonight. I didn't write the idea down. When she changed the subject, I let the bridge remain where we'd left it.

We talked until I was sleepy. I said good night, and she answered. She didn't ask whose hands would hold the returned book next.

After closing the channel, I filed the old loan slip and new receipt together with my own receipts. A small space remained on the shelf, while the other books crowded their original places. I took out a sheet of my own paper and drew a small rectangle, wanting to try how a bridge narrowed into the distance. The first slanting line leaned too far. I erased it and tried again.

After a few minutes, I stopped. I wrote Tuesday Bridge Study on the back and placed the sheet with my own drawings. Tomorrow, I could continue. For now, the eraser crumbs needed brushing away, and I was ready for bed.
