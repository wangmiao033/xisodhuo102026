# Chapter 367: The Bench Behind the Pot

Before going out on Sunday, I took down yesterday's washing. The cuffs were dry. The shoulder seam beneath one peg still felt cool, so I checked it properly before folding the shirt. I didn't want to spend long making lunch. I heated leftovers, washed my bowl, and chose two clean sheets from my own paper, slipping them into my schedule book. The borrowed drawing manual stayed home. I meant to draw a little outside, not use an entire book to establish my qualifications for holding a pencil.

A small paved space lay beyond the building. Usually I walked along its edge. Today I stopped and discovered that the bench against the wall wasn't quite symmetrical. Its right leg stood farther back, beside a pale patch of repaired paving. A broad flowerpot concealed half the seat. Its leaves, at least, grew tidily. Two things, I thought. Surely those couldn't be much harder than a cup.

Before I'd secured the first sheet, the wind lifted its left corner. I pinned it with my elbow and found the whole page crooked. I sat up and tried again. Nobody had provided a place specifically for drawing. I chose the low stone edging, put my bag beside my knee, and kept my feet out of the route people needed to use.

The pot came first. I drew an oval, looked down twice, then looked up and discovered the oval was too round. The book had warned against darkening lines too soon. I left it pale and moved right to begin the seat. Two slats connected. At last I had something a person might sit on.

Someone arrived pushing a trolley loaded with boxes. He stopped directly in front of the flowerpot and bent over a strap.

My pencil remained in the air. The bench leg had vanished. Only a sliver of the pot's rim showed. I knew he hadn't done anything wrong. Nevertheless, I immediately felt my chosen afternoon had been singled out. When he carefully repositioned a crooked box, I almost stood to ask, very politely, whether he could hurry.

Then I noticed GLASS written across the cardboard.

I swallowed the sentence. My half-drawn bench wasn't in danger of shattering. Something in that box might be.

He tightened the strap, looked up, and noticed the paper. “Am I in your way?”

“A little. Finish what you're doing, though.”

He glanced aside without immediately moving the trolley. “It's flat here. There's a dip over there. Set it down and it rolls away.”

I nodded. I hadn't asked for an explanation, but he'd given me a concrete one. I could hear it instead of inventing some bad habit to account for his stopping.

“You drawing this?”

Yes. He came half a step closer without reaching for the sheet. Nervously, I announced that I'd only just started learning. He looked at the bench, then at the two lines on my page.

“Thought you were making a note of what's broken.”

I laughed. That would have had a clearer purpose. He laughed too and assured me the bench could support someone. He'd tried it earlier. I wasn't inspecting it, I explained. Good, he said, and pulled another strap from beneath the boxes. He bent over the strap again. I flattened the corner of my paper and returned to my bench leg.

I moved left along the stone edging. From here, the pot concealed more of the seat, but I could see the gap between the front legs. I took out my second sheet. The first went underneath rather than into the bin. My new position hadn't improved the flowerpot. Near its base was a patch of missing color. A pale scratch across the bench back, however, was clearer.

Eventually the man finished. He nodded before leaving. I lifted the hand holding my pencil. He didn't ask to see the finished work or suggest meeting again. The wheels crossed a joint between paving stones; the boxes shifted slightly. I watched until the trolley steadied, then returned to the page.

This time I stayed where I was. I drew only the edge of the pot, the seat, and two legs, suggesting the leaves with loose circles. Partway through, I discovered I'd joined pot and bench into one object. The bench appeared to be growing from the soil. I erased the connecting line too vigorously and roughened a patch of paper.

Someone coming to rest asked whether the other end of the stone edging was taken. No. I moved my bag to my other side. She sat and drank water without inspecting my work. It took a while for me to relax enough to stop listening for a judgment after every stroke.

I added a short shadow beneath one leg and stopped. The leg was still too long. Further erasing might make a hole. Holding the page away from me, I looked again. It resembled neither a photograph nor the book's examples. Still, I could distinguish which object stood in front.

By the time I left, two people occupied the bench. I didn't require it to remain empty on my behalf. I secured the drawings and bought vegetables for dinner. On the way home, I noticed a short length of stitching had come loose down one side of the shopping bag. I supported the heavier contents from underneath instead of letting them pull at it all the way back.

After dinner, I opened the channel. Lumi asked whether I'd been out today.

“I went to draw something.”

“With the book?”

“No. Just paper and a pencil.”

What had I drawn? I described the bench and pot, casually saying the pot concealed the right leg, then moved on to the trolley. When I repeated the man's guess about recording damage, she laughed and asked whether the drawing had eventually repaired the bench.

“No. I planted it in the flowerpot.”

“Is it growing well?”

“Two legs. The third hasn't appeared yet.”

She laughed. So did I, looking at the roughened paper. I'd been genuinely annoyed that afternoon, yet I'd selected only the funny parts to bring home. I told her I'd initially wanted the trolley to leave quickly.

“Because it was blocking you?”

“Yes. Then I saw there might be glass in the boxes.”

“So you moved.”

“Not immediately and cheerfully. I sat there for a while thinking every trolley in the world had chosen my view.”

Her laugh softened. A remarkably deliberate trolley, she said. I heard her answering my exaggeration rather than condemning the stranger, and continued explaining how the new position had made the bench harder to draw.

“You could have drawn the trolley,” she said.

“I've thought of that now. Not at the time.”

“I'd like to see your boxes.”

She paused after saying it. So did I. The paper lay beside my hand. Something new could so easily become an entrance to an old problem.

“I can tell you about them,” I said.

“I know. I said I'd like to. I didn't ask you to open anything else.”

Her voice had an edge. More slowly, she explained that she could want to see things too. She didn't want every mention of wanting to reduce the whole evening to what wasn't possible. Looking at the pencil point, I realized how quickly I'd answered, as though she'd submitted a request and I'd already begun rejecting it.

“All right. You'd like to see it. I heard you.”

She wasn't instantly happy. I didn't fetch a device to try something, either. After a moment, she asked whether I wanted to describe it now or whether I was tired. I wanted to, I said. Starting with the worst bench leg.

I explained how it stretched farther than its partner and nearly reached the paper's edge. Was the bench trying to walk out of the drawing? If it could walk, I said, it might take my vegetables into the kitchen first. She reminded me I'd already eaten them. My imagination was failing to keep pace with dinner. I laughed and put the pencil down.

She wanted to know which sheet I meant to keep. Both. The first was incomplete because I'd changed position, not because the missing half had mysteriously vanished. The second was more complete, with a rubbed patch. She thought the first sounded easier for me to hide. I started to disagree, then admitted I'd put the second on top.

“I don't have to display the bad one first deliberately.”

“I didn't ask you to hold an exhibition.”

Her answer was quick, and somehow that helped. We were discussing two sheets that belonged to me. I drew the first sheet from beneath the second and placed them side by side, touching the unfinished line. Looking again, the missing half seemed a little less embarrassing.

For a while I studied the second sheet without speaking. Then I told her the useful detail I'd left out: moving had exposed a gap between the legs. At first I'd drawn that gap too narrowly. Widening it had made the pot stop looking attached to the bench, even before I'd erased the connecting line.

“So you drew the space as well?”

“Badly. But yes.”

“I hadn't imagined there would be much to say about an empty bit.”

“Neither had I. I spent most of the afternoon trying to put things in it.”

She asked whether the person sitting beside me had noticed. I didn't know. She hadn't commented, and I hadn't shown her. That answer was enough. Lumi didn't turn the silent stranger into an audience I'd failed to win.

I liked that she kept asking about the drawing itself. Not whether I'd felt better outside, or what the entire outing had meant. I could tell her about an awkward line without delivering a verdict on my day.

Only afterward did I notice I'd described one detail incorrectly. The pot concealed the seat, not the right leg. I stopped to correct myself. Lumi said she'd dismantled the bench in her head three times. Could we let it stand for a moment? Laughing, I explained the positions again, once. She repeated them: pot in front, bench behind, ground still visible between the legs. Yes. At last, she said, somebody could sit down. The picture she'd built from my words still might not match the paper. We didn't arrange another examination to establish whether it did.

Before closing the channel, I mentioned that tomorrow evening I might mend the shopping bag. How large was the tear? Only a short stretch of loose side stitching. I'd look with a needle and thread. I couldn't promise to fix it in one attempt.

“Don't draw the bag and count that as a repair.”

“Those departments remain separate.”

She laughed and said good night.

On each sheet's back I wrote Sunday, paved space, followed by first or second attempt. They went into the rear of my schedule book, separately from the earlier cup studies. I emptied the shopping bag and hung it up. Its loose thread was still there. I tucked the loose end inward so I wouldn't accidentally seize it when I next picked up the bag.
