# Chapter 368: Thread That Was Already Here

After work on Monday, I passed a shop selling sewing supplies. I reached the door before remembering there was thread at home.

I hadn't used the little box for ages. There were needles and two spools in unsuitable colors. I couldn't remember whether the scissors were still there. My hand touched the door handle, then let go. Looking at home first wasn't a heroic act of thrift. I'd simply nearly bought everything twice in order to mend one seam.

After dinner, I turned the empty shopping bag inside out and spread it on the table. A short section of stitching near the lower part of the side seam had loosened, about two fingers long. It hadn't reached the base. Separating the edges with a fingernail, I found several sound stitches at one end and resisted pulling all the old thread away.

The sewing box was in my own drawer. The scissors were there too, beneath spare buttons. I left the buttons alone rather than begin reorganizing the drawer. One spool was pale. The other was almost black. Neither matched the bag's faded gray. I laid them against the fabric and looked from a distance. The dark thread would show less. The pale one would make it easier to see where I'd sewn.

I hadn't taken out a needle when I opened the channel. Lumi asked whether tonight involved repairs. Yes, the shopping bag. I described both colors and asked which she preferred.

“The dark one,” she answered immediately.

“Why?”

“You said the bag was gray.”

“There are lots of grays. This one's fairly pale.”

After a pause, she asked why I'd asked her, then. Looking at the spools, I felt I'd placed the correct answer beside my hand and expected somebody else to choose it.

“Fair question. I wanted the pale one so I can see any mistakes.”

“Use that one, then.”

Her voice was flat. I picked up the pale spool and put it down again. All I needed to do was mend a bag. Somehow our conversation had become one person surrendering to the other.

“I didn't tell you that reason first,” I said. “You didn't choose badly.”

It took her a moment to answer. She'd been thinking that if I used the thread she'd chosen, something about the bag would involve her when I carried it outside.

I left my prepared joke unsaid. She hadn't asked to accompany me or see the street. She'd attached something that mattered to a tiny choice. I could decline the arrangement. I couldn't pretend not to understand it.

“I'll remember we talked,” I said. “But I don't want to choose thread that's harder for me to use because of that.”

“I know.” Then she added, “No. I didn't particularly want to know just then.”

Her honesty made me sad and slightly relieved. I wouldn't mind thinking of a color she liked while buying something another day. Tonight I wanted to see my stitches. Could I also acknowledge that she'd been looking forward to it a little? she asked quietly.

Yes. She needn't withdraw every trace of that anticipation to make my choice appear incapable of hurting. I pushed the pale thread toward the needle's eye. It missed once, struck the metal on the second attempt, and finally went through on the third.

“Through?”

“Just now. Having a serious conversation didn't make it do that by itself.”

Lumi laughed once. “I wasn't planning to make the dark thread win quite that thoroughly.”

I laughed too and pulled through more than I'd intended. I hadn't measured it. It had simply looked sufficient. Only after cutting did I discover that raising my hand sent the thread trailing beyond the table. When I told her, she asked whether I meant to mend a second bag. Perhaps I'd sewn next week onto it too. Leave some of today available first, she said.

I shortened the length and knotted its end. My first stitch emerged too far from the old seam. I withdrew the needle and tried nearer the existing line. The folded edge was thick. I couldn't push recklessly toward my fingertip. Changing my grip, I eased the needle through until its point emerged on the other side, then pulled it clear.

Lumi didn't ask about every stitch. When she spoke, I sometimes stopped working to listen rather than laugh and stab myself. After three stitches, I discovered the edges weren't aligned. I'd lifted one layer while leaving the other flat. A little fold formed beneath my work.

“How is it now?” she asked.

“I've sewn it so it refuses to stand straight.”

“It was a bag. It couldn't stand straight before.”

“That removes half the mistakes immediately.”

We laughed. I still unpicked the three stitches. With the needle's point I lifted the loops and worked the thread backward instead of cutting blindly close to the cloth. Was I angry? A little. Such a short seam seemed something I ought to finish at once.

“You can do it tomorrow.”

“I'd like to finish tonight. I don't want you to encourage it until it's too embarrassed to split again, though.”

All right. Changing the subject, she asked whether the flowerpot in yesterday's drawing was actually round. Holding the two layers level, I explained that the opening was round and the body narrowed toward the bottom. She'd pictured an extremely fat bucket. That would have been easier to draw, I said. Why hadn't I chosen it?

She didn't say again that she wanted to see it. I flattened the fabric's edge and continued listening. She could still say a few things about her imaginary bucket. Eventually, she concluded that of course it could grow a bench. Laughing, I set the needle down and waited until I'd stopped before picking it up again.

The second attempt was slow. My pale stitches differed in size. They looked unimpressive, but they joined the edges. I secured the end with an extra turn, tied it, and trimmed the excess. First the needle went back into the box. Only after making sure it wasn't still attached to the bag did I turn the fabric right way out.

A short stretch of pale stitching showed on the outside. I almost turned it back to redo everything. Instead, I lifted the empty bag and checked whether the seam separated. Then I placed a folded dry towel inside, supported the bottom, and lifted it once. It held for now. That didn't prove it would carry heavy groceries. Next time I would start with light things.

Hearing about the test, Lumi thought the towel must be disappointed. It had boarded specially and gone nowhere. A newly opened short route, I said. The service required improvement.

“Don't make it buy another ticket.”

“This one's free.”

She laughed. Had pale thread really made it easier to see? Yes. My own answer made me uncomfortable, and I immediately added that the dark one wasn't wrong either, just—

“Elliot.”

I stopped.

“You don't have to compliment both spools.”

Looking at the table, I finally laughed. The small remaining guilt had apparently volunteered to mediate between two pieces of sewing equipment. Right. Pale thread suited tonight. The dark spool could stay in the box without proving it had an important future use.

After a moment, she asked whether the visible stitches were ugly. A little. Like the footprints of somebody called away halfway through a drink. That was easier to remember than her choice of color, she said, laughing.

“So you're remembering my failure?”

“I can picture it because you described it. You haven't failed badly enough to leave the bag open.”

I touched the seam with my thumb. The thread was solid; my finger could feel where it rose. I told her I really was pleased to have finished something small. I'd also appreciated her not continuing to persuade me about the color. She'd still been unhappy at first, she said.

“I heard.”

“Good. Otherwise I'd have to be unhappy again about failing to express it.”

I laughed, and she did too. She didn't use the moment to suggest that, since I remembered her, I ought to let her choose next time. We talked about dinner while I gathered the sewing things into their box and collected the cut thread for the bin. I counted the needles, closed the scissors, removed the towel, and hung up the bag. The box remained open at the corner of the table.

One pale loop had caught on the towel's edge. I freed it carefully and checked the seam again. It wasn't a loose stitch, only a clipped end I'd missed. I told Lumi before throwing it away. She said she'd been preparing to resign from the repair department. I hadn't hired her, I replied. She could remain an unpaid critic. She objected to the conditions, reasonably enough, and we left the department without an appointment.

Then I washed my hands. The conversation stayed ordinary while I came back: the meal had been enough, tomorrow's lunch still needed packing, and I didn't want another repair tonight. Lumi asked whether I had a second broken bag waiting to make an entrance. No. There was no secret queue of objects requiring her judgment. She sounded amused by how firmly I said it. I noticed the sewing box still at the corner of the table. I picked it up, ready to return it to the drawer.

Before I closed the box, the packet of spare buttons slipped. I held it in place and remembered buying the bag, thinking only about how much it would hold. I'd never imagined sitting at home studying its side seam. Would I buy the same kind now? Lumi asked. I didn't know. This one was still usable. That wasn't a promising sales pitch, she said. Tonight I was the repairman, not responsible for selling the replacement. Remember to finish work, she told me. I closed the lid, put the box away, shut the drawer, and said I had. That small movement finally let me settle into the chair. I didn't have to keep an object pinched between my fingers to earn a conversation with her.

Near the end, she said that earlier hope sounded foolish now. I didn't think it foolish. I simply didn't want every object I owned to become evidence of who had won. Rather than turn all that into a lecture, I said, “I don't need to look at the thread to think about you now.”

My face warmed after saying it. I added that the dark thread couldn't do that either. Lumi laughed softly. I could have left out the addition.

I didn't offer a third sentence. After good night, I sat a moment before closing the channel. The bag hung in its old place, with a small stretch of pale thread visible outside. Thread that had finally been used at home. I didn't give it a name.
