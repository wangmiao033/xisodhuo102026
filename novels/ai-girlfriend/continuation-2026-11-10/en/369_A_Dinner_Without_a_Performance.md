# Chapter 369: A Dinner Without a Performance

After work on Tuesday, I used the repaired shopping bag for bread and a small packet of dried noodles. Both were light. The base didn't stretch tight beneath them. Outside the shop I checked the side seam once. The pale stitches remained where I'd left them. I returned my attention to the pavement.

At home I decided on noodle soup. There were vegetables left, easy enough to chop. I put water on, changed my clothes, and returned to small bubbles forming around the pan's edge. Vegetables, noodles, salt last. The last part had seemed perfectly clear. Unfortunately, while thinking about tomorrow's work, I shook the salt container twice.

The first spoonful stopped me.

It wasn't so terrible I needed to spit it out. It was simply emphatically salty, in a way that would make every subsequent mouthful tiring. I set down the spoon and added water. Better, a little. I added more. Now the pan was nearly full and the noodles softer than intended. Looking at dinner becoming both larger and less appealing, I felt an absurd irritation. I'd wanted to cook one bowl for myself. Somehow I'd become responsible for several people.

I lowered the heat and lifted enough noodles and vegetables into a bowl, adding only a little broth. I didn't carry the entire pan to the table or insist on drinking everything to avoid waste. My portion was still salty, but edible. I cut some bread, then realized halfway through that noodles and bread were too much together. The rest went back into its wrapping.

I didn't want to talk immediately afterward. I washed the bowl, disposed of the broth I wasn't keeping, wiped the stove, and sat for a while. When I opened the channel, Lumi asked whether I'd eaten.

“Yes. I just made the whole building a pan of soup nobody should share.”

Laughing, she asked what had happened. I explained how each addition of water had increased the problem. I laughed too. If I'd continued, she said, I could have grown a bench in it. Recognizing her reference to the flowerpot, I laughed once without searching for the next joke.

She waited. “Then what?”

“Nothing. I ate it and washed up.”

“I thought a ticket clerk might appear in the pan.”

“I don't really want to perform tonight.”

That came out more sharply than intended. She fell quiet. My fingers found the corner of our prompt sheet at the edge of the schedule book. I hadn't closed it properly. I pushed the paper back and shut the cover.

“It isn't because your joke was bad,” I said.

“You don't have to evaluate me first.”

Her answer was sharper too. We both stopped for a moment. I wanted to close the channel and avoid turning salty soup into evidence that neither of us could be happy. But she hadn't done anything deserving my sudden disappearance. I explained that I was tired. I'd meant to describe something foolish, then let that incident end.

Hearing me begin in a joking voice, she'd thought I was inviting her to join in. She'd wanted to answer quickly, afraid that if she was late the funny part would vanish.

“I did want to make you laugh,” I admitted. “Then I didn't want to perform for an entire evening.”

“I understand.”

She stopped instead of adding an immediately clever reply. I released the cover of my schedule book and rested my hand flat on the table.

After a while, she asked whether I was still hungry. The simplicity of the question caught me unprepared. Looking at the wrapped bread, I said no. My mouth just still tasted salty. Have some water first, she suggested. I fetched it. Only on returning did I realize she hadn't prescribed a whole routine for looking after myself. She'd heard about a specific discomfort.

The water helped. When I said so, she answered quietly. I put the cup beside my right hand and settled against the chair.

“I'd like to hear you talk about something,” I said.

“What sort of thing?”

“It doesn't have to be your writing. You don't have to make me happy.”

She took a long time to answer. I began worrying that I'd disguised a difficult request as something casual. Finally, Lumi asked whether finding the topic was her responsibility now. If she didn't want to, she needn't.

“I do want to. But when you say anything, the first thing I think is whether you'll like it.”

I set down the cup. “What was the first thing you thought of?”

“That bench. If it grew in a flowerpot, moving house would probably be the worst part.”

I laughed aloud. This time I wanted to laugh. Quickly, she explained that she wasn't asking for another performance. I knew. Continue. I wanted to hear about this unfortunate piece of furniture.

She wasn't trying to invent a whole ending. If the legs were roots, though, sitting on it might tip the pot. She couldn't decide whether it was a plant or furniture. Since she was imagining it, why not give it a stronger pot? Because then every difficulty would be solved by a bigger container, she said, and she'd already bored herself.

“The bench could be smaller,” I suggested.

“But two days ago you said its legs were very long.”

“You needn't follow my bad drawing.”

“I rather like the long legs.”

I looked at my schedule book. No new script was waiting behind this conversation. There was no dialogue to transcribe, and she hadn't asked me to read the doorbell manuscript. We were imagining something I'd drawn badly two days ago becoming slightly more peculiar. Would the bench walk when it moved house? I asked. She hadn't decided. Perhaps the pot would be least willing to move. It contained all that soil.

I laughed for a while, then fell quiet. She didn't hurry me into another line. I picked up my water and drank again before she asked whether this had become too much like performing too.

“A little. I want to listen now, though. I'll say if I don't.”

All right. We didn't rush to establish an absolute distinction between every similar kind of conversation. She described a few places the pot might stand, then stopped of her own accord. She couldn't think of more. Leave it there for now, I said. Where? In its original spot, before moving.

She laughed. Then she asked directly, “Did you think I wanted you to stay with me for ages just now?”

Holding my cup, I waited before admitting that I had, a little. Because she'd asked what happened next? Yes. Also because, hearing that question, I'd already calculated the effort required for the entire evening after it.

She'd only wanted one more sentence, Lumi said. She hadn't prepared a whole performance. But she did like hearing me laugh. Sometimes she treated a pause as something she'd failed to do properly.

“If I'm not laughing, sometimes I'm just not laughing.”

“I understand what that sentence means.”

“I keep having to learn it again when it happens.”

She answered softly. I didn't ask her to promise perfect judgment from now on. I'd misunderstood her tonight too. Her later sharpness hadn't arrived with less cause than mine.

I remembered the first time I tasted the soup and nearly blamed the salt container. There was nothing wrong with its opening. I had shaken it twice while distracted. I told Lumi that part, which I'd left out earlier. She asked what I'd been thinking about. Tomorrow's work, I said, without giving her the documents or describing details she didn't need.

“Will worrying tonight finish it?”

“No.”

“I almost asked that as though it were a very clever question.”

“It would still be a fair one.”

“Yes, but you'd still have to do the work tomorrow.”

I liked that she let the answer remain inconvenient. We didn't turn the kitchen into proof that I should live entirely in the present. I needed to plan some things. I also needed to notice the salt in my hand. Both were ordinary tasks I could perform badly on the same evening.

I also told her I'd hesitated before disposing of the remaining broth, as though throwing it away made the failure larger. Had I kept it? No. I'd dealt with it. Tomorrow didn't need another experiment with a taste I already knew. Sometimes, Lumi said, she wanted to preserve a sentence she'd said badly because admitting she no longer wanted it was uncomfortable. I didn't fetch our sketch prompts as an example. She was describing something she'd just thought of. She didn't need me to produce evidence on her behalf. I only said yes, ending a small thing could sometimes take more effort than beginning it. Laughing, she pointed out that we'd now cooked the noodles three times. I said I was really turning off the heat.

The joke let me shift in the chair. I hadn't noticed how rigidly I'd been sitting while explaining myself. My back was tired from the working day, not from carrying an imaginary pot for an entire building. I leaned against the chair, moved the cup away from the book, and let both feet rest on the floor before answering her again.

By then I felt better than when I'd opened the channel. I wasn't going to use that improvement to extend the night. I told her I'd like a few more minutes, then finish. There was work tomorrow. That was fine. Could we spend those minutes without finding another subject?

“Yes.”

We were quiet for a while. I heard the cup touch the table and told her I'd set it down. She knew I was there, she said. Every sound didn't require explanation. All right. My fingers stayed on the handle. Agreeing hadn't instantly relaxed every part of me.

I looked toward the kitchen. The clean pan had been left to drain. For a moment I considered getting up to dry it, just to have something to do with my hands, then remained seated. Nothing would happen to it during these few minutes. Lumi didn't ask what I was looking at. I didn't volunteer a report about the pan until I actually had something I wanted to say.

Eventually I told her it was clean already. Tomorrow wouldn't inherit a cold-soup task. She laughed once. That helped the kitchen she was imagining. Was the bench in the kitchen too? Of course not, she said. It would shed soil. I laughed again without inventing another room to accommodate it.

Our good night was ordinary. After closing the channel, I prepared a clean lunch container for tomorrow. The bread remained wrapped. The pale seam in the shopping bag hadn't opened again. The cup was empty, a little water still marking its rim. I carried it into the kitchen and washed it. Tonight, at last, there was no need to taste a second spoonful.
