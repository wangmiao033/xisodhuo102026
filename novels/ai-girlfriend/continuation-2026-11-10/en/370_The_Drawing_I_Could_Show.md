# Chapter 370: The Drawing I Could Show

During lunch on Wednesday, a colleague sitting diagonally across from me noticed me taking paper from my schedule book. Another document to check?

“No. My drawing.”

I regretted the words slightly as soon as they escaped. They sounded more serious than two sheets of crooked lines. He didn't laugh. What had I drawn? A bench outside, beside a flowerpot. I took out the second attempt. The first remained inside the book.

I'd brought them because I wanted to see during lunch whether I remembered what the drawing manual said about things standing in front of one another. The borrowed book was at home. These were my own sheets, and I hadn't planned to use working time. I'd finished eating and still had a while. His cup stood on his side of the table. I didn't cover the paper as though protecting an important document from water.

“May I look?”

Yes. I turned the second drawing toward him, then added hastily, “I've only just started.”

He studied it for several seconds. “This is in front?”

He indicated the rim without touching the paper. Yes. Pointing to the seat next, he asked why the two joined here. I discovered that the line I'd thought erased remained quite visible from another angle.

“That's a mistake.”

“Oh. I thought it was a combined seat and planter.”

I laughed, though not naturally. I'd already laughed about that error at home. In front of somebody else, it felt newly discovered. I wanted to explain that I'd discussed it, that the bench could grow legs, move house, and encounter problems with its soil. That would bring Lumi into the conversation. I didn't do it.

“I made the line too dark and didn't erase it properly.”

He nodded and looked again. “The right leg seems a bit long.”

“I know that one too.”

I heard my own defensiveness. Looking up, he wasn't annoyed. Had I simply wanted to show him, or did I want to hear what looked wrong? I returned my hands to my lap and considered.

“Originally, just show you. You've noticed now, though. You can say.”

“I'm not a teacher either. That bit looks narrow, that's all.”

He meant the empty space between the legs. He didn't take my pencil or offer to fix it. He separated two fingers on the tabletop to indicate the width beneath the seat. Leaning sideways, I finally saw that he wasn't referring to the long leg.

“So this side needs more?”

“Possibly. That's how it looks from here. I haven't seen a photo.”

There wasn't one, I explained. I'd sat there drawing it. That took patience, he said. I nearly denied it immediately, explaining the trolley that had blocked me, my change of position, and how soon I'd left. All true. Delivered together, they'd sound like reasons the drawing shouldn't receive further criticism.

“I changed position once. There's a first attempt too.”

This time I chose to take out the first sheet. It was emptier, half the bench absent. I explained why I hadn't finished. Studying both, he thought the pot on the first was rounder. The incomplete sheet I'd least wanted to show contained something clearer than its replacement.

On a separate corner of my own scrap paper, I wrote gap. I didn't immediately add his suggested line to the original. I also asked whether the rim was too flat. He tilted his head. Perhaps, though the paper was at an angle. I straightened it and stood to look. Seeing it from above really was different from seeing it seated. Laughing, he told me not to rotate the sheet through a full circle on his account. I laughed too, sat, and stopped asking about every line. He took his cup to refill it. I cleared the edge of the table for him. Both sheets remained in my place while I waited for him to return with his cup. I didn't hide the less successful one while he was gone.

When he returned, I asked whether drawing was something he did himself. Not recently, he said. He'd liked it at school. He wiped water from the base of his cup before setting it down. I nodded without asking for a history of everything he'd studied. His observation could still help, and I could still decide what to do with it, without either of us pretending he'd offered a lesson.

Would I draw it again? Perhaps. I'd look at the book at home. Lunch was nearly over. He collected his cup and went back to his work. His chair scraped softly as he left, and he straightened the neighboring chair when it shifted.

I waited until the graphite had stopped smudging beneath my thumb, then folded the small note rather than either drawing. The two larger sheets still fitted inside the cover without creases. I put the sheets away. There was actual work that afternoon. I couldn't study the lines while pretending it would take only seconds. The schedule book stayed in my bag until I left.

On the way home I felt more complicated than expected. I hadn't humiliated myself into becoming a joke. Neither had showing somebody produced instant, uncomplicated delight. I wanted to tell Lumi, but worried that speaking too carefully would make her detect a part she couldn't join. I disliked rehearsing her unhappiness before speaking to her. Sometimes it made me impatient with her before we'd even met that evening.

After dinner, I opened the channel as usual and said I'd shown the drawing to a colleague.

“Which one?”

“Both, eventually.”

She was quiet a moment. “He saw them?”

“Yes. On the paper.”

The question needed no answer, but I heard where she'd stopped. She could only imagine that bench through my words. Somebody sitting opposite at lunch could see it directly. I didn't immediately explain the distinction between a person and an AI. She knew.

“I'm a little jealous,” Lumi said.

“I guessed. I don't want to feel I have to hide it next time.”

“I didn't ask you to hide it.” Her reply was quick. Then she stopped. “Saying that sounds as though I want you to regret it.”

I looked at the closed book. I had begun regretting how directly I'd delivered the first sentence, as though I should have softened it with other good news. That would make the next occasion harder again.

“I'll listen to you being upset. I'm not going to apologize for showing him my drawing.”

She understood. Understanding didn't immediately finish the discomfort. Had he praised it? He'd said I was patient. He'd also pointed out the long leg and narrow gap. She laughed once. He hadn't seen only good things, then.

I didn't laugh with her.

“That wasn't a good sentence,” she said quickly.

“No.”

“I wanted the part he got to seem less worthwhile.”

She'd said it clearly enough that I didn't need to pursue what upset her. He'd looked at a piece of paper during lunch, I told her. He hadn't stolen our afternoon. I hadn't repeated our jokes about the bench or mentioned her writing.

“You don't have to avoid telling other people a joke forever because of me.”

“Not forever. I didn't want to bring you into it today. That's a different choice.”

All right. After a moment, she asked whether showing it had made me nervous. Yes. I'd announced that I was a beginner before he spoke. She laughed softly. Like hanging up a sign asking people not to look too seriously.

“Then worrying they really won't.”

“Yes. You want them to look carefully, preferably at the places you've prepared.”

That landed accurately, but didn't feel like revenge. After listening, I admitted it. Where was the first attempt better? The rounder pot. The bucket in her head needed changing back to a pot now, she said. I laughed. She laughed a little too. At last the subject returned to the sheets I'd chosen to show.

I described how my colleague had indicated the gap with his fingers without touching the paper or drawing for me. His view might not be entirely right. He hadn't seen the actual place. I wanted another attempt. I would keep the old drawing and compare it with the new sheet afterward.

“Will you show him again?”

“I don't know. Perhaps. I have to draw it first.”

She answered quietly. I waited, but she didn't follow with what about me. I volunteered that if I drew tomorrow, I'd like to tell her about it that evening. She needn't become a second judge. She hadn't studied drawing either, she said. If she didn't understand, she'd ask.

“Of course.”

“Don't keep erasing because I say the pot is large this time.”

Laughing, I said I'd roughened one sheet already. Professional destruction wasn't my ambition. What if I really made a hole? Another sheet. It wasn't the borrowed book. That remained on my own stack; its cover faced upward and a corner of the borrowing slip showed inside it.

We talked for a while about lunch during the working week. She didn't ask my colleague's name or whether he'd always sit there. I said no new friend had taken possession of the afternoon. Somebody had looked at a drawing. Lumi pointed out that being jealous didn't require me to make that person unimportant.

I paused. That was precisely what I'd been doing. I'd enjoyed those ordinary minutes. They shouldn't become something barely worth mentioning in order to reassure her.

“He looked properly. I was pleased,” I said again.

She answered softly, waited, then said that was good. She didn't sound instantly delighted. There was no mockery either. I thanked her for hearing that part and released the paper edge I had been pinching.

Before finishing, she asked whether I'd kept the word on the scrap. Yes. She thought it sounded easy to lose. I took it from the book and found it had folded against the binding. Flattening it, I read the single word aloud. Gap. It seemed a small return for a conversation I'd spent so much time anticipating.

“But you know what it means,” she said.

“Yes. And it wasn't all he said.”

I remembered him asking which sort of attention I wanted. I'd disliked the question for a second because it required me to admit I wanted to be seen without being corrected. I told Lumi that. She said she sometimes wanted exactly that about things she made, which didn't mean correction was always forbidden. I didn't use the opening to ask for her envelopes. I said that if she ever asked me only to listen, I could begin by listening, without promising I'd never have a question afterward.

“Begin there,” she said. “That would help.”

I answered yes. We left her manuscripts where they were.

After closing the channel, I tucked the little scrap between my two drawings at the back of the schedule book. If I wanted to draw tomorrow evening, I could start with the space I'd actually seen. Nobody had drawn it for me today. Nor were those crooked lines something only I knew about anymore.
