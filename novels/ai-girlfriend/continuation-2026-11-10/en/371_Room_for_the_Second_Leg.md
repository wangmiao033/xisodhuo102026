# Chapter 371: Room for the Second Leg

After dinner on Thursday, I moved my water away before taking out the drawing manual.

The borrowing slip remained inside its cover. My bookmark marked the cup exercise I'd last consulted, not today's page. I turned a few pages to an example showing spaces between objects, studied it, then tried two lines on my own scrap paper. The example used different objects, not my bench. Copying it wouldn't mean I'd returned to Sunday's paved space.

I took both drawings from the back of my schedule book. The scrap marked gap lay between them; I placed it above the sheets. There was one new page. The pencil was the one I'd bought and sharpened recently. Its point had dulled, so I sharpened it first and discarded the shavings. When I sat again, the table finally stopped looking as though three separate jobs were waiting to begin.

I studied the second attempt. The overlong leg was obvious. So was the mark connecting pot and seat. Usually I'd have attacked the most irritating part first. Tonight I tried the book's suggestion and looked at the shape between the legs. Wider at the top, cut diagonally by the ground below, with one corner obscured by the pot.

I outlined that space lightly on the fresh sheet. It looked rather like a crooked paper bag.

No bench, no flowerpot. A four-sided shape waiting for somebody to guess its purpose. I wanted to add handles. Remembering the bag I'd just repaired, I smiled without drawing them.

Next came the leg along the space's right edge. My hand lengthened it again. I stopped and compared the old drawing. Apparently my hand remembered the mistake more firmly than the correct position. Keeping the line pale, I shortened it and placed the other leg to the left. At last neither seemed determined to walk away alone.

When I opened the channel, I told Lumi I was trying yesterday's gap. Had I finished? No. Two legs so far.

“The same bench again?”

“Yes. I haven't brought a new one home.”

Laughing, she asked where today's pot was. I hadn't drawn it. She paused, then suggested leaving it out for now to see the bench without it.

I nearly said no immediately. Looking at my new sheet, I remembered she hadn't reached over to erase my original. She'd heard about something unfinished and wondered how another version might look.

“I can leave it for a moment. But it hid part of the left side. I didn't see that whole leg.”

“Couldn't you complete it from memory?”

“Yes, but I'd be inventing it. It might not be the actual bench.”

That was fine if I wanted to draw it, she said. Looking down, I realized I didn't want to complete what I hadn't seen. Tonight I wanted the space between the legs to feel less cramped. When I explained, she agreed. The pot could return. I needn't dismantle everything for her.

“It hasn't arrived yet. It can't return.”

“Invite it onstage, then.”

Laughing, I drew one curved side, obscuring the left corner. Lumi asked whether the pot stood on the page's right. Left, I said, then realized I'd kept referring to the leg on the right of the gap without actually locating the pot.

“What are you picturing?”

“A pot, two legs, and a seat that hasn't arrived.”

“Where?”

“Pot on the right. Legs going left.”

I told her she'd reversed it. Could she turn the whole sheet around? I started explaining why rotation wouldn't solve that, then heard her laugh softly. She hadn't requested a geometry lesson.

“You can hold yours backward.”

“Mine doesn't have paper.”

“Easier still.”

We laughed. I set down the pencil until I'd stopped rather than let the sheet suffer my unsteady hand. She hadn't meant to make me draw her version, she said. I knew. Her misunderstanding left and right didn't require me to report coordinates for every line.

Adding the seat produced a genuine difficulty. The original drawings offered different angles. There was no photograph. I remembered the bench against the wall but couldn't recall precisely how the seat's edge slanted from my second position. The point hovered too long, then left a black dot where I hadn't meant to press.

“What happened?” Lumi asked.

“I can't remember the angle.”

“Look at the first one?”

“That's from somewhere else.”

“You don't necessarily have to finish it tonight.”

There was nothing surprising in the suggestion, yet I immediately wanted to object. Why use fresh paper if I ended with another half-drawing? Looking at the gap, now wider than yesterday's, I swallowed that reply. At least this part had changed. The entire sheet hadn't stayed where it was, waiting for me.

I'd leave the seat's approximate direction in pale lines, I told her. If I passed again, I could look. Tonight I wouldn't declare a remembered angle correct. All right. Would I leave out the wall too? I hadn't intended to draw it.

“You didn't tell me.”

“I only meant to tell you about the bench.”

“Mine has had a wall behind it all this time.”

“It can stay.”

Lumi laughed. Apparently her job tonight was bringing extra things while mine was removing them. That difference no longer made either of us careless. Listening, she naturally added things. Drawing, I naturally left some out.

After a while, I noticed I'd rubbed the same corner several times and stopped. This page hadn't roughened yet. I needn't wait until it tore to establish that a break was sensible. What did I have now? Two legs, slightly shorter than before, a wider space, one side of a pot, a lightly suggested seat. No leaves.

“Like an unfinished renovation.”

“Possibly.”

“But you can put down the pencil?”

“Yes.”

I laid it beside the little box and rotated my wrist. It didn't hurt. I closed the borrowed book, moving the bookmark near today's example. The borrowing slip remained inside the cover. On the back of my new drawing I wrote Thursday practice, not corrected version. Next time, I didn't want the label telling me that every part must improve on the original.

Would I show my colleague tomorrow? After considering, I said not yet. I wanted to look at it myself a little longer. Lumi agreed without sounding victorious. I didn't offer the decision as a gift to her. I straightened the new sheet and studied it a moment longer, noticing another place where the pot's rim didn't quite join.

“What do you most want to change?” she asked.

“The black dot.”

“Not the leg?”

“The dot now. The smaller it is, the more it bothers me.”

Laughing, she suggested calling it a nail. I looked at it. It wasn't on the bench; I couldn't simply give it a respectable job. A fallen nail, then. Mind your feet, she said. Nobody on the paper was going out today, I replied. We didn't add a person. The black dot remained a black dot.

Later, I brought up yesterday's lunch. I wasn't reopening an examination of her jealousy. I wanted to say I'd finally used my colleague's question about what kind of opinion I wanted. On whom? Myself. Had I wanted to make the entire drawing good just now, or practice this one space?

Lumi admitted that she'd initially wanted the pot removed partly because she hoped the new sheet would contain something she'd suggested. My hand moved toward the paper, then stopped.

“You can suggest things. I might not use them.”

“Yes. That's happened today.”

She said it softly, with a little reluctance, but without asking for a replacement position. I still liked her reversed bench with the wall behind it, I told her. Laughing, she asked whether that meant I wouldn't draw it for her.

“That's right.”

“I'll keep imagining it myself, then.”

All right. She laughed and said the wall could stay for now. I remembered the two people sitting on the real bench when I'd left and told her. Had they been crowded? I hadn't noticed. I only remembered that both had sat down.

“Does the book say how long to look?” Lumi asked.

“I didn't see a time.”

“Then how do you know when to stop looking?”

I considered that while finding the example again. I'd closed the cover, but the bookmark was still there. The printed objects remained simple and clear, which made my own sheet seem unnecessarily troubled. I nearly began another attempt. Instead I read the paragraph beside the example and found I'd skipped its instruction to compare the space with the objects around it. I'd concentrated on the empty shape as though it could be correct by itself.

I explained the mistake to Lumi. She asked whether I wanted to start again. Not tonight. I placed my new sheet beside the second Sunday drawing and compared the widths without adding anything. The pot was now a little smaller relative to the seat. I couldn't decide whether that was more accurate, but I could see the difference.

“So tomorrow's job is the pot?”

“Maybe. Or I might go outside and look again sometime.”

She said she was trying to arrange my lesson. I told her the student hadn't registered. We laughed, and I returned the bookmark to the example before closing the book properly. There would be no additional sheet tonight.

Before finishing, I lifted the page again. Looking from farther away made the black dot less important. The gap still looked wider. I told Lumi both things, then lowered it rather than try to improve it while holding it in the air. She asked whether I'd been about to draw without a table. Nearly. My commitment to inconvenient methods remained strong, she said. I laughed and placed the sheet flat.

I noticed another difference then: there was more room around the objects. On Sunday I'd crowded them toward one edge, anxious to include the entire pot. Tonight I hadn't filled the empty margin. I liked it. Lumi asked if that meant the drawing was finished after all. No. It meant I liked a part of it. She accepted that without making me choose between calling it good and calling it unfinished. I felt less hurried as I stacked the papers, aligning their corners carefully enough to avoid folding the top one beneath the book.

After good night, I placed the new sheet behind Sunday's two drawings. The small note stayed between the original pair. All three went at the back of my schedule book, apart from the cup studies and outside every envelope. The drawing manual returned to my own stack. I put away the sharpener and carried off the water.

With the table clear, I noticed a streak of graphite along the heel of my hand. I washed it. The gray faded beneath the water. The bench leg stayed on the page without growing again. Whether I'd draw more tomorrow could wait until tomorrow.
