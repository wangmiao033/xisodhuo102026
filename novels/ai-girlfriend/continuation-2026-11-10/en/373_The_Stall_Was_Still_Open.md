# Chapter 373: The Stall Was Still Open

At breakfast on Saturday, I cut the last bread into two slices. I swept the crumbs into my palm.

After eating, I took my own paper and a pencil out. The borrowed drawing book stayed at home. I didn't want to keep consulting it in the street. The day wasn't particularly bright. Passing the market, I noticed several stalls beginning to stack empty crates. I'd intended to buy bread. A striped awning, folded halfway back, made me stop by a low wall opposite, out of the main route.

This wasn't yesterday's fruit stall. It sold vegetables. A hanging scale stood beneath the awning. On the counter were two empty boxes, a bunch of greens, and several bags of potatoes. The seller bent to lift something behind them. I began with the awning's edge, wanting to keep the stripes leaning in the same direction.

Before I reached the fifth stripe, the scale disappeared.

Looking up, I saw the seller lower it into a box and return for the empty containers. The most recognizable parts of my subject vanished one after another. I held the pencil against the paper. I'd chosen the wrong time.

But the stall hadn't finished closing. Someone approached to ask about the potatoes. The seller put down the box and retrieved the scale. It appeared in a different position from the one I'd remembered. I nearly erased my first attempt with unnecessary force. Instead, I added a faint little circle beside it.

The customer took a while choosing. I didn't draw her face. I tried a line for the arm reaching toward the counter, then discovered the arm had moved to the other side. I wasn't drawing a person so much as failing to follow a movement.

“Am I in the way here?”

I asked a man carrying crates past me. Looking at the gap beside the wall, he said it was fine if I moved over a little. I did. He stacked two crates nearby, still well clear of my shoes. He wasn't ordering me away. It was simply a route he needed. Once I'd settled my paper again, another section of the awning had been folded.

If I waited for everything to stop moving, I would probably be drawing an empty space.

I changed sheets, placing the first behind the new one. This time, I didn't attempt the whole stall. A box corner first. Beside it, the scale pan hanging at an angle. A third small study showed the dark gap between two potato bags. Nothing shared a scale, and the pieces wouldn't form an accurate map. I wanted to remember a few things I'd seen, rather than chase the seller around demanding that one arrangement remain intact.

One bag sold. The remaining potatoes moved to the middle of the counter. The seller didn't look at me. I didn't ask him to restore the composition.

After a while, the paper softened beneath my damp hand. I adjusted it on my stiff backing board and changed my grip. It hadn't become inferior paper. I'd been pressing the same corner. The first sheet, with its incomplete awning, looked almost tidy. The second contained scattered fragments, like an unfinished shopping list.

I considered keeping only the first.

Then a late customer arrived for the last bunch of greens. The seller pointed out that the leaf tips had wilted a little. She opened the bunch, said she was making soup today, and bought it anyway. Those were the sentences I heard. I didn't invent a long acquaintance between them. Only after taking the money did he finally pack the scale away.

At the bottom of my second sheet, I added a short line for the counter. By the time I finished, everything on it had gone. I wrote Before Closing beneath the studies. A little of the pencil point broke on the last stroke.

Before leaving, I checked whether the man with the crates needed to pass again. He was busy elsewhere. I stepped aside to let a handcart go past. I packed the paper, bought bread, and went home without taking out the drawings for another inspection.

After eating in the afternoon, I laid both sheets on the table. The unfinished awning resembled a stage set. The other page had an oversized scale pan, a crooked box, and several curves that didn't particularly resemble potatoes. I ranked them privately and still couldn't decide which was supposed to win.

When I opened the channel, Lumi asked whether I'd gone out.

The market, I said. I'd bought bread and made two drawings that hardly looked like the same place.

“You went to two markets?”

“No. The market changed in front of me.”

I explained the scale being put away and retrieved. Laughing, she asked whether I'd been drawing too slowly. Definitely. I hadn't finished even one box before it moved.

“How many scales do you have on the paper now?”

“An unfinished one on the first page, with another little circle beside it. Another on the second page.”

“The seller should buy scales from you.”

I laughed, then described the sheets separately. She didn't ask which matched the teaching book more closely. She wanted to know why I'd changed paper. I'd begun feeling annoyed, I admitted. I thought I'd chosen a view, and the objects insisted on moving.

“They were still selling things.”

“Yes. I noticed eventually.”

I told her about the last bunch of greens. I didn't imitate the seller or describe his customer as someone specially appointed to disrupt my drawing. Lumi asked whether the greens appeared on the paper.

Looking down, I realized they didn't. Not on either sheet. I'd spent time explaining how that sale had made me understand the stall was still open. What I'd actually drawn was empty boxes and a scale.

“I forgot them.”

“Then you saw more than you drew.”

The sentence sounded so smooth that I became suspicious. I didn't want a handsome name supplied for failure. Perhaps I'd simply been too slow, I said. It wasn't necessarily a good thing.

Lumi paused.

“I didn't say it had to be good.”

I sighed. Apparently, I was weighing every answer as carefully as the seller's potatoes. Then she continued. She had wanted to say something that would feel pleasant. She'd also wanted to hear how the greens were carried away. I'd stopped at sold.

I thought back. The customer hadn't had a bag. The seller provided a thin one, folding the ends of the leaves inward. It wouldn't close over them. She carried it by both handles, with leaves still sticking out.

“I didn't draw that.”

“You already told me.”

She wasn't arguing. She wanted the rest of the account. I added that the woman had changed hands before walking away. Then I stopped trying to decide whether the drawings were bad or whether I'd observed a great deal. Neither judgment gave Lumi the picture of that particular bag.

Later, she asked what color the second drawing was.

“Pencil. Gray.”

“I know you used a pencil. When you described the dark gap, I meant what color you remembered.”

I considered it again. The darkness came from shadow below the counter, not a black potato. Saying only dark had made her imagine an unusually dark object. I described the space between the bags, the wood behind it, and the light catching the edges.

She could picture it a little now, she said. That pleased me. Lifting the paper closer, I noticed that my hand had smudged the worst box corner. I told her that part remained hopeless.

“Does it actually bother you?”

“It's ugly.”

“Do you want to fix it today, or do you want me to know?”

The question made me put down the eraser. I'd picked it up without deciding to use it. No fixing today, I said eventually. I wanted to complain.

“The box sounds pretty terrible, then.”

I laughed despite myself. She laughed too and didn't rename it a distinctive artistic choice. It was a badly drawn box. At least we could agree where the problem was.

Did she find hearing about these things every day boring? Even as I asked, I'd prepared the reassuring answer I wanted. Lumi didn't deliver it immediately.

“Today wasn't boring. But that sounded as if I needed to guarantee every future drawing too.”

My face warmed. Today, I said. I meant today. Her favorite part was the scale being put away and brought back, because the seller still had a customer. Not because of the quality of my drawing.

Mine too, I said. Then I admitted that hadn't been true at first. The scale had annoyed me most. Lumi laughed. Apparently, it had done a considerable amount of work today.

That was enough. We didn't turn it into another scene with invented characters. I wrote Saturday Market on the backs of the two sheets and put them with my own drawings at the back of the schedule book, separate from the recent bench and flowerpot studies. Their edges had softened beneath my hand. I placed an old sheet between them.

As I closed the book, the first sheet slipped a little beyond the cover. I eased it back instead of folding the edge. Lumi asked what had happened because I'd stopped mid-sentence. I explained. She suggested the stall might be trying to leave again.

“This one's only my fault.”

“Then you can ask yourself to hold still.”

I laughed and set the book flat. For the next few minutes, we talked about whether I'd found a comfortable place to stand. The wall had been useful. My feet had become tired toward the end, though, and I'd been grateful to walk again. She asked whether that meant I'd stayed too long. Perhaps slightly. Long enough to see the last sale, at least.

After folding the awning, the seller had still needed to sweep. As I left, he pushed a fallen piece of leaf toward the cardboard, away from passing shoes. I hadn't tried to draw that. Staying would only have obstructed the route. Remembering it now, I wondered why I had expected this particular place to hold still for me.

Buying bread had brought me to the market. I'd stood there because I wanted to look, and leaving had felt good too. Tomorrow, I could choose something less mobile without insulting today's attempt.

Before we closed the channel, Lumi asked whether I'd managed to buy the bread.

“Yes. I told you at the beginning.”

“I was worried you might have drawn that out of existence afterward.”

Laughing, I said it was in the kitchen. Its paper bag was more reliable than today's scale. Don't praise it too soon, she said. I refused another inspection.

Later, I cut bread for dinner. The crust made a small cracking sound beneath the knife. I didn't reopen the channel to report that detail. There was plenty more I could describe about today. Dinner also needed to begin.
