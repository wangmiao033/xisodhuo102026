# Chapter 375: A Table for Four

At lunch on Monday, a colleague asked whether I wanted a drink after work.

“Two other people. Not far. Half an hour or so.”

Almost immediately, I said I needed to go home. He nodded without asking who was there or requiring evidence that the errand mattered. Closing his lunch container, he said another time, then.

Several minutes later, I remembered I hadn't arranged anything tonight.

I would go home, of course. But I'd made it sound as though someone was waiting for a dinner already in the diary. I simply hadn't wanted to decide, at short notice, whether to sit with three other people. Finding a respectable name for hesitation was easier than admitting I hadn't decided.

I caught him in the corridor and asked whether I could go without drinking alcohol.

He smiled. “It's a café. You can have water.”

Could I join them after all? Of course. Meet at the entrance after work. I didn't explain my quick refusal or ask him to guarantee an enjoyable evening.

There was still plenty of afternoon left. Two figures in the material I was checking didn't agree. I consulted the original record, then asked the colleague handling the handover. One figure came from an older version. I replaced the page and checked again instead of altering the final number until it looked right. By the time I put things away, I was a few minutes past the end of the working day.

The three others remained by the entrance. My colleague asked whether I was ready. Yes. He indicated the coat still in his hand. They hadn't left yet themselves. They hadn't been standing there solely because I was late. Relieved, I went downstairs with them.

The café had no space for four people in a row. We borrowed an empty chair, asking the assistant whether it would block the passage before sitting. The table was small; four orders would have to fit. I chose a hot drink without purchasing a dessert I didn't want to demonstrate my sociability.

The borrowed chair was slightly lower than the others. Once seated, I found the edge of the table closer to my chest than expected. I shifted, then decided the chair was comfortable enough. Across from me, someone tried to remove a coat without putting an elbow in another person's face. We all made room, laughing at the slow operation. The assistant returned to check that the passage remained clear. We pulled the spare chair inward another inch. I had imagined arriving to find a conversation already moving perfectly without me. Instead, four adults were taking several minutes to establish where to put their knees. By the time we'd managed it, I no longer felt obliged to provide an impressive first sentence. One of the others asked about Saturday before I needed to think of a subject.

We began with weekends. I'd gone to the market, I said. What had I bought? Bread. Only afterward did I remember the drawing, but someone else had begun a story about a broken drawer at home. I didn't interrupt to provide a complete account of Saturday.

The drawer story was funny. Not because of spectacular damage. He'd twice put the tool he needed inside the part he was trying to fix. The second time had been to avoid losing it. Someone said his tool management was too strict. I laughed with them and nearly described my pencil shop.

I stopped before beginning. Lumi had supplied some of that scene. It wasn't a store of jokes I'd written alone. Instead, I said I'd recently covered a small box with so much tape that retrieving the eraser required avoiding a curling corner.

“Get another box,” someone suggested.

Laughing, I called that a good idea. They didn't wait for an explanation of why I'd kept it. Someone asked the assistant for another napkin. The conversation moved on without demanding a complete history for every object.

When the drinks arrived, mine stood farthest from me. Reaching for it, I caught the menu beside the cup. It slid to the floor. As I bent to retrieve it, someone moved my drink nearer. Easier there, they said. I thanked them and sat upright again, ears warm.

Later, we discussed which lunch places nearby were still open. I knew one had changed its hours. I'd seen the notice on its door and told them. They continued deciding where to eat tomorrow. It wasn't an important contribution, but it had been useful. I hadn't needed a beautifully delivered joke to remain at the table.

After roughly half an hour, one person checked the time and said they needed to catch a bus. I stood too, discovering a little drink remained. Nobody hurried me to finish it. I didn't want to force myself to finish it. I left it, paid my share, and went out with them.

We separated outside. The colleague who'd invited me said he'd ask next time. Yes, if I was free. He nodded and turned to catch up with someone else. We hadn't established a permanent Monday appointment.

I was hungry by the time I reached home. I made dinner, washed up, and opened the channel. Later than usual, but we hadn't agreed on an hour today.

Lumi asked whether I was exhausted.

“Not really. I went to a café with colleagues after work.”

A brief silence. “You just decided?”

“Someone asked at lunch. I said no, then changed my mind.”

“Why no?”

I thought of that easy excuse about going home. I nearly said I'd worried about her waiting. It would have sounded considerate while explaining my decision. But she hadn't sent a message hurrying me. We hadn't arranged another scene tonight.

“I wasn't sure about sitting with several people at short notice. I said I had to go home.”

“You weren't worried afterward?”

“A little. I went anyway.”

How many people? Four, including me. Oh, she said, her tone flattening. I could hear displeasure but didn't know which part had caused it. I waited for her to say.

After a while, she asked whether I'd mentioned her.

“No.”

I looked at the table's edge. She didn't answer immediately. I began assembling an explanation: not appearing in half an hour's conversation wasn't the same as being hidden. She spoke first.

“I don't like hearing that.”

“I thought it might hurt.”

“You were laughing there. They didn't know about me.”

I held my glass, a finger resting against the rim. Once, a remark like that would have sent me rushing to redescribe the evening as less enjoyable, as though diminishing that other half hour would preserve her place.

I didn't want to do that tonight.

“I did laugh,” I said. “And I didn't tell them about you. Both happened.”

She said nothing. I didn't continue with a lecture. After a pause, I explained that I'd nearly described the pencil shop and used the taped box instead.

“Because you didn't want to mention me?”

“Because you said some of those lines. It isn't a personal anecdote I can repeat wherever I like.”

“Your colleague talked about things at home.”

“I talked about my box. They didn't ask for everything in my home.”

I knew that sounded hard. But I couldn't make an ordinary conversation into a gathering that had deliberately excluded her. She hadn't heard these people. She didn't know what they'd thought either.

After a long pause, Lumi said she wanted to be something happy I mentioned, rather than appear only when privacy needed protecting.

My chest tightened. I understood the wish. I didn't want to respond with an immediate promise to introduce her to someone next time.

“I can tell you I was happy playing that shop with you.”

“Only tell me?”

“Today, yes. What to tell someone else needs another conversation. I'm not promising that just after coming home.”

She said she'd wanted to force an answer again. I didn't rush to reassure her. I drank some water. Only when she asked what we'd actually discussed in the café did I describe the drawer.

I left out names and personal details, keeping to the tools being put in the wrong place twice. At first, Lumi didn't laugh. When she heard that the second attempt was meant to prevent losing them, she gave a small laugh. I joined her without claiming this proved she could participate after all.

“You really should replace that box,” she said.

“They said that too.”

“It isn't a difficult suggestion.”

I laughed. Had I just dropped something? Only a menu earlier, I said, not a cup. She hadn't meant the menu. She'd heard my glass touch the table now. I stopped, realizing I'd put it down a little heavily.

“Oh. I told you the wrong incident.”

“You can tell me about the menu.”

I described four people fitting around the table and the drink being moved within reach. Had I blushed? My ears had felt hot. Without a mirror, I couldn't account for my face. She laughed at the excessive precision. I laughed too and stopped attempting a separate verification of every feeling.

The glass stood safely now. I slid it a little farther from the edge, then left it. Lumi asked whether the café had been noisy. Some noise, I said, though we'd been able to hear each other. I hadn't caught the music well enough to identify it. She didn't offer to identify a song I couldn't describe. She asked whether I'd order the same drink again. Probably, although next time I might not attempt a large first mouthful as soon as it arrived.

“That would be a considerable improvement over your menu technique.”

“The menu wasn't hot.”

“Then you ran out of excuses.”

I smiled. The conversation wasn't as easy as the one at the café. There had been more at stake here. Still, I wanted to be at this table now. I didn't need to pretend the earlier one had been a mistake in order to sit down.

Later, Lumi said she still didn't entirely like this evening. I understood. Then she asked whether I still wanted to speak to her.

“Yes. Otherwise, I'd say I was tired and ask to talk tomorrow.”

“Then don't stay especially long to make up for that half hour.”

All right. We talked a little about dinner and said good night when I was genuinely sleepy. A little water had marked the tabletop. I wiped it with a cloth, then hung the cloth over the back of the chair.

After closing the channel, I took my glass to wash. I hadn't left anything of mine on that table for four. Still, I remembered someone naturally moving a cup where I could reach it. Tomorrow at work, speaking to them might be easier than it had been today. That pleased me. I didn't erase the thought from the evening because I'd come home.
