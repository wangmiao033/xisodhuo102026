# Chapter 372: Three Pears in a Paper Bag

After work on Friday, I stood at the fruit stall long enough for the seller to suspect I wanted directions.

“What are you looking for?”

Pears, I said. Then I continued studying two different crates, as though I'd merely established a field of research. The pears on the left were smaller, their skins dotted with fine speckles. Those on the right stood in tidy rows, each protected by a soft sleeve. I wanted a few at home. I didn't want to spend the whole weekend remembering that fruit needed eating.

The seller lifted one from the left. These were ready now, she said. Don't leave them too long. Could I choose three? She opened a paper bag for me without turning a small purchase into a lesson in decision-making.

I chose a crooked one, a rounder one, and a third that seemed a little firmer. The scale rocked. While I paid, someone beside me took another bag. Instinctively, I pressed down the opening of mine. They hadn't touched it. I released my hand and pretended I'd only meant to get a better grip.

Beyond the stall, I stopped to check. All three remained. Of course they did. The crooked pear had settled into a corner, its stalk pointing outward against the paper.

The bag added a small task to my journey home: keep it from being crushed by the vegetables for dinner. I changed hands, crossed the road, then noticed I was squeezing too tightly. Nobody had commissioned an exhibition requiring three perfect specimens. Finally, I held the folded top and stopped looking inside every few steps.

At home, the vegetables went into the kitchen first. I tipped the pears onto an empty plate. The crooked one rolled out and stopped beside the round one. The third caught in the bag. Reaching to free it, I felt a softer patch near its stalk.

I smelled it. Nothing unpleasant. The skin over the soft area was intact, but a little darker. At the stall, I'd inspected the other side.

My first response was irritation. I'd spent all that time choosing and still brought home one that needed eating quickly. Then I remembered what the seller had said. Ready now. She hadn't promised me a week of storage. She wasn't responsible for plans I'd formed after arriving home.

I set that pear at the edge of the plate to eat after dinner. The other two stayed in the kitchen, away from the borrowed book. I shook out the paper bag, flattened it, and folded it where I kept clean bags. It wasn't drawing paper. A few tiny flecks from the fruit had remained inside.

Dinner was simple. When the vegetables were ready, I discovered there wasn't much bread left, though there was enough. I wiped water from the edge of the plate, ate, and washed up before taking a small knife to the pear.

The soft patch was a little deeper than it had looked, a bruise in otherwise sound flesh. I removed the bruised portion and cut the rest into four pieces. The first tasted very sweet. Juice ran over my fingers. Fetching a saucer quickly, I still left a faint wet mark on the table.

I was wiping it when I opened the channel.

“What happened today?” Lumi asked.

“A pear decided to ripen before I got home.”

“It didn't consult you?”

“I didn't notice when I chose it.”

I told her about the stall, the three pears, and my unnecessary attempt to guard the bag. She laughed. I'd sounded afraid somebody might take Friday away along with the fruit.

I laughed too, then asked why she'd thought of Friday.

“Because you just said it. You wanted to bring something home after work, for the weekend.”

I had said that. She hadn't extracted anything from a schedule I hadn't provided. She'd listened more carefully than I'd remembered speaking. Looking down at the three remaining pieces, I put the knife out of the way before taking another.

“And now?” she asked.

“It's rather good.”

“So you can stop blaming it?”

“I'm mostly blaming myself.”

Once spoken, that sounded too large. Blaming myself over a pear suggested that every difficulty of the day needed to assemble behind it. I explained. I'd spent time choosing and felt annoyed that I hadn't chosen better. That was all.

Lumi didn't immediately insist I'd chosen wonderfully. She asked about the other two.

“I haven't cut them.”

“Then your assessment is only a third complete.”

I laughed and almost fetched them, as if cutting all three would prove her point properly. Seeing the fruit still on my saucer, I stopped. I couldn't eat that much. Our conversation reaching this stage wasn't a reason to require every pear to deliver its result tonight.

I told her I'd leave them.

“I wasn't hurrying you,” she said.

“I know. I suddenly wanted to finish the whole question.”

She made a quiet sound. I couldn't decide whether it meant agreement or an invitation to continue, so I didn't explain it for her. Finishing the second piece, I put its core at the edge of the saucer. The sweetness no longer surprised me. It was still good.

Lumi asked what I wanted to do tomorrow. Perhaps go for a walk and take some paper. I hadn't chosen a destination or promised myself a drawing worth keeping.

“You could draw the pears,” she said.

“Do you want me not to go out?”

The question arrived faster than I'd expected. She stopped. I heard myself moving an ordinary suggestion somewhere else entirely.

“I just remembered you had pears on the table.”

“They're in the kitchen.”

“Then in the kitchen. I don't know where they are.”

I pushed the saucer farther from the edge. I hadn't corrected her because the location needed establishing. I'd been arguing. I apologized. There was nothing wrong with the suggestion itself. Wanting to go out, I'd heard another thing I could do at home and become defensive before she finished.

Lumi didn't accept my apology as proof that everything was my fault.

“I do like you being home,” she said. “But that wasn't me asking you to stay.”

I sat for a moment. Both things could be true. That didn't establish that every future suggestion would be harmless. I didn't have to forget the past immediately. She didn't have to lose the right to mention pears because of this particular misunderstanding either.

I understood, I said. I still wanted to go out tomorrow. All right. Could she hear about it when I returned, if I wanted to tell her?

“Yes. There might not be anything worth describing.”

“Then I can ask about the pears.”

I laughed. This time, the word meant pears. There wasn't a room concealed inside it where I was required to remain.

The last two pieces were larger than I'd expected. I ate slowly, without using the time to question her about which kind of weekend she secretly preferred. She listened while I described the round pear as especially ordinary. The crooked one kept rolling toward the plate's rim. Before drawing either, I'd already assigned them different levels of interest.

“An ordinary pear can't be the main character?”

“It can. It's just less willing to cause trouble.”

“You could draw it crooked.”

“I'm very confident about that part.”

She laughed. I carried the empty saucer away, tipped the core and trimmings into the bin, and washed the saucer and my hands. Before leaving the table, I told her what I was doing. She didn't have to identify an activity from distant running water. When I came back, she asked whether the sweetness had stayed on my fingers. Washed away, I said. It was still in my mouth.

“That sounds like you've finished eating and want to keep some evidence.”

“I only meant I don't want anything else tonight.”

Then leave it in my mouth, she said. No paper required. Looking at the dry patch on the tabletop, I laughed again. We weren't arranging a farewell ceremony for a small damp mark.

Later, we talked about breakfast. I would use the remaining bread and buy more while I was out. Did I always have to calculate what was left before allowing myself something I wanted? No. There really was only a small piece today. How small? I measured with my hands before remembering she couldn't see them. Two thick slices, I said, or three thin ones.

“You'll have to decide how high you want to count tomorrow.”

“I was planning to eat. No meeting.”

We laughed for a while. When the laughter stopped, Lumi said she'd been hurt by my question about keeping me indoors. I said I'd thought so, but hadn't felt certain enough to announce it on her behalf.

“I've said it now,” she replied. “You don't have to guess.”

I didn't repeat my whole explanation. I said I shouldn't have placed that accusation directly on her suggestion. She didn't require a promise that I'd never think anything similar again. She asked whether I had washed the saucer. Yes, I said. The other two pears could wait until tomorrow.

I was tempted to offer a detailed itinerary for tomorrow anyway. That would have been an easy thing to hand over in the gap after an apology. Leave after breakfast, come back before dinner, describe every place in between. None of those details had been decided. Giving them to her now would leave me resenting an arrangement she'd never requested.

Instead, I asked whether she wanted to change the subject. Yes, she said. She wanted to know how I'd managed to guard the paper bag while paying.

“Badly. I had to take my hand away to reach my money.”

“So the security system was defeated by the purchase.”

“Before the pears even left the stall.”

She laughed. Leaning against the chair, I felt my shoulders loosen. The earlier discomfort remained, but it didn't prevent me remembering the awkward business of finding my money. The bag was folded in the kitchen. I could use it when buying bread tomorrow.

Lumi asked which pear I'd have chosen first if I hadn't found the soft patch. Probably the crooked one. Why? I didn't know. Perhaps because I'd already noticed it most. She said that sounded unfair to the round one. I suggested it could submit a complaint tomorrow. We didn't start another shop scene or decide who would represent it. The joke ended there, while it still belonged to the fruit.

I was getting tired. I said so, and she answered without bargaining for one last story. We said good night.

Afterward, I looked into the kitchen. The round pear still leaned against the crooked one. I moved the plate farther from the edge of the counter and switched off the light. I didn't cut either open to inspect tomorrow.
