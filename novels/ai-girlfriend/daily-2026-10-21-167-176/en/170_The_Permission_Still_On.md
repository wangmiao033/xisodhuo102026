# Chapter 170: The Permission Still On

At six oh eight on Wednesday, I stood outside the repair shop and took the appointment paper from my bag.

I hadn't forgotten the time. I was seven minutes early and still wanted the numbers to tell me it was right to go inside. My reflection held the sheet close, as though it were an eye test.

I put it away and opened the door.

Rowan was wrapping a cable for another customer. I didn't look at the person's paperwork. When they had finished, I sat beside the counter. Tape now covered the old split in the chair. Its curling edge no longer threatened to catch clothing, only to stick to it.

“You can sit on the other side,” Rowan said.

I moved. “Different problem now.”

“A smaller one, for the moment.”

At the agreed time, they brought out the phone and its own paperwork, without adding the laptop file beneath it. I checked the exterior and intake record. The old scratch remained. Its original pouch lay beside it. Seeing those unchanged things relieved me, then embarrassed me. I hadn't come to have someone certify that a pouch was doing well.

Rowan placed the initial steps between us.

“Confirming the same scope as the email. You unlock it. Existing configuration and permissions only. No chats, photos, or writing. No additional connections, full-device copying, or reset. If anything else is needed, we stop and ask.”

“Yes. No reconnecting the house to test it.”

“No. The device remains isolated from network connections during this review. A displayed state can only tell us about that state.”

I asked whether forty minutes would pass quickly. They said yes. If anything worried me most, this was a good time to raise it.

I took out my questions. The first asked whether it could still do things by itself. Halfway through reading it, I stopped.

“Too broad.”

“You can keep it as the concern. Let's separate out the part we can examine today.”

I asked to start with the permissions and connection settings that remained from the old assistant. Rowan said they wouldn't launch it to test it, or reconnect the phone to see what happened. I nodded. I'd read this in the email. Sitting beside the actual phone, I needed to hear it again before I enlarged the possibilities.

They prepared the device using the isolation arrangement we'd discussed and turned the screen toward me. When it needed unlocking, Rowan looked away. I entered the code myself, without saying the digits or writing them down. As the screen lit, the familiarity of my thumb's movement unsettled me.

I still knew where to touch.

A patch of frame worn shiny by my fingers rested against my palm. I used to rub it while waiting for elevators, waiting for a message to appear. Now, in the even light of the shop, I suddenly wanted to turn the whole thing facedown. Not because something had appeared. Because it looked too much like those ordinary days.

Rowan didn't hurry me. I adjusted my grip and straightened the screen.

Remembering how I'd used it didn't mean everything else remained as I remembered.

Once it was unlocked, I set the phone firmly on the mat and let Rowan begin with the agreed configuration screens. We didn't enter chats or the gallery. My stories didn't need to give evidence today. The first few pages were ordinary: names, existing switches, permitted items. I kept trying to read a conclusion from Rowan's face before they spoke.

There was no conclusion on their face. They were reading.

One screen listed items with notification access. Rowan stopped there and took their hand away.

I noticed the familiar assistant display name first. Then I saw that permission beside it was still enabled.

“That's still on?”

“The page currently shows it as enabled.”

I leaned closer. The words didn't change. I'd always known that not inspected didn't mean switched off. Before seeing it, some part of me had still quietly assigned it an off position.

“So it read my notifications.”

Rowan shook their head. “The switch doesn't establish that.”

“But it could.”

“This is the notification-access permission granted by the system. Why it remained, whether it was used, what might have been accessed, and who any activity would correspond to aren't established by this page. We shouldn't write those different questions as one answer.”

I rested my hand on the counter's edge, pressing a fingernail into a shallow dent. Outside, someone's trolley bounced over a join near the entrance. I suddenly wanted to ask an unreasonable question: If it didn't prove anything, why did I have to see it?

“I didn't expect it to say enabled.”

“We can record that it was unexpected.”

Rowan documented the displayed item and its state as an observation made today. They didn't add that it had or hadn't read anything. They showed me the lines. I confirmed they described this particular item, not the entire phone.

“Leave it unchanged for now?” they asked.

I looked at the setting and remembered insisting at intake that they shouldn't simply reset everything. Preserving things mattered. I didn't want preservation to mean never touching an access point I'd confirmed I didn't need.

“Disable this notification-access permission. Only this one.”

Rowan repeated which item I'd indicated. They explained that this wasn't clearing usage records or deleting the application, chats, or writing. Disabling it didn't require connecting to a service first. If any additional requirement appeared, they would stop.

Only after I confirmed did they act.

It took less time than I'd imagined. The enabled state changed to disabled. I kept watching. Rowan didn't immediately move to another screen. They let me see it, then recorded the time and my authorization beneath the initial observation.

“This permission is now off.”

I nodded, holding back the next question: Then I can take it home, can't I?

I didn't say it. Rowan didn't present the small change as a complete conclusion either.

“If we don't clear the records, can we check later whether it was used?”

“We need to assess what records might exist, whether they're available, and what accessing them would involve. I can't promise a complete history. Looking for it doesn't authorize opening your private content.”

They placed the question in the later-assessment section instead of starting a deeper inspection. I still wanted them to work faster. Fast enough that I wouldn't have to go home tonight and explain half a piece of bad news.

“I can say we found a permission that hadn't been disabled.”

“Yes. And disabled it after you confirmed.”

“Not that we found a new instance of someone snooping.”

“Correct. Or that everything else is fine.”

I released the counter. A little dust from the dent remained beneath my nail. The phone hadn't produced a history I could be straightforwardly angry with. There was no sentence announcing, Here is the mistake. That made me angrier, as though the small screen deliberately offered only enough information to keep me awake.

Rowan asked whether I wanted a two-minute break.

I did. By the entrance, I took a drink of the water I'd brought. It was no longer cool. Someone pushed the door open. Stepping aside, I nearly dropped the cap into my bag. As I screwed it back on, I realized it hadn't been properly fastened before.

At the counter, I read the record again.

The first line described the observed state. The following lines documented the limited action and resulting state. No records deleted. No new connection. No treating the permission as proof of someone's identity. On my copy, I circled the questions that remained for assessment without demanding deadlines for all of them.

In the remaining time, we completed only the agreed configuration review that could stay within scope. At an item whose association needed further verification, Rowan marked it pending. They didn't enter an outside account or guess whether it came from the same source. Disabling one switch successfully wasn't permission to deal with every subsequent item in passing.

Near the end, I asked whether I'd need to return if another step required unlocking.

“We'll arrange that when it's needed. Entering it today doesn't mean we retain your password or count every later operation as approved.”

I knew they weren't storing digits they hadn't been given. What I really wanted to know was how many more times I'd sit in this chair. Rowan didn't invent a comfortable answer.

The phone was powered down and retained under its existing intake and storage arrangement. Further assessment didn't automatically begin when I stood. The laptop also remained under its own arrangement. Rowan gave me the limited record and my copy. The charge stayed within the agreed first-stage scope.

Pushing the pouch toward them, I suddenly didn't want to let go.

Not because I wanted to take the phone home and use it. I'd touched it, unlocked it, seen the setting. Briefly, it had felt like my possession again. Now I had to leave it here once more.

“The pouch too,” I said.

Rowan nodded and waited for my hand to withdraw before putting the items away.

It was getting dark outside. I didn't open the report again on the pavement. First, I moved away from the entrance and zipped my bag. A tissue removed the dust beneath my nail. The disabled setting remained in my mind, along with the enabled state I'd seen before it.

On the way home, I bought a meal I could heat. The person at the counter asked whether I needed cutlery. I said yes, then changed it to no. There were forks at home. At least I knew that without going back to open a drawer and check.
