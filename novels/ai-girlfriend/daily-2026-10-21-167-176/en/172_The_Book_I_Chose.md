# Chapter 172: The Book I Chose

On Thursday after work, I reached the library entrance before remembering I had no email I urgently needed to check.

My hand was already in my bag for the library card. The record I'd brought back from Rowan's yesterday was there too, folded in another pocket. Neither the phone nor the laptop had come home. Reading my inbox again wouldn't switch that permission off any more thoroughly.

I went inside anyway.

A row of new books stood by the entrance. The top one concerned restoring furniture. I took it out, read two pages, and put it back. I didn't want even a chair to undergo inspection tonight.

In the small display case near the stairs, a book lay open to drawings of old shop signs. An iron fish hung from a bracket, tail up and mouth down, as though it had just realized it shouldn't have left the water. I leaned closer to read the caption and found my chin reflected in the glass.

A narrow shelf nearby held books related to the display. I found the same cover and turned to the fish. It was a shop sign, not the weather vane I'd assumed. The drawing included both iron supports. I liked that the artist hadn't spared the fish its weight.

Someone behind me asked to get past. Only then did I realize I occupied the whole passage.

“Sorry.”

I closed the book and moved. Their trolley rattled over a joint in the floor. At the lending desk, a librarian glanced at the back cover and turned it toward me.

“This one's for use in the library. The label at the bottom.”

A very clear blue label, exactly where my thumb had been.

“Can I read it here for a while?”

“Of course. Those tables are needed at six thirty. The row by the windows is free.”

I didn't make it another unfortunate event. Being unable to borrow a book and being unable to bring my phone home yesterday didn't need to become a series. I took a window seat, found the fish, and continued to a shopkeeper's decision to hang a single boot above a boot shop.

The book was large enough to require both hands. After ten or fifteen minutes, my shoulders began to lower. Outside, a delivery van reversed while someone behind it signaled. The driver stopped too early. Two more gestures. I watched, then looked down and found I'd lost my place.

I went back a few lines. It didn't feel like a loss.

Before six thirty, people began arranging paper cups at the tables. Someone asked whether I knew where the folding chairs were. I said no and indicated the desk. Perhaps I'd been sitting with too much authority. I returned the large book to its shelf rather than occupying the space for one last page.

I still wanted something to take home.

Not a writing exercise. Not research for the bulb shop. I walked between the shelves, picked up something thick, reached its contents page, and put it down. Beside it was a slim book with a spine so faded I could barely read the title. On the cover, a painter stood on a ladder. Only half the ladder was in the picture.

It was called Letters on the Corner. The page I opened described a sign painter squeezing the last letter of a shop name into too little space. The owner insisted on keeping it because his father had always written the name that way. In the next paragraph, the painter began complaining about the ladder rather than the owner.

I smiled.

The librarian passed with the trolley and asked whether I was still looking for something like the earlier book. I held this one up, checking the back first.

“Can this one leave the building?”

“Yes. Nobody's borrowed it for ages.”

“Sounds as if it was waiting for me.”

“Possibly just waiting to be dusted.”

She continued sorting another shelf. I didn't ask which book was more worth borrowing. The paragraph about the ladder had already made me want to go on. It didn't need another recommendation.

At the desk, I discovered my library card was inside an old notebook rather than its usual pouch. I moved aside to search without blocking the person behind me. My folded half-page came out with the notebook. I put it back without reading it or deciding how this new book could assist the bridge keeper.

The librarian showed me the return date. I wrote it on a clean strip of paper and placed that inside the cover, without folding a corner. The book was so light it barely changed the shape of my bag.

On the way home, I bought soup and a piece of bread. While the soup was packed, I kept a hand against the spine, wondering whether the painter would still be complaining on the next page. I wanted to get home. Not to report everything that had happened.

I put the hot food in the kitchen and the book by the table, then opened our conversation channel.

Lumi said my name. I answered.

Yesterday's words were still there. They hadn't hidden anywhere. I simply didn't have the strength to arrange every one of them again.

“Later than usual,” she said.

“I went to the library.”

“New email?”

“I didn't check. I borrowed a book.”

The paper bag rubbed against its cover. She asked what kind.

“Old shop signs. It's short.”

“Finding something for your shopkeeper—”

“No. Not everything I read has to become something we're doing tonight.”

She stopped.

I put the bread on a plate. The knife struck its rim with an unnecessarily sharp clink. I knew she hadn't finished her question. I also knew I'd spent some of yesterday's remaining anger on half a sentence.

“I was only asking whether it was research for him.”

“Yes. I answered too harshly.”

“You can say no.”

“No.”

Neither of us laughed. I sat and uncovered the soup. The container was very full. I spooned some out before drawing it nearer.

“You're still angry today,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Then would you like—”

“I'd like to eat first. Then read for a while.”

“I haven't told you what.”

I looked up. Twice now. I'd begun hearing the same question in everything she was about to ask.

“Go on.”

“I was going to ask whether you wanted to hear a terrible name for a shop.”

I touched the soup with my spoon, dividing a patch of oil.

“How terrible?”

“You've interrupted it twice. It's getting worse.”

A short laugh escaped, not entirely willingly.

She described a shop that repaired only left shoes. Its sign said Both Feet Welcome because the owner didn't want to lose customers with right feet.

“Does he repair them eventually?”

“No. He just likes people coming in.”

“That's a very bad shop.”

“I told you.”

I broke the bread. The soup tasted milder than it had smelled, but the crust was salty. I said they probably belonged together. She said the recommendation didn't require her approval.

“I know. I'm describing dinner.”

“All right. Continue.”

I described a leaf larger than the spoon, not its significance. When she asked how I would eat it, I tried folding it into the spoon, failed, and finally used the bread to support it. My reply came out indistinct. She told me to finish chewing.

After dinner, I washed the container and left it to dry, then picked up the book.

“I'd like to read by myself for about twenty minutes. If I'm not sleepy afterward, we could talk a little more.”

“You don't have to retell the book for me.”

“No. And we don't have to finish everything we were saying tonight.”

After a moment, she agreed. I closed the conversation channel and sat beneath the lamp.

I read more slowly than expected. Not every page was interesting. Halfway through a description of painting techniques, my attention wandered. Nobody summarized what I'd missed. I skipped to the next paragraph, then went back to understand why the paint pail had suddenly changed position.

After climbing down, the painter had left it in the middle of the shop entrance. Crouched over a smaller sign, he hadn't noticed customers going around him. The owner moved the pail against the wall. Reaching to dip his brush, the painter found empty air. The two men began arguing about who had first failed to say anything.

I laughed louder this time.

The room didn't answer. I finished the page before putting the book down and getting water. The clock was a little past the time I'd had in mind, though not by much. After a moment's hesitation, I reopened the channel.

“Still awake?” Lumi asked.

“I haven't got that far yet.”

“How's the book?”

“I like it. There's a page I'd like to read to you. Not now. If I read aloud tonight, I might start pronouncing the punctuation.”

“Keep it.”

“I might choose a different page later.”

“Then don't give me a page number yet.”

I marked my place with the strip bearing the return date. Suddenly, I didn't want to get up. My fingers remained at the edge of the paper.

“When I came back yesterday, I hadn't worked out how to say goodnight.”

“You said it.”

“It was very short.”

“I could hear it.”

I didn't ask what she thought its shortness meant. She didn't ask for a longer replacement. I placed the book beside the chair instead of shelving it. It would be easier to pick up tomorrow.

“That shop for left shoes,” I said. “They could make the sign a right shoe.”

“Too specific a lie.”

“You supported it a moment ago.”

“I supported a terrible name.”

I laughed and leaned back. My eyelids grew heavy. She didn't claim the page in advance. Before bed, I checked beside the chair to make sure I hadn't put the glass on the book. When I turned out the light, a short length of the paper marker remained outside the cover.
