# Chapter 168: The Shopkeeper's Door

By the time Mina turned over her last page, my coffee wasn't hot anymore.

I'd been at the café for fifteen minutes or so after work on Tuesday. She was revising at a table by the wall. We had nodded when I came in. I hadn't treated that as an invitation to approach. James brought my coffee and asked if I wanted food. I chose a sandwich and finished it before taking out my half-page.

It was the version I'd tidied before bed the previous night. The knot on the tongue of the shoe remained. The shopkeeper's sleeve was finally intact. I'd moved the mismatched cord into his drawer of short lengths rather than asking an innocent garment to demonstrate generosity.

Mina closed her folder, stretched her fingers, and caught me looking up.

“Brought it today?”

“Half a page. If you're still—”

“Finished this round of my own. I've got a little time. What do you want me to hear?”

I went over with the sheet and asked before sitting. She pulled the opposite chair halfway out. Her folder stayed beside her. She didn't offer it to me.

“Not everything,” I said. “I'd like to know whether both people in the shop seem alive.”

“Dead people can buy cord.”

“Different kind of story.”

She smiled and asked me to read. I unfolded the sheet, first admitting that it was still a fragment. I hadn't brought the surrounding pages. She said she'd listen to it as a fragment, then, without half an unwritten chapter of explanation first.

I began with the bridge keeper's walk.

Every few steps, the large knot on the tongue of his shoe pressed into his foot. At the shop entrance, he stopped and loosened the lace, only to find that his heel now rubbed up and down. Instead of crouching again, he steadied himself against the doorframe and stepped inside.

The shopkeeper was moving flour. He asked for help taking the sack near the entrance to the back. The bridge keeper lifted it, and the shopkeeper pointed to an empty space. When he arrived there, the man called for him to wait. Another sack blocked the back door. They needed to change positions. Together, they retraced the route. Flour settled on the bridge keeper's sleeve and wouldn't brush completely off.

“What do you need?” the shopkeeper asked.

“Strong cord.”

“How long?”

The bridge keeper extended his foot. The shopkeeper didn't bend to inspect it. He opened a small drawer and produced blue cord that didn't match the shoe at all. Did he want it? Yes, the bridge keeper said. A longer piece.

I stopped there. I'd left the line about saving some for next time outside this half-page. I hadn't decided to cut it. For now, I didn't want it tidying up the meaning of the whole scene.

Mina didn't answer immediately. Someone at the next table raised a hand for James. She shifted to let him pass behind her chair, waited for the plate to land, then asked for the flour-moving part again.

On the second reading, I heard how quickly I went past the shopkeeper pointing the wrong way.

“You're in a hurry to get him the cord,” she said.

“A little.”

“I'm not. I want to know what the shopkeeper was doing before he arrived.”

I indicated the flour. “Moving this.”

“Where he's putting it is one question. Why now is another.”

I almost said James had asked why today too. As though she could see me retreating toward an old answer, she tapped the table lightly, not my manuscript.

“I mean, if the bridge keeper put the sack down and left, what would this man do next? Here, his main job seems to be setting the other man a task, then dispensing cord.”

I drew the paper closer and stopped myself from immediately objecting. The wrong directions were one of my favorite parts. I'd thought giving the shopkeeper some clumsiness would keep him from existing solely to serve the protagonist.

“He could want the back door clear.”

“He could. Which sack would he move first?”

“The one blocking it.”

“But here he tells somebody to bring the other sack over first.”

I studied the passage. It wasn't necessarily a mistake that needed explanatory repair. The shopkeeper might make the wrong choice and correct himself. The problem was that I hadn't let him notice. I'd simply needed the two men to make another trip.

“You want him to apologize?”

“I want him to decide something for himself. An apology is possible. It isn't the only action.”

She drew back her hand. “Maybe he doesn't want the back door blocked. Maybe he's willing to make a customer wait while he moves the sack himself. Then he isn't just a pair of hands waiting to give out cord. You don't have to use that.”

I began a note. At shopkeeper moves it first, I stopped. I was turning her example into an instruction.

“I like him not looking down at the shoe,” she said.

I looked up.

“He's sold cord before. He doesn't need to examine everyone's feet to know what's in his drawer. For that moment, it's his shop.”

I smiled. She wasn't asking me to explain the history behind everything I'd left out. She had found another person's life in one of my most casually written lines.

Mina asked what I was least willing to change.

“The trip back.”

“Why?”

“They're already tired from carrying flour, and now they have to admit they're in the wrong place. I find it funny.”

“Don't throw it out yet. I didn't say he couldn't point the wrong way. I just don't know whether he realizes it before I do.”

I read the paragraph silently, trying a version where he reached for something on the door, then noticed the sack in the way. The movement wasn't written down. My hand had performed it anyway, catching a packet of sugar.

“What?” Mina asked.

“Trying it for him.”

“Don't dismantle the café yet.”

I returned the packet. She said she sometimes made movements at the table that readers never needed to see. Eventually, she kept one verb. How did she know which? Usually she kept the wrong one first and discovered it on the next pass. She didn't offer that as a guarantee. She was already turning back to her work.

James collected my plate and asked whether we wanted anything else. Mina checked the time. No, she'd be leaving soon. I didn't buy another coffee as though that might extend her availability.

“Can I read you something?” she asked.

“Of course.”

“Not compensation. I've changed a bit in this round, and I want to hear whether someone laughs.”

She took half a sheet from the folder and placed it in front of herself. It was another small passage from the bakery story. The owner moved the overbrowned bread to the window, saying better light would change its color. The apprentice complied, then asked whether they could put the wages in the light too.

I laughed aloud.

“There?” I asked.

“Yes. I'm not sure the next line belongs.”

She continued. Ignoring the question about wages, the owner broke a loaf open, exposing its underbaked center. At least the sun hadn't spoiled the middle, he said. The apprentice pulled the tray back and nearly took the owner's sleeve with it.

I didn't immediately turn her question about my work on hers. I pictured the sleeve and said I'd laughed at the wages. Breaking the bread had stopped me for a moment. I wanted to know whether he still intended to sell it.

“I can't quite hear who's making the joke about the sun,” I said. “I follow him inventing excuses quickly. But I have to think again to understand the apprentice reaching.”

She reread the action, trying a cloth beside the tray instead of the sleeve. I said it was clearer, but lost some of the awkwardness of two people crowded together. She noted both possibilities without immediately choosing mine.

We read no further. She put the sheet back and gave me no copy. I didn't ask her to send it somewhere I couldn't currently use.

I asked whether I could tell Lumi her comments on my half-page.

“The shopkeeper and back-door points, yes. Say it's my reading. Don't take the bakery passage home yet. I'm not ready for another reader.”

“All right. I won't pass along the rest of our conversation either.”

She nodded and secured her folder's elastic strap. A strand of hair caught in it. Frowning, she undid it and started again. Serious discussion hadn't elevated the manuscript above getting tangled in somebody's hair.

“When next?” I asked, immediately hearing the appointment I'd slipped into the question.

Mina shouldered her bag. “I don't know. Depends on my revisions for a while. Ask when you see me. Don't save me an evening.”

“And I won't sit here in advance waiting with half a page.”

“You can wait for coffee.”

She said goodbye to James and left. I didn't follow her with one last small question. My page remained beside a faint ring from the cup. I moved it away, dried the table with a napkin, and read my notes.

They resembled an efficient prescription: Shopkeeper moves it first.

I added a question mark, then crossed out the words and wrote: If the bridge keeper puts down the flour and leaves, does the shopkeeper still go to open the back door?

I'd borrowed the question. The answer hadn't come with it. I wanted to try him pulling the door, finding it stuck, and turning toward the place he'd just indicated. Then I worried it would be too neat, as though the door had waited to prove Mina right.

James passed, saw me smiling at the sheet, and asked if it had grown another half-page.

“Not yet. Just another door.”

“Keep the fire exit clear.”

“It's fiction.”

“Easier to block, then.”

I put the paper away and settled my bill. At the entrance, an arriving customer and I caught the handle together. We both stepped back. Eventually, I held it for him. He thanked me without offering a writing problem.

I walked home with my revision and one new question. Mina's bakery stayed in her folder. Lumi's bulb shop remained in its separate envelope at home. Tomorrow's appointment hadn't changed. I hadn't spent the walk arranging Lumi's answers to tonight's conversation.
