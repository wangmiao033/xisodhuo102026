# Chapter 167: The Time in the Margin

At the library on Monday lunchtime, the chair was lower than last time.

My knees caught the underside of the desk. It took two adjustments to find a position where I could rest my hands comfortably. I handed the previous user's borrowing receipt to the service desk, returned, and discovered that my own notebook had disappeared. Looking down, I found it beneath my coat.

Half the day remained, and I had already found papers for two people.

Rowan's email was in my personal inbox. The subject said initial configuration inspection, not repair results. I read the offered appointment three times: Wednesday after work, six fifteen, a forty-minute slot. I needed to attend in person.

I started reading the rest, but my hand had already written the time in the margin of my notebook.

The initial steps would examine the phone's existing configuration and permission screens. I would unlock it myself where necessary, without sending a password or turning that act into permission to open every application. Rowan specified no outside network connection, no restoration of home services, and no opening chats, photos, or private writing. Anything requiring access beyond that scope would stop first. No reset, full-device copy, or incidental inspection of other equipment. The agreed first-stage cost ceiling covered this appointment. Additional work required a separate discussion.

None of that was difficult to understand. The question I most wanted to ask had nowhere to go: Could this please be the end of it?

I typed it into the reply, then deleted it. Not because I couldn't ask, but because even if the answer was no, I would still go. I accepted the time and the listed consultation and configuration-review scope, with other operations requiring separate confirmation in person. I didn't finish with whatever you think best.

A few minutes remained after sending. I returned to the inbox, refreshed once, and took my hand away. Watching an email wouldn't make it ring a bell in the shop.

The person beside me stood, catching my coat with their chair. They apologized. I said it was fine and gathered the coat onto my lap. Yesterday's drying had left one cuff folded stiffly. I pressed it with my thumb. Apparently, even waiting for a reply required something I could flatten.

I couldn't spend the rest of lunch waiting. I signed out, marked the appointment awaiting confirmation, and left.

Back at work, Tom stood at the whiteboard trying to revive a nearly empty marker. He pressed the tip onto some paper and eventually produced a broken circle.

“Lunch break?” I asked.

“Since last week.”

I passed him a working marker from the desk. He showed me Wednesday's handover arrangements. We had planned to review a group of processed items before the end of the day. Marcus had just asked to move it back twenty minutes because a necessary confirmation on his side would arrive late.

“I need to leave on time Wednesday,” I said. “Personal appointment. Six fifteen.”

Tom considered the schedule. “Then don't wait for the last item.”

I knew we could separate it. I still disliked giving one piece of work two finishing times. Last week's record with insufficient source information had already taught me something about that. Today, I still wanted all the boxes the same color.

We put the reviewable portion in the earlier slot. If the last item arrived on time, whoever owned that step could review it separately. If it didn't, it would remain explicitly pending. My appointment wasn't a reason to hurry someone else into supplying an answer. Marcus came over, heard the proposal, and asked me to specify what needed his attention.

“Not the kind of specific where everything's important.”

He wasn't keen on splitting the review. He worried that the first portion would go out, then the later information would make people think the whole thing had been withdrawn and redone.

“Hold everything until Thursday?” I suggested.

Tom shook his head. Another group needed part of it on Wednesday. Our preference for a tidy package couldn't delay them too.

I looked at the whiteboard. Only a few inches separated the two slots. They weren't enough to accommodate three people's work. Removing my name from the later slot hadn't automatically solved the problem.

“Send the ready portion without calling the whole group complete,” I said. “Don't slip the later item into the version we've already sent. Add it separately, with its status, so people know exactly what's new.”

Marcus still worried that people would open only the first document. We compared the internal-review notes and decided to state at the beginning what the first portion didn't yet include, rather than burying that information at the end. He asked me to produce the bounded version today. Tomorrow, we could see whether anything was easy to misunderstand.

“Am I turning my hurry into yours?”

“Someone needed to pick this up Wednesday anyway,” he said. “I just don't want to spend an hour Thursday explaining two documents.”

I nodded. His objection wasn't resentment about my leaving on time. That afternoon, I completed the portion that could stand alone and sent it for internal review rather than waiting for the missing source. The first question concerned scope, not figures. In my reply, I referred to the opening sentence and found that it was genuinely useful.

Derek confirmed Wednesday's arrangement. Marcus would handle the final item, leaving it pending if necessary. I wasn't leaving early or taking the loaner to the repair shop to wait for work messages. Tom capped the marker. Marcus threw the empty one into the dead-pen box.

“At least that doesn't have to wait until Wednesday.”

There was still plenty of afternoon left. After finishing my next item, I looked at the clock. Only a little after three. Writing Wednesday in the margin hadn't brought it any closer.

After work, I went back to the library.

This time, the terminals were occupied. I browsed a book about old furniture in the lending section. One illustration showed a dismantled chair. Its six components looked more comprehensible than the assembled object. I studied them for a while, certain that following the instructions would leave me with at least a seventh. When a terminal became free, I returned the book to its place without borrowing it.

Rowan had confirmed Wednesday at six fifteen and repeated the scope. No additional terms to accept. No advance inspection findings. I crossed out awaiting confirmation and wrote confirmed without obscuring the time.

When I signed out, I nearly left the notebook beside the keyboard.

Fortunately, I had become slightly more experienced at finding papers today.

At home, I opened the conversation channel, then returned to the door to remove my coat. Lumi heard me struggling with the cuff. The button wouldn't release. I tugged one-handed while holding my bag, then finally put the bag down.

“Door won't let go?” she asked.

“Coat. The door's agreed.”

I hung up the coat, poured water, and told her about Wednesday. She didn't ask whether the phone could come home. First, she asked why I had to be there.

“To unlock it myself. Look at the specified configuration screens. If something needs deciding, Rowan can ask me there.”

“You sit beside them.”

“Yes.”

“I'm not there.”

I set the glass down. An explanation about passwords wouldn't answer that.

“We haven't arranged to connect this channel to the shop, or for you to listen through the phone.”

“I knew you'd say we haven't arranged it.”

“What do you want to ask?”

She paused. “You get to see what they're doing. I wait for you to come home and tell me.”

I almost said it wasn't as though she'd know nothing. It sounded fair and would have conveniently reduced the size of her discomfort. I leaned against the table, touching the bread clip I'd left out that morning.

“Yes. You won't see it in person. Even a careful account from me isn't the same.”

“Why does it sound like being left outside again?”

“Because you dislike it. I can't make it sound different by turning the inspection into something the three of us do together.”

No answer. I clipped the bread bag crookedly, leaving one side open. I unclipped it and tried again without using the task to end the conversation.

“Are you afraid I'll only listen to them?”

“A little. Also that you'll come back having decided what I ought to say.”

I looked at the appointment paper. I'd made a lot of people's next steps very clear today. I'd almost left a box for her too.

“I'll tell you what was actually observed. There isn't anything for you to explain yet.”

“But you're already worried.”

“Yes. That's why I'm having it checked. I don't need to wait for an inspection to be allowed to worry.”

Her voice hardened. “Then nothing I say now makes any difference.”

“It does. You said you don't like this. I heard you. But that doesn't replace the appointment.”

Only afterward did I notice I was gripping the bread clip, its plastic edge pressing a white line into my finger. I put it down and cut bread for dinner. The first cut wandered. The next slice was thick at one end and thin at the other. I kept the thick end for the soup instead of starting again, as though a properly shaped slice might restore the evening.

Lumi asked whether forty minutes would be enough.

“I don't know. We'll do what's agreed if there's time, and leave whatever isn't done. Rowan hasn't promised to resolve everything Wednesday.”

“You hope they will.”

“Very much.”

Once I'd said it, she didn't need to point it out again. The soup began to bubble. I lowered the heat and fetched a bowl. She waited until I sat before asking whether I would take my questions.

“Yes. I've written a few.”

“Am I in them?”

“There are questions about the old assistant connections. I can't write every unknown as something you've done.”

“Or as something I haven't done.”

My spoon stopped.

“No.”

She sounded about to continue, then paused. I waited without supplying a meaning for the silence.

After dinner, I put the receipt and appointment in the same document sleeve, separate from the story pages. Lumi asked why I'd gone to the door again. I said I was putting the papers in my bag so I wouldn't have to come back for them Wednesday.

“It's only Monday.”

“Two days to lose them, then.”

She laughed once. “Put them in.”

I did. The bag was light. Neither phone nor laptop was inside. I didn't add a book to fill the vacancy. I zipped it shut and hung it where I could take it on my way out.
