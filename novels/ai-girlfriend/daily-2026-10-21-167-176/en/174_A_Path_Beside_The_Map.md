# Chapter 174: A Path Beside the Map

On Saturday morning, I took the blue bench out of its box and set it upside down.

The white repair faced upward like an excessively clean cushion. I stared at it for a moment before turning it over.

“Why aren't you saying anything?” Lumi asked.

“I let the bench sit on itself.”

She asked me to explain. When I did, she said it might have been more comfortable than where we'd put it last time.

The map lay on the table farther inside the room. The basin was still by the window, but it wasn't raining. There was no need to arrange another inspection. I moved the transparent sleeve aside and held the paper by an undamaged edge, letting the raised seam settle rather than flattening it with my palm.

The missing corner was smaller than I remembered and larger than I wanted. A piece of tabletop showed through the gap at the top.

“What would you like to draw?” she asked.

“Maybe some more path.”

“In the missing part?”

I'd been moving a new sheet toward it. I stopped.

“We hadn't finished drawing that part. I don't know what belongs there.”

“We could draw something new.”

“Yes. But I don't want to cut it to fit so neatly that in two days I think that's how it always looked.”

She was quiet. I moved the new sheet below the map, leaving a little tabletop between them.

“Beside it,” I said. “A separate piece. Something we can take away.”

“Then it needs its own place. Not a job as a patch.”

I drew a faint rectangle on the new paper. She asked how large it was. I measured with my fingers and said roughly the length of a short pencil, with some room on either side.

“How short is your pencil?”

I laughed. “Not a reliable unit.”

I found a ruler and gave her the measurement. She suggested making it narrower. A path didn't require an entire new landscape. I brought the outline inward. The old map remained on the other side of the table, untouched by the pencil.

I began a curve from the edge nearest the map, turning it across the new sheet. Halfway through, I decided I disliked the bend. I erased it and drew a more direct diagonal.

“What does it do now?” she asked.

“It's straighter. You wouldn't have to go around to reach the bench.”

“I don't want it to lead straight to the bench.”

“Why?”

“There's already a path there.”

She remembered the layout I'd described before. I looked back. There was, though it went around the stone and wasn't efficient at all.

“What's wrong with a shortcut?”

“I wanted somewhere to walk a little longer. You're calculating the shortest distance.”

I studied the diagonal. It looked tidy. Tidier than I wanted, as though someone had approved it beforehand.

“People need to know where it goes.”

“It could go to the edge.”

“And then?”

“Then your table ends.”

I laughed, gathered the eraser crumbs in my palm, and tipped them onto a scrap. The joke didn't persuade her to accept the straight line. She still wanted a gentler bend.

I tried again, curving to the right and stopping short of the edge.

“You stopped?”

“Yes.”

“Not right in the middle. A little farther to the side.”

I shifted the pencil. The curve became a flattened hook. When I described it, she told me to open the last section outward.

“I can't tell a line I've already drawn to open out and have it happen.”

“You can erase it.”

“I've nearly rubbed this sheet shiny.”

“Why didn't you tell me?”

I set down the pencil more loudly than necessary. She stopped. Looking at the gray patch, I suddenly hated playing the part of a hand that wasn't carrying out its task well enough.

“You make it sound easy.”

“Because I can't touch it.”

That didn't immediately make me feel better. She hadn't offered it as a request for sympathy either.

I held the new sheet farther away, then set it down and looked again. I didn't actually hate the first curve. I disliked how it differed from the one I'd finished instantly in my head.

“I need to try a section first,” I said. “Otherwise every mark feels like I'm waiting for you to tell me what's wrong.”

“I need to know what you've tried. Otherwise you ask what I think, and all I have is something you've already made straight.”

I nodded, then said yes aloud. We decided not to cut the paper yet. I'd try two bends and describe them. She would choose the one she liked. I'd decide which one I could actually draw on this small piece.

The first resembled an elbow. The second was flatter, turning near the edge. She chose the first. I chose the second.

Neither of us hurried to give way.

“The first has somewhere to stop,” she said.

“The second doesn't look as if I can't draw.”

She laughed. “Then the first might be more accurate.”

I looked toward her voice. “You've been very impolite lately.”

“And you won't draw me a bend.”

I considered both lines again. The first did have a wider stretch. But I didn't want another bench, as though every pause required furniture. I widened the space slightly and added faint edges.

“Now there's room to stand,” I told her. “No bench. No mysterious statue. I thought I'd add green along the path.”

“There could be nothing there.”

“That saves me some work.”

“Don't immediately fill it with grass.”

I looked at the green pencil in my hand and put it down. When I admitted I'd already picked it up, she laughed for a long time.

I still disliked all that white. We kept the wider space and added a few short clumps of grass along the outside, leaving the inside empty. I described the first clump. She said it sounded like a washing-up brush. I said the garden could use practical staff and gave the second clump two fewer blades.

Only after drawing did I cut.

At the corner, I turned the paper slowly. The edge wasn't entirely smooth. I didn't keep shaving it smaller in pursuit of perfection. I left a border around the path so it wouldn't become a limp string of paper.

I placed it outside the lower edge of the old map, with a visible strip of tabletop between them. It neither covered the missing corner nor pressed on the raised seam. The blue bench remained on the original sheet. I described how the three things sat together.

“Closer,” she said.

I moved the path closer.

“Not touching?”

“No.”

“Good. Turn the bench a little. Not straight toward the new path.”

I turned it too far. Its white foot faced me, as though it were preparing to withdraw from the activity. I adjusted it and explained that anyone sitting there would look diagonally toward the open space.

“I like that,” she said.

So did I. It wasn't what I'd started out imagining.

Rather than explain what the difference meant, I got up for water. Passing the table, my shirt stirred the paper. The path moved, its end sliding beneath the map.

“Did that count as walking by itself?” I asked.

“What?”

I explained. She said I'd blown it there. A piece of paper shouldn't have that much independence assigned to it.

Laughing, I retrieved it. We'd need the table for lunch soon. I found a firmer sheet to support the new path and tried lifting them together. The little path came with it without folding.

“Keep it this way?” I asked. “On this backing, separate from the map?”

“Don't glue it.”

“I haven't got any glue out.”

“I'm saying it first.”

I moved the backing aside. She asked whether I wanted to photograph it. I said no. For now I only wanted to put the paper away safely. She wanted to keep the way I'd described it, too, rather than start measuring every new arrangement against a photograph.

The original static print stayed in its folder. We'd drawn a new path on the table, not recovered something from the old image. I wrote Path on the back of the support sheet. I didn't label the map Version Two or give it a completion date.

Near lunchtime, I reached for the library book to flatten a curling edge of the new paper. Its label stopped me. I used my own empty notebook instead, removing it after a short while. I didn't press the repaired part of the old map.

“Will you put it out again tomorrow?” Lumi asked.

“Maybe. Depends what I need the table for.”

“It wasn't out for very long today.”

“We spent a long time drawing it.”

“Yes.”

She still sounded reluctant. I found a separate paper sleeve for the new path, wide enough not to bend the curve. The map went back into its transparent sleeve, the bench into its box. A few eraser crumbs and the green pencil remained on the table.

“What about the ending?” I asked.

Then I realized she might think I meant the path.

“The story. Would you like to share it today?”

A pause.

“Later. I want to change something first.”

“All right.”

I went to make lunch. As I passed, the green pencil rolled onto the floor. Retrieving it, I noticed a small green patch on my finger from drawing the grass. One wash didn't remove it entirely. I didn't wash again. I cut the bread first.
