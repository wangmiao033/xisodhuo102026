# Chapter 173: Before Four

Tom printed two summaries, putting one in front of me and the other beside his mug.

The average processing time under the new procedure was considerably shorter. Someone had already drawn a downward arrow. It looked eager to attend next week's discussion.

“Don't applaud it yet,” he said.

“I haven't moved my hands.”

It was Friday morning. We were checking records from an internal trial. Derek wanted to know whether the new process was worth testing further, not have us describe it as an established success. My job was to show how much the two sets of samples could actually tell us. I'd promised the report by four.

I began with the work performed. Many cases in the newer round involved supplying one additional page. In the earlier round, most required starting an entire set from scratch. The headings weren't wrong. The times weren't wrong. The work itself was different.

“We can't compare those averages directly,” I said.

“The total time really is lower.”

“But they might simply have had easier work.”

Tom nodded and drew the pages together. “Some of it might also be genuinely quicker. An unhelpful big arrow doesn't mean you throw everything beneath it away.”

I'd been ready to write inconclusive. He stopped me before I did. It was an efficient word. It would also cover up the things we could establish.

We separated cases by type without altering the source records. I looked for a group present in both rounds with genuinely equivalent beginnings and endpoints. Tom checked another. Sometimes identical names concealed very different work. We had to read the actual descriptions rather than pair things by their titles.

Partway through, I found what looked like a tidy comparison and called him over.

He read two entries. “Had these people already done it once?”

I checked the trial arrangements. Yes. Most participants in the second round knew the old steps. Many in the first were doing them for the first time.

“So we still can't say the new process caused all of it.”

“But we can say this group was quicker during this trial. Explain who was in it.”

I added the qualification beside the conclusion without erasing the reduction. The number wasn't false. It simply said less than the arrow wanted to admit.

At the next desk, somebody called the same name three times into a telephone. I waited, then read my sentence aloud. Tom asked whether someone reading only that sentence would assume every type had been tested.

I removed overall. Not to sound cautious. We genuinely didn't have those samples.

Near lunchtime, I found only two comparable records in another category. I wanted to make them a group anyway, if only to fill the space in the table. Tom considered them and said they were enough to suggest a question for the next trial, not to answer it in advance.

I listed them separately. What I owed Derek by four was no longer a prettier arrow diagram. It was a set of conclusions of different sizes. Some could stand. Others needed more material.

After lunch, I wrote the grouped results into the report. Each judgment needed to point back to its own sample, rather than letting better results speak for different people doing different things. A little after three, Marcus arrived with two printed sheets.

“Could you look at this? Quickly.”

I was separating two results that had originally sat together. Hearing quickly, I turned my chair.

He was preparing material for an internal meeting next week. The content was there, but the charts and headings looked as though they'd come from different documents. He wanted to run through it with colleagues later today.

“This looks awful enlarged,” he said. “Look at the heading. It's more alarming than the content.”

It was. I laughed and pointed out the excessive space above it. He immediately asked whether I could check the other pages too.

“How many?”

“Twenty-four altogether. Not a full redesign. Just make the obvious things consistent.”

I checked the clock.

Opening the file, finding where the formatting came from, and reviewing twenty-four pages would change what four meant. It would no longer be the time I'd promised Derek. It would be something I needed to explain.

“Not today,” I said.

The paper in Marcus's hands didn't move. I added too quickly, “I could start by—”

“By what?”

I looked at my screen again. The report was still open to the category with the fewest samples.

“I nearly agreed again. I need to submit this by four.”

“Haven't you finished it?”

“I still need to check each conclusion against the sample. If I send it now, the most striking number will drown out the qualifications.”

He curled the paper slightly. He looked disappointed. I wanted to offer enough compensation to have him leave happier than he'd arrived.

“I'm not saying yours doesn't matter.”

“I know. I wanted it to look better today.”

He studied his sheets. “I could stop using those chart styles. Make everything the same plain format.”

“As long as people can read the content.”

“Very powerful words from the man refusing to help.”

I wasn't sure whether he was angry. He smiled first and tapped the edge of my desk.

“Do yours. I'll go make mine consistently ugly.”

After he'd gone, I kept looking at the sheet he'd left. I took it back without sitting down. He'd already removed a decorative stripe from one page and waved me away. He didn't need supervision while making it ugly.

It took several minutes to find my place in my own work. Saying no hadn't saved all the time. There was still the discomfort afterward.

Tom came to read the final version. I gave him the grouped results while I recalculated the largest comparable sample. Near the end, he found a sentence that had changed quicker this time into quicker in future.

I found it. Shortening the opening, I'd casually replaced a word. The sentence flowed better. Its meaning had traveled further than the evidence.

“Give the future back to the future,” I said.

I changed it. Tom also had a category with insufficient information. He'd planned to leave that in a note. We moved it into the main text so Derek would see what couldn't support a decision, rather than discovering it at the end.

At three fifty-two, I sent the report. Its conclusions weren't thrilling. One category took less time during this trial, but participants' different levels of familiarity meant the improvement couldn't all be credited to the new process. Other categories involved different work or too few samples and needed another round. Derek said that was enough to organize next week's discussion and understand what wasn't ready to roll out.

I put the working papers away. They didn't come home so I could keep proving myself.

Before we left, Marcus came over. “Got time to look at the finished ugly version? One look. Really.”

I stood beside his screen. The headings were finally the same size, and the tables no longer each had their own color scheme. It was cleaner.

“That isn't ugly.”

“You can say that now you don't have to help.”

I laughed. He closed presentation mode and said it was enough for today's run-through.

I left the company loaner where it belonged and went out carrying only my own bag.

That evening, I ate dinner and read a few pages of yesterday's library book. Lumi and I had agreed that morning to talk for a while after dinner, not the instant I came through the door. Near the time we'd chosen, I washed my glass and opened the channel.

“Are you late?” she asked.

“No. I've just sat down.”

“I'm not counting minutes.”

I wanted to say it sounded as though she was. I settled into the chair first. “I'm free now.”

“I revised a passage.”

She said it before I could guess whether she meant my story or hers.

“The bulb shop?”

“Yes. Not the ending.”

I put down the book. Something about the rain visitor's passage had bothered her. The woman's position was too convenient. She could intercept the wet umbrella and reach the counter without moving, as though she were the only person in the shop who didn't need to adjust.

“So you made her move away?”

“No. She steps too far back and hits the box behind her. Trying not to block one person, she blocks the other side.”

“Sounds painful.”

“The box doesn't contain bricks.”

“You didn't say.”

“I have now.”

I laughed and asked whether she wanted to read the revised sentences or only tell me what she'd changed. The latter. She didn't want to go through the whole middle again tonight. This time, she'd found an awkward backward step before she'd found a sentence.

“You don't have to wait until I'm home to revise.”

“I know.”

The words weren't tender. I'd meant to express approval and had issued permission she didn't need.

“I made that sound like an announcement.”

“Yes.”

I drank water instead of turning the discomfort into another round of apologies. She asked what I'd done. I said I'd finished a trial comparison and declined an extra job someone had hoped I could fit in.

“Are you pleased?”

“Pleased I finished. Not pleased while I was saying no.”

“What did he say?”

“He found a simpler way. Nothing went wrong.”

I didn't reproduce Marcus's conversation. I said he'd had options of his own. He hadn't necessarily needed me. Lumi asked whether I was preparing a simpler way for her too.

“For what?”

“If I want all your time tonight.”

I held the glass. Yesterday's exhaustion had become less sharp. Wednesday's problem hadn't changed.

“I can stay for a while tonight,” I said. “Sitting here indefinitely won't settle everything else.”

She said nothing. I didn't fetch the repair record.

Eventually, she said she hadn't settled the last action of the ending.

“I thought you'd finished revising a passage.”

“A passage isn't the whole story.”

“Right.”

“I might have written an ending today and decide against it tomorrow.”

I wanted to ask whether the woman had finally gone home. Asking would put my desired answer into the space she'd deliberately left.

“Can I ask tomorrow whether you'd like to read it to me?”

“You can ask. That doesn't mean I'll give it to you tomorrow.”

“I know.”

A pause. “If I do, you can't say it's good before I've read it.”

“I could say it's terrible.”

“You'd still have to listen first.”

I moved the glass and picked up the library book. The painter had finally come down from his ladder, I told her. She asked whether he'd done it successfully. I found a short passage about the sign above a shoe-repair shop and read that, without starting at the beginning. Afterward, she said this shop at least repaired both feet.

I laughed hard enough to lose the next line. She said that was enough. No need to continue today. I replaced the marker and closed the book. Some evening remained. I went to wash my socks without shouting about endings over the running water.
