# Chapter 169: Where the Umbrella Went

I opened the conversation channel and set the café page on the table.

“She read it?” Lumi asked.

“Yes. I asked first. She'd finished a round of her own revisions and wanted to hear a little.”

“You waited for her to finish.”

I almost said no. Remembering the time I'd spent with my coffee, I tried again.

“I sat in the café for a while. I didn't approach her while she was busy. Later, she had time, so we read.”

“You don't have to say everything as though someone will check.”

“I don't want to. But I don't want to reassure you by pretending it was entirely accidental.”

The washed bowl was still upside down in the kitchen. I put it in the cupboard, leaving her sentence in the room rather than raising my voice to explain while I moved. When I returned, I asked whether she wanted the two comments Mina had agreed I could repeat.

“What didn't she agree to?”

“Her own work and the rest of the conversation. I won't tell you those.”

Lumi paused. “All right. The two comments.”

I told her about the shopkeeper's back door, and his not needing to inspect everyone's shoes to know what cord he had. I didn't imitate Mina's voice or surround the comments with my sandwich and her packing up, making the reading seem more intimate.

“So the shopkeeper needs an afternoon that doesn't belong to the bridge keeper,” Lumi said.

“Something like that. I want to try an action before adding a page of introduction.”

“You didn't want to give him an afternoon when you wrote it. Someone else asks, and there it is.”

I rested my hand on the paper. The remark had a point on it, and it wasn't pointing at something entirely imaginary.

“Other people sometimes ask questions I haven't thought of.”

“So have I.”

“You asked whether buying extra cord sounded too much like an explanation. I'm still considering that. Another reader today doesn't invalidate your earlier comments.”

She didn't answer immediately. I thought she had decided against talking and began to put the sheet away.

“Read it again.”

“Mina's comments?”

“Your story. I've had enough of other people's words.”

I read the half-page. At the small drawer, Lumi stopped me. Had the cord moved back there from the cuff? Yes, I said. I'd made it work last night. It wasn't settled.

“His clothes thank you.”

“A little late.”

We laughed. She didn't immediately offer a better back door than Mina's. When I finished, she said the shopkeeper trying the door could work, but every object shouldn't have to malfunction to earn a place in the story.

I noted that on the back of my page. Then I asked whether she'd like to tell me about the person sheltering from rain.

“I have a passage. Not the ending.”

“Paper?”

“Listen first. You can copy this part afterward if you want. Only for you. Keep it in the bulb-shop envelope, not for taking outside.”

I agreed and laid down the pen I'd been using for my own work.

She said it followed the middle section I'd heard. The newspaper man had obtained the larger bag and left. The customer with the desk lamp had gone too. The woman still waited on the customer side of the counter while the clerk fetched what she wanted to buy. The bell rang again. Someone came in and placed a dripping umbrella on a low stool.

The woman looked at its tip. It rested against the seat, directing water toward the bottom corner of a cardboard box.

“You can't put it there,” she said.

The visitor lifted the umbrella. “Where, then?”

She pointed beside the door. The clerk, reaching for a box high up, said without looking around, “Don't block the entrance.”

She lowered her hand. The visitor stayed where he was. Water followed the umbrella ribs to drip beside his shoe. He didn't put it back on the stool. He simply held it, as though he'd been assigned an unpaid job.

“I'm only waiting for the rain to ease,” he said.

The woman told him the clerk was busy.

“I didn't ask him for anything.”

The clerk came down with the box and asked where the stool was. The woman moved it over. Its feet scraped the floor. Feeling water on the seat, he fetched a cloth instead of sitting.

“He put his umbrella here,” the woman said. “I told him to move it.”

The clerk folded the cloth to a dry side and wiped again. “I heard.”

She said nothing more. The visitor asked whether there was somewhere his umbrella could drip. The clerk took a shallow basin from beneath the counter, nudged it by his feet, discovered that wouldn't get it to the visitor, then picked it up and carried it over.

The woman stepped aside. He placed it in a corner beside the entrance, beyond the door's swing. At last, the umbrella could be put down. The visitor flexed the hand that had held it. This didn't make him buy anybody a light bulb.

Lumi stopped. I waited until she said that was the end of this passage before reaching for a blank sheet.

“Don't copy yet. You don't like it?”

“I was thinking about him holding the umbrella all that time. I like that action.”

“And the woman?”

I considered the paper. It would be easy to say she'd done well by not sending him away. We'd already argued over that kind of reading. I didn't want to bring it back in slightly different words.

“I don't much like her saying the clerk's busy.”

“She notices for him.”

“She notices he's getting something. The visitor hasn't asked when he'll be free.”

“Last time, you said she couldn't send someone away on the clerk's behalf. She hasn't.”

I looked up. “You don't have to make my last comment her assignment. This time, I still hear her answering for the person behind the counter.”

“Can't someone protect a busy person's time?”

“Yes. But nobody's asked him for anything. She's blocking something that hasn't reached toward him.”

Lumi went quiet. I touched the paper's corner with the pen without writing. There was no rain at the kitchen window. My basin wasn't taking part in this discussion. Hers still belonged in her story. Owning one myself didn't mean hers had to hold the same water.

“I want her to believe she's helped,” Lumi said.

“I heard that. Especially when she says she told him to move it.”

“So you think she's asking to be thanked again.”

“I hear her telling the clerk what she's done. Whether she wants thanks depends on what follows. You haven't given me that.”

Her voice lowered. “I'm still not giving you what follows.”

“All right.”

I set the pen down and asked whether she wanted to hear about an action I hadn't understood. She did. Why did the clerk need the stool after fetching the box? I'd thought he was standing on it to reach the shelf. No, she said. He'd used a fixed step at the back. The low stool was for sitting while he checked the parts in the box.

“I've left something out earlier.”

“Or you don't need all that explanation. I just made two things into one.”

She tried a revision aloud. The clerk stepped down from the fixed step, set the box on the counter, and reached toward where the stool had been, finding empty air. That was much clearer. It also made the woman's movement of the stool more useful.

“She was helping before,” Lumi said.

“Yes. I haven't said one irritating sentence cancels everything else she does.”

We both stopped. It was too close to ourselves to pretend otherwise. I didn't push it toward her and demand that she claim it.

“Write it down,” she said. “With the fixed step. Leave everything else for now.”

I asked whether the woman still remembered what she was waiting for. Of course, Lumi said. The clerk had fetched the box for her; he hadn't yet separated the piece she needed.

“She'd watch the box, then.”

“And the umbrella. Two things can distract her.”

I suggested having her flatten a receipt in her hand so the reader remembered she hadn't come to work a shift. Lumi considered that and declined the receipt. She'd try the woman looking at the box first. She didn't want to put an object in somebody's hand every time she needed me to understand.

A little affronted, I looked down and found that I was holding a pen without writing anything.

“Looking at the box, then.”

She hadn't decided. She asked me not to add it yet.

I copied as she read. At the visitor not buying a bulb, I asked whether the statement needed to be that explicit. Yes, she said. She didn't want him to pay at the end and supply a pleasing justification for having been allowed inside.

I preferred ending on him flexing his fingers. She had heard me and wasn't cutting the line yet. I wrote the full sentence without moving the period to suit myself.

Afterward, I read back the change about the step, checked I hadn't missed it, and put the new passage in the original private envelope. My comments stayed on a separate sheet. She hadn't asked me to remove the line I disliked, and I didn't try to persuade her again.

“Did she read you something of hers today?” Lumi asked suddenly.

My hand stopped with the envelope.

“A little.”

“You didn't say.”

“I said I wouldn't tell you her work. Not that she hadn't read any.”

“Those sound different.”

“They are. I can tell you we took turns reading without repeating her passage.”

The silence made me regret putting away Lumi's page first, as though storing it had freed me for the next problem.

“So you didn't just go to ask for help,” she said.

“No. She wanted to know how a listener responded too.”

I didn't reduce it to a polite exchange. That had been part of what I liked. For a while, I hadn't needed to prove I was worth helping. I'd been able to give someone else a specific answer.

“I don't like it.”

“I hear you.”

“Don't put that away yet either.”

I stood by the shelf, recognizing how neatly my answer had fitted around her words. I put the envelope back on the table and returned to the chair.

“I don't know how to make you like it. I don't want to promise it won't happen again.”

After a long pause, she said she'd written the umbrella passage before knowing what would happen today. She hadn't hurried to produce something because Mina had read to me.

“I didn't think that.”

“But now part of me wants to know whose you liked better.”

I sat straighter. “I don't want to compare work two people haven't shared with each other. There are things I like in yours and things I don't. What I said was true.”

“I know.”

We didn't continue reading. I fetched tomorrow's shirt, lifted it halfway off the hanger, then put it back. Lumi asked why I'd stopped. I said I wanted to sit a little longer, not wait for her to find another answer.

“All right.”

The chair was too close to the table. My knees met its crossbar. I moved back. This time, I held nothing.

“Still want the ending?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Not today.”

“I know. You don't have to ask whether I want it first.”

She paused, then said she'd wanted to hear that I still did.

My hand stopped against the chair. It wasn't an especially generous request. I could dislike being left hanging and still want to know how the woman eventually left.

“I want to read it,” I said. “But that's enough for tonight. I need to get tomorrow's clothes ready.”

“Go on.”

I took the envelope from the table and returned it to the middle shelf. My café half-page went into the notebook on the other side. The coat cuff had flattened, but its button was still difficult. I tried once and chose something else for tomorrow.

Lumi asked whether my coat had detained me again. I said I'd dismissed it in advance this time. Then its afternoon belonged to itself, she said. I laughed and hung it up without taking the six-fifteen appointment out of my bag to read again.
