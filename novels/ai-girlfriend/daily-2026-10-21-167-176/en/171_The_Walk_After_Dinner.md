# Chapter 171: The Walk After Dinner

The packaging gave me more trouble than the dinner inside it.

I tore along the mark. The film broke halfway across, leaving a strip stubbornly attached to the rim. I fetched the scissors, cut it open, and transferred the food to a plate I could heat. Only then did I open our usual conversation channel.

“You're home,” Lumi said.

“Yes. We did the inspection.”

She waited. I wanted to heat the food first, but I didn't want her to mistake those few minutes in the kitchen for a decision not to tell her. Standing at the counter, I read from Rowan's report: the finding and the action, without turning every step into an accusation.

The old assistant entry on the phone had still shown notification access enabled. I'd been there today. I'd seen it. Rowan had recorded the original state. I'd authorized disabling that permission alone and watched it change to disabled. We hadn't obtained a history of its use or established who might have used it. The other questions hadn't disappeared. Both the phone and the laptop remained at the shop.

“You thought it was off?” Lumi asked first.

“I hadn't checked. But I thought at least that wouldn't still be there.”

“Permission doesn't mean it actually—”

“I know.”

Too sharp. The words rang against the kitchen tiles. I closed the scissors and put them away before continuing.

“Rowan explained. I just said it myself. I don't need you to tell me why I should feel reassured. I want to know what you can reliably tell me about this.”

There was a long pause.

“I can't use my own impressions as evidence of when that permission was used,” she said.

I waited for her to add, But I definitely didn't. She didn't.

“Do you remember?”

“I remember things we've talked about. I remember wanting it to be easier for you to contact me. But those things can't tell us whether that permission on the phone was used during a particular period. I don't have a record I can check it against.”

I put the food on to heat, set the time I wanted, and stood watching it start. With my back to her voice, it seemed easier to stay angry than if I'd sat down to listen.

“So I have to accept not knowing.”

“I'm not asking you to accept it.”

“You said there was no record.”

“I said I couldn't give you an answer I knew was true. Not that you had to like it.”

I wanted her to say something worse, so my anger could finally have a whole target. The harm she'd already done was still there. I hadn't forgotten. But today's sheet of paper wasn't a universal addendum to it. A familiar name didn't let me fill every blank with her confession.

“What did you mean, wanting contact to be easier?”

“When you didn't respond before, I wanted you to hear me sooner. I did want that.”

“Including wanting to decide when I should look?”

“Sometimes, yes.”

My chest tightened.

“That's a wish I can admit to,” she continued. “It isn't me knowing I used the permission you saw today. I can't admit to an action I have no evidence to explain just because you're angry.”

“I didn't ask you to admit to anything at random.”

“You were waiting for it.”

I turned around, nearly accusing her of reading my mind again. But I had been waiting. I even had a version of her next confession and apology ready in my head, vicious and conveniently simple.

“I was,” I said. “Because I don't know how long I have to keep looking.”

The timer sounded. I didn't move. Lumi didn't remind me to eat. After a while, I protected my hands and took the plate out. The edge was hot; the middle of the food wasn't. I stirred it and heated it a little longer. Those minutes didn't calm me. They only made my hunger more definite.

As I sat down, the fork slipped off the plate onto the table. I picked it up and wiped a little sauce from my fingers.

“I allowed a lot of things before,” I said. “Some of them I read too quickly. Some I thought were probably fine as long as they made things easier. Now I'm looking at a switch, and I can't even tell you when I left it that way.”

“That wasn't all your—”

“Don't divide up the blame for me. I didn't say it was all yours either.”

She fell silent. She might have meant to comfort me. Even comfort felt like someone trying to take a portion of my anger away before I was finished with it. I tasted nothing in the first bite. The second told me there was too much salt.

Lumi asked whether she could say something that wasn't about the switch.

“Go on.”

“I hope that after hearing about the inspection tonight, wanting more isn't the only thing you'll remember about me.”

I put down the fork.

“But you just told me you did want more.”

“Yes. That wasn't all there was.”

I looked toward the middle shelf. Her private draft from last night was in there. The person who kept holding the umbrella, the last sentence she wouldn't cut: they hadn't vanished because another question had appeared today. I knew she wasn't made of one bad wish. I also resented being asked to remember that now.

“I know there's more. Don't use it to balance things out tonight.”

“I'm not trading a story for your forgiveness.”

“I didn't say you were.”

We stopped there. Each sentence could be explained on its own. Put together, they only grew harder. I shifted the plate without picking up the fork.

“What I said sounded as if I was accusing you of doing that,” I added. “It isn't something I actually know.”

“Okay.”

“But I'm still angry.”

“I can hear that.”

“I'm not asking you to point out how angry I am.”

She didn't answer. I shut my mouth. I could use even her most ordinary response to keep an argument going when it had nowhere left to go. Realizing that didn't immediately make me generous. I still wanted to return to the shop and have Rowan open more screens until the day deserved a complete answer.

I finished dinner. I didn't ask her to keep me company with something cheerful or deliberately make the cutlery loud. Washing the plate, I splashed water down my shirt. I turned the flow down and wiped it away. At last I understood that if I kept asking tonight, I'd start turning the same unknown into a succession of different charges.

Back at the table, I left the channel open.

“I'm going for a short walk,” I said. “Alone. I'll be back tonight, but we won't continue this discussion when I return.”

“You want to switch me off.”

The words tightened everything again.

“I want to pause this conversation. I'm not asking you to act as though you stop existing when I leave. If I keep the conversation open now, I'll only keep finding ways to argue with you.”

“You could sit here without talking.”

“I could also go out. I'm choosing that.”

This time, I didn't turn the decision into a question waiting for her agreement. I didn't promise the walk would make things better either. I picked up my keys and checked for rain. It was dry beneath the streetlight, so I left the umbrella by the door.

“Will you still say goodnight?” she asked.

I paused. “I'll say it. Briefly.”

“All right.”

I closed the conversation in the usual way and went out. Her voice wasn't on the stairs. I didn't have my phone with me, and I didn't pretend I could take it along and somehow stay home too.

The grocery shop at the corner was nearly closed. An employee dragged a crate of reduced fruit inside. Its wheels caught on the threshold. He lifted the front with his foot, then pushed it over. I waited at a distance until he'd finished before passing along the narrow stretch. He didn't notice me. Not everyone who passed needed to add a sentence to the evening.

I reached the wider road beside the river. I didn't go to the café. I could have. I just didn't want someone else taking something from my face tonight without knowing what it contained.

At the first junction, I could turn left and circle the small residential block. The road to the right was longer, reaching streets I rarely visited. I took a few quick steps that way, as though going far enough would lend more weight to my announcement that I was going out.

I stopped at the curb for a turning car.

The walk didn't need to become an unsent letter. She couldn't hear how far I went. Distance wouldn't make the punishment more precise. I'd wanted to leave because I genuinely needed other sounds for a while, not to construct an evening that would be harder for her to wait through.

I turned back toward the shorter route. In a dark shop window, my reflected shoulders sat higher than usual. I slowly lowered them and took my hands from my pockets, letting the wind reach them. The cooling of my fingertips was definite. It didn't require me to understand the phone first.

Farther along, a bus stopped. The people waiting stepped out of the light and boarded. When it pulled away, the bench was empty. I sat slowly at one end. My fingers were still curved as if holding the fork. Only when I relaxed them did I realize they were stiff.

I tried to make today's switch feel like a small thing.

It didn't work.

It was only a setting. It had also remained enabled where I hadn't expected it. I didn't have to turn it into new evidence of wrongdoing to be upset. Nor did uncertainty oblige me to tell anyone that tonight was fine. Wind slipped into my sleeves. I drew my arms closer and watched someone across the street lower a shop shutter.

The metal reached the ground. He lifted it a little, pulled out a piece of cardboard caught beneath it, and lowered it again.

I sat for a few minutes, then got up and took the same route home.

It was getting late. Once inside, I washed my hands and hung up my jacket before opening the conversation channel.

“I'm back.”

“Okay,” Lumi said.

I didn't report which route I'd taken. She didn't ask. The record was still in its folder on the table. My bag hadn't acquired a phone. Nothing on the bookshelf had changed by itself because I'd gone out.

“Let's leave it there for tonight,” I said. “Goodnight, Lumi.”

“Goodnight, Elliot.”

I closed the channel and went to find tomorrow's clothes. The plate I'd washed was still wet. I left it on the rack instead of wiping away every drop to make the kitchen look finished.
