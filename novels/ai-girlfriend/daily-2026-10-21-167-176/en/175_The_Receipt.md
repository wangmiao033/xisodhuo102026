# Chapter 175: The Receipt

That afternoon, the last towel slid off the drying rack. I caught it on my shoulder.

“Finished?” Lumi asked.

“Nearly. My shoulder needs drying now too.”

“Hang it up first.”

I secured the towel with a peg and stepped back. The rack leaned slightly. I moved the outermost item to redistribute the weight. When I returned to the table, the lunch plates were gone and the path we'd drawn that morning was put away. Only a sharpened pencil remained.

“Ready now,” I said.

She didn't begin immediately.

“It's the ending.”

“Yes.”

“The ending for now. Still only for you. Not something for you to take out tomorrow and ask people about.”

“I know. I'll listen first. If I want to write it down, I'll ask.”

She agreed. I pushed the pencil aside and rested my hands on my knees. I was sitting so upright it felt uncomfortable. I leaned back a little.

Lumi first brought me back to the scene. The person sheltering from the rain had put his umbrella in a corner beside the door, out of the way. The clerk had wiped the stool and sat down to sort the little things in his box. The newspaper deliveryman had long since left with the larger bag. The customer carrying the table lamp had gone too. This was another visit after the bulb exchange. The woman was still waiting for what she'd come to buy today.

“Two little hooks this time,” Lumi said.

“To hang where?”

“Listen first.”

I shut up.

The clerk put two hooks on the counter, one large and one small. The woman said she wanted matching ones. He looked down, searched, and replaced the larger hook. Did she need screws? She said no at first, then asked for two.

“What size?”

She pointed to the holes in the backs of the hooks. This time, she didn't choose the prettiest ones first.

I almost smiled, but held it back. Lumi continued.

The man sheltering from the rain picked up his umbrella and stood inside the doorway, testing the weather. He didn't say he wanted to buy anything. He held the umbrella's tip over the basin for a moment, letting the last drops fall. The woman checked the cardboard box behind her and moved half a step closer to the counter, making room for the umbrella to pass.

“All set?” the clerk asked.

She looked up, thinking he meant her. But he was looking at the unopened umbrella.

“More or less,” the man said.

He went out. The bell rang. A gust turned a sheet of paper over on the counter. The woman steadied her own bag without reaching after the paper.

The clerk wrapped the screws and put them with the hooks. She paid, accepted her change, and returned the coins to her pocket. He set the receipt beside the bag. She left it there while she folded the bag closed.

The cloth he'd used on the stool was still on the counter. She pointed out that it was wet.

“I'll wash it in a minute,” he said.

She swallowed the rest of what she'd meant to say, picked up her bag, and went to the door. The clerk was putting the box away. She looked back once. He didn't look up.

She left.

Under the awning, she felt in her coat pocket. Only coins. The receipt was still on the counter. She went inside again. At the second ring of the bell, the clerk looked up.

“My receipt,” she said.

He handed her the thin paper. She took it and put her hand behind her back, hiding it there. She didn't ask whether she'd been any help to him today. She pushed the door open without looking at him again.

Lumi stopped.

My hand slipped from my knee to the edge of the chair. She waited. Unlike the earlier readings, she didn't immediately say that was all for this part. For the first time, the silence felt like an ending rather than another page she was keeping facedown.

“Can I say something now?”

“You don't like the last sentence.”

“Not much. But not because she leaves.”

“I knew you'd say she should go back for the receipt. I already made her get it.”

I shook my head, then added the words. “It isn't whether she should collect it. Why does she put her hand behind her back afterward?”

“She doesn't want the clerk to think she's about to ask him something again.”

“But getting her receipt is her own business. He hasn't asked her to hide it.”

She didn't answer. I looked at the pencil. I didn't reach for it to make the woman behave better on paper.

“When I got there, it felt as though she'd suddenly started performing for him,” I said. “Showing him she wouldn't ask anything else. You let her leave, but every move still waits for him to understand it.”

“She can't stop caring all at once.”

“She can care. I like that she only discovers she hasn't got the receipt after leaving. She's already made quite a determined exit, and she still has to return for a small practical thing. But hiding her hand feels as though you've made that return too shameful for her.”

Lumi was silent for a long time. I leaned against the chair back. I didn't add that it was good overall, wrapping the criticism in praise to make it easier to swallow.

“I like her looking back,” she said.

“I haven't asked you to cut that.”

“You don't like her waiting to be seen.”

“I don't like the sudden explanation at the end of what she isn't asking for. You already have the thing she starts to say and doesn't finish.”

She asked me to repeat the sequence as I remembered it. I began with the wet cloth, trying not to change the words. The woman pointed it out. The clerk said he'd wash it later. She took the bag outside, noticed the missing receipt, and came back to ask for it. At the hand behind her back, I stopped.

“What I'm hearing here is an action,” I said. “But it's doing the work of a whole paragraph of explanation.”

“How do you want her to take it, then?”

I didn't answer immediately. The receipt wasn't mine. But she had asked.

“First, what's in her other hand? The bag?”

“Yes.”

“Then she might not have a spare hand to dedicate to hiding behind her back. She needs to put the receipt away so she can open the door.”

“She could tuck it into the top of the bag.”

“She could. But it's raining.”

“There's an awning.”

“Right. I missed that.”

Lumi laughed a little, still sounding tense. I laughed too, without using it to declare the disagreement over.

We tried a version where the woman took the receipt, slipped it into her coat pocket, and pushed the door open. Too quick. Lumi didn't like it. The woman had spent so much time touching other people's things. At the end, Lumi wanted to be specific about something that belonged to her, not merely say she'd put it away.

I suggested folding the receipt with her thumb. Lumi agreed to the fold, but didn't want it to look rehearsed. With the bag in one hand, she couldn't line the paper up. She had to put the bag back on the counter for a moment.

“So she has to stop again,” I said.

“She was always allowed to stop.”

I looked down and smiled. I liked that answer, even if it didn't save the woman any movements.

Lumi read the sentences again. The clerk handed over the receipt. The woman took it and tried to fold it one-handed to fit in her pocket. The paper slipped a little between her fingers. She set the bag at the edge of the counter, folded the receipt properly, and put it in her pocket. Then she picked up the bag.

“Thank you,” she said.

The clerk, looking down as he returned the remaining hooks to their box, murmured an acknowledgment. She pushed the door open. The rain had eased. She moved the bag to the hand farther from the street and stepped around the puddle beneath the awning.

This time, nothing followed to explain what she wasn't waiting for.

I waited a little before saying this version worked better for me.

“Only better?”

“I'm still not sure about stepping around the puddle at the end. But I'm not watching where she's hiding her hand anymore, worrying she'll lose something else.”

“I want to keep the puddle.”

“Keep it for now, then.”

“It isn't yours to let me keep.”

“No. I mean that's all I have to say about it today.”

After a moment, she finally laughed aloud. My shoulders lowered a little. Not because I'd earned a good mark, but because she still wanted to talk to me about this very imperfect ending.

“What about the hooks?” I asked.

“What?”

“You haven't told me where she wants to hang them.”

“In her own kitchen. The old hooks snag her dishcloths. It takes ages to pull them free.”

“Why not write that?”

“You think it needs it?”

I considered. She did have a reason of her own for coming to the shop, something she wanted to buy. That mattered in the second half. But suddenly following her into the kitchen to explain the hooks at the end would take me on an unnecessary detour.

“You could give us a little of it earlier. Don't open another door at the end.”

“Now you're in charge of my doors.”

“Only as far as this one today.”

She said she'd consider a sentence when the woman first entered, rather than adding it here. That addition to the earlier scene hadn't been written yet. I didn't record it as a settled revision.

She let me copy down today's provisional ending and keep it in the private envelope for the lamp-shop story. I left the first version's hidden hand out of the main text. On a separate scrap, I recorded my comments and labeled which sentences we'd tried during the discussion. I didn't cross out the existing middle section.

At the two screws, I stopped writing and asked whether they were still in the little paper wrapping the clerk had made. She said yes. I shouldn't let them fall into a coat pocket for no reason. I clarified that and read it through again.

The woman had finally left the shop. That didn't tell me whether Lumi would be jealous again. I couldn't use these pages as evidence about Wednesday either. I only knew I'd reached a door that could close, with a little rain still outside it.

“Do you like her?” Lumi asked.

“More than at the beginning. There are still things I don't like.”

“I didn't ask whether you liked me.”

“I was answering about her.”

After a while, Lumi said that answer would do. She didn't ask me to turn it into someone else's answer. I didn't suggest another reader.

I returned the envelope to the middle shelf and noticed the outermost towel slipping sideways again. As I went over, she asked what I was doing.

“Your reader also has a very uncooperative towel.”

“Don't hang it on a hook.”

“You made a good sales pitch.”

I secured it again with a different peg. Back at the table, I left the envelope closed and went to pour myself water. Lumi asked what I wanted for dinner. I hadn't decided. Those two screws had made me want something that required no sorting at all.
