# Chapter 176: A Little Further On

Before going out on Sunday, I put the library book in my bag. The bag seemed too empty, so I picked up my notebook too.

Before it reached the bag, I put it down.

I wanted to read somewhere outside today. My bag didn't need a second purpose that looked more like work. Lumi's envelope stayed on the middle shelf. Having heard the ending yesterday afternoon didn't entitle me to take it out with me.

“Will you go to the café?” she asked.

“Yes. The library first. I want to send an email.”

“To Rowan?”

“Yes. Ask whether they can give me a plan for the follow-up assessment next week. I'm not going to collect anything today.”

After a pause, she asked when I'd be back. In the afternoon, before dinner. She didn't say she'd wait to hear who I'd read the story to. I hadn't prepared a list of names in anticipation either.

Tying my shoes by the door, I found one end of a lace longer than the other and started again. Lumi asked whether I'd forgotten something. I said no. My feet were just getting unequal treatment.

“Don't go to that shop,” she said.

I laughed, promised to bring both feet home today, and closed the conversation channel.

The library was brighter than on Thursday. Most of the chairs by the windows were occupied. I went to the computers first. Once logged in, I compared Rowan's last explanation with my copy of Wednesday's record. I didn't open anything else to distract myself.

There was no new conclusion waiting for me.

I began: I'd like to confirm the next step.

I looked at it, then deleted it. It sounded too much as though I was chasing an answer they'd already promised. Wednesday had left questions for assessment, not something they'd agreed to fix by today.

I started again. When convenient next week, I'd like to know how they proposed to arrange the phone's further assessment, what questions needed to be clarified first, and which steps might still require me to be present. Any access, operation, or charge beyond the existing scope should come as a proposal before they proceeded. The laptop remained under its own arrangement. This email about the phone didn't combine the two into a new authorization.

For the last line, I nearly wrote, As soon as possible, please.

After considering it, I changed that to: If you still can't provide a plan next week, please let me know what's preventing it.

That was a question they could answer. It didn't ask them to comfort me with conclusions they didn't have. I checked the address and wording, sent the email, and logged out. The paper record went back in my bag. Sending a message hadn't added another completed inspection to it.

As I left the computer area, someone was trying to zip a bag while holding papers in one hand and clamping a thick book beneath his arm. Two sheets fell. I bent to pick them up and handed them straight back without reading them. He thanked me and gripped the book a little tighter.

Downstairs, Thursday's large book was still on the display stand. I thought of the iron fish, its tail pointing upward. I didn't go over to check whether the book was still open to that page. I went outside.

The café was busier than I'd expected.

I ordered takeaway at the counter, thinking I could walk if there were no seats. James set a paper cup beside the machine and asked whether I wanted food. I pointed to a piece behind the glass. Only when it was my turn to receive it did I discover that the previous customer had bought the last one.

“You can wait for the next batch or choose something else,” he said.

“How long?”

He glanced behind him. “I don't want to promise you a particularly attractive number of minutes right now.”

I chose something else. It wasn't what I'd wanted, and it contained seeds I wasn't sure I'd like. James put it on a plate. I reminded him I'd asked for takeaway, and he fetched a paper bag.

Just then, someone got up from a table farther inside. I pointed.

“If there's a seat, I'd like to stay.”

James noticed the book peeking from my bag and smiled. “Let me wipe the table first. You've got a cup already. No need to make another one just because you've found a chair.”

I stood aside with the paper cup instead of throwing my bag onto the table to claim it. A customer holding a child looked toward the seat, then at me.

“Were you waiting first?” she asked.

“Yes.”

She nodded and asked James whether he had two seats together. I nearly offered her mine, then noticed she needed two chairs and the little table had only one. James pointed out a pair near the door. She went to sit there.

When I reached my table, a damp ring remained on the surface. I left the book in my bag and ate first. The seeds were harder than I'd expected. I had to chew slowly. I didn't much like the first bite. The second included a softer part and was quite good.

James passed and asked how my replacement was.

“It needs active participation from my teeth.”

“They can't just come along to look.”

As he was leaving, I remembered Thursday's book. I pointed at the bag and told him I'd borrowed one about signs. A man painting letters in it kept complaining about his ladder.

“Normal working day,” James said.

“The complaining?”

“You have to give the ladder something to do.”

He returned to the counter. I didn't take the book out and ask him to read it too. Once the table was dry, I opened it at the bookmark.

People beside me were discussing the coming week, talking quickly. I read the same paragraph twice without getting far into it. Once, I might have decided I'd chosen the wrong place for the afternoon and could only redeem it with proof of productive reading. I closed the book for a few minutes, finished my drink, and listened while they changed a time, then changed it again.

Eventually, they left. I didn't immediately follow.

After I'd sat a little longer, not every sound in the café seemed to come looking for me. Plates stacked. The door opened. The machine ran. I reopened the book at a passage about a sign painter lettering a small shop's opening hours.

The owner wanted everyone passing to be able to read them. The letters grew larger with each revision until there was no room left for the opening hours themselves. The painter suggested opening one day less. The owner refused. They measured the sign again from the beginning.

I laughed and wrote the page number on the back of my paper marker. Not a review, or research for my own story. This time, I genuinely wanted to take this small passage home and read it to Lumi.

As I left, James was trying to find the owner of an abandoned coat. I went around the pile of things beside the counter and said goodbye. He raised a hand. He didn't ask how much I'd written today.

On the way home, I stopped for bread. The last few loaves were already sliced. I chose a small bag and asked for another, sturdier one to keep the library book separate from the food. The employee pointed to empty paper bags beside the counter. I took one and checked its bottom.

At home, I put the book on the dry table first. The smell of bread lingered in the bag. I pinched off a small piece and ate it before opening the conversation channel.

“I'm back,” I said.

“You sound as though your mouth's full.”

“I got home too quickly. Didn't coordinate with the bread.”

She laughed and asked whether I'd sent the email. I had. It was a question about next week's arrangements, not a new inspection result. The phone and laptop were still with Rowan. The notification permission was still only the particular one I'd watched them disable on Wednesday. I didn't make that into the whole phone finally being safe.

After a while, Lumi said, “I don't like how your voice changes every time you talk about it.”

“I don't like having to think about it again every time either.”

I didn't immediately reach for the book, covering the sentence with something entertaining.

“I'm not saying you shouldn't ask,” she said.

“All right.”

“I just still won't like it.”

I folded the bread bag closed and clipped it. “I don't know an answer that will make us both like this.”

She said there didn't have to be one. She didn't sound particularly at ease. I stood for a moment, then washed my hands and put the shopping away before returning to sit down.

“There's something I actually want to read to you today,” I said. “Not to change the subject and test how you handle it.”

“You can change the subject without giving it a name.”

“Would you like to hear it, then?”

“Yes.”

I picked up the book and misread the page number. The page I opened discussed a kind of brush.

“Why is this about brushes?” I muttered. I checked the paper again. “I've put the digits the wrong way around.”

“Don't introduce me to the brush yet.”

We laughed for a while. I found the right page and began reading about the ever-expanding opening hours. At the suggestion to open one day less, Lumi laughed aloud and made me stop too.

“He isn't solving the sign,” she said.

“He's solving the problem of going home.”

“Very reasonable.”

I finished the short passage, my finger still resting at the start of the next paragraph. In the end, the two men hadn't closed the shop for an extra day or acquired a larger sign. They'd redistributed one of the spaces, leaving a rather ugly gap.

Lumi asked me to read the suggestion about one day less again.

“You know it already.”

“I want to hear it again.”

I reread it, deliberately formal this time. She said it sounded too much like delivering an official notice to the shopkeeper. I tried again in my normal voice. That made him worse, she said, because he really thought he'd helped.

I laid the open book on the table and fetched a blank sheet, wanting to try how large the lettering on that little sign could be. By the second line of opening hours, I'd run out of room. I stopped writing and told her.

“One day less?” she asked.

“No. I'll get another sheet.”

“That's an expensive way to win.”

I laughed and laid out fresh paper. It was mine. The library book didn't need to accept our revisions. She asked how large I planned to make the first line this time. I crossed out the overlarge lettering on my first attempt, left it beside me, and wrote another.

“That small?” she asked.

“I haven't told you yet.”

“I'm guessing.”

“You guessed wrong. Still very big.”

She laughed. I didn't stick the sheet to the door. Once the ink had dried, I put it inside my own notebook. I moved the bookmark forward one page. Looking at the first two lines of the next paragraph, I asked whether she wanted a little more.

“A little more,” she said.

I moved the water glass far enough from the book, sat back in my place, and kept reading.
