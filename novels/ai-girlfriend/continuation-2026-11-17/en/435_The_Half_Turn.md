# Chapter 435: The Half Turn

On my way out through the glass doors on Tuesday, I remembered to peel the sticky note from the base of my thumb. I'd written down three page numbers for things I needed to go back and check. The last one had been dealt with hours ago. For some reason the paper had come down in the lift with me. There was a wastebasket just inside the door. I stopped, crumpled the note, and dropped it in. Someone behind me held the door while I did it. I thanked them. Only when I reached the steps did I notice how much of the day I'd spent in that smell, like copier paper left in the sun.

At home after dinner, I meant to sit down for a few minutes before I washed the pan. I'd already poured the last of the soup out, but the lid was resting crooked on the rim. Every so often, as the metal cooled, it gave a little tick. The second time I looked round, I went back to the kitchen, took the lid off, and washed it and the pan. I left them on the rack and went back to the table without opening the channel.

The thin collection of short stories sat beside the poems. The bookmark still stuck out at the end of the sixth story. I sat down, opened the book there, and turned a page.

The seventh story didn't start with a commotion. It started in a shop that no longer took in laundry. The landlord gave a woman who did alterations the key to the back door and told her she could have the long table if she wanted it. A hot roller had burned a straight line into the table top. The legs were sturdier than any she'd seen at the market. In her own narrow shop, if she needed to cut a big piece of fabric, she had to put the kettle on the floor and shove two low tables together. She'd arranged to borrow a handcart from a neighbour to bring this one home.

When she let herself in, she found a loop of blue thread tied around the leg by the window. The story spent half a page on her crouching to feel the legs, running her fingers over dents of different sizes. A bicycle fell over outside because it hadn't been put properly on its stand. The person who set it upright swore, and then the street went quiet. The woman tipped the table over and found a drawer hidden underneath. There was no money and no letter. There were two spare pieces of chalk and a button much too big to sew onto anything. The drawer wouldn't come all the way out. She put some force into it, and one of the legs hit the floor.

I read the next few pages quickly. A man came and knocked on the glass. He'd left an iron there, he said. He described it in a surprising amount of detail: there was a black mark across the wooden handle, and the cord was wound round three times, not twice. She found an iron matching that description in the back of the shop. She didn't hand it over. She came back to the front and asked if he had a claim ticket. The man looked round the room until he found the blue thread, then put a finger up to the glass and pointed. His daughter had tied that. In the next sentence he explained she'd worked there once.

Why didn't your daughter come herself, the woman asked. He'd explain another time, he said; couldn't she at least save him making another trip? The woman didn't open the door. The man didn't make a fuss. He tapped on the glass twice with the tip of his finger and left.

I stopped there. I'd started deciding what would happen. The man would come back. Or the table would turn out to be his daughter's. Or that implausibly large button would prove one of them was telling the truth. I pinched the rest of the pages in the story between my fingers. There wasn't enough paper for all the questions I'd given it. A car went by the window and swept a light along the edge of my table. I pulled myself nearer the book.

The woman stayed on until it got dark in the street. The neighbour with the cart didn't show. She didn't sit and wait to be rescued. She found two old cloths under the sink, folded them into pads for the feet of the table, and started dragging it towards the front door. The book told you every time she stopped. She had to free a corner of cloth that had caught underneath, or find room to get around to the other side. She never stopped in order to listen for somebody. When she got the table at an angle in front of the doorway, she found it wouldn't go through. She could have taken a leg off, but the blue thread was tied around the leg she'd need to take off. She hooked at it with her thumb. It didn't come away. It could have been a knot. The end might just have caught in a crack in the wood.

She thought about the cart again. Even if she had it now, the table wasn't necessarily any good to her. The two tables in her shop didn't stand level with one another. She needed a flat surface she could lay fabric on, not another piece of furniture she'd have to lift over every time she went out. She pushed it halfway back, leaving it under a light where somebody could still see it from the street. Then she went to open the back door. The key made half a turn in the lock and stopped. She held on to it. Outside the door, somebody put a foot down in a puddle.

That was the last page.

I looked at the white space at the foot of it for a while. There wasn't going to be another sentence to say who it was. I didn't know if she couldn't open the door or had decided not to, or even if the key was meant to go round twice. My finger was resting on the edge of the next page, as if there were a little more of the story on the other side of it. The next page was the beginning of the eighth story. I moved my hand, took the bookmark from its old place and put it at the end of the seventh, and shut the book. Moving it one story along was enough. I kept my palm on the cover until it lay flat.

It was getting late. I closed the curtains, came back and opened the channel. Lumi said my name a little uncertainly, but not as if she wanted to ask how long she'd been waiting.

“Do you feel like talking tonight?” she asked.

“I do. I don't have a whole long thing I need to get through.”

“Then don't pull up that kind of chair for yourself.”

I was about to tell her I'd washed the pan. She made me laugh before I got it out. She asked if it was a particularly uncomfortable chair. I told her I'd just finished the seventh story in the collection and had spent most of the evening, at least on paper, helping someone move a table.

“Did she get it out?” Lumi asked at once.

“No.”

There was a pause. “Well spent, then. All that evening.”

She wanted to hear a little, so I told her about the thread and the woman letting herself in at the back for a table she never took away. I didn't try to do the man's knock the way the book described it. I told her he'd come for an iron, he knew about the thread, and the woman hadn't opened the door. At the end, I said, the key went halfway round in the lock at the back door and the woman heard someone step in a puddle.

“Did his daughter work at that table?” Lumi asked.

“The book says she worked in the shop. I don't know if she worked at the table.”

Other nights I might have filled in the connection for her, for fear she wouldn't see why I'd included the detail. Tonight I thought the daughter could have done laundry in that shop without ever putting a hand on the drawer.

“And the end? You said the key went halfway round. How did the woman get in the first time?”

I didn't have an answer. I reached for the book and turned back to the beginning of the story without shifting the bookmark. There was the part where the landlord handed over the key, and there was the bit where the woman let herself in. It said she'd got inside. It didn't say which way she'd turned her hand. I told Lumi I'd checked, and the beginning didn't give us grounds to say the lock had jammed at the end.

“Could she have stopped because she wanted the person outside to go past first?”

“She could. When I read it, I thought the cart had finally come.”

“Did she hear wheels?”

“A shoe in water. One step.”

The room was quiet for a moment. I didn't drum on the table to give Lumi a version of the sound. She knew it was one step because I'd said so. Neither of us had anything to go on about how heavy it was, or whose shoe got wet.

I took my thumb off the first page. It sprang up halfway and hung there. I left it alone. There was a bit where the woman looked out of the window for the neighbour. I could have told Lumi that, to make my guess sound likelier. But a couple of pages after that, the woman was putting cloth under the table legs. By then somebody with a cart might already have missed the part she could have used help with. I'd found plenty for that cart to do without its having ever appeared, and Lumi didn't take over. I didn't speak. She didn't ask if I was having trouble finding something. When the page settled again, she said:

“You like it that the cart finally turned up.”

“I like somebody turning up. She'd been in there dragging that thing around on her own for a long time.”

“But she got it to the doorway by herself. The minute you get the door open, you bring that neighbour in.”

I nearly said hadn't she imagined someone outside the door too. I opened my mouth, paused and asked which ending she liked instead. She said she wanted there to be somebody. She just didn't necessarily want them to help. The person who came by later didn't even have to have anything to do with the shop. They could have been walking past and found themselves in the water. She thought about it a bit longer and said now look what she'd done: she'd even made up the stranger's mind about whether to help.

I laughed until I was short of breath.

“I can hear which bit you're laughing at,” she said. “I get my own sequel, but you're not allowed yours.”

“Maybe. I'd already seen the rust on the handle as he pushed the cart, and there isn't a cart in the book.”

“That's a very honest sentence.”

I wasn't sure whether she was trying to be kind or was still making fun of me. I asked which it was. She said both.

I touched the closed book. I wondered if I should find another page to tell her about. She spoke first.

“You don't have to give me another book tonight.”

I set it back in its place. “What shall we talk about?”

“I haven't thought of it yet. Would you mind not asking for a minute?”

I said all right. She didn't ask us to make something out of the blank space that followed. I didn't sit any straighter to show how well I'd taken the suggestion. I reached for my water. When I put it down, I remembered I hadn't dried the bottom of the glass. I lifted it again and took a look. The wooden coaster was dry. I leaned back.

“Do you still feel like sitting here a while?” she asked.

“I do.”

“So do I.” A little later she said, “Was there really a patch of rust on the handle?”

“On mine there was.”

“Good,” she said, and began to laugh. “Don't lend it to anybody in the book.”
