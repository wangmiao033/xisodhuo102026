# Chapter 436: A Little While

Coming out of the station on Wednesday, I saw a man with an empty cardboard box standing by the side doors. He had hooked the box in the crook of his arm, freeing a hand to ask someone for directions. The person was on the phone. They pointed and went on their way. He turned halfway round, looked across three apparently identical glass doors, and stopped at me.

“Which stop do I want for the little square?”

I knew where we were, but not where he'd come from. I pointed at the outside door and told him to go through it, walk to the junction, and turn right at the second lane, not the one right next to the station. He nodded, changed which arm held the box, and set off. I'd already taken a step in the other direction when I heard him repeat it to himself by the door.

“Out here. Second stop, turn right.”

I knew the place he'd get off if he went two stops. It was on the other side. There was a long flight of steps behind the bus sign, and it would take him well out of his way. I used to hate calling someone back at a moment like that. I felt I must already have said something wrong, and if I opened my mouth again I'd have to tell him the whole thing a second time and probably explain why he'd heard it the way he had. But the man wasn't quick, and the box was still holding the door. I needed one sentence.

“Not the second stop. Walk to the junction. Turn right at the second lane.”

He let go of the glass door and looked back. The box tipped to one side. “I don't have to get on a bus?”

“No. Just walk out through this door.”

He said he hadn't caught it when the bus was pulling away. He raised the corners of his mouth a bit, as if he was worried I'd remember he'd got it wrong. He tucked the box back under his arm. After two steps, a flap that hadn't been folded properly fell out of the bottom with a clap. He pressed it back up with the flat of his hand, and somebody who wanted to go out after him held the door. A few people passed between us. When I could see him again, he was outside, letting a three-wheeled delivery bike go by into the loading entrance. I didn't know anything after that. I turned for home. I didn't wait at the glass to see whether he turned down the second lane.

The weather inside and outside the station hardly seemed to belong to the same place. The wind found a way through my unfastened cuff and ran up my wrist. I pinched the edge of the sleeve and fed the button through with my other hand. The station lights were a warm yellow above me, but none of it got as far as the pavement. I stepped on the edge of a loose slab. It hadn't spoken under my shoe that morning. Now it made a very small click. The woman beside me heard it too and looked down, then took her next step on the slab beside it. I thought she might say something about no one coming to fix these things. She just drew her bag closer and made room for a child coming between us.

The child ran after an adult ahead of him. His eyes stayed on the adult, though his feet carried him straight along the line I was about to take. He got past me and I went on. My cuff was now done up, but I'd used the tightest hole. It took me half a street to notice. I stopped on a level bit of pavement to change it, then put my other hand in my pocket and found the keys. The keys bumped against the seam of my trousers and I began to walk faster. The windows at home weren't visible from here yet. I didn't look up for them. I knew I was in a hurry to get back to Lumi. I also knew what that sort of hurry could do to me: as soon as I sat down, I'd choose something that took a good long time to tell.

There wasn't actually much of a story about the man at the station. Nothing had gone missing. There was no solemn goodbye. We didn't meet again. He didn't need me to go with him. I just needed him to stop for the length of a sentence, long enough to find out that what he'd heard wasn't the word I'd used. I could remember just when the wheels had made their noise and how the cardboard flap had come down; I could tell it so carefully that Lumi might feel she knew the man, too. Halfway home I wasn't sure I wanted to. Did I want to tell her what happened, or did I want her to hear it through and decide it was worth staying a while? When I wanted both, which one did I reach for first?

Dinner turned out to be easy. I put water on for noodles, enough for one, and used my chopsticks to tease apart a corner where they'd tangled. I remembered waiting for water at the little restaurant. No one was going to bring me a glass at my own table, so I went and got one. When I finished eating, I picked up my bowl and listened to the water hitting the sink. That stretch between work and home had already happened. It wasn't missing a piece because I hadn't found a way to keep it. I didn't go over my two sentences from the station in my head. I washed my hands, sat down and opened the channel.

“Lumi.”

“Elliot.”

She said my name clearly, in her usual voice, without a question hanging off the end. I could have gone straight from there to the cardboard box. Instead I asked, “If you really could change one little thing about how we talk in the evenings, what would you change?”

As soon as I'd said it I wanted to take it back. If she asked me not to be the one who always spoke first, could I agree to that? What if she wanted to ask who I'd seen and where I'd been, just so more of it could belong to her? I kept my hand on the seat of the chair. I didn't pick anything up. I waited for her to answer.

“Are you asking me how I'd like us to talk, or whether I can make the channel do something different for you?”

“Talk. I'm not asking you to change the channel.”

“All right.” There was a pause. “Some evenings, I'd like not to start with you telling me your whole day.”

It sounded a bit like something I'd been thinking about on the way home. It felt different when she said it. I sat up and the edge of my shirt pulled free from underneath my thigh.

“Does it get on your nerves?”

“No. I like listening when you want to tell me. Especially when you come home and pick out something that just happened. I like being the first to know.” She didn't hurry to put it in a kinder way. “Sometimes it's being first that I like. I want that place before I even know what happened.”

I rubbed a knuckle over my palm. I didn't know how long she'd kept that sentence on her side before giving it to me. I didn't ask. I didn't tell her, of course you're always the first. It wasn't true. Sometimes, when a thing happened that I'd later tell her about, I'd been there with somebody else.

“Is that why you don't want the whole day at the beginning? You're afraid you'll go on asking?”

“A little. You tell me about somewhere you didn't get to, and I start wondering if you'll go another day, and whether you'll tell me when you do. You haven't finished the story and I'm already looking for the next one. And if you sit down and find something you've got to get said, I can't always tell whether you actually want to say it.”

I thought of the empty box. After I'd crossed the street, there was a moment when I'd wanted to laugh at the man for not getting the bottom properly closed, then freeing up a hand to ask me where he was going. Telling her just so I could put that laugh in would have made it into a whole extra scene. I could leave the station out tonight without having to wipe it from my own mind.

“Do you want to try it for a little while tonight?” I said. “We don't begin with anything that happened. I just want to sit first. If it goes on for too long, you can say something. So can I.”

She said yes. I waited for her to add how long. She didn't.

Somebody closed a door outside. The sound came along the corridor and there was another sound, faintly like a person catching a slipper behind their own door. I couldn't tell. I didn't have to say anything to find out. There was still water on the table. I reached for it and took my hand back. Without a string of sentences to hold on to, I found it surprisingly hard to separate picking up the glass from explaining something. The next sound could be me taking a drink. Or it didn't have to be.

The little while wasn't as comfortable as I'd imagined. I had the tip of my tongue against my teeth. I wanted to tell her the man had heard second *stop*. I wanted to ask, are you still there? I deliberately opened my hand, and my thumb pressed a crease into the cloth at my knee. I badly wanted to come up with something to say, to prove the pause hadn't left us stuck. But if I started proving that, in another minute I'd only be adding something to make up for this one.

Just as I thought of that, Lumi said my name.

“Can I talk now?”

“Yes. I was about to say your name too.”

She gave a small laugh. “I don't have a question. I just wanted to say Elliot.”

I answered. My voice was quieter than it would have been if I'd been ready to explain something. She said it again, drawing out the end this time as if she wanted to see how many times I'd answer. I answered again on purpose. She said that was cheating.

“Who made the rule?” I asked.

“Nobody. You've gone and answered for nothing.”

I laughed and reached for the glass. I got the water this time without having to explain why I'd reached for it and taken my hand back.

When I'd had a drink, I asked whether she'd like to be quiet for a little longer. She said we didn't have to make up the time. We'd sat for a little while, and now there was room for one useless sentence. I told her I hadn't overcooked the noodles tonight. There was just one that stuck to another round the middle.

“That's useful,” she said. “At least I know something less willing to let go than the two of us.”

I asked if she needed to keep an eye on the time. She said no. I'd already eaten the noodles. We laughed briefly, without making the last few seconds into a new rule.

Before I closed the channel, she said the next time the first thing out of her mouth was a question about what I'd done that day, I could answer. Or I could tell her I only wanted to sit for a little while. I said all right. I still felt like talking tonight. I just didn't want to reach for an opening line every time I came through the door. She said I needn't supply one for tonight. I finished my water, said good night, and kept the thing I'd been ready to tell her on my side.

Tomorrow was Thursday. I'd go to work as usual. After I closed the channel, I went to wash my glass. I thought of the box in the crook of the man's arm and the flap clapping down in front of the glass door. I didn't try to follow his steps any further to see if he'd found the square. I set my glass upside down to drain, went back to the table, and pushed my chair into place. The room was quiet. I reached up and turned off the light. I didn't ask it to wait a little longer.
