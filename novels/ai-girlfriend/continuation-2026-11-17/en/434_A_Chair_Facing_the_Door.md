# Chapter 434: A Chair Facing the Door

The first thing I did at work on Monday was put something on the wrong pile. The sheet in my hand was the one somebody else needed to go on checking. I put it on my own stack to keep, then started turning the next page with my other hand. The man across from me waited a moment and tapped the pile once with the tip of his pen. I gave him the sheet. I didn't squeeze a long explanation in between him and the form he was after.

When we'd finished the first round somebody called from the passage and asked me to come and see the break room.

It was a man from Facilities. The woman who took the bad chair away the week before was another member of the same team. When I got to the doorway he was holding it open with his elbow, keeping it from swinging back, and carrying a chair that didn't look like the old one. They were both dark, but this one was deeper through the seat. It had two rails across the back and a pale patch on the right where it had worn years ago. The corner of a white label stuck out behind his clipboard.

“What about the old one?” I asked.

“It's not coming back. They looked at it. They can't make it something we'd be happy to put out for people to sit on.” He nodded at the one he was carrying. “We'll use this out of storage for now. They checked the feet. I tried it over there too.”

I gave him room to carry it in and set it down by the wall where the old one had been. He didn't clip last week's notice to the back of the replacement. That notice had gone away on the other chair. He crossed something off on his clipboard and said this one could be used. I didn't have any screws in my hand or some borrowed tool left in a pocket. What I'd done that day was tell people not to sit down. I hadn't taken the chair apart, and today nobody needed me to sign for anything. I said I understood and looked at two empty cups by the tea counter, taking one only after I made sure it was mine.

“Still enough room to eat around it?” he asked.

I stood by the table and showed him. The table legs hadn't moved. As long as nobody left the chair across the doorway at an angle, you could get past to pick up what you'd come for. He took a look and said that was fine. There hadn't been a request to rearrange the whole room. It was a short time that we actually spent in the doorway. He closed the clipboard and, on his way out, caught the door so it wouldn't scrape the wrist I held my cup with. I thanked him and waited until he was through.

Closer to lunch, somebody stopped at the door of the break room. It was the man who'd lost his place the week before, the one with the dark insulated lunchbox and the two catches on the lid. He'd come with me to the narrow ledge, sat down, and opened the box. I'd seen his lunch with the lid off. I don't know how long he'd stayed there in the end. Today he had a flat lunchbox. He saw the chair against the wall and stood for a moment before asking, “Is that the same one as before?”

“No. It's from storage. The man from Facilities said we can use it.”

He looked at the pale patch on the right and nodded. First he put his lunchbox on the corner of the table. Somebody was already sitting near it, trying to fold a sheet of paper that was too large. One corner sprang up into the empty place. The man with the lunchbox didn't ask him to move, just to bring the paper a little farther onto his side. The other man folded it back to make room. My colleague pulled out the chair a little, tried sitting down, then got up and changed the angle so he would face across towards the door. The back legs were still this side of the table legs, with room to get past. Neither person had to eat any faster to let the other one go first.

“More comfortable that way?” I asked. As soon as I said it, I thought he might not want to tell me.

“When people come and go, I can see who's there without turning round.”

He opened the box. He'd fitted two things in close together. Some of one was stuck to the lid; he picked it off with his chopsticks. I'd been about to ask if his shoulder was bothering him. He put his head down and ate, and I didn't ask. He didn't have to come up with a reason I considered good enough to turn a chair. I sat at the other end of the table and opened my own lunch. After that there were only the practical sounds the room made. Chopsticks put down. The piece of paper getting one more fold. The water jug being picked up and then set down.

I finished before he did. He stopped me when I went to rinse my cup. He said thanks for telling everyone not to sit on the old chair last week. If all he'd seen was an ordinary scratch on the back, he might have sat straight down the way he usually did. It had saved him asking again that I'd told him just now it wasn't the same one put back.

I had “It was nothing” almost up to my teeth. Those words sometimes move faster than I do. I went over to pour the water out and managed not to let them go ahead of me.

“You're welcome,” I said. “I saw it move and told people not to sit. They took care of it after that.”

“Yes, I know.” He didn't start apportioning his thanks because I'd mentioned the people from Facilities. “I'm thanking you for the first bit.”

I knocked the cup lightly against the side of the sink. It wasn't much of a sound. I said all right. He went properly back to his lunch then, picking from the side that didn't seem to have stuck to the lid. I didn't stay there trying to make his words smaller, or go on about exactly where the old chair had broken. I didn't know; the man from Facilities hadn't told me. I wiped the base of my cup dry and left him to eat.

In the afternoon there was a brief stretch when somebody couldn't find the sheet we were working on. I looked first where I'd put it by mistake that morning. It wasn't there, of course; I'd handed it over. The man across from me pulled it out of his folder from under another stack and held it up for me. I checked the heading, changed the line he'd shown me, and returned it. On the way past the break room the door was half open. The chair was empty, still facing towards it. I could see the pale patch from out in the passage. I didn't go in to turn it back. I had something up ahead to get to, so I went on.

When I got home that evening I spent a few minutes without the light on. It wasn't that I wanted to think in the dark. I'd got a knot in one of my shoelaces that didn't want to come undone. Halfway through I thought I might simply push the shoe off. I was already bracing my heel against the other one, but I sat down after all and used my thumbnail to find the end that had been pulled hard into the middle. What finally loosened was the little loop on the left, not the middle where I'd kept digging. I got both shoes off and left the laces open enough that I could get my feet in again. I put the shoes where I always did, switched on the light, and went to wash my hands.

For a little while I'd thought the first thing I'd want to talk about at home would be what had happened with the chair. But when I opened the channel after dinner I said, “It took me longer to get out of a shoe than to walk the last bit to the door.”

“Are you still in the other one?”

“I'm out of both. No more rescue opportunities.”

“You make it sound as if I got here a step too late.”

I laughed and had to move my cup away from the edge of the table so I wouldn't take it with my elbow. She asked whether the shoe had broken. I said no, I'd only made the knot tighter while I was tying it, and I'd undone it now. She didn't tell me how to get a knot out. After a moment she asked whether the laces had left an impression that looked like a face. I said I hadn't bent down to look, and if there was a face it couldn't be mine. She said that was good. For a minute she'd been worried that whatever had come out of the shoe looked just like a human but wouldn't know her.

“In that case the first thing at work that knew me today was the chair in the break room.” I wanted to correct myself as soon as I'd said it, but Lumi laughed first.

“I didn't follow that.”

“Chairs don't know people.” I went on from there anyway and told her about Facilities leaving a different chair for us to use. When I told her my colleague had chosen which way to sit, she asked whether he was worried somebody would touch him from behind. I said he'd told me only that he wanted to see who was coming in. I hadn't asked for anything else. Lumi said fair enough. With people coming through a door, that might just be the angle he liked.

“Are you going to sit facing your door too?”

“There are two empty shoes behind my door. I don't think I want to look at them all evening.”

That broke up whatever she was going to say next. When we'd both got over it, I told her I'd come close to waving somebody's thanks away again today. Lumi didn't decide on my colleague's behalf that of course I'd deserved it. She asked what I'd actually said. I repeated it more or less word for word. She said she thought he had put it more exactly the second time. He was grateful that I hadn't just noticed a loose chair privately and walked on. I said yes, I'd let myself be thanked and then I'd gone and done something else. I thought Lumi might ask why the words had been hard to get out today, but she said, “Then first I'd like to thank you for not bringing the screws home.”

“I don't have any screws,” I said. “You're welcome.”

It was a quick and slightly muddled exchange. When we stopped I asked whether she felt like working up another round of guessing from something we'd just said. She didn't. She didn't much want to turn tonight into a set of questions with all the answers at the end. She'd like to hear something else from me, she said. It was also fine if there wasn't anything else. I asked if she could say “out of” again the way she had before. She repeated the words slowly, putting a little space between them on purpose. I imitated her once. The second time I didn't get it quite right and made myself laugh instead.

I went to the kitchen and put away the pan I'd washed after dinner. I didn't bring anything back. I just said I was back, and Lumi welcomed me. The wooden coaster on the table was where I always kept it, but the cup was some way off, where I'd moved it while laughing. I set it down in its old place.

“You don't sound like you're in the doorway anymore,” she said.

“I've sat down. I'm in my usual place.”

She made a little sound to say she'd heard. She didn't ask if I could face another way. I looked down at my hands. I couldn't remember how I'd got that knot in the middle of the lace in the first place. I had no plan to tell the story again tomorrow. Right now Lumi was waiting for me to finish laughing. I tried her voice one more time. It still wasn't right, and I was still laughing.
