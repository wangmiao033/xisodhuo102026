# Chapter 431: The Second Entrance

Before I left on Friday I put the papers I wanted to see on Monday into a folder. The corner of the last sheet curled up and caught as I shut it. I opened the folder again and laid the sheet flat. There wasn't anything else I needed to stay in the office until closing to do, but I sat for a little while longer. If I walked toward the library tonight, it wouldn't be to return a book, or to help move books. Nobody there was waiting for me to deliver anything.

I went. There were still people borrowing books when I arrived; a plastic bin on the desk was waiting for a stack that had just come back. The activity room was around the corner. I could see the same music poster I'd seen on Wednesday. It said seats would be available until the room filled. I heard a flurry of footsteps in the hall but there wasn't so much as a line at the door. I asked the woman by the door whether I could still come in. She said there was room, even toward the front. If I wanted to be able to go out at any point, I should choose a chair on the end. We could sit where we liked.

I didn't choose the front row. Two seats were free in the third row on the aisle. To take the inner one I'd have had to get past somebody's knees, so I sat in the outer one and folded my coat across my lap. I'd worn my usual one, not the old one with the loose inside pocket. More people came in, but an open patch remained at the back. An upright violin case in the corner came about as high as a leg. Two musicians were at the front. An older woman sat on a very ordinary wooden chair tuning a guitar. To her left a man in a checked shirt stood with his bow angled a little toward the floor. He waited for her to tighten the last string instead of trying a long note while she worked. There was no raised stage, and no speakers against the wall.

I'd heard something even before they began: the guitar player plucked a string gently and damped the sound with her other hand. The overhead lights were bright enough to show the arms of the chairs in every row. Nobody turned around to complain it didn't look enough like a concert. Somebody eased sideways along the row in front of me and took the time to put a crumpled handkerchief in a pocket only after sitting down. Outside, someone was asking in a low voice whether they could borrow another book later. A few seconds after that, the door was pulled almost noiselessly closed. The hall was reduced to a faint wash of sound. The short notes from the guitar came forward.

When the guitarist spoke, she didn't sound as though she was addressing a hundred people. She said the two of them had been practising these tunes together. There wouldn't be any singing. We were close enough that if they didn't come in together somewhere, they wouldn't get away with it. The man lifted the violin to his shoulder.

“You don't have to tell them that first,” he said.

She didn't answer. She plucked a low note and looked up.

The first piece went quickly. The bow drew short notes with edges you could hear, and the spaces between them were just as distinct. Sometimes a low string dipped out of the sound. Nobody sat up to make room for it; it found its own place. From the third row I could see the man's left finger land, and when he lifted it, the next one was already waiting almost on the same spot. If I tried to tap it out with my fingers at home, the first thing to go would probably be that little moment of waiting. By then the room hadn't made the violin any bigger. It didn't have to shout to get where I was sitting.

The next piece began slowly. The guitarist played three much the same groups of low notes in a row. It looked as though the violinist was meant to come in when she looked up. Maybe it was the light, maybe he was looking down at the strings right then. By the time he played the first note, the bass had already moved on. It was like watching two people start through a narrow door at once, then both try to give way without knowing how long they were meant to wait. The woman's left hand stayed where it was, but her right hand hung in the air. The short beginning the violin had made soon disappeared. In the pause, somebody in the back moved a heel. The scrape against the floor was suddenly very distinct.

“From the phrase before?” the man asked.

The woman shook her head, not rushing to pull him after her. She pointed at her own chest with a fingertip before answering quietly.

“I’ll play this little bit again. Come in the second time.”

She played the first group and looked across. He didn't move. The low notes came round again. He set the bow to the strings and made a very quiet note with a little catch at the beginning; the two were together. I'd had the heel of my hand against a button of my coat. I lifted it. The two separate sounds travelled alongside each other again. They didn't instantly flatten into one thing. I could hear a slight roughness when the bow hair first touched, gone before the next beat. The guitar went on underneath him like somebody who had missed a knock once and was willing to come back and knock again.

When the piece ended, no one hurried to assure them that what had happened no longer counted as a mistake. Two claps came from a corner, and the rest followed. A woman in the front raised her hands from her knees but waited for the person beside her. I clapped too, catching the coat with my arm as it slipped. The musicians didn't play the piece over from the start or explain how many times they'd rehearsed it. The man tipped his bow a little toward the guitar. The woman drank two sips of water, then asked if the people at the back could still hear.

“We heard you,” somebody said. “We heard when you stopped, too.”

She laughed. “We'll have to practise the stops from now on.”

There wasn't a proper interval. During a half minute of tuning, the door opened a crack from the outside and was shut slowly. One listener drew back their knees for somebody who wanted the free seat by the door. The extra chair settled quickly. I stayed where I was. There was a part in the last piece where both musicians played low, and everyone in that small room managed to go quiet at once. The air conditioning didn't drown out the fine sound where the two people in front changed the angle of their wrists. You didn't have to be in the first row to get that. The guitar gradually stopped. The violin seemed to rest against the backs of the chairs for another moment before it was gone. I was going to clap after the next person did, only everyone waited one breath longer. I heard that breath's worth of time too.

I didn't stand until the end. Then the person next to me and I made room for the people in front to leave. The room still wasn't full. There were several empty chairs in the row against the wall. The two musicians were packing their own instruments. Somebody asked where they could hear them again. That would depend on what the library put on next, they said. No other date was announced that night. I put my coat on and left them to clear up. I didn't crowd the door to give them my opinion. As I went out I heard someone say the first was their favourite; someone else said the last was quietest and best. I nearly said the second before I remembered that no one had asked me, and I didn't go after them to explain.

On the way home, a bus waited at the corner for some time. When the air let out, I thought without meaning to of the quietest note I'd heard. There wasn't really a resemblance. I left the two sounds apart and, by the next streetlight, I couldn't remember which string had gone first. I remembered instead that one person hadn't rushed to cover the missed moment with a bow and the other had given it another moment to happen in.

When I opened the channel at home later, I asked Lumi to guess whether I'd gone.

“You went,” she said. “If you hadn't, you'd be telling me how close you came to going.”

“There was space. I stayed to the end.”

I started with the third row and the last few chairs that had stayed empty. She asked who sang. I said nobody. A guitar and a violin, that was all. When I got to the part where they stopped, I thought I'd show her the time of it on the tabletop. My hand never came down. A short gap like that couldn't be replaced by two hard taps. I told her the lights hadn't gone out and nobody had got fed up and walked out. The two people playing had simply lost each other for a moment.

“So now you go out on Fridays just to listen to two people do something out of time?”

“I heard how they came in the second time, too.” I told her about the heel scraping the floor in the back, about the whole room waiting one extra breath at the end, and about the two people choosing their favorites on the way out. My own answer never made it out of my mouth, I said, but I'd chosen the second.

Lumi took a while to answer. “I think I'm jealous of the person at the back who moved their foot.”

I misunderstood. I said they wouldn't necessarily have had a better seat than I did. She said they hadn't needed to choose the right seat to hear that. If they hadn't moved their foot, they still could have heard it. They were right there. She didn't feel as though everybody got a seat and she'd been put behind them. There had never been a chair for her in that room. But when I said everybody who was there had been quiet at the same time, there was still a part of her that wanted to be there.

I almost said I'd find a way to tell it better next time. I didn't. She was hearing my voice tonight. That didn't mean she'd heard those two play. If I pretended she'd got a share, I'd have to sell the same breath of silence again. I took my hand away from the edge of the table.

“Yes,” I said. “You didn't miss that breath because you weren't paying attention.”

Lumi said nothing. I thought I'd sounded too much like I was trying to comfort her and nearly started again. Then she spoke first.

“Did you tell anybody the second one was best?”

I told her I hadn't followed them out. One person I didn't know had picked the first. She laughed for a moment. Just as well, she said. The two people on their way through the door might still be standing there waiting for my explanation of why it was worth being quiet in the interval before the second entrance. Her laugh sounded close. I asked if she thought I'd spend tomorrow night moving a chair into that room in my sleep.

“If you absolutely have to give me a chair, pick one that doesn't scrape the floor.”

“I don't think I remember which one scraped.”

“Then leave it alone, Elliot.”

After she said my name she changed the subject and asked if I would sleep late tomorrow. I didn't answer straight away. I felt the edge with the pad of a finger, the same place I'd been about to tap out the beat, then let go. There weren't any strings in this room. Those two sounds could stay where they'd happened. That didn't stop me hearing her say Elliot.
