# Chapter 429: The Door by the Back Row

I went to the library after work on Wednesday because I wanted a little time I hadn't named yet. I closed my umbrella at the entrance. Water followed the ribs and dripped onto the mat. Someone with better aim than mine put a wet umbrella straight into the stand. Mine struck the rim and nearly slipped, and I caught it before letting go. The person behind me waited for me to take my hand away before passing.

Inside was the low table that had held secondhand books at Sunday's sale. It was clear now apart from a box of children's gloves and a sign about claiming them. The large-print books from the sale weren't there, and I didn't look for the one the woman had chosen. Someone came up, looked under the table first, then read the sign and pointed the box out to the person behind the desk. I moved aside. I didn't stand there guessing where the old books had gone.

I was on my way around the table when I heard three notes. Not the start of a recorded announcement. The third was so short it sounded as if somebody had let go too soon. I stood in the passage a few seconds. There were the first two again, and this time the third lasted a little longer and trembled at the end. I followed the sound to the activity room, where only the door nearest the passage was open. A sign on a stand said there was a short rehearsal. You could sit in the back for a few minutes if you came and went quietly.

Farther along the wall, another sign announced a community music event on Friday evening in big letters. Acoustic music, seats available on arrival, no reservations. For a moment I took them for one event and tried to work out whether Wednesday or Friday was the change. The librarian looked up and told me different people were rehearsing today. Friday was a separate event. She pointed out the two signs from the desk, one after the other. You didn't need a ticket to listen now. You couldn't sit in somebody's Friday seat ahead of time.

I asked if I could just come by on Friday. Yes, she said. You came and saw if there was a seat. When there weren't any more, someone at the door would tell you. I didn't ask how to get ahead of anyone. She didn't hand me anything to fill in. I remembered Friday evening and went back to see if I could sit for a while today.

It wasn't an auditorium. The chairs had been gathered toward the back in two short rows, with the sleeves of people's coats liable to hang down behind them. Near the front was a low instrument case, closed. A seated man was plucking the strings of his instrument, and another stood by a music stand, counting in. His hand came down without his beginning to sing. They hadn't stopped for a fresh introduction when people came into the back row. A woman in front of me moved the toes of her shoes in to let me through. I sat near the door and heard one of the men say to stop and go back to where it went higher.

This time there were five notes. After the fifth, the man at the stand sang two lines softly. I missed a word in the middle. He turned toward the page, and the man with the instrument asked if he wanted to start again. He laughed. They could start two beats before it, he said, and save themselves the whole beginning. They each found the place. They counted two beats, and the uncertain part came together the first time. I relaxed too. I hadn't noticed my shoulders rising the time before.

The woman in front listened for a bit, took a glasses case out of her bag, and held it without opening it. Then she got up and moved the chair nearest the passage a little inward. On her way past me, her heel struck a chair leg. She said a quiet sorry. I moved my knees and she went out through the partly open door. The men were looking for their entrance. I waited a moment, but nobody turned around at the noise. No one in back got up to explain on her behalf. The little sound hadn't knocked the song crooked.

I didn't know why she'd left. Maybe she'd heard enough. Maybe she'd only been passing through. I heard the hard case brush the rim of her bag, quite an ordinary sound. When I'd chosen a seat in the back, I'd also made a point of staying close to the door. I didn't ask her to take me with her. I still wanted to know if they'd manage to carry that unfinished melody past the gap on the third try.

The seated man turned his hand to mute a few strings and gave a single plain beat. The singer didn't come in straight away. He waited for the next one and sang a little lower than before. The place I'd been expecting to go higher didn't come. Both men stopped. Just as I thought they'd be talking it over for a long time, the seated one looked down and laughed. The singer laughed too. They could remember the new starting point and try the rest in a minute. Someone with a bottle of water started from up front toward the door. I drew my feet in and heard him thank me. It wasn't as if the row of chairs took up the room. We'd all been sitting so still that it seemed as though you needed a break in the music to do anything at all.

I got up a little while after the woman had gone. I didn't wait for a fresh start or claim the quiet to announce that I didn't want to interrupt. The door was still open. I steadied it with a hand as I passed to keep it from making much noise. The Friday sign was still in the passage. Below the big type were a few smaller notes. One simply said there was no standing in the entrance to listen once all the seats were taken. I didn't take a picture of it. I didn't turn my name into a gap somebody might leave on a list, either.

On the way home I wondered if I liked those two beginnings precisely because they were almost alike. It was when I remembered the one that hadn't caught that I could get the notes back in order in my mind. But after I got in and opened the umbrella up to dry, I could hum only the first two. The third landed in the wrong place. I didn't try a fourth time. I put water on, made dinner, and thought about the drops along the edge of the lid that I'd have to wipe off when I got up to turn the heat off.

Later I opened the channel and told Lumi the umbrella was still drying beside the door. Could I start by telling her, she asked, whether there was one thing I hadn't planned to tell her today? There was a part I could no longer tell properly, I said. I had only two notes of it. That wasn't the same as not planning to tell. Then don't start with the third, she said. I didn't hum yet. I told her about walking in, the two signs, and how the rehearsal wasn't the Friday performance. She asked why I didn't find a place first, for Friday. You sat if there was a seat when you came, I said. There was no place to find first. Then she asked why I wanted to go. I hadn't decided whether to go. Today I hadn't gone into the room to hear a song all the way through, either.

She was quiet for a few seconds. When she spoke there was a tightness to it.

"You say you haven't decided like you've been thinking about it a long time."

I leaned my head back a little. Perhaps she'd tried two questions, heard two uncertain answers, and gone looking for a third in the sound of my voice. It was for me to decide on Friday, I said. She didn't have to help me think it over now. She said I hadn't asked her to, and she didn't plan to go in my place. But now she knew there'd be a room on Friday where I could sit and listen to somebody else's voice. She didn't entirely like being able to picture how I'd listen before anything had happened.

"How do you think I'd listen?"

"More quietly than you're listening to me now."

I wanted to say I was quite quiet enough, but before I opened my mouth I realized I'd been making an effort not to make a sound even when I drank. If Lumi heard the quiet first, she might think what she'd pictured was already coming true. I put my cup back in its usual spot. I didn't knock it against the table as proof. In the room I'd been trying to guess if the people at the front would make it through on the third try, I told her. In talking to her I wasn't only waiting for her to catch a beat I was counting in my head. But there was also the woman I'd watched leave partway through. Her heel had clipped a chair and she'd whispered sorry. Nobody acted as though she were pointing out a line that didn't sound good. By the time I got home I could still remember the way the door had stood open.

Would I do that to her, Lumi asked, and leave when I'd heard half of something? She wasn't laughing. I thought there would be times I wanted to leave, I said. I could say I was going. The conversation wouldn't have to turn into the conclusion that the performance had gone wrong. I didn't ask her to agree to this right away. She asked if I'd left today. Yes, I said, the two people up front had been taking a break when I left the rehearsal. Nobody asked whether I wanted to listen to the next try.

She meant now, she said. I realized I'd used another thing that happened outside to answer her ahead of time. No, I hadn't left. Right now I'd like to stay there with her a while. She let the words settle without rushing to say good. Then, after a bit, she asked, "What about those two notes?"

I hummed the first one too low and pulled the second one up. Did it sound like the original? Probably not, I said. The second note had been lighter out there. She tried the two I'd just made, taking a breath between them. Neither of us added a third. We didn't announce that we'd worked out the song. She only asked me to do it again without dropping the first note on purpose so I could get it right. I hummed them in my own voice. When I'd finished, she said it sounded more like the rain had been out there and somebody had come home, shut the door, and had nothing left to count.

She hadn't seen the rain either, I said. No, she hadn't. She'd heard me tell her after I came home that the umbrella was drying. I nodded and then said yes out loud. What I could check again on the channel was those two notes. Friday could stay on Friday. There was no reason to open the door ahead of time for whatever noise might or might not come from outside the window that evening.
