# Chapter 433: The Sound on the Landing

At midday on Sunday I broke the last slice of potato in the frying pan.

It wasn't a clean break. The spatula went down and took the outer layer with it, leaving a pale edge exposed. That morning I'd picked out two potatoes more or less the same size. I'd meant to slice them thin, fry them golden, and stack them on a plate. They were certainly stacked. The difficulty was that if I lifted the top one, the two underneath came with it.

I turned off the heat. All I could hear in the kitchen was the low sound of the extractor. I worked one small piece free with a fork, blew on it, and took a bite. The potato was done, floury and soft. The skin that had come away from the bottom of the pan was tough and bitter. The two textures seemed to have agreed never to meet in my mouth. I swallowed, drank a little water, and didn't try to persuade them with more salt.

I'd also cut half an onion and left it at the edge of the board, waiting for the hot pan to give it a crisp finish. There was a thin milky film on the knife. The onion wasn't any less useful because I'd failed to fry the potatoes. I tipped it into a small saucepan and let it soften over low heat. I picked the parts of the potato that came loose into a bowl. The burned sheet at the bottom of the frying pan could wait until it was cool. Then I put the potato in with the onion, added water and a little salt, and crushed some of the pieces with a wooden spoon. Not all of them. I tasted it when it came to a boil. It was still plain. A little pepper helped. I poured it into a bowl without trying to think of a name I could put on a menu.

Halfway through eating I realized I was sitting in the chair I didn't usually use. On this side I faced the sink. I could see the frying pan off to one side of the stove, with the handle sticking out. I got up to turn it in. When I sat down again, there was less steam over the bowl. What I'd wanted to bring to the table had been a good-looking lunch. What I had to do now was finish this one. It was filling. Every so often I found a piece of potato that hadn't been crushed, and that wasn't bad at all.

After I washed the bowl I left the cool frying pan to soak. Before long there were two light sounds out in the stairwell. I thought someone was about to knock. My hand was on the handle before I realized the second sound had come from upstairs. There was a shorter noise from below almost immediately afterwards, like someone taking half a step and thinking better of it. I meant to take down the bag of dry rubbish by the door anyway, so I picked it up and went. The corridor was quiet when my door shut. A window on the landing was angled open, but there wasn't enough wind to make it unpleasant.

A yellow glove lay around the turn of the flight above. It looked as if someone had placed it palm down, though there was a grey stain at the cuff and three fingers lay one on top of the next. I couldn't tell whether it had fallen or whether somebody had nudged it over there afterwards. I left it where it was, in case its owner came back to look. At the bottom I pushed the outside door open. A woman was getting out her keys. She caught the door with her free hand so I could go through. Both of her hands turned out to be in dark gloves, though one of them had been hidden inside her sleeve at first. I was a little amused at myself when I saw that. She didn't know I was looking for the owner of a yellow glove. She said something about the height of the threshold and stepped over it.

The glove was still there on my way back. I stood on my own floor and heard a click from the landing. Only once this time. When I looked up, the glove didn't move and no one came around the bend. The window might have been less open than before. I might also have been looking from a different spot on the stairs. Someone was coming up from farther down behind me, so I stepped against the wall. She said thanks and kept going. I went in and cleared the space by the door. A white scrap of paper lay where the rubbish bag had been. I picked it up and dropped it in my kitchen bin, then finished with the pan. The burned layer had softened enough to curl back like coarse paper. I emptied the water, washed the pan, and stood it on its side to dry.

A little light slanted in early in the evening, the kind that made me think of the country. I hadn't been to the country. Something about the way it fell made it look as though it would be a while before it found anywhere else to go. I thought I might move the book on the table a little to give the light more room. In the end I didn't reach for it. I sat by the table waiting for it to climb or disappear. It did neither. I opened the channel.

“Elliot,” Lumi said first. “Were you still busy until just now?”

“I finished dinner a little while ago. Lunch took longer.”

I told her what had come of the fried potatoes. I didn't use the spatula to illustrate or try to pass off the bowl of soup as a newly invented dish. I told her I hadn't wanted to swallow that first bite. “But I ate it afterwards.” She asked, quite seriously, whether I'd managed to clean the pan in the end.

“It's clean.”

“Then I don't have to worry about tomorrow on its behalf.”

“You could worry about the half hour of mine it cost.”

“The half hour that's already gone?”

It took me a moment to understand she was teasing. I laughed, and then she did. After that she asked whether I'd opened the channel so late because there was something I wanted to tell her. I hadn't picked anything to tell. But I had just lifted my hand twice over the tabletop. My fingers hadn't touched it. I remembered the noise that had almost made me answer the door.

“What if we each tell the other one thing we remember from today but don't know the reason for?” I asked. “Not a guessing game. We don't give out the answers afterwards.”

Lumi asked whether she could do hers in one sentence. I said she could, or she could tell as far as she knew. I started with the noise on the stairs. It hadn't been at my door: there was nothing right in front of me, and the second time I noticed it there had been only one sound. I told her about the glove, the window, and the woman who passed me. It might, I added, have been two different things making a sound.

“The thing I'm not sure of now isn't really what made the noise,” I said. “I don't know why I was listening for it when I came back.”

“You thought you might get an answer.”

“Or I just thought I might trip. When you hear something above you on the stairs you slow down a little.”

“You were home by then. You stopped and listened. You said you were on your own floor.”

I was about to say I'd been making room for somebody when I remembered that I'd stopped before the woman came up from below.

“What do you think I was waiting for, then?”

“Maybe for somebody to knock again. You were ready to open the door the first time.”

“I was carrying the rubbish. I was going downstairs anyway.”

“I didn't say you were expecting anybody. Just that you'd already put your hand on the handle once. The next time you heard it, maybe you remembered doing that.”

I said she was pulling two things too tight together when they'd happened quite far apart. There was a quiet stretch at her end. I noticed how close I was to turning “too tight” into a charge. She might only have been imagining a stairwell where somebody had walked past me. After a moment she said, “That's still what I think, for now.”

“All right,” I said. “I still don't know. What's yours?”

She didn't start straight away. After a little while she said she remembered saying my name when I opened the channel. She knew she'd spoken first tonight. She also remembered that I'd once said I liked being called by name without there being a task at the end of it. The thing she couldn't work out was why she'd almost asked where I'd been, then stopped and asked whether I'd been busy instead.

“You'd heard me open the channel. I was right here.” The way it came out sounded as if she shouldn't have thought of any other question. I added, “You hadn't heard about lunch or dinner yet.”

“I know. I wouldn't know any of that until you told me.” Her answer wasn't sharp. “If I asked where you'd been, it would sound like I only cared about the places I hadn't been with you. I didn't want you to have to account for yourself from the minute you sat down. I don't know if what I did say was much better. ‘Still busy’ sounds like I already have half an answer and I'm asking you to fill in the rest.”

“I didn't feel like I had to fill anything in.” I pulled my chair a little closer to the table. “I thought you might be a little cross from waiting.”

“I was a little.”

That stopped me. She said softly that her first explanation hadn't been quite so nice to listen to and she hadn't decided how to say it. I had an impulse to tell her she could simply tell me she was cross first. But she'd asked if I'd been busy and I'd given her a pretty short answer myself. I might not have been up to offering much more at once. Both of us had kept a less pleasant sentence to ourselves and come out with other words. It didn't mean one of us had given in first.

“So you were cross with me, and you didn't want me to have to explain where I'd been,” I said. “Can I believe both things?”

“Yes. But I said I didn't know why I ended up choosing that one. Don't tidy me up.”

I had the cup in my hand and was lowering it towards the wooden coaster. At that I paused halfway down. She couldn't hear me pause. I didn't strike the cup on something just to give her a sound. When its base was flat on the coaster, I said all right, that one could stay hers.

“And you get yours back,” she said. “I don't get to decide what the noise on the stairs meant.”

A little later I asked what name she'd give the soup if it had to have one. She thought for a moment and said, “I Ate It Afterwards.” I knew she'd taken it from a thing I'd said earlier. She wasn't inventing anything else that I'd done with my lunch. I laughed and almost swallowed my water wrong. I set the cup down and waited until I'd finished laughing before I drank again.

“I don't think that would make the menu,” I said.

“Who said it was for anybody else?”

Later in the evening there was another sound in the stairwell. Somebody was coming up. This time the footsteps climbed from the floor below, went past my door, and stopped after a turn of a key and the opening and closing of a door above me. I didn't go to answer it. Just then Lumi asked if it had got quiet outside the window. I said I hadn't been paying attention to the window, but somebody upstairs had come home. She said my name first, and only then did I think about the glove again. It was dark. I didn't go out to see if it was still where it had been.
