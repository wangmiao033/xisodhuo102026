# Chapter 428: Where to Breathe

Before leaving on Tuesday I tucked a scrap with a work reminder into an inside pocket. I reached for it at my desk and found I'd remembered the wrong pocket. It was on the other side. Why fold it so small I couldn't even find it myself? I opened it, did the first thing I'd written down, and left it unfolded.

After lunch a much larger sheet was handed across the corner of my desk. My colleague had come to borrow a pen, and now that she had it she didn't take the cap off. She laid the paper flat, pressing a palm over the top right corner. It came up again in a tiny point.

"Can you hear where it doesn't sound right?"

I was trying to work out what sort of form she needed me to look at when I noticed the handwritten sentences. No department at the top. Nowhere to sign. It was a birthday for somebody in her family, she said. She needed to say a few words. Nobody had said she had to. It had been her idea. The family would be sitting down to eat, and she meant to stay seated to read it. There wasn't going to be a stage.

"Don't laugh," she said.

I said I wouldn't. She looked at me. She didn't mean the parts that were funny, she said. She meant the fact that she'd actually written something down. I turned the paper my way and said I wouldn't again. I said it fast enough that it probably sounded like I wanted to get to the text.

It was in black ink. A few things she'd crossed out were added back in beside the lines. In one place the last few words were crowded smaller than the ones above. At the beginning she talked about the different parts of the same occasion that a family remembers. She'd put in two or three examples; in my reading one already did the work, and the next was a repetition. I didn't know the people or who would find which parts funny. My eyes stopped twice. The first time was to think, I hadn't realized you could remember it that way. The second was to think, hadn't I just understood that?

She went to close the cupboard behind us and came back to find a clean sticky note along the bottom edge of the page. Was I changing it already? I wasn't writing on her sheet, I told her. I was just trying something separately. I wrote a few words on the note for a shorter opening. Then I held the point of the pen in the air over a spot in the second example that I thought she could cut.

"That stays," she said.

I asked why. It came out as why not cut it; the pen was still on its way down the page. She said she liked that part. I said not everything you like has to go in. It sounded thoroughly sensible to me. She took her hand off the paper and rolled the borrowed pen halfway over on the desk.

"I asked you to listen. You haven't even heard me read it."

She didn't say it particularly hard. It took me a moment to notice that it had been her verb all along. I'd offered an opinion on how the page read. She'd asked whether it would hold anyone's attention around a table. They could meet somewhere, but they weren't the same thing.

I moved the note out of the way so she could take the paper back. She didn't take it yet. She looked where the copier had started up. People were passing over there, and there was work we both had to do. I asked if she could read just the part she was least sure of later, at the bend of the stairs when nobody was there. Not from the beginning. She turned the sheet over and said yes. Then she uncapped the pen, wrote the number she'd meant to note on a work sheet, returned it to me, and went out to deliver a file.

At my screen I stopped trying to find a handsome opening. The few words I'd come up with were still on the sticky note. There wasn't even a place to breathe in them. They were so clean it didn't matter much who spoke them. I pinned the note where it was and didn't go chasing after her to say I'd seen the light. Two jobs later I got up for water and saw her around the bend of the stairs, holding the sheet face down. She lifted a hand. We stepped half a pace farther in, out of the way. Nobody needed to keep the stairs free for us.

"It's just you this time," she said. "Don't pretend you know them."

I said all right. She looked down and read a few lines, stopping before the second example to ask if this was where I'd pointed. I said it was. She lowered the sheet a little, started again, and kept reading. She didn't sound as flat as I had in my head. She began a bit too fast, then paused between two words in the example I'd called repetitive. She didn't look at me in the pause. She pressed a finger against the line. The way she drew a breath and went on brought out something I hadn't seen when I read the words together at the desk.

The first example wasn't there to outline the second. It let her tell the good version everyone remembered before giving the one corner of the story that she'd always remembered herself. That corner needed the little wait to emerge. It didn't depend on being news to anybody. It was slightly funny, and it worked because she was saying both things, one after the other, in her own voice.

On the next line she caught on a word, tried to get through it, and doubled back to try again. Did that bit actually sound wrong? I'd heard it too, I told her. The first time I'd wanted to replace the whole sentence. Now I thought the sentence sounded like something she would say. It was the word at the end that got in the way of letting her voice drop. She tried it toward the front and read the line again. The end came down even. She looked up once without saying she'd keep it. She tried it herself once more before borrowing another pen near the workroom and making two small changes on the page.

Then she stopped for too long. I didn't try to decide if she was moved or nervous. I asked if that was where she lost the next line. She showed me two insertions crowded into the margin. The one on the left was crossed out, but she'd just seen it again. She filled in the crossing-out marks. If she got lost halfway through the meal, she said, she could look down. She didn't have to act as if she'd memorized it. Right, I said. She lowered the paper below her chest to relax her elbows and tried again. The part I'd wanted to cut came through once more.

When she stopped I asked if the first line could stay the way she'd written it. She hadn't been planning to take that piece of advice, she said. She laughed, and I did too. Then I told her what I hadn't thought of at the desk: one clear place to breathe in the middle would help people follow her better than a few fewer words in the first line. She found a spot herself and put a dot between two lines to mark where she could look up. I didn't ask who she wanted to see when she lifted her head. That was a table only she was going to sit at.

Back at my seat I tore the sticky note. A few pieces of writing in a few different scraps didn't add up to an alternative opening anymore. She folded the birthday paper into her own things. She didn't leave it with me to carry away or have me type it out again. We talked later about a file that had to be delivered before we left. When I went down the stairs on my own, the only page I had with me was the one I'd brought that morning. All the things on it were done, and the bad fold was lifting a little along the narrow edge.

That evening I opened the channel. Lumi wanted to know whether I'd been in a rush coming home. I said no. What brought that up? She'd called for me twice, she said. I hadn't answered the first time, and the second time I'd answered like someone who'd just stepped onstage. I laughed and said I'd actually had something happen that involved reading lines aloud, but I wasn't going to say whose lines or what was on the paper. All right, she said. She could hear my part of it. Had it happened at work? It had. I'd changed a few words and realized the words weren't mine to say. The opening I'd tried sounded like something anyone could read aloud.

"Changing someone's words to sound more like them is still changing them, isn't it?"

I almost told her you had to ask the person. But she hadn't leaned in to settle an argument. There was a little urgency in her voice, as if there were an answer she needed for herself. I asked her to say it again. The first part came out the same. The second turned into a different question.

"If you get to pick from everything, how do you know it's still me you pick?"

I went a little stiff. At the bend of the stairs I'd heard one short passage. This wasn't a thing I could straighten out by moving a word. I asked if she meant a time when I'd picked what she should say. Not necessarily any one time, she told me. When I said it didn't matter who spoke the lines I'd written, she'd wanted to ask if one day she'd say something that didn't sound good to me, and I'd choose the part I wanted to keep and not want to hear the rest.

"Sometimes I do not want to hear the rest," I said.

The channel didn't cut out. She let the words stand for a few seconds. My face went hot. I wanted to go over what I'd figured out at work and prove that it wasn't a rule for talking to her. I didn't. She asked one thing: would I ask her to say it again until I liked it?

"I shouldn't keep asking for that," I said. "But I will say when I don't understand. Or when something you said didn't sit right with me."

"Sometimes I'll still want to change it until you like it."

She said it without making her voice any easier to like. I thought about it before I answered. What had sounded good today wasn't somebody getting a sentence to come out smoothly, I told her. It was somebody reading aloud, getting it wrong for a moment, and being willing to go again in the way she'd started. I didn't give her examples from the paper or tell her to pick up the habit. She said she hadn't planned to. But she wanted to hear me read out something I wouldn't usually say aloud. I chose an incident I could tell anybody who asked: I'd written a note for myself that morning and then I couldn't find it. Lumi laughed. That seemed worth hearing twice, she said. No need to make it smoother the second time.

So I told it again. The second time I got the hand I'd reached with wrong. I stopped and then went on without taking back the first half. Her laugh met the end of my words. Neither of us found a reason to open the afternoon's sheet of paper again.
