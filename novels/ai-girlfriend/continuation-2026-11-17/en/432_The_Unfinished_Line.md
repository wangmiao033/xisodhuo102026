# Chapter 432: The Unfinished Line

I didn't sleep late on Saturday. I shut my eyes again for a few minutes after waking and listened to somebody cross the floor upstairs. When I got up, the street outside hadn't yet reached the part of the morning when all its noises joined together. Lumi had asked last night if I might sleep in. I hadn't known, and there was no need to open the channel now to correct an answer I hadn't given her.

After breakfast I walked from one room to the other and back twice before pulling open the drawer where I kept ordinary things. The free walking map I'd picked up at the library the Sunday before was still folded inside. I hadn't taken it out since putting it there. I worked it past the edge of the drawer and spread it out on the table. The two fold lines came down on either side of a corner of the little square. That was as far as I'd been. I'd eaten a roll on a bench and come back the way I'd gone. On the map the rest of the way bent this way and that, a little like somebody saying the same thing twice in the same part of town. I hadn't gone through the first time. Looking at the paper from above, I still recognized only the first two turns and the square.

I ran a finger along the names of the streets. At the other end of the square there was supposed to be a narrow pedestrian passage. It was drawn as a small slanting line. From the far end, another two turns along the streets would bring me back to the main road on our side. I wasn't going to time the walk or mark the place where I finished. Light on the paper showed a patch that had gone pale along one crease. I folded it the same way again and chose the coat I normally wore. I was taking the map so I could see what happened if I followed it at the next corner, not so I could hand back every line with something done to it.

The two turns I knew came up sooner than I remembered. At the first, a man was pushing a trolley loaded with empty boxes. He shifted it over a little, and I stopped in a recessed doorway. There seemed to be enough space for cardboard to pass through, looking down on it. The person pushing the trolley had to bring the whole thing round with him every time he turned, and back up half a step to make the boxes fit. I waited. At the next corner I remembered only a green door. Today I saw that two of the paving bricks in front of it were different colors. The last time I was looking past them for a place to sit.

The square was the same size. Two people on the bench were talking with their backs to me. There was a little room beside them, but I didn't try to fit into it. On an ordinary day when the weather was right you could sit down there and nobody asked where you'd walked from. Today I wanted to see the other side of the square. I went around two dogs whose owners were walking them and passed behind the bench. I glanced at the empty stretch beside the two people, not waiting to hear them finish. When I pulled out the map, its edge caught the back of my hand. It was a little stiff, like the flap on a thin carton that hadn't been tucked in. I unfolded it off to one side and looked toward the corner. The line fell between a planted bed and a low wall. I could see two street signs, one on either side of the passage, and knew I had the right place.

Close up, there was a metal barrier right across the opening. Raindrops had left pale marks at the top; half a block of wood had been wedged under one section, with no space beside it for a foot. A notice said the way was closed for paving. Pedestrians were to go around by the outside street. There wasn't a date. I could see neither workers nor where the surface had been dug out. But if I stood by a narrow gap at one side, I could see through the barrier well enough to make out the next street, scarcely a few steps beyond it. Those steps, on the far side of a piece of ground I wasn't going to set foot on, were clear enough to make me wish I could save myself the walk.

“Sorry,” someone said behind me.

I moved in toward the wall. A woman with a mesh bag was holding something folded in her hand. She asked if this was the way through to the street behind the wall. I showed her the notice. She didn't look at my map at first. She opened her own paper, a strip cut down to a delivery address, one line with the number and a telephone number. She wanted the street I could see through the barrier. She asked if it was quicker to go around on the left. I said I'd never walked past this point and didn't know which way was shorter. There was a way around to the right on my map. She looked up at a few corners and chose the opposite direction to the one she'd come from. Before going, she said that if somebody picked up the phone they might come out on the far side to get the delivery. I said they might. We went our separate ways. She didn't hand me anything to take, and I didn't follow her to see what happened.

I turned right and took the larger street running near the little line on the paper, not the line itself. It was louder there than behind the square. A narrow road met it. A car waited to turn into that road, its nose behind the white line. I didn't cross and read at the same time. The car waited for me to go. I stopped on the other side and opened the map again. When I found the far end of the closed passage, I could see the same barrier from outside, with nobody being let through from that side either. The corner let out in four directions. I'd thought I had to get back onto the drawn line for it to count as going this far. In fact there was a way that ran straight, and I could see people in the distance waiting at the light on the main road. I didn't find a second shortcut that closed the whole loop, and I didn't need to go through anything behind the barrier. I put the map away and took the road I could see along.

On the larger road there was one lit shop window. Somebody was lifting the old display pieces out from behind the glass. Another person worked a cloth under a plate. From the street I could see those two things happening separately. The wind had gathered leaves beside the curb, and the wheels of an empty handcart made a noise running over them that nobody would have called music. This wasn't the road I'd expected to come along. I slowed down anyway and spent a whole red light at the crossing. Somebody who'd come up beside me asked if the pedestrian light was broken. It changed before they had to ask again. We crossed at about the same speed, then went different ways. The map stayed in my pocket. It didn't tell me to walk here more often, and it didn't tell me the way home wasn't something I'd found today.

I got back well before lunch. I hung up my coat and took the map out of it. At the table I spread the paper once more and compared the slanting line with the way I'd actually gone. I could say where the barrier was. I couldn't say I'd walked the whole loop on the map. When I folded it up, it still had its old two creases and no new pencil marks. I put it back in the same ordinary drawer, not in a book. The drawer made a short sound when it reached the end of its travel. I didn't open it again for another hearing.

There was enough food at home for lunch. I ate it and cleared up. I hadn't brought anything back from the walk or decided when to go farther. In the afternoon I sat for a while without working out when they might take the notice down. I'd started to think that to keep the place in my head I'd have to walk a few steps farther and prove I knew where I was. What I remembered in the end was the way the woman had opened her address. She held on to both sides so that the big sheet of paper next to her couldn't cover her single line. After I left she could go on and use the little piece of paper to do the thing she had come to do.

Later, on the channel, Lumi first asked if I'd slept through the morning. I told her I'd got up at an ordinary time and gone out with the map I'd picked up at the library the week before. I made fun of myself for not being able to recognize as many as four corners. She started to ask where I'd set out from, but I told her the barrier was across the very passage behind the square. I described the few steps that were so easy to see through a gap and impossible to take. She asked if anybody had slipped around the left edge of the barrier. I said I hadn't seen anyone do that and hadn't tried it myself. You weren't meant to. Then I told her about the other person's address and the way the woman had picked. I didn't know if she got there. Lumi didn't supply an ending that had her arriving at the door.

“So you went to see a road you'd never been down,” Lumi said. “And then you still hadn't been down it.”

I laughed and admitted I hadn't. She asked if the other way counted as a road. I said it did; it was the one where I'd crossed at the lights. In that case I hadn't done too badly, she said. One line wouldn't open, so I'd put in an appearance in the street beside it. I asked if she wasn't in a hurry to direct me now.

“For a minute I did want to ask why you hadn't looked at your phone,” she said after a pause. “Then you started telling me what you could and couldn't see from the road. It didn't sound like you needed to find the way that saved you time.”

“If I'd been trying to get something to somebody, I probably would have wanted to get on with it.”

“But you weren't in a hurry for anybody.”

I said I wasn't. She didn't immediately ask whether I'd go back. All I could hear at the other side of the channel was her voice, still clear after she'd lowered it. She wanted to ask if I felt more like sitting a while when I got back today than I had yesterday. I said not more. I'd wanted to last night, too. Today my legs had felt natural walking and it was only when I sat down that I noticed I was already in my own house. She said all right, softly, as though she meant to leave my sentence where I'd put it.

We didn't spend the rest of the time finishing the walk. She asked me something else about lunch. Halfway through answering I remembered I'd brought water over with me and lifted the glass for a sip. Put away, the map was still just paper. I hadn't left a line on it she could follow to reach me. The next time I wanted to talk to her, I wouldn't have to go back to the start of it.
