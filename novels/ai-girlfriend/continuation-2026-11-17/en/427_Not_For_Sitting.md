# Chapter 427: Not for Sitting

It wasn't eleven yet on Monday when one of the chairs in the break room decided it had sat still long enough.

I went in with an empty cup. The window chair sat crooked, one foot wedged under the table leg. I pulled it free: a faint tick. All four feet were flat, but the back yielded. I let go and pushed again. Same sound.

Across the table a colleague ate cold toast. What was I looking for? I wondered aloud if anyone had sat there earlier. He'd found it like that, assumed somebody had left to take a call without pushing it back.

I crouched. The frame was intact, but where one corner of the back met the seat, there was a gap the other side didn't have. Dust, no clue when it came loose. I reached halfway before remembering I hadn't come to look for a screw. There was no tool kit, just a roll of tape everybody trusted.

"Don't use this one," I told him first.

"I'm using this one." He patted his own chair.

"The next person in won't know."

He put down his toast and shifted the bin. I asked if I could use the space and carried the chair there, out of the way and away from anyone likely to pull it up to the table. From the sheet where we noted when napkins ran out I tore off a strip and wrote, "Not for sitting just now. Loose back." I folded it over the back of the chair and taped both ends down, clear of the gap.

My colleague read it from across the table. Did "not for sitting" mean you'd fall off, or that you'd do well to think it over?

I took the strip down and added, in parentheses, "Please do not use."

He nodded at the revision. Nothing to think over now, he said. I put it back up with the same tape and only then remembered that my cup was still empty.

At nine I'd promised to go over a routine entry with someone else. First I called Facilities from the hall with the location and the loose part. Was it blocking the door? No, I'd labeled it and put it against the wall, not repaired it. Someone would take it away to look before lunch. I passed on asking whether it would be back that afternoon. They hadn't even seen it yet.

When I got back to my desk, the colleague with the records was sliding two sheets apart so I wouldn't try to look at them at the same time. The first was the last checked total. The two additions were on the second page, and only one had a source beside it. I started turning the first sheet over to look. He put a hand on it and explained that it was the date missing beside the later addition, not a discrepancy between the two totals. I started again at the line his fingernail was holding down. Before I copied anything into the worksheet, we both looked once more at the records we had. Fine, he said, taking his two sheets back. Then he pointed out that my cup was practically underneath his sleeve.

By then I did have water in it. I moved it, brushed the rim against the desk, and spilled two drops. I wiped them up with a small piece of paper. A cart went by outside, but I didn't take my cup and go straight after it.

When I went to the break room again before lunch, the worker from Facilities was crouching by the wall, reading the sign we'd left. She tipped the chair a little, pressed near the joint, and set it down flat again. She asked if the sign was mine. I said it was and told her how I'd found the chair caught against the table leg. Moving it out had been the right thing to do, she said. There was no telling from that alone which bit had worked loose on the inside. She'd take it away to check, and the label could stay.

The door of the break room closes on its own. I held it as she pulled a small cart into the hallway and came back to lift the chair with both hands. A wheel caught on the threshold; she backed up a little and went through at a different angle. She said thanks and I said no problem. Somebody in the hall asked her how bad it was. She didn't turn around to offer an opinion. It had to be looked at first, she said. What disappeared from beside the wall was one chair, not a repair that had already been done.

It was nearly twelve, and I could hear forks knocking against each other on the table, when I discovered there was a difference between being one chair short and being one place short. A colleague who didn't usually sit by the window came in with his lunch, shifting his insulated box from one hand to the other. I lifted my chin toward the open bit of table as a sort of direction. He came over. There was indeed a corner of the table where he could set the box. There was nothing to sit on underneath it.

Somebody said he'd be done in five minutes, if the man wanted to wait. My colleague gave a little smile and moved the catch of his dark lid back and forth twice. He said he'd go outside instead and didn't move. The toast eater had gone by then. A few people by the window were talking about a game the previous day, and none of them noticed the lunch box. I could remove myself from the room, but I wasn't in a chair, either. I was standing by the door waiting for water. What he needed wasn't a place I could give up.

The kettle clicked. I switched it off and asked if he was looking for somewhere he could sit and eat. He liked to take his time, he said. If he waited for the person who had offered his seat, that person would have to check that he was settled before he could go. He didn't want somebody to keep him in mind all the way through lunch. He smiled once more without looking toward anyone already eating. I told him there was a public bench and a narrow ledge around the farther corner, near the outside wall. It wasn't part of the meeting area. I'd passed it that morning and nobody had left any material on the ledge; I didn't know if the bench was free now. He repeated the location. He'd always thought that was just how you got to the back door.

I hadn't poured my water yet. I walked with him to the corner. The ledge by the bench was cool to the touch and wide enough for his box. Somebody already sitting there got up to retrieve a bag from the far end and didn't ask any questions. My colleague touched the bench, took a seat, and opened both catches. He asked if I was going to sit. My lunch was still back by the table, I told him. I'd find somewhere in a minute. He moved over a little and said there was no hurry. Now that he knew there was a place to sit here, he could look for himself next time.

I went back to pour the water. I didn't wait for the chair, or bring the bench with me. The space where the chair had stuck out gave you another half step when you came through the door. I stood inside with my hands around the cup and watched people squeeze out past the window. After a moment I realized I could go and eat just as I'd meant to. My lid was a bit stubborn. I caught the edge on the first try and got it off on the second. When I finished, I washed my own box, carried my cup back, and got on with work.

Nobody told me what they'd found wrong with the chair that afternoon. On my way out I passed the break room. The bin was back in its usual place against the wall and there was still a gap by the little table. The chair hadn't come back. I remembered sending the label with it. There was no reason to go in and see whether I'd left part of a strip of tape behind.

After dinner I opened the channel and told Lumi the chair by the break-room window had come loose. Was it wooden? A wooden frame, I said. When I got to the fine gap at the joint, she broke in.

"So you found exactly the right screw?"

Her voice lit up. She was listening for the kind of story that goes from something wrong to something right. I almost gave her a decent ending then and there.

"No. I put a sign on it. Facilities took it away."

"You got your hands on a chair, and now there's one less chair."

I laughed half a laugh. It was a clever line, but it also put me right back on my haunches before lunch, as if noticing which part made a noise meant I owed everybody a fixed chair. I said she'd really wanted to hear me produce a screwdriver. She admitted it. But if I'd already told her I hadn't, what was she supposed to do, let out a sigh for me? I said she could. She gave me a spectacularly long one. I couldn't help finishing the laugh.

Then she asked if I'd wanted to fix it. I remembered my finger almost finding its way into the gap, and then how the frame itself hadn't been broken. For a moment I'd wanted to reach for whatever was handy, I said. But tightening something might only have made it tick a little more quietly. The worker hadn't even guessed where the trouble was. I was the one who'd wanted an answer before long.

"You still haven't got one."

"Not the one I wanted."

"What have you got, then?"

I told her about the wall away from the door and the space the chair had left. Then I told her about lunch, when he'd said he'd go out and hadn't moved. She let me finish before asking where he'd gone. I described the narrow ledge and the bench around the corner. I'd only gone as far as that, seen him sit, and left. I hadn't waited for him to finish eating. She went quiet for a moment. She hadn't thought as far as a lunch box, she said. She'd just wanted to know what happened to the chair. If it wasn't going to be fixed in the story, she'd assumed we'd have to throw it out of the story.

I asked, a little nastily, whether she could throw me out of my own story for a while too. She heard the edge in it. There was a wait before she answered.

"If you want to stop telling it, you don't have to pretend you've gone missing."

I looked at the empty coaster. I didn't go looking for a line that would explain why she shouldn't have guessed the way she had. She didn't decide for me that I must be tired. I sat a little straighter and told her there was one thing I did want to go on and say. After he was settled on the bench, he'd asked if I wanted to sit. It wasn't some great warm invitation. There was probably steam rising out of his lunch by then, and he still left a little room on the bench. I'd been uncertain whether I had time to wait. He hadn't asked me to wait at all.

"What did you tell him?"

I told her exactly what I'd said. So it was a place he'd made, she said, not a place I'd made for him. Then she asked if I could stay where I was a little longer now. This time, she said, she actually was waiting for an answer.

I asked if I had to answer right now. Lumi said yes, and I could hear her smiling already. So I said I could. I also had something I could tell her that she probably wouldn't guess. I explained the difference between "Not for sitting just now" and "Please do not use." She made a point of saying both slowly, but when she got to the part in parentheses she didn't draw it out. Each word came through clearly. I looked down and pictured the paper folded over the chair back. It was only then that I thought no one had sat in that chair at lunch. Monday was already a better day than it had been when I heard the tick.
