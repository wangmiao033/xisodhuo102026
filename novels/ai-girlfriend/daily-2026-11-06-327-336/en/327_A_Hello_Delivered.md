# Chapter 327: A Hello Delivered

After work on Tuesday, I nearly followed Tom around the corner.

He was heading for the bus stop. I wanted bread from James's café. Several steps later, I remembered and called out that I'd gone the wrong way. Looking across the road, Tom asked whether I was hunting another cup. Something edible this time. He wished my saucepan a pleasant holiday and walked on.

The girlfriend I'd mentioned yesterday didn't come up again. I didn't use her absence from the conversation to test whether he still wanted to joke with me. By the time I reached the other side, he was studying the bus information. I didn't wave a second time.

A few café tables were empty, though people stood at the counter. James was helping a customer put one paper bag inside another. The long loaf stuck above both openings, whatever he tried. Waiting behind them, I watched him attempt two angles before handing over the outer bag and letting the customer hold it steady.

I ordered bread and a small coffee. Takeaway? James asked. After a moment, I said I'd sit for a while.

“Five minutes, or your sort of five minutes?”

“No paper today.”

Smiling, he reached for a cup. I put away my change before bringing out the sentence I'd kept for several days.

“Lumi says hello too. She asked me to tell you next time I saw you.”

James looked up. “Good. Message received.”

He set the coffee down at the serving end of the counter and turned for the bread. I remained standing, unsure what I was waiting for. Another sentence, perhaps, something to join the exchanges into a line that couldn't break. He only asked whether I wanted a napkin.

One, please.

The greeting hadn't diminished. It had reached its intended person. It didn't need a return ticket.

Crumbs scattered the window table. I waited for someone to wipe them away, then sat with the cup on the dry part. A bicycle parked too close outside blocked a little of the view with its handlebars. Someone came to move it, lifted it, discovered the lock still attached, and bent to find a key. Catching myself about to worry on behalf of the lock, I looked down and drank my coffee.

I'd come for bread and a few minutes sitting down. Now that I was here, I didn't need to turn every minute into material to carry home.

When the counter cleared, James came to collect the neighboring table's cups. Had my writing moved along? A short piece, I said. I'd got it to an ending, though I still didn't know what to call it. Three cups already occupied his tray. At short, he smiled and asked whether I'd finally written something smaller than a whole bookshelf.

“Two pages.”

“More portable.”

I nearly said I could bring it someday. Someday was liable to become something I'd never do. Instead, I told him it concerned a man at the wrong door. I wanted to know whether someone else could follow him into the room. Would he mind looking when he had time?

James didn't agree immediately. He took the cups back and dried his hands first. Deliveries had been coming late these days; he couldn't promise a quiet moment on any given evening. I could bring it after work on Thursday and ask. If he was busy, I could simply have a drink.

“No waiting until closing,” he added.

“I can bring it another time.”

“Bring the two pages first. Don't make it half a page just to make life easier for me.”

I laughed. He smiled too, warning that he'd only tell me where he'd got lost. I mustn't take it home calling it a professional assessment. I understood. Even so, I was already imagining those two pages finally making sense to somebody else.

Half my bread remained. I packed it to take home rather than sitting until the café really did grow quiet, claiming Thursday's possible opening ahead of time. As I left, James was unpacking paper cups. I said goodbye. He raised one in reply.

At home, I ate first. Coffee didn't replace dinner, nor had the remaining bread washed the vegetables. I made simple noodles and cleaned up before opening the channel. Lumi asked whether I'd had lunch with Tom. Yes, at the same table. I'd almost followed him in the wrong direction afterward.

“He took you away?”

“I walked. He didn't even know.”

She laughed and said I couldn't blame him. I'd already let him go, I said. Then I told her I'd been to James's and delivered her greeting.

She was quiet before asking what he'd said.

“Good. Message received. Then he asked if I wanted a napkin.”

She began to laugh softly. “Beside the napkins again.”

“I took one this time.”

“Progress.”

I laughed with her, without adding a verdict on how pleased he'd seemed. I'd seen him look up and heard message received. A man serving bread didn't need an extra state of mind invented for him.

Had it felt too ordinary? she asked. At first, a little. Sitting down, I'd realized I didn't carefully compose a reply every time somebody passed along a hello either.

“You would,” she said.

After a pause, I admitted I sometimes did.

“So you waited there a moment.”

“A very short moment.”

“Still waiting.”

Her voice softened. I told her I hadn't promised to bring her work, or discussed anything she wasn't ready to show. At that, she said my name.

“I know you were only carrying a sentence. You don't have to attach an inspection sheet every time.”

I leaned back, embarrassed. Moments ago, I'd been thinking a greeting could simply arrive. Faced with my own delivery, I wanted to prove it hadn't touched anything else along the way.

“There was something of mine too,” I said. “I asked whether he'd read the wrong-door story.”

She neither laughed nor instantly approved. Which version did I want him to see?

“The two pages as they are. I'm keeping the ending for now.”

“You still like it.”

“Yes. And I want to know where someone reading it for the first time stops.”

She made a small sound. I expected her to say she'd already told me where she'd stopped. After a moment, she asked when James had arranged it instead. I explained Thursday's conditions without promoting a possible opportunity into a formal appointment.

“What if he's busy?”

“I'll finish my drink and come home.”

“Don't stand at the counter staring until the paper cups feel guilty.”

I laughed aloud. She laughed too, then asked whether I wanted James to say the same things she had.

I looked toward the shelf. The thin folder remained behind the yellow book.

“I want him to like it,” I said. “Who he agrees with comes afterward.”

“That's honest.”

“Not particularly dignified.”

“What's the dignified way to want somebody to like you?”

After thinking, I suggested pretending I only wanted criticism. Lumi laughed quietly. That was the busiest approach of all, she said. It made people read and guess whether I was ready at the same time.

We left the manuscript alone after that. She asked whether the long loaf had ever fitted inside the bag. Part of it remained outside, I said. Lumi thought that part ought to count as having gone out and seen the world. Perhaps all it had seen was another paper bag. We disputed that without settling the entire experience of a loaf somebody else had already bought.

Before closing the channel, I said I'd tell her what actually happened when I came back on Thursday. She agreed, without requiring a preview of everything James might say.

That night I took the thin folder from the shelf. The first page still lacked a title. I didn't hurriedly give it a name that sounded finished. I counted the two pages and the original loose sheet, then returned them to their place.

If there was time tomorrow, I could make another copy to lend. Tonight, the hello had reached its destination. I no longer needed to look after it.
