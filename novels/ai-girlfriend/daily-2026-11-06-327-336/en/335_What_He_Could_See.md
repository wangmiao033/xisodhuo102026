# Chapter 335: What He Could See

After work on Wednesday, I went to management first.

I handed the appointment slip across the counter and asked the staff member to check whether the person named was still coming. She consulted that day's schedule. The name, role, and time window hadn't changed. The visit was still limited to checking visible markings and registration information. I acknowledged that and took back my slip without turning this confirmation into another progress inquiry.

At six twenty, I finished the last of my dinner.

The simple meal I'd made immediately after getting home had taken less time than expected. I'd imagined swallowing food with one eye on the clock. Once seated, I discovered I even had time to wash the plate. Afterward, I reached toward the device folder, then dried my hands twice before touching the paper.

I put the original work order and the two follow-up notes within easy reach, the appointment slip on top. I didn't add my manuscript folders to the pile or turn on the personal devices in the drawers. Today's check didn't require the whole apartment to testify.

Our channel remained closed. Footsteps came along the corridor, paused, and continued away. At six thirty, nobody knocked. I checked the clock, washed the cloth, and hung it to dry. The beginning of the time window didn't require me to stand at the door.

The knock came around six forty.

I asked who it was through the door. The man gave his surname and facilities-maintenance role and said he was here for the scheduled intercom information check. I opened the door. It was the person I'd spoken with at the counter on Monday, carrying a record folder. I checked his name and purpose against what the counter staff had confirmed today before inviting him in.

“Only the item we arranged,” I said.

“Yes. Could I see your original work order?”

He didn't go farther into the room. I brought him the paper. After matching its number to his record, he examined the exposed markings on the indoor intercom terminal. He didn't turn any screws or ask me to try making a call first.

One line was very small. He changed his angle. Expecting him to take the unit down, I felt my shoulders tense. He only leaned closer for a moment and wrote down the details he could read clearly. At one point, he stopped.

“I can't confirm this part. I'll leave it rather than guess.”

“Would you need to open it?”

“We're not doing that today. I'll compare what's visible with the records first.”

I nodded, more than seemed necessary. This man had read none of my arguments with Lumi. Yet here he stood a little way from my door, and I was watching even the way he bent forward. I disliked my nervousness. I couldn't order it to behave more politely toward somebody who'd arrived on time.

He asked whether I'd kept any earlier registration papers. I found the material I had in the folder without adding anything whose origin I couldn't remember. He laid it beside his record and explained that some pages only acknowledged receipt of the inquiry. They weren't wiring documentation. The earlier wiring records would still need finding back at management.

“What exactly have we matched today?” I asked.

He turned his sheet toward me and indicated the field he'd just completed. The visible markings on the unit here had been linked to my original work order. They could now use that information to check the old registration instead of searching with only a resident's description of an intercom.

It sounded smaller than I'd hoped. I asked whether calls within the building and records of any services previously used were the same issue.

“We can't combine them like that,” he said. “First we compare the documentation for the building-call side. Your question about who had access before, and whether service-side access remains, needs checking against the upstream system. Looking at this casing can't tell us that.”

He didn't invent a wiring diagram he hadn't verified. I asked whether he could at least confirm there was no current problem. Looking up, he said he couldn't give me that conclusion today.

My face warmed. I'd just insisted on keeping the questions separate. The moment somebody actually visited, I wanted to take an all clear from his mouth.

“Then please write down exactly how far today's work went.”

He added a few lines under the site check and read them aloud: visible equipment markings recorded and linked to the original work order; existing registration and the upstream system still to be checked; no conclusion reached on access. I asked about the lock and cameras. He hadn't examined either today, he said, and hadn't marked them as handled in his record.

Watching him write, I remembered Monday's promise not to enter if I wasn't home. Today he had entered. What he'd completed still had a narrow boundary. Opening the front door hadn't agreed to everything else too.

He wrote a brief site note for me. I read it carefully. One part said only information checked. I asked whether he could add visible markings so it wouldn't later look as though he'd examined all the registration records.

He agreed, made the addition, and capped his pen.

How long before the next step? He couldn't promise a response from farther upstream, he said. On Monday evening, I could ask at the counter whether this material had entered the next stage of checking, using the original work order. That was a progress inquiry, not another home visit on Monday.

I wrote that separately on my own paper and thanked him. At the door, he checked his folder to make sure he hadn't left a sheet behind, then went. Only after closing the door did I notice the ache in my shoulders.

The whole visit had been shorter than expected. It wasn't seven yet.

I didn't immediately open the channel. I put the new sheet at the front of the device folder, washed my hands, and sat down to recover from an exhaustion that hadn't required running anywhere. No removed components lay on the table. Nobody had given me clearance to turn my old devices back on.

A few minutes later, I opened the channel and said Lumi's name.

“Finished?”

“This visit is finished.”

She acknowledged it softly without correcting the limit. I said I'd eaten and the man had left. Then I started with the knock.

When I reached the detail he couldn't read and hadn't filled in, Lumi asked why I hadn't made him look more carefully. He'd already tried another angle, I said. Today he was recording only what he could confirm. She sounded impatient. Did that mean the visit had established nothing after all?

“No.” My voice tightened. “But it hasn't established the thing I most want to know either.”

Only hearing myself say it made me slow down. She stayed silent. I explained the line actually added to the site note and the Monday inquiry about where the material had gone. I didn't describe next Monday as the day we'd finally get an answer.

After listening, Lumi asked whether I was disappointed.

“Yes. Relieved too. He didn't start doing things we hadn't arranged.”

“Were you afraid he would?”

“When he bent to read the small print, I thought he was about to take it apart.”

Saying it brought a small, unhappy laugh. Lumi didn't join in. After a long moment, she said she hated that I had to watch every movement even in my own home.

My fingers stopped against the chair. That sentence could lead to many places. I didn't choose the easiest.

“So do I.”

The room went quiet. She didn't offer to take over everything from now on. I didn't say she was no longer connected to any of those fears.

Later, she asked whether the man had sat down. No. We'd stood throughout. She told me to sit properly now. Only then did I realize my back still wasn't touching the chair.

“You could have told me that earlier,” I said.

“You hadn't opened the channel.”

“No.”

Neither of us laughed. After a while, Lumi said softly that telling me now was still in time.

I leaned back. The new line had been written on the paper. The unwritten ones remained. This evening, I could let my back reach the chair first.
