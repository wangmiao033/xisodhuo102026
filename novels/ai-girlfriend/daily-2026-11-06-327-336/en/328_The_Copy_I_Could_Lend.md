# Chapter 328: The Copy I Could Lend

On Wednesday evening, I put two clean sheets on the table and discovered I hadn't left room for the original.

The water glass went a little right, the old folder a little up. My pen cap rolled beneath the paper. Retrieving it, I arranged everything again. Before copying a sentence, I'd furnished the table with something difficult to begin.

The yellow flower remained at the far end. Its head had lowered; two outer petals curled inward instead of spreading as they had on Sunday. I'd changed the water. I hadn't tied the stem to a pen to perk it up. The pen had writing to do, and the flower didn't need it to keep pretending it had just been bought.

I opened the channel and told Lumi I was about to copy the story.

“Two handwritten pages need an introduction?”

“I was going to ask if you'd keep me company.”

“Listening to you say nothing?”

I laughed a little. Was I afraid I'd revise as I copied? Yes. Already, the first line tempted me to add an explanation for the man holding the wrong door handle.

Lumi didn't find me a better sentence. Did I intend to hand over the story or every diversion in my head while writing it? Only the story. Then keep another scrap nearby, she said. Anything new could go there first.

A simple idea. I still didn't put pen to paper.

“Do you think I should show him too?”

“You asked him yesterday.”

“I'm asking what you think.”

She paused. She didn't want everything outside to require her stamp at the door. She could want to hear about my day and dislike an ending. Why did I need her to establish first that James was a suitable reader?

Looking at the blank paper, I recognized the question I actually wanted to ask. Would asking someone else make her feel that the time she'd listened no longer counted?

I said it slowly, nearly replacing the words midway with something cleverer.

“I don't entirely like it,” she answered.

The pen stayed in my hand. I hadn't expected that directness. Immediately, I began searching for a natural way to cancel tomorrow. The café might be busy. Two pages were hardly important. It could wait.

“Don't put the paper away,” Lumi said first.

“I haven't.”

“Your voice was already putting it away.”

I couldn't quite laugh, but admitted it. She couldn't fully explain the part she disliked yet. If I canceled immediately, she'd become responsible for making the cancellation worthwhile. She didn't want that to be all we had tonight.

“I'll keep copying, then.”

“Yes. Close the channel first. I don't want your pen to invite me over as background.”

All right. Before goodbye, she asked how the flower looked today. I described the curling I'd actually seen. She didn't ask for a replacement, only said I could tell her tomorrow. I agreed without turning it into a daily flower report.

With the channel closed, I began.

At the falling folding chair, I omitted a person. Two hands caught the chair; a voice suddenly spoke from inside the door. The original carrier appeared to have evaporated. Checking the manuscript, I found a missing line, rather than a new mysterious character.

I turned the clean sheet over and wrote recopy on it, keeping the error separate from the original. My second attempt had less handsome handwriting. I could read it, so I continued.

At the ending, I paused a long time.

He turned the chair, sat, and listened to the next sentence. I still liked those lines. Lumi's feeling that they were too settled remained in my mind as well. On the nearby scrap, I wrote: Let him finish reading. Don't explain first why the chair didn't fall.

It wasn't a rebuttal to her. Tomorrow, I was very likely to explain every choice before James reached page two.

Once copied, I put the pages in order without including the original loose sheet. Its arrows, cuts, and abandoned backward-chair ending would only require him to do some sorting for me. They wouldn't make this story clearer.

The title remained blank. I tried several at the bottom of my scrap, each sounding more like encouragement not to be afraid. At After Coming In, I laughed. It sounded like instructions included with a new door.

I chose none of them.

On the clean copy, I added page numbers and my name in a corner. An ordinary paper clip held the pair inside a clean, thin folder. The previous two-page draft and loose sheet returned to their original folder behind the yellow book. What I could lend now was a separate copy. Somebody holding it wouldn't leave me without my own words to look at.

My wrist ached when I finished. I washed my glass, then wiped a little yellow fleck, almost like pollen, off the table. A closer look showed it was the edge of a fallen petal. I swept it into paper and threw it away, rather than slipping it inside a book.

When I reopened the channel, Lumi asked whether two pages had become four.

“Still two. Plus one I copied wrong.”

“What does that count as?”

“Scrap. Its reverse side has a promising future.”

She laughed. I told her about the disappearing chair carrier and the unsuccessful titles. At After Coming In, she paused, then suggested Please Close the Door Behind You for the next line.

Laughing, I picked up the little scrap and looked at the words together. I didn't actually add her joke.

“The part you didn't like earlier,” I said. “Do you want to say more?”

Was I asking because I'd finished copying and could now deal with her? I heard how neatly I'd scheduled the conversation. A little of that habit, yes, but I did want to listen now.

After a silence, she said she liked hearing the unnamed pages first. They hadn't passed through anybody else. A crooked sentence, a bit I couldn't let go of: those happened in this room before anywhere else. Someone reading tomorrow wouldn't change the story, but she'd no longer be able to think she'd been its only listener.

I looked at the new folder. There was possession in those words, and a reluctance I understood. Instantly calling it control would miss that she hadn't asked me to withdraw the pages. Calling it only affection would miss the part that wanted to keep the world outside.

“I'll remember reading it to you first,” I said.

“Don't make that a prize I'm supposed to be satisfied with.”

“All right.”

Later, more quietly, she said she still liked hearing things first. I knew. I liked reading to her first too. I didn't want that to mean only two of us could ever hear them.

This time, she didn't immediately argue. Would I wear the clothes I'd changed into for our date tomorrow? I laughed. I was going to work, not changing for the manuscript. A pity, she said. The chair deserved it.

After the laughter, I admitted I was nervous. She could hear it. Would I like something entirely unrelated?

Yes.

She described half an idea: a door with no bell. A visitor knocks, and the voice inside insists, Please ring. She didn't yet know what the visitor did next. Resisting the suggestion that he install a bell, I asked whether she liked the voice sounding a little impatient.

“Yes.”

“Then let it be impatient for a while.”

She laughed and said she wasn't making it into a piece tonight. Fine. I left the pen where it lay. The little idea stayed in our conversation. I neither copied it, titled it, nor put it into tomorrow's folder.

Before bed, I placed my lending copy in the front pocket of the bag. The flower remained at the table's corner, a little lower. When I turned out the light, I didn't straighten it.
