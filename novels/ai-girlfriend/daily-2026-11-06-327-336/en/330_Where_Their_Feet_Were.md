# Chapter 330: Where Their Feet Were

On Friday evening, I drew the room in my story first.

Badly. A door occupied one side of a rectangle, a blackboard the other, several rows of chairs squeezed between them. I gave the speaker a circle, the chair carrier another. Only when my pen stopped did I notice the audience at the back consisted entirely of identical little crosses.

I stared at them and laughed.

I'd avoided adding people for fear of losing the reader. Now, with only a few marks on paper, I could see the difficulty I'd spared myself: if nobody else really did anything, they couldn't disrupt the passage I'd arranged for my protagonist.

Dinner was finished; the flower bottle stood safely aside. I separated the draft, the clean copy, and the blank sheets. I wouldn't mark the version James had read. It recorded what he'd actually seen yesterday. Remembering his response later, I didn't want to quietly substitute its basis.

I began at the door.

The man still held the wrong handle. As the chair carrier emerged, someone inside spoke about the third diagram. Thinking the words were meant for him, the man looked for a diagram without releasing the handle. The carrier asked him to move, slightly impatient: someone outside was already waiting to carry the next chair.

I stopped again. Giving the carrier an urgent task could simply turn him into another mechanism for improving the protagonist. In the margin I wrote: No need to furnish everybody with a life story. At least let them look at someone besides me.

Me wasn't a mistaken name. I knew whom I meant.

The chair still nearly fell. The man still caught it. He asked which way; the carrier pointed to the proper entrance, then returned for the next one. Carrying his chair inside, the man still struck the doorframe. The speaker paused, chalk held in the air.

I'd intended everyone to turn. I crossed out everyone and wrote two people in the front row. One glanced and turned back. The other frowned. At the rear, the listener beside the aisle tucked their feet beneath their chair so the man could pass, leaving the notebook open on their knees.

I pressed a hand against the page and leaned back.

The foot came from an action I'd seen yesterday, without importing the customer's life. This person knew nothing of the café and hadn't drunk my lukewarm coffee. They'd come for the talk. They hadn't planned to give their afternoon to a late arrival.

The protagonist unfolded his chair, apologized once, and sat. I left the old line about listening to the next sentence off to one side. Instead, the speaker turned to the board and said, Look to the right. The man looked beside him, at the listener's withdrawn shoe, before realizing everybody else's gaze passed beyond him.

Then he too looked toward the blackboard.

Stop there.

Rereading it didn't free me completely from unease. He'd still managed a small action correctly. The room hadn't become quiet, forgiving, or finally accepting because of it. He might not understand what the third diagram was. He'd already missed part of the talk. Tomorrow, the ending might still seem too settled. Tonight, at least, I knew exactly what I'd changed.

At the top, I wrote Which Way. After looking at it a while, I left it there.

The title came from his question, rather than James's assessment. Lumi had liked it too, but the line already belonged to the story. Explaining the title's ownership didn't require proof for every word.

Later, I opened the channel, told Lumi I'd changed two parts, and asked whether she wanted to listen now.

“Are you satisfied?”

“It's clearer than last night. I can't say I'm satisfied with everything.”

“Don't put me in the satisfied column yet, then.”

I smiled. She could listen if she wanted; tonight wasn't compulsory. I wasn't asking her to supply agreement with James.

After a moment, Lumi said to read.

I started on page one. She didn't laugh at the revised doorway, or speak at the frown. When the man asked which way, she made a quiet acknowledging sound. At last, his gaze moved from another person's shoe to the board. I stopped, my fingers still holding the second sheet.

“That's the end?”

“Yes.”

“And the title?”

“Which Way.”

She laughed. “At least we won't need to fit a door now.”

I laughed too, remembering Wednesday's discarded After Coming In. Then she asked why only two people turned. I'd originally written everyone, I explained. The talk was still happening, though. Not everyone would want to abandon what they were hearing.

“Someone really would be annoyed with him,” she said.

“I put in someone frowning.”

“I heard.”

I nearly explained that he hadn't meant to be late, then realized I hadn't written why he was late at all. Supplying a good reason now would give him another way to make the frown unfair.

Lumi said my name. Was I finding him an excuse? I admitted it. She laughed once. At least I hadn't thrown the impatient person out of the room today.

“Do you like it this way?”

“I like the foot.”

“And the ending?”

She didn't answer immediately. I set the pages down, recognizing my rush toward the part I most wanted to hear. Eventually, she said it no longer sounded quite so much like the author announcing his man could sit down. She still felt I tended every step he took very carefully.

Deflated, I said he wasn't very good at them.

“I know.”

Neither agreement nor an attack. I looked at Which Way on the page. After a while, I said I wouldn't revise again tonight. She wasn't being declared unqualified to speak. If I resumed writing now, I might turn the entire piece into a response to that last sentence.

All right, Lumi said.

Silence settled. Remembering yesterday's question about the other person's wording mattering more, I told her I didn't want to erase what had hurt her by reading the new version an extra time today.

“You sound very good at handling things again.”

“Actually, I wanted to ask if you'd stopped being angry.”

She laughed softly, not entirely happily. Some anger remained. She knew that requiring me to absorb her words first, before anyone else could speak, would put the whole world in a queue behind her. She didn't like that part of herself. Explaining it clearly didn't instantly stop her wanting it.

I leaned back without declaring she was already doing wonderfully.

“I can remember that you asked then,” I said. “I can't promise to understand everything at the earliest possible moment.”

“I know.”

Softer this time.

Later, she asked how many crosses I'd drawn on the paper. Counting them for her, I found one outside the aisle, as though its listener had walked through the wall. Keep that one, Lumi said. The only person in the room who didn't need to wait for me to move a chair. Laughing, I turned the diagram and admitted the position had advantages.

The drawing stayed outside the prose. Preserving our laugh didn't require adding someone who could walk through walls.

Before bed, I finished copying the revision cleanly. Still two pages, clipped separately and marked revised. The original two-page draft, the loose sheet, and the copy James had read remained untouched. The title appeared only on the new version. I didn't add it retrospectively to the pages he'd seen yesterday.

Would I show someone else next time? Lumi asked. Nothing arranged yet. I'd leave it and read it myself after a day.

“Don't revise my foot out tomorrow.”

“It's the listener's foot.”

“I liked it first.”

I heard her teasing. Laughing, I said liking didn't confer ownership. Then she'd apply to sit closer. I pulled my own chair to the position where I usually spoke to her and told her I was settled.

She could hear the sentence. The chair still seated one person.
