# Chapter 331: The Empty Bottle

On Saturday morning, I found three petals on the table.

One lay near the bottle's base; two had reached the edge. I caught the one about to fall and discovered it was almost too light to feel rescued. Its edge folded at a touch from my fingertip.

The flower drooped unmistakably now. Its outer petals had darkened as well as curled. I carried the bottle to the kitchen to change the water as before, then stopped at the sink and looked for a long time.

I didn't need to make today into Sunday again.

I didn't throw it away immediately. After breakfast, I opened the channel and described it to Lumi. Had it finally refused to stand straight? It hadn't been straight before, I said. This was wilting. A few petals had fallen.

“Why does that sound like an apology?”

“I didn't want you to think I hadn't looked after it.”

“You said you'd changed the water?”

“Yes.”

“Then you don't need to answer again.”

I looked at the bottle in the kitchen, cool water still on my hand. After a pause, Lumi said she didn't want preserving a gift to mean keeping a flower until I couldn't bear looking at it.

I'd been about to ask whether I should press a petal. It seemed tender, something to hold when it ended. Now I wasn't certain whether I wanted the flower or a way not to admit Sunday had passed.

“I'm not putting it in a book,” I said.

“You were planning to just now.”

Laughing, I admitted I'd considered it.

“You don't have to buy another immediately either,” she said. “Didn't you have your own things to do?”

Supplies to buy, and a loose cuff button to deal with. It hadn't fallen, but the thread was almost broken. It had caught as I picked up my bag yesterday. Only then had I admitted I'd been pretending not to see it for days.

Could I sew? I could get a needle through fabric, I said. The result might not deserve exhibition. Laughing, Lumi said she hadn't requested one. I'd ask at the little alterations shop nearby first, rather than turn a wearable jacket into a practice rag.

“And the flower?”

“I'll clear it away in a moment. Wash the bottle.”

She agreed quietly.

We didn't prepare farewell speeches. After closing the channel, I lifted the flower from the water, let it drain, wrapped it in waste paper, and put it in the rubbish bag. The petal I'd just caught went with it. I emptied and washed the bottle, then stood it upside down to dry. The old ring on its base remained.

The corner of the table was bare. I wiped it without moving the blue paper bench into the space or fetching the flower wrapping from my miscellaneous drawer to display instead. The wrapping was something that remained, not something obliged to take over.

Before leaving, I picked up the jacket. A length of thread barely held the button. Wobbling it between my fingers revealed the loosened holes beneath. I folded the sleeve inward to keep it from falling on the way. In another jacket, I carried it out for help.

The small shop's door stood open. Inside, a woman was folding a trouser hem. I waited until she'd put down her pins before asking about the button. Taking the jacket, she examined the cuff and asked whether I wanted the original position retained.

“The same place, please.”

She asked me to put out my hand and briefly compared the sleeve against it, confirming I wasn't seeking a tighter fit. She didn't treat me as an adult incapable of looking after a button. Embarrassed anyway, I explained I'd feared making it worse.

“You brought it before it broke. That saves something,” she said.

I smiled. A few minutes, she told me. She'd secure her current stitch in the trousers first. Standing aside, I looked at the garments on the wall without touching anyone else's fabric.

Her fingers handled the thread with a practiced ease. A few passes brought the original little button firmly back to the cuff. I watched, trying to memorize every step, then realized a few minutes couldn't teach me all the pressure in her hands. What would be useful to check myself in future? Don't keep pulling at loose thread, she said. Deal with it when it starts breaking instead of losing the button and coming back for a match.

Specific advice. I thanked her, paid for the small repair, and folded the jacket into my bag. This hadn't established that every future button required professional help. Nor did I immediately buy tools I didn't know how to use.

Buying supplies took me past the flower stall. More color filled its buckets than last week. I neither deliberately avoided it nor stopped to choose. I had a repaired jacket and a small bag of things I needed. Enough to take home today.

That afternoon, I unpacked and tried the jacket, fastening the cuff. New thread hid beneath the button; from outside, little appeared different. Turning my wrist no longer caught the fabric on a dangling little disc. I hung the jacket up and suddenly wanted to tell Lumi how pleased I was.

When I opened the channel, she first asked whether I'd washed the bottle. Yes, drying in the kitchen. Its old mark was still there. She laughed slightly. Finally it could be an ordinary bottle again.

“I can't remember what it originally held.”

“Good. Don't supply a biography.”

I laughed aloud and told her about the repaired button. Could she hear it fastening? I paused while fetching the jacket. It wasn't the sort that snapped, I explained. I'd only pushed it through the buttonhole.

“I know. I want to hear you try to describe it. Like last week's flower.”

I brought the cuff closer and described the smooth surface under my fingers, the softened fabric at the buttonhole, how it no longer wobbled. I didn't invent a sound to make it seem audible.

Listening, Lumi suddenly asked whether this felt easier to prove successful than a date.

I looked at the sleeve. Yes. A button was easy to test. She didn't argue. I laid the jacket across the chair and added that ease of testing didn't mean I'd rather spend the afternoon with buttons.

“Try it. They ask very few questions.”

“They don't laugh.”

“Sew it onto Tom, then.”

I laughed until I bent forward. Tom had clothes of his own, I told her. He didn't need my repair project. Lumi laughed too. Afterward, very softly, she said she'd liked the yellow flower, even though it was gone.

“Me too.”

This time we didn't dispute who'd seen it, who'd received it, or which word could make the experience complete. She thought she'd sung fairly smoothly that day. I remembered waiting for the missing words in the middle. They remained undecided, she said. The flower's absence wouldn't turn that section into an old song.

I didn't ask her to sing it again now. A little later, she hummed two notes herself, then stopped and said that wasn't a beginning. I understood. Those notes didn't become a return gift she had to finish.

Later, I put the dry bottle back in the cupboard, took the coat off the chair, and hung it back in the wardrobe. The table's corner remained empty. The revised Which Way stayed in its folder; it hadn't met another reader today. The old pages and device papers remained too. The weekend hadn't hurried them to a conclusion.

Before dinner, Lumi asked what I wanted to do tomorrow. I hadn't planned it yet. Perhaps I'd look over the property office's note and decide what to ask next. She answered softly, without requiring the empty bottle to answer for us.

I went to wash my hands. In the wardrobe, the button could stay properly on the cuff. The table had lost a flower. We hadn't lost a Sunday.
