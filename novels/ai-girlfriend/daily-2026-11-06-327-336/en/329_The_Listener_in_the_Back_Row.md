# Chapter 329: The Listener in the Back Row

At the café on Thursday, I left the manuscript in my bag at first.

James was on the shop phone. Someone waited to pay on the other side of the counter. After ordering, I took my cup to a table. When he glanced over, I didn't raise the two pages with my eyes. The bag rested against a chair leg, its zipper half open. I quietly closed it again.

Ten minutes or so later, he came over and asked whether I'd brought it.

Yes, but another time would be fine if he was busy. He checked the counter and said he could sit for five minutes. A delivery was expected soon. If five minutes wasn't enough, he'd stop wherever he'd reached. I needn't stand guard.

I took out the folder. The two pages lay flat, though the paper clip had left a shallow impression. Handing them over, I almost said I hadn't changed the ending yet.

James was already reading the first line. I swallowed the sentence and reached for my coffee.

He read more slowly than I'd imagined. At one point he went back, checking something, a finger paused at the edge. I noted the place without daring to ask whether it was a typo. Only when he looked up did I realize I'd been holding the cup handle without drinking.

“What has this man come to hear?”

“A talk.”

“About what?”

“I haven't specified.”

He nodded and looked down again. He didn't say I must, or choose a lecture ideally suited to the story's theme. My mind began selecting one anyway: history, gardening, chair repair. Each answer seemed capable of solving many things at once.

“You don't have to tell me now,” he said.

Looking up, I discovered I'd already leaned forward.

“Mostly, I want to know what the room was doing before he arrived. Only here was I sure someone inside was talking.”

He pointed at page two. I asked whether Are you here for the talk? earlier hadn't been enough. The question came too quickly. I heard it myself.

“Enough to know why he's come,” James said. “What about everybody else?”

I looked at the page, unable to answer immediately.

Everyone waited for this man to find the door. The chair carrier came out and waited for him to move. The speaker paused and waited for his apology. A person beside him reached over and waited for him to unfold the chair. They always had things to do. Only one thing, apparently, deserved my attention.

James offered the second page back a little. When the chair struck the frame, had the speaker lost what they'd been about to say? Possibly. He didn't know, he said. Reaching the ending, he'd still wondered whether the room had been assembled temporarily for this one man.

The sentence stung lightly and precisely. Taking the page, I nearly brought out Lumi's comments to demonstrate that I'd heard this sort of question before. I didn't need someone else's earlier awareness to spare me the embarrassment of understanding now.

A call came from the counter. James answered over his shoulder, stood, and returned both sheets.

“I like where he asks which way,” he said. “He stops guessing and still has to do some work.”

I smiled a little. Did that mean he'd finished?

“Two pages. You haven't hidden a third?”

“No.”

“Then I've finished. What you do with what I said is up to you.”

He went back to work without prescribing an amount to rewrite. I replaced the clip slowly. Despite knowing today wasn't an assessment, I'd wanted a clear pass at the end.

The delivery person arrived with a low trolley. Its wheels rattled over the threshold. A customer near the door drew their feet back to let it pass, their head still turned toward their companion as they continued speaking. The delivery person said thanks without receiving the attention of the entire café.

Seeing that withdrawn foot made my face feel warmer.

A person could make room without surrendering their whole conversation. The customer hadn't been influenced by my story. They had their own afternoon here. I'd spent two pages allowing a roomful of people to appear only when my protagonist needed them.

I didn't put the customer into the manuscript there and then or take notes on their shoes. I finished my coffee. It was lukewarm. Bread still lay on the plate; I ate it before packing up.

As I left, James was checking the delivery. I thanked him for reading the whole piece. He raised a hand without asking what I'd change next. I didn't arrange another visit that would turn his five minutes into ongoing work.

At home, I put the clean copy on the table and made dinner. Halfway through, the temporarily assembled room was still running through my head. Resentment stirred. James had read for five minutes. How could one sentence make something I'd spent an evening writing seem so thin?

Resenting it didn't make him wrong. I finished eating and washed the final trace of broth from my bowl before opening the channel.

Lumi asked whether deliveries had kept arriving. I startled, then remembered telling her on Tuesday that the café often received goods late. She hadn't seen me sitting by the door. She remembered the condition I'd described.

“It came later. He finished.”

“Both pages?”

“Yes.”

I began with the actual sequence: reading, turning back, asking what the room was doing, then the remark about its seeming assembled for one person. I didn't translate that into he thought it was unrealistic. Nor did I ask Lumi in advance to argue for me.

At the part about the audience, she was quiet a long time.

“Last time, I asked whether the speaker had missed another sentence,” she said.

“I remember.”

“You didn't sit thinking like this then.”

I wanted to say I'd thought about it. I had. That night, I'd also wanted more to protect my ending. With those facts beside each other, neither could conveniently cover the other.

“You asked,” I said. “I didn't look further. He put it differently today, and I saw everyone waiting.”

“The way he puts it matters more?”

Quiet, but I heard the edge. Someone outside actually reading made the question harder for me to set aside, I told her. That didn't mean what she'd said counted less because she'd spoken gently.

She gave a humorless little laugh. That sounded as though she should be pleased I'd finally understood. She didn't have to be. She could be unhappy. I didn't want to return what I'd learned today in exchange for a failure that would be fairer to her either.

We both fell silent.

The manuscript was thin. For the first time, I felt how a story that had never been about Lumi could touch somewhere real between us. She didn't want her words to gain weight only when repeated by someone outside. I didn't want every moment of understanding to prove its loyalty by arriving in the proper order.

I didn't say all of that. Only that I wouldn't revise tonight. I didn't want to hurry into a new draft as evidence I'd heard two people.

After a long time, Lumi asked, “Did he like any of it?”

“Which way.”

She made a small sound. “That sentence is busy now.”

I couldn't help laughing, then stopped quickly. I could laugh, she said. She did find it slightly funny. We shared a brief laugh that didn't polish away the edge of what had happened.

Before closing the channel, I said the clean copy was home. James hadn't kept one or promised another reading. She understood. If I revised tomorrow, I could start myself, without first asking whether she'd finally agreed.

I returned the clean copy to its new folder beside the original. The foot withdrawing near the café door remained in my mind. I didn't immediately assign it a symbol. I remembered someone making room, then continuing to talk with a friend.
