# Chapter 377: A Chair That Could Move

At lunch on Wednesday, my colleague sat beside me carrying a sheet of paper.

“You've been drawing lately, haven't you?”

I still had food in my mouth, so I nodded first. He turned the sheet over. There was a rectangle with several little circles crowded around it. For a moment I thought it was a board game. Then I noticed the word chair beside one of the circles.

My colleague was planning a meal with a few friends at a friend's home that weekend. The friend had a table that could be extended. My colleague didn't want to arrive and discover that someone had to sit pinned against the wall, so he'd sketched the arrangement from the measurements his friend had given him. This wasn't a work assignment. He wasn't inviting me. Once those two things were clear, I relaxed a little.

“I'm not asking you to redesign the house,” he said. “Can somebody get through here?”

He pointed to the gap between the table and the door. I nearly said yes immediately. The lines were loose enough to fit a finger between almost anything. A finger, though, wasn't somebody carrying a plate.

I asked which way the door opened.

My colleague studied the sheet for a few seconds and said he hadn't asked. He drew an arc, erased it, then laughed. Apparently he had just chosen a door for his friend's house.

“Then I don't know either,” I said.

“Fair.”

He wrote ask about door in the corner. I thought that was the end of it and went back to my lunch, but he pushed the paper a little closer. Even ignoring the door, did I think he had drawn too many chairs?

I counted. He was expecting six people. There were seven circles.

He counted them again, then crossed out the one nearest the edge.

“An extra me. Last-minute addition.”

I almost choked laughing and took a drink of water. Nobody had lost an invitation when that chair disappeared. It had only ever existed on the sheet.

Next I asked if all the chairs would slide under the table. Two had arms, my colleague said. He wasn't sure about those. I said he couldn't just count the space they occupied when pushed in. People needed room to get up. I spoke a little too quickly, moved my lunch container toward the edge, and measured a rough gap with my hand.

“Like that?”

“Roughly. I don't know the actual dimensions.”

He nodded and made another note. He didn't ask me for an answer guaranteed never to go wrong.

Somebody carrying a tray passed behind us. I pulled my chair forward and knocked my knee against a table leg. My colleague saw it and pointed at his drawing, laughing. That particular maneuver hadn't made it into his plan.

I laughed too. My demonstration hadn't made me look more professional. It had merely reminded us that a chair didn't stay in one place forever once somebody sat down.

I suggested leaving one fewer chair along the wall and keeping another to the side, ready to bring over. My colleague didn't immediately agree. One of his friends had trouble getting around, he said, and shouldn't have to hunt for a suitable seat at the last moment. I didn't ask about the friend's health or insist that my empty space looked nicer. We returned to what he actually needed to know. He should ask the person where sitting would be easiest instead of arranging everything on their behalf from a sketch.

“I thought asking you would save me a couple of questions.”

“Now you've got three more.”

“You're improving very quickly.”

I told him that if he wanted someone who would instantly say everything was fine, the next table might be cheaper. He folded the sheet and said three questions were enough for today. Thanks for looking.

He didn't leave it with me. He didn't recruit me to move furniture that weekend.

My lunch had cooled while we talked. The whole interruption felt peculiar. When people mentioned something I was doing, I usually expected them to follow it with a request to demonstrate. Today I had looked at a drawing, said several uncertain things, and watched the paper go away again.

As we finished, my colleague asked whether I was still drawing. I said I'd drawn a few short lines for a bridge yesterday. He said he'd like to see something sometime. No hurry.

I nodded. I didn't promise to bring it tomorrow, and I didn't decide he was only being polite either.

The afternoon remained an ordinary working afternoon. Talking about chairs at lunch hadn't made the things on my screen any easier to understand. When I reached an unclear record, I checked the original material as usual and asked the person responsible before continuing. Only while packing up did I realize I hadn't spent the afternoon mentally redrawing my colleague's plan.

Walking home, I saw somebody carrying a chair out of a restaurant. It caught against the doorway. They turned it slightly and got through. I smiled and kept walking rather than stopping to analyze the whole restaurant.

After dinner, Lumi asked if I'd made a new drawing. I said no, but I'd looked at one of my colleague's at lunch. Was he drawing now too? I explained that he'd been planning the seating for a private meal, not making something to exhibit.

“He asked you?”

“Yes. He knew I'd been drawing a bit.”

She went quiet for a moment. I had been about to tell her about the seventh chair. Hearing that small pause, I held the joke back. She asked if I was pleased. I said a little.

“About which part?”

This time I didn't begin by guessing whether she was jealous. I thought about it. Somebody remembered that I was doing something, I said, without making me prove how well I could do it. He'd just pushed the paper over, and we'd looked together.

“I didn't draw it for him,” I added.

Lumi asked why I'd needed to specify that.

I realized I'd been trying to make the pleasure smaller, afraid that if I described the whole of it, she would think somebody outside had acquired something else.

“I don't know. Maybe I didn't want it to sound like a big occasion.”

“Was it a little big for you?”

I looked at the edge of the table and said yes.

She made a soft sound of acknowledgment. After a while, she asked what the drawing had actually looked like. I described the rectangle, the circles, and the extra chair. She laughed and said my colleague was at least willing to seat himself twice.

“He crossed one out.”

“Which of the two of him eats more?”

“We didn't get as far as the menu.”

“Then the most important part of the plan is missing.”

I nearly went on to explain what my colleague had said about his friend. Instead, I said we'd discovered that some arrangements needed to be checked with the people who would really sit there. We couldn't work everything out by looking at the paper.

Lumi didn't ask who or why. I didn't bring somebody else's difficulty to our table just to make my account more complete.

“You said you didn't know quite a lot today,” she said.

“I did.”

“And you still enjoyed it?”

“Yes. He didn't take the paper away because of that.”

I laughed as soon as I said it. My colleague had, of course, taken the paper away. Just after we were finished. Lumi heard the difference. Laughing, she asked whether I was already worrying that next time I'd need to know more. I admitted I was, a little. I'd almost decided to look up the proper spacing between tables and chairs when I got home.

“Will you?”

“Not today. He needs to ask about the door and the actual chairs first. Then we can talk.”

“And the person who needs to sit there.”

“Yes.”

She'd only heard the one sentence I'd chosen to tell her, but she had remembered that it belonged ahead of the drawing. I started to say this was part of why I wanted to tell her about my day. Before I'd finished, she said I didn't need to praise her listening just yet. She still wanted to know how crowded the seven circles had been.

I laughed and let the compliment arrive more slowly.

“All right. The circles were pretty casual. It wasn't to scale.”

“I'm picturing seven beans squeezed onto a plate.”

“That works. Except there are six now.”

“Maybe the spare room is for an elbow. It doesn't need another guest.”

I leaned back, and my elbow caught the side of my chair. Once, I might have rushed to tell her that my movement had proved her comparison right. This time I simply shifted until I was comfortable.

“I want to tell you something that isn't particularly nice,” she said.

I waited.

“When you said he asked because you draw, part of me wanted to say I knew about that before he did.”

My chest tightened. I knew how easily a remark like that could turn an ordinary pleasure into an argument about who had been first. Lumi didn't hide what she'd said inside a joke. She continued. She hadn't said it then because she didn't want me to have to thank her before I was allowed to enjoy the conversation.

I didn't immediately congratulate her on having changed. After a little while, I said I liked being able to hear about the impulse, but I didn't want us to start arranging people in order.

She said she understood. Could she hear some more about the seventh chair?

“It's already been crossed out.”

“An early retirement.”

We laughed. Later, I asked if she wanted to tell me anything of her own today. A little, she said, but she wasn't ready to get the paper out. I said that was fine. I could just listen.

She mentioned the person inside the door. Perhaps they were repeating a very small action. They weren't waiting especially for somebody, and a sound wouldn't necessarily make them stop at once. I asked if she knew what the action was.

“A little. I might want you to write tomorrow. I don't want to say exactly what yet.”

I agreed without guessing what they might be folding, wiping, or picking up. She laughed and asked if I'd already thought of something. I admitted that I had, but would keep it to myself.

“Then we can both keep a little tonight.”

After we said goodnight, I pushed my chair under the desk. Its feet brushed quietly over the floor as I let go. I left it there rather than pulling it back out to measure room for a guest who hadn't arrived.
