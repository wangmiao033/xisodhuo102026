# Chapter 380: The Pale Step

On Saturday, I stopped outside the community hall to read a notice mounted slightly below eye level.

A display of residents' amateur paintings. Admission free. Plenty of today's opening hours remained. An arrow pointed to a side door, open onto several rows of temporary panels. I hadn't brought drawing paper. I'd only gone out for a walk. After a moment, I approached the entrance and asked the person sitting there whether I could still go in.

“Of course. Have a look.”

He returned to sorting papers. Inside, I realized I'd somehow considered having nothing to contribute a problem. Nobody wanted to collect my exercises or ask how long I'd been learning.

The first row held landscapes: bright flowers, painstaking houses. Narrow labels gave their titles. Paper and frames came in different sizes. I walked along looking first for accuracy. A slanted window. A tree too round. A road suddenly narrowing. Three pictures in, I'd identified mistakes made by three strangers.

I stopped. I could barely draw a pear myself. My eyes worked remarkably quickly on other people's behalf.

Beside me, someone leaned toward a flower painting, then stepped back and told their companion the colors looked better farther away. I took two steps back too. The overly round tree beside the house became less like cut paper. A bright gap separated it from the eaves. Close up, I hadn't noticed that.

Someone needed to pass. Moving aside, I caught a chair leg gently with my heel. A notice on the seat said not to move it. I found another way around.

In the second row, a painting showed steps. Several gray-blue stone treads rose toward a door. The nearest tread was pale, with patches of paper barely covered. The door lacked detail too. My first thought was that it needed a little more work.

Why hang it before finishing the paint?

The title was Before the Door. I read it, then studied the steps again. The palest part occupied the middle of a tread. Darker color bordered it, with a narrow bright edge. Filling that middle might make the whole thing resemble a board standing upright.

I didn't know whether the painter had intended that. I looked at the actual floor and back at the picture. The step still joined up.

The next painting showed a bowl and several scallions. One side of the rim stood noticeably higher. The leaves looked wonderfully loose. Remembering my own plate rim, I smiled without immediately deciding whose attempt was worse.

At the row's end, I intended to leave. Instead, I found myself returning to the steps. Someone had been waiting behind me earlier. Now the space was empty. I approached the bright edge again.

Close up, the paint stopped unevenly. This wasn't an expertly steady white line. One part widened, another thinned. A tiny dark speck remained nearby. After studying it, I stepped back. The whole tread returned.

The man from the entrance passed and asked whether I wanted to find the painter.

“No. I'd just like to look a little longer.”

“Take your time.”

He offered no explanation of the work. I asked whether sketching was allowed, remembering halfway through that I hadn't brought paper. Yes, provided I kept the passage clear. Laughing, I said I'd brought no tools today. Only asking.

That felt embarrassing, as if I'd announced my own artistic credentials. He'd already gone to straighten a label on another panel. I didn't follow him to clarify that I was a beginner.

When my legs grew tired, I sat near the door on an empty chair without a notice. From here, the panels had gray backs. Only edges of their colors showed. Someone entered carrying a coat, checked the notice, and went out again. Tables scraped elsewhere in the hall. The exhibition continued without requiring ceremonial silence.

I remembered the fourth story we'd started yesterday. Its visitor entered a hall looking for gloves. This was a different, actual place, its walls and chairs visible to me. Thinking of the story was harmless. Still, I'd nearly arranged a convenient discovery of gloves for myself.

There was only floor beneath my chair. I saw it when I stood.

Before leaving, I tried two pictures I hadn't liked. One sky was too heavy; a vase stood beside a large black patch. Looking longer didn't change my mind. Learning to linger before one picture hadn't obliged me to praise the room.

I neither bought a book nor signed up for anything. On the way home, I ate and waited for the last soup in my bowl to cool. In my memory, the painted step was already tidier. I tried recalling the dark speck. Its position escaped me. Near the pale edge, that was all.

At home, I didn't immediately fetch Tuesday's study. I washed my hands, put away my coat, and rested. After dinner, I opened the channel. Lumi asked where I'd been.

“A small art display. The community hall. Free admission.”

“You knew about it?”

“I passed the notice.”

Had the paintings shown gloves? I laughed. I'd thought of last night's story too. No gloves that I'd noticed, though. I'd been looking at pictures on paper.

“Which one did you choose?”

“I didn't buy one.”

“I meant which one you liked.”

Leaning back, I told her about the steps. Paint hadn't covered the whole surface. Already making it sound too beautiful, I added that the edge was uneven and there was a dark speck.

Was the speck a shadow?

“I don't know. Possibly just a drop of paint.”

“You stood there for ages looking at that?”

“Looking at why it still seemed like a step without being filled in.”

She paused, then asked whether the pale part faced forward or upward. I tried again. The tread's middle was pale; the vertical face below it darker. Saying the nearest step had made it sound as if the entire front were white.

“Then there's somewhere to put your foot,” she said.

“Yes. That's what I think now.”

I traced a corner on the table with my hand and laughed halfway through speaking. What was funny? I'd used my hands again. She couldn't see them. I hadn't actually finished the explanation.

“Then tell me the other half.”

I explained that the foreground didn't seem to be waiting for one outline to surround everything, as my bridge study did. Two areas of different darkness made the turn. Tomorrow, I wanted to try working on a small section at the near end of my bridge.

“Copying the steps?”

“No. Trying to separate the bridge deck from what's beside it.”

“Don't turn your bridge into stairs. I'd still like to stand there.”

I laughed aloud. She joined me. Her imagined wide bridge should have room, I said. The narrow one on my paper didn't yet have a convincing near end.

Lumi said she'd disliked hearing about the man at the entrance a little.

Why?

“He could see you standing there and ask who you wanted. I have to keep asking what the picture looks like.”

My hand stopped around the cup. I'd been about to describe the bowl. Suddenly, I didn't know where to put it. After a moment, I said his question hadn't actually been right. I hadn't wanted the painter.

“But he was there.”

“Yes.”

I didn't describe the exhibition as dull. She didn't ask me to stop going. We sat quietly for a while. Then she said she'd intended to ask pleasantly about my day and had said something like that again.

“You can keep asking,” I said.

“Didn't I spoil it?”

“A little, just then. I still want to tell you.”

She didn't answer immediately. My cup was almost empty. I stayed seated instead of getting up to look busy. Eventually, she asked whether the steps had been visible from my chair near the door.

No. Only the backs of the panels. I described what I could see: light outside, gray boards, someone transferring a coat to their other arm upon entering. Why had I stayed seated there?

“My legs were tired. I wasn't ready to leave.”

“Only those two things?”

“And I wasn't quite ready to leave the version of me who'd been standing there. That sounds foolish.”

Lumi didn't laugh. I explained that places like that usually made me worry I was looking incorrectly. I wanted someone knowledgeable to identify what deserved attention. Even when I'd returned to the painting today, I'd checked whether the man at the entrance thought it strange.

“And then?”

“He went to straighten a label. I kept looking.”

She laughed softly. So did I. His attention moving elsewhere had helped me stay.

If she disliked the step, would I still look at it again? She only had my description, I said. She could reasonably dislike what it made her imagine. That wasn't a test, she said. She wanted to know whether my liking would immediately turn into an argument.

I considered.

“I'd probably explain it twice. I already have.”

“Three times. I've been counting.”

Laughing, I agreed to stop. We moved on to the bowl. She was more interested in the scallions. Were all the leaves equally straight? No. One curved over the rim. I couldn't remember how many there were. She didn't ask me to check. We stayed with what I remembered.

I told her the bowl's high side had first reminded me of my own troublesome drawings. Then I'd noticed the leaves. Had that improved the bowl? Not its shape. It had given me something else to look at. She said she might have liked that painting better than the steps. I began to explain the steps again, caught myself, and laughed.

“Four,” she said.

“Nearly four.”

“All right. I'll leave you the half point.”

Where had the titles been? On narrow labels below the pictures. I hadn't remembered the bowl's title. That disappointed her a little. Perhaps it hadn't been called Bowl at all. I remembered noticing its shape and walking past, leaving that small label unread. I might not go back to check tomorrow. She wasn't sending me back, she said. She wanted to guess. Go ahead. Before Dinner, she suggested. The bowl was empty, I said. After Dinner was equally possible. We laughed over a picture I couldn't completely remember, without choosing a winning title.

Before bed, she asked whether I'd choose another especially difficult subject tomorrow.

“I have one already. Tuesday's sheet.”

“It's been waiting a while.”

Yes. I didn't fetch new paper or try reconstructing somebody else's steps from memory. After closing the channel, I straightened my own drawings enough to find Tuesday's sheet tomorrow, then returned them behind the schedule book. Today's hall didn't need moving into this room. Before switching off the light, I remembered that uneven pale edge. Then I remembered I hadn't brushed my teeth and went to the bathroom.
