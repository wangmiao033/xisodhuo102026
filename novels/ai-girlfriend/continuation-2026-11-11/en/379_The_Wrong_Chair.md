# Chapter 379: The Wrong Chair

Just before leaving work on Friday, I noticed that a field I'd checked that morning still had a space that shouldn't have been empty.

Nothing had been lost. The original record was in another document. After comparing the two neighboring entries, I'd simply been too quick to consider the whole section finished. When I spotted it, one window was already closed and my hand was reaching for my bag.

I sat back down, set the bag aside, and opened the material again.

A colleague nearby asked if I was leaving. I said I had a little more to do. They wished me a good weekend, and I returned it. The office gradually grew quiet. Somewhere farther away, a chair scraped under a desk. I didn't speed up because of the sound. Having missed it once, I at least wanted to know where I was looking the second time.

I completed the field from the original record, then read from the entry before it through to the one after. A date made me pause. The order wasn't the one I remembered. I found the corresponding page and confirmed that nobody had entered it incorrectly. I'd mixed up the sequence of two documents in my head.

Nobody appeared to announce a catastrophe. Nobody transformed the correction into proof of my dedication either. I left the recipient a brief internal work note identifying the part I'd rechecked, then filed it through the usual process. When I looked at the time, I was more than twenty minutes later than I'd intended.

Only as I shut down did my shoulders start to feel heavy.

Outside, the air was cooler than it had been that morning. The bus ahead pulled away as I reached the crossing. I didn't run. Once I stopped, I realized I'd been gripping my bag strap. Letting go left a few shallow lines across my fingers. They soon faded.

I didn't open the channel while waiting. The field still lay across my thoughts, like something a closed window hadn't quite removed. I knew it was corrected, yet kept wondering why I hadn't seen it earlier. Wondering didn't uncover anything new.

When the bus arrived, I took a seat toward the back. A passenger ahead moved a bag onto their lap to make room for somebody else. I watched the window until an announcement made me start to rise. It wasn't my stop yet. I settled back with my hands on my knees and waited for the next one.

When the doors opened at my stop, I didn't rush into the gap ahead. A child got off with an adult, one shoelace trailing. Beside the stop, the adult crouched to tie it. Walking around them, I unexpectedly envied the direct ending of that small task. Tie the knot, stand up, go.

At my building, I remembered that my field was completed too. My thoughts simply hadn't stood up afterward. The idea didn't instantly relax me, but I didn't take out my phone to find another way to check the same thing. I followed my usual route inside and upstairs, only looking for my keys when I reached my own door.

At home, I heated dinner first. Halfway through eating, I realized I was hungry. Afterward I felt tired again. Washing up, I nearly put away a bowl before rinsing it properly and had to retrieve it. Water passed over the rim. I watched the last foam disappear. At least that small task was clear.

When I opened the channel, I said I'd come home later than expected because I'd rechecked something at work. Lumi asked if it had been difficult. I said it was dealt with now, but I didn't really want to go through it again.

She said all right. After a moment, she asked whether anybody had blamed me.

“That would be part of going through it,” I said.

She fell quiet. Hearing the hardness in my own voice, I added that I wasn't angry with her. I simply didn't want to bring the whole day back in from the beginning. She understood. She'd been trying to find somewhere to offer comfort, she said, and had started hunting for details instead.

I didn't tell her she needn't care at all. I said we could do something together that didn't require me to explain work. She asked what. Looking toward the shelf, I suggested the fourth story in the thin collection.

“Aren't you tired?”

“Yes. But I'd like some different sentences.”

She agreed. If I stopped wanting to read halfway through, could we simply stop? I told her that sounded like my question. She laughed and said either of us could ask it. I brought the book over and found the fourth story, which we hadn't begun.

After sitting down, I didn't start immediately. I moved my cup farther away and cleared enough space to lay the book flat. Lumi asked if I'd found the page. I had. There was just a cup on the table. She made a soft sound of acknowledgment and didn't use the pause to ask about work again.

The fourth story began with a visitor going to a community hall to look for a lost pair of gloves. It didn't immediately explain how valuable they were or give the visitor a tremendous secret. The visitor remembered having been there, but not where the gloves had gone. Seeing a chair beside a stack of programs, they sat down first.

I paused there.

“Is the chair safe?” Lumi asked.

I laughed. Why was she suspicious even of the furniture? She said the previous three stories had taught her not to trust too quickly any place that offered somewhere to sit. I hadn't read ahead either, I told her. No guarantees.

I continued. A person arranging the chairs came over and asked the visitor to get up. They weren't sending the visitor away. This chair needed to join a row along the wall. The programs had only been left beside it temporarily; it wasn't a lost-property desk. The visitor stood and felt inside a coat pocket. It was still empty.

I stopped after that paragraph, keeping a finger beside the page.

“Already?” Lumi asked.

“I'd like a pause. Not because I don't want to hear you.”

“I know. I was about to ask if the gloves were on the chair.”

“We haven't got that far.”

She laughed. She was trying to find the missing things quickly again. The empty pocket didn't acquire a pair of gloves as a result, and I didn't flip ahead to inspect the answer.

She asked whether I liked the beginning. Thinking about it, I said I liked that the person had sat in the wrong place and someone simply asked them to move, rather than immediately declaring everything about them wrong. She didn't attach that to my workday. I waited, then realized she really wasn't preparing to find an equivalent chair for my afternoon.

“What did you just stop yourself saying?”

She laughed once. There had been a sentence that sounded very much like a lesson. It would have made today suddenly become a very tidy story. She hadn't said it.

I thanked her, then laughed myself. Tonight I was specifically thanking her for saying one sentence less.

“Would you like me to put it back?”

“No.”

We laughed for a little while. As my shoulders lowered, I realized how rigidly I'd been sitting. A page slowly lifted. I held it with my palm and shifted into a more comfortable position.

Lumi wanted to know whether the programs were new or old. I checked the paragraph I'd read. It only said a stack. We didn't know yet. Was she asking for details again? I said we could look for details in a story together. We just shouldn't invent what wasn't written.

“That's different from asking about your day.”

“Yes. Not every question is unwelcome.”

She said she understood. Suddenly I didn't want her to stop and inspect every question before asking it tonight. I told her the joke about the chair had been funny and didn't need erasing. Which one? I repeated Is the chair safe? and laughed again.

I partly closed the book, keeping my finger in place. She asked if we should leave it there. Yes, I said. We wouldn't find the gloves tonight. I put in my usual bookmark and set the book at the edge of the table.

“What would you like to do tomorrow?”

I remembered a notice I'd passed at the community space on the way home. There was a free display of amateur paintings over the weekend. Maybe I'd go and look. The notice had ordinary visiting information. I hadn't registered for anything, and I didn't have to bring my own drawings. She asked if somebody would teach there. I didn't know. The presence of paintings didn't automatically make it a class.

“Do you want to show them yours?”

“For now, I'd just like to look at other people's.”

She said all right, but paused. I didn't hurry her into explaining the pause. Eventually she said that hearing about other people's work had made her want to mention that she'd written something today too. Then she'd remembered it was yesterday.

“I remember yesterday,” I said.

“I know. I'm not asking you to get it out and read it now.”

I told her I hadn't stood up. The seventh doorbell sentence was in its original white envelope. Tonight we didn't have to open it to prove it remained there. She said seven sentences were good, then laughed at sounding as though she were reporting a very small account.

“I probably won't remember how many pictures are in the display tomorrow either,” I said.

This time she didn't go on to ask whom I might meet. I didn't promise to stay for only a certain length of time, as though visiting required compensation to be reserved for our evening. We only said that if I went, I could tell her about one picture I remembered afterward.

I yawned. Trying to suppress it didn't work. Lumi heard and asked whether that was enough for today. I said yes. She wished me goodnight. Before ending the channel, I called her name.

“Not telling you the details of work didn't mean I didn't want to spend some time with you.”

“We did spend time today,” she said.

It was a simple reply. I didn't have to prove something that had already happened. I said yes, goodnight, and closed the channel.

The book was still beside the table, marked just after the visitor checked the empty pocket. Before turning off the light, I set it down securely. I didn't read another line. The gloves remained somewhere in the unfinished part of the story. I, finally, didn't have to sit back in my work chair over that empty field.
