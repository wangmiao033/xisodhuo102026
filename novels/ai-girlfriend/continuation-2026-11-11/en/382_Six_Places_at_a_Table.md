# Chapter 382: Six Places at a Table

After work on Monday, a sheet on the library's glass door made me stop.

I noticed the tiny drawing first: a cup beside several lines that hadn't been made deliberately straight. It wasn't inside a frame like the work I'd seen over the weekend. Tape held it beneath an activity notice, with one corner lifting slightly.

Open drawing table. Wednesday, half past six until half past seven. Free. Six places, first come, first seated.

I read the lines twice. They didn't promise to teach a particular skill. They didn't require a good drawing as evidence of suitability. At the bottom, the notice said to bring paper and a pencil. Beginners welcome. People passed through the doors. I moved aside before examining the cup again.

One side was higher than the other. My first thought was that if even their example looked like that, perhaps I could go. Then the reassurance struck me as ungenerous. Whoever had drawn it probably hadn't intended an uneven cup to become a step for me to climb.

Inside, I approached the information desk. The person on duty was attaching reshelving slips to a stack of books. She looked up and asked what I needed.

“The drawing table on the door. Is that this Wednesday?”

She checked the activity list beside her. Yes. Did I need to register beforehand? Not for this session. I could look for a place at the long table behind the reading area before it began. If it was full, they wouldn't add chairs. The passage needed to remain clear.

I nodded, already imagining a race for the sixth seat. She continued. It wasn't a structured course. The person leading it would bring several ordinary objects. Everyone could draw for a while, then look at each other's work if they wanted. No homework and no grading.

“Is it all right if you're not very good?”

Smiling, she said the notice welcomed beginners. I looked down. I'd asked something she had already answered.

“I saw that. I wanted to be sure it didn't mean knowing a little first.”

“If you can hold a pencil, that's enough. The facilitator will explain things at the table.”

I didn't ask whether holding a pencil included sharpening it badly. She moved the list aside to make room for the next reader. I thanked her and stepped away without turning a free seat into a contract guaranteeing comfort.

I didn't borrow a book. After returning the drawing manual, I still had enough paper at home to know what I didn't understand. Today, I wanted to ask whether I could sit with a few people and try. I wasn't looking for another volume of answers to read alone.

Walking farther inside, I found the long table. For now, it was an ordinary reading table, with two people absorbed in their books. Six chairs stood along its sides. The innermost one was near the wall. I didn't stop behind the readers to inspect their pencil grips. I passed, remembered the location, and turned toward the exit.

The notice remained on the door. I checked the start and finish times and wrote them on a small sheet I carried. The writing had crowded into a corner, as if reluctant to occupy space. Turning to the blank side, I wrote it again, adding bring my own paper and pencils beneath.

Not compulsory attendance. Not perhaps, either, to make the decision appear lighter. I wanted to go. Whether a seat remained was something I'd discover on Wednesday.

Just as I was about to cap the pen, someone beside the notice asked whether I was helping with the activity. I wasn't staff, I explained. I'd only just asked inside. They pointed to bring paper and a pencil. They had ordinary pencils at home. Would those work?

The staff member had said holding a pencil was enough, I replied. I planned to bring ordinary ones too. I didn't add a professional grade to sound better informed than I was. Where was the table? Behind the reading area, I said, indicating the direction. They could ask the person on duty once inside.

The conversation went easily. Not because I'd suddenly become good at talking to strangers. The questions were specific; I'd just obtained the answers. Nobody needed a history of my drawings before asking me for directions. While I still held the pen, they smiled at the finishing time. Half past seven. They probably wouldn't leave work in time. They'd look at the other notices instead.

I almost offered suggestions for leaving work early. Then I stopped and simply acknowledged what they'd said. They weren't allowing this table to determine the entire week's arrangements. They moved to another sheet, and I stepped clear of the doorway. Our brief conversation hadn't become an appointment to attend together.

Only a few steps later did I realize I'd told another person I planned to bring something. That sounded more like a choice than the indecision I'd been rehearsing privately. I needn't wait for them to accompany me. Their being unable to attend didn't prove the activity wasn't worth visiting either.

Several other notices surrounded it. Some activities had finished; others would begin later. I didn't copy them all and arrange a suddenly crowded life for the coming weeks. Today, there was only Wednesday's sheet. I capped the pen, put the note somewhere it wouldn't be crumpled with loose change, and finally left the doorway.

On the walk home, dinner suddenly became a practical consideration. Going directly would mean eating something first. I couldn't stay hungry until half past seven and blame every irritation on a cup. I didn't know whether work would be busy. But it was Monday. I needn't invent overtime on Wednesday as an escape route in advance.

I bought what I needed for dinner at a familiar shop and carried it home. The shopping bag repaired with pale thread held only light things. I wasn't conducting another test of its side seam. Once everything was put away, I cooked. Only after eating and clearing up did I remember the uneven edge of the cup on the notice.

When I opened the channel, I told Lumi I wanted to go to a drawing table on Wednesday.

“To draw a table?”

“To sit at one and draw. Not to study furniture.”

“You've drawn a bench recently. I thought I'd check.”

Laughing, I explained the particulars: free, one hour, no more than six places, no reservations. I'd asked twice whether beginners were allowed. The second question had almost been the first turned around.

Lumi laughed. The staff member sounded patient. Yes. She hadn't handed me a pencil for an immediate examination.

“What do you want to draw?”

“The facilitator will bring things. I don't know yet.”

“What if it's another cup?”

“I should be qualified to recognize it by now.”

She laughed for a while, then asked whether the six places included the facilitator. I stopped. The notice hadn't said, and I hadn't asked. I almost decided to check tomorrow. Lumi added that she'd merely imagined seven people crowded around the table. She wasn't asking me to return for supplementary information.

I knew. I could see how they arranged it when I arrived. The table already contained too many details that hadn't happened: who would sit first, who would draw well, whether my paper would obstruct someone. The number she'd mentioned was only one of them.

“You've started choosing your seat already?”

“Near the end would make leaving easier.”

“That isn't necessarily an escape plan. You might just need room for your legs.”

Looking down at them, I laughed. She didn't know the table's dimensions. She hadn't diagnosed my personality either. She'd brought a question I'd made very serious back to a chair.

There was some escape planning, I admitted. Lumi didn't immediately argue. Sometimes she'd arranged something she wanted to do as though she might cancel at any moment, she said, rather than admit how much it mattered. She was describing her own thinking. She didn't require me to establish that our experiences were identical.

“You could still leave early,” she added.

“Yes. I could stay for the whole thing too.”

“Then don't choose bad weather for it now.”

I laughed. The weather hadn't even appeared. Her remark let me hear what I'd been preparing: no seats, work running late, dinner delayed, an object too difficult to draw. Each was possible. None had happened.

I brought the small sheet to the table and told her I was looking at the times. Was I planning to erase them? No. I was putting the paper in my schedule book so I could find it tomorrow.

“You can write it larger.”

“I already did.”

“Good. The notice needn't hide from you.”

Smiling, I placed it in the book. My paper marker from the returned library volume remained there too. I didn't assign it a new task because of this activity. I put the new note on a page where I'd see it and closed the cover.

Would I take an earlier drawing? Perhaps one, though the staff member hadn't asked. Then choose something I was willing to show, Lumi said. Not something required to defend the past several weeks. Remembering the crooked cup studies, I almost wanted to take an especially poor one so I couldn't pretend I already knew what I was doing. I told her that thought too.

“Don't deliberately choose the worst either,” she said.

Why not? It sounded like saying every critical thing first, leaving nobody else a chance to notice anything different. I didn't immediately answer. She hadn't seen the drawings and couldn't choose for me. Her remark still touched precisely what I'd begun planning.

I would leave choosing until tomorrow. Fine. She changed the subject and asked about dinner. The speed of the change disappointed me slightly. Talking about that table had made me more excited than I'd expected.

I told her. Quietly, Lumi said she'd heard that. I didn't need to compress Wednesday into an ordinary evening before it arrived.

We talked for a few more minutes. I didn't promise a complete report afterward or make the fact that she would remain at home a problem we had to solve tonight. Her last question was whether I'd take an eraser. Yes. Laughing, I said it was beginning to sound like a serious activity unrelated to furniture.

Saying good night, I remembered the drawing on the notice. I hadn't actually examined it well enough to decide whether it was good. What I'd seen first was somewhere I might sit. I didn't need to judge that cup incorrectly before allowing myself through the door.

After closing the channel, I put my bag where it belonged. There was no new loan slip in my pocket, and the bag hadn't grown heavier. I straightened my coat on its hook, then went to wash my glass without returning to read the times again. Wednesday hadn't arrived. The hour on paper had a place now. Tonight's bedtime still did too.
