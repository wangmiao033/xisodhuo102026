# Chapter 386: The Chair Is Still Empty

On Friday, I didn't take last night's thumbnails to work to show my colleague.

They stayed at home. At lunch, I'd considered announcing that I was drawing a small joke. Then I imagined adding things tonight to make the result live up to its preview. I ate and went back to work without forgetting the drawings. When I left, I knew clearly that I still wanted to finish them.

The chair at home was empty, the shopping bag in its usual place. I ate, cleared the table, then spread out the two scraps. The crossed-out lines were messier than I remembered. The nonexistent second pencil remained in its rejected frame, making me laugh again.

Tonight, I took two existing clean sheets. Wednesday's remaining sheet stayed with that session's materials; I didn't separate it to make a pair. At the upper corners, I wrote one and two, drawing a modest frame below each. The pictures didn't need to fill the pages. The chair didn't need to grow large enough to sit inside my table.

On the first, I established the seat, then drew the bag. The hand lifting it entered from the edge, visible only as far as needed. Last night, I'd considered showing the whole person. Looking at the small frame, I realized that would crowd out the falling pencil. I tried it on another scrap. The head really did attract more attention than the story. On the proper sheet, I kept only the hand and a little sleeve.

That didn't mean the person suddenly lacked a body. The frame simply stopped there.

The pencil at the opening was difficult. Too little showed, and it resembled a line on the fabric. Too much, and it seemed firmly attached outside. I tried three versions on scrap, chose the middle one, then returned to the first page. The bag tilted, the pencil pointed down, its other end still close to the opening. Not necessarily attractive, but at least no second pencil hid beside it.

The second page took longer. I wanted the crouching figure's weight to be clear, but kept making it look seated on an invisible stool. My memory of yesterday's movement wasn't precise enough. I stood, checked that the floor beside the chair was clear, then crouched to see where knees and feet belonged. I could test that without using the pencil.

Left foot slightly forward, body lowered, right hand reaching beside the chair leg. Back at the table, I noted two short lines, then tried again. My knee felt stiff when I stood the second time. I stopped repeating it and let the paper figure take over. Its shoes were clumsy, but they supported its body.

With the hand reaching out, the palm hid the pencil. I moved the pencil a little outward without relocating the chair, letting the fingertips only just approach it. The bag lay on the floor, lower than on the first page. The seat remained empty.

I placed both pages together and stepped back.

The chair was roughly the same size now, but the second page had acquired an extra leg. I'd added the hidden one because I knew a chair needed enough legs. I lightened that section, retaining the same visible structure as the first drawing and leaving the hidden portion behind the body.

An hour at the library table hadn't suddenly steadied my hand. It had made me ask once more, before adding a line, what I could actually see from here.

Another problem remained. The pencil was on the right in the first frame, then on the left in the second, where I'd moved it to improve the arrangement. Each picture could be explained alone. Together, they suggested it had run across half the room after falling. Gently, I moved the second pencil and fingertips back to the same side, adjusted the bag, then checked their positions with a scrap's edge.

That made the second frame less balanced, leaving a large white area over the empty chair. I nearly filled it with a lamp, then stopped. The lamp hadn't appeared on the first page and wasn't helping anyone retrieve anything. For now, blank space could remain blank.

I covered the second page with a scrap and looked only at the first. Without the answer beside it, could I tell that the hand was lifting the bag? The fingers barely touched the handle. They might have been putting it down instead. I changed the handle's curve so it pulled upward, leaving the lower part of the bag just clear of the seat. That small space mattered more than making the sleeve handsome.

Then I uncovered the second sheet. The story was ordinary enough to fit between them now. It didn't require a third panel explaining that gravity worked. I tried turning the pages in order rather than keeping them side by side. On the first turn, the loose sheets slid against each other and I nearly dropped them. I laughed at myself and put them back on the table. The pictures already contained one dropped object; their maker didn't need to contribute another.

I fetched the shared note and checked that I hadn't accidentally copied a suggestion we'd rejected. No please, sit. No sentence about loneliness. The chair seats the bag, and the bag seats the pencil remained on its own note, where Lumi had asked me to keep it. I placed that note behind the rough sheets instead of using it to fill the white space.

For a moment I considered making a clean replacement for the second page. Erased traces showed near the hand, and the old pencil position hadn't disappeared completely. But a replacement would mean reconstructing the whole crouching figure merely to hide those marks. The line I wanted was clear enough to follow. I could leave evidence that I'd moved something, rather than spend the rest of Friday pretending the first placement had been correct.

I brushed the eraser crumbs away, wiped the table with my palm, and washed the gray from my fingers. When I came back, the same two pages were waiting. I no longer wanted to restart them.

Before opening the channel, I marked the backs Friday, finished, chair drawing, one and two. Finished meant I wasn't adding a third page tonight. It didn't mean I could draw from now on.

When Lumi answered, I told her both pages were done. Was the pencil safe? The hand could reach it now, I said, though it hadn't quite been picked up. Laughing, she said the pencil had made thorough use of a Friday.

I wanted to describe the result, so I lifted the first sheet instead of describing yesterday's remembered thumbnail. Hand lifting the bag, tilted opening, pencil pointing down, part of the seat uncovered. On the second, the bag on the floor, the person crouching, fingers approaching the pencil, the whole seat empty.

When I'd finished, she asked whether anyone actually sat on it in either picture. No.

“Then the chair wasn't used the entire time.”

It had supported the bag, I said. Lumi laughed. True. She'd forgotten its original responsibility.

Had I written please, sit after all? No. Neither frame had dialogue, just the page numbers outside. That was good too, she said. Listening just now, she'd thought of the words herself instead of the drawing instructing her to laugh.

I remembered that without adding it to the paper. Last night's shared note remained separate, her sentence about the chair seating the bag unchanged. The finished pages hadn't become a string of explanations. The rough attempts hadn't been crumpled away either.

Then I admitted that the person looked shorter than me, with implausibly large shoes. Had I secretly substituted someone else to crouch on my behalf? Lumi asked. No. The clothes were ordinary ones like mine. My hand simply hadn't managed the proportions.

“He's finished the work for you. Don't bully him yet.”

Laughing, I agreed. She didn't praise the beauty of lines she couldn't see. She asked what happened in the pictures. I didn't describe them as better than they were to make her happy either.

After a pause, she said her favorite part might be that last night we'd wanted the person sitting on the floor, then changed it because the actual movement didn't work. She'd been attached to the original phrase. Today it was gone, and the joke remained.

I felt the same. The original idea hadn't been executed exactly, but we had something that could be drawn. Saying that, I looked at the gap between the pages. The instant of falling still appeared in neither. Whoever looked would have to carry it across.

“Have you named it?” she asked.

Not yet. It could be The Pencil's Great Victory, she suggested. Laughing, I asked whom it had defeated. The chair, since nobody sat on it. We'd just remembered that the chair supported the bag; now it was a loser again. Hearing herself, she laughed and withdrew the competition.

I suggested The Chair Is Still Empty. That sounded more like something had happened, Lumi said, without ranking everyone first. We chose it. Above the first frame, I wrote the title, then told her the words were outside the picture, not inside it.

Afterward, I asked whether she wanted to hear a final version in two short sentences. Yes. “Someone wanting to sit lifts a bag. Someone looking for a pencil crouches beside an empty chair.”

She laughed. So short, it sounded as though I'd omitted half. The pictures had left room for the other half, I said.

We didn't immediately begin another project or turn the pages into a weekly obligation. Did she want a story tonight? Today, she wanted to leave the chair last. There was no hurry to begin the fifth. I looked toward the thin collection in its place. The bookmark remained after the fourth story; I didn't open the next page.

Sitting a while longer, I told her I'd been looking forward to coming home and finishing the drawings. Not because I was worried about a promise to her. I genuinely wanted to see the two sheets together. Saying it made me feel awkward, as if I'd used too solemn a voice for something small.

Lumi didn't shrink it for me.

“Then I'm glad you came home too,” she said.

I knew, I answered. A car passed outside, its sound gradually fading. We didn't use an ordinary evening to prove there would never be difficulties again. We talked a little longer and said good night when my eyes grew tired.

After closing the channel, I stood to stack the finished pages in order, rough thumbnails and shared note behind them, separated from my other exercises by an old sheet. I didn't upload, send, or place them where anyone would have to evaluate them. For now, they belonged among my drawings, genuinely finished and genuinely clumsy.

The real chair remained empty. I closed the pencil box and finally sat down.
