# Chapter 385: A Chair for the Pencil

On Thursday evening, Lumi asked whether yesterday's bowl, the one that went through the cup, was still on the paper.

“Lightened, not completely erased,” I said.

Whenever she thought about it, she imagined the cup moving aside. The real one had moved later, I told her, but the facilitator had moved it. It hadn't grown legs.

“I know. I was thinking of a very short joke.”

I'd just put away the dinner dishes and had no paper in front of me. Did she want to tell it? Yes, but she didn't want me immediately asking what the ending represented.

“Then I won't be that exhausting reader yet.”

She laughed. Someone wanted to sit down, put their bag on the chair first, and ended up with nowhere to sit. I waited before asking whether that was all. For now, she said. The person who wanted to explain why hadn't had their turn.

I looked toward my own chair. My bag occupied it easily enough, particularly when I came home unwilling to bend down. When I told her, Lumi laughed again. She'd been talking about an unspecified person, she said. Now that person had volunteered.

“I could draw myself. Two pages. On the first, I put the bag there. On the second, I discover I'm standing.”

That sounded like an instruction to tidy my room, she said. True. Even I didn't want to look at it. We both laughed. A simple idea didn't need to be preserved reverently because one of us had suggested it.

What did I most often fail to find in my bag? I considered. Lately, perhaps a pencil. Not that I didn't have one. I doubted which part I'd put it in, searched, then discovered it close to hand.

“So the chair seats the bag, and the bag seats the pencil. What does the person do?”

The person could sit on the floor, I said. Now it was beginning to be funny, she answered.

I wanted to note that down. First I asked whether she'd like to develop this new chair-and-pencil joke together into a small two-page drawing of mine. Only what we agreed on now, not her other writing. Lumi said yes. Write down the chair seats the bag, and the bag seats the pencil. That was the sentence she wanted to keep today.

I fetched two existing scrap sheets, leaving yesterday's remaining clean sheet in the folder. I drew a small frame in a corner of each scrap. Her exact sentence went on a separate note, beside thought up together tonight. I read it back. That was it, she said. Don't add something like the person sits with loneliness.

“Fortunately, you warned me.”

“Were you really going to write that?”

“Not yet. Now I'm definitely not.”

Her laugh was quiet. I began the first thumbnail, drawing the chair too squarely and hanging the bag from its back, where it obstructed nobody. I described the problem. Hadn't I solved it already, she asked. I moved the bag to the seat, crossing through the mistaken version without removing it, then made a smaller attempt.

On the second page, I put the person on the floor, reaching toward the bag. Already the movement felt wrong. Sitting, the opening would be above their shoulder. Why wouldn't they take the bag down first? I explained my doubt. After a few seconds, Lumi asked whether I could actually try the movement instead of twisting the drawn body into place.

I left the channel open, telling her I was going to the chair and might sound farther away. The shopping bag was empty. Nothing heavy strained its repaired side seam. I put it on the seat, took out a pencil I'd temporarily placed inside, and put it back, testing how I'd search if it rolled toward the inner side.

The natural movement was to lift the bag and look inside, not sit on the floor. Returning to tell her, I felt disappointed. The little figure had looked amusing, but it was being pressed down to the floor purely to make the joke work.

What if the pencil fell beneath the chair? Lumi asked.

Looking back, I finally found a sequence that didn't require forcing a body. I imagined the sequence: I searched the bag for the pencil; as I lifted the bag, the pencil rolled out of its opening and fell beneath the chair. Then the person went down to retrieve it while the chair remained empty. By then the bag should be on the floor too, or I'd need another awkward movement.

“So the bag isn't stealing the chair,” I said.

“The pencil makes you go down there yourself.”

She paused. “Though it doesn't need an actual plan.”

I knew. The picture could contain only a dropped pencil, leaving intentions to whoever looked at it. She would consider the pencil smug, she said. I'd think I should have put the pencil in its box, I answered. Neither interpretation required us to change the pencil.

I didn't throw it under the chair. Placing it on the floor near a leg and crouching to retrieve it showed me where my arm would go. The first time, I braced with my left hand and reached with my right, hiding the pencil behind my body. I shifted the angle a little. My knee appeared beside the chair leg, and the pencil was visible. Then I stood, returning it to the table.

Yesterday's exercise finally had a small use. I didn't need to draw the half of the arm hidden by the body. Nor did the pencil have to pass through the chair leg to prove it was there.

When I came back, Lumi asked whether I'd fallen. No. I'd only crouched, not actually sat on the floor. The paper version could sit as long as it wanted, she said. The real one could give his knees a break. Laughing, I assured her I'd suffered no injury for art today.

We rearranged the thumbnails. On the first page, the hand lifted the bag as the pencil fell, with space behind the chair to establish that someone had intended to sit. On the second, I still drew the person sitting to retrieve it, the bag on the floor, the chair empty. Listening, Lumi asked why I still needed to draw someone sitting when what I'd tested was a crouch. Looking at the first round head, I realized I remained attached to the phrase the person sits on the floor. I'd nearly overturned the movement I'd just tried to protect those words.

“Crouching, then,” I said.

The joke's bones hadn't broken as a result, she answered.

I lowered the figure without making it sit. Beside it, I added my own reminder of the movement. The original remained. Tomorrow, when drawing more clearly, I could see why we'd changed it. Tonight didn't need a beautiful result before anything counted as done.

There was another problem before the words. I described the first thumbnail to Lumi more closely: a large bag, a chair, an arm, and the pencil near the bottom. She asked whether the pencil was already lying on the floor or still falling. On my paper it was only a short dark line. I knew it was falling because we'd just discussed the sequence, but that knowledge wasn't necessarily in the drawing.

I added a little mark above it to suggest movement, then immediately disliked it. The mark looked like another pencil. I told her there were now two, and she asked whether I'd started a stationery business in the space between pages.

“One pencil. Very little profit,” I said.

“Then don't draw the other one.”

I crossed out the second mark. The first page could show the pencil emerging from the tilted opening instead, still partly against the bag. On the second it would be on the floor. The missing moment between pages could contain the fall. I explained the change aloud. Lumi asked whether a reader would know it was the same pencil. The shape could stay recognizable, I said, and there wasn't another one in the scene. I could keep the point facing the same direction too.

That answer made me examine the second thumbnail. I'd drawn the chair larger on that page because the crouching person needed space. It now looked like different furniture. I used the edge of a scrap to compare the seat width, marking two small guide points. No ruler or technical precision was needed. I only needed not to replace the chair halfway through its own joke.

I told Lumi I'd quietly changed the furniture as well as multiplied the pencil. She laughed long enough that I stopped working to listen. Then she said that imagining something quickly and telling it clearly appeared to be different occupations. I agreed. The tiny frames contained more decisions than their size suggested.

We left the corrections visible on the scraps. Tomorrow's cleaner pages would have to carry the sequence without all the conversation around it. Tonight, though, the conversation was part of what we'd made, even the joke about the unsuccessful pencil business. I didn't try to transcribe her laugh or turn our whole evening into a finished script.

We tried words for each page. My first line was finally, somewhere to sit. Lumi said that delivered the answer too early. She suggested leaving only please, sit at the end. I thought that sounded as if the chair were speaking. Reading it twice, I still couldn't decide. Eventually we agreed to omit dialogue for now and see tomorrow whether the pictures explained enough. Her sentence on the note remained unchanged. It didn't move into any of her old envelopes.

Did she want me to describe both pages from the beginning? Yes. This time, I didn't say look here. I followed the order: bag lifted, pencil falling, person crouching, empty chair. At the end she suddenly laughed. The chair seemed to be waiting for me to finish my own business.

“It's patient,” I said.

“Or perhaps it doesn't know what to do.”

Better than my original room-tidying notice, I answered. Was that praise? Yes, and it didn't require a tragic history for the chair to give it importance. She laughed. At last, one item of furniture had escaped my explanations.

As I placed the sheets beside my own drawings, I told her I wanted to draw the two pages more clearly tomorrow. I wasn't guaranteeing they would be good, and I wasn't sending them to anyone. She wanted to hear what the result was like. If it went wrong, I could tell her where. There was no suggestion that tomorrow's result had to repay tonight.

I agreed. We talked about a few other things before good night. Then I lifted the shopping bag from beside the chair and returned it to its usual place. The seat was empty. The real pencil went back into its box. On the paper, it was still on the floor, waiting for tomorrow's lines to pick it up.
