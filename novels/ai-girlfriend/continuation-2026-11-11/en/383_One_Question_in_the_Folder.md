# Chapter 383: One Question in the Folder

At lunch on Tuesday, I realized I'd copied tomorrow's drawing notice twice without deciding what paper to take.

It wasn't a shortage. I had sheets with one used side, several clean ones, and more unsuccessful attempts than successful ones from the past few weeks. But the possibility of somebody seeing them across a table made ordinary white paper seem like clothing that needed choosing in advance.

My colleague came over with water. Seeing me fold and unfold a small sheet, he asked whether I had a time to remember. A drawing session at the library tomorrow evening, I said. Six places. I intended to see whether I could join.

What would I draw? I didn't know; the facilitator would bring things. He nodded, said it sounded good, and went to heat his lunch. I'd expected questions about when I'd started, whether he could see my work, or whether I intended to change careers. There had only been sounds good.

While eating, I volunteered that it wasn't a class and nobody promised to teach a particular skill. Hearing myself, I knew that was unnecessary. My colleague put down his spoon. Had something he'd said suggested I needed to learn something? No, I explained quickly. I was still explaining to myself.

“See how it goes once you've drawn, then.”

I laughed without attaching further disclaimers to the hour. I hadn't invited him; he hadn't promised to attend. We ate our lunches, and the conversation moved to why today's vegetables had been cut so large.

That afternoon, I finished the last piece of checking in front of me and clearly marked where tomorrow's work should continue. I wasn't calling unfinished work finished to secure an early departure. I didn't want to find the starting point again from scratch tomorrow. The work material stayed where it belonged. I kept my little activity note with my own things.

After dinner at home, I took out the drawings from the back of my schedule book and arranged them in their existing groups.

The early street corner. Cups, bench and flowerpot, two market pages, pears, and the bridge begun last Tuesday and developed at its near end on Sunday. I opened each place separated by old paper. Pencil marks remained where they were. Choosing one tonight didn't require erasing every unsatisfactory area.

I wanted to bring the market sheet. Its separate objects and smudged box corner at least suggested I'd visited somewhere. But once it was inside the stiff folder, I couldn't think of a question. I only wanted somebody to know this page had a history.

I took it out and substituted the bridge study.

The distant end remained simple; Sunday's additions made the nearer section clearer. The difference wasn't exhibition-ready progress. It gave me a question, though. As the railing became denser toward the far end, which lines should I leave out?

On a separate small sheet, I wrote three sentences. They grew longer, trying to account for the original painting in the library book too. When I stopped, I didn't want to read them myself. If somebody was willing to look tomorrow, they shouldn't first have to hear an entire book about my looking at pictures.

I crossed out the sentences, turned the sheet to its blank side, and began again: When the far railing becomes hard to see, how do I keep the lines from crowding together?

That might not be answered either. At least I knew what I wanted to ask. I wasn't bringing a sheet and waiting for somebody to tell me whether continuing was worthwhile.

With the bridge in the folder, I added three clean sheets. Not every old drawing. The rest returned to their order behind the schedule book. I chose two usable pencils from the new and old ones, tested their marks, sharpened the one that needed it, and emptied the shavings into the bin. The eraser went into a small paper sleeve so it wouldn't roll into the bottom of my bag.

Testing the pencils turned into a small, necessary experiment. The first point snapped when I pressed on the back of a used sheet. I stared at the tiny broken tip, then recognized the pressure: I was trying to make one line dark enough to count as finished. Tomorrow I would probably be nervous enough to do the same thing.

I sharpened it again, less narrowly this time, and tried a row of lighter marks. Beside them I made the sort of close upright strokes that crowded my distant railing. At first I drew every one separately. Then I reduced them to a few interruptions in a pale horizontal mark. Neither version looked much like a bridge. The second was at least a different mistake, and I could explain it without waving my hands.

I almost slipped this test into the folder too. Instead I put it beside the question. Was it useful because it showed the difficulty, or was I quietly rebuilding the whole stack I'd just removed? I held the two sheets together. The bridge already showed enough. The pencil test could stay here.

There was a small practical problem with the eraser sleeve. I'd folded the paper tight enough that getting the eraser out tore a corner. I loosened the fold and tried again. Now it slid out without dropping. I used it on one of the test marks, brushed the crumbs into my palm, and emptied them into the bin. A pencil, a piece of paper, an eraser. They were simple objects, but tomorrow I wanted to spend the hour using them rather than searching beneath the table.

Before putting the test sheet away, I wrote Tuesday pencil test on its back. It wasn't an exercise assigned by anyone. I had made it while getting ready, and that was enough reason to keep it with the other scraps rather than smuggle it into my chosen question.

Even then, I wanted to buy a more respectable drawing folder. The one I owned supported the paper. Its corners weren't broken. It merely wasn't handsome. I pressed the sheets flat, fastened them, then loosened them to check. Nothing fell out. I could use it tomorrow.

When I opened the channel, Lumi asked what I'd been doing. Packing luggage for an hour, I told her.

“Such a short journey requires an overnight stay?”

“I've already withdrawn most of it.”

I explained the final selection: the old bridge, three clean sheets, pencils and eraser. Was the bridge the one I'd worked on Sunday? Yes. She knew because I'd described those changes then, not because she could inspect the folder.

Why that one? I read the question aloud. Lumi didn't answer it immediately. Was tomorrow's facilitator teaching that subject in particular? I didn't know. I could ask. Otherwise, I'd draw something else.

“One question, then. Not a list that has to be repaired.”

“Yes.”

Laughing, I admitted I'd written a small list and dragged a neighboring painting into it. What happened to those words? Crossed out. They remained on the other side of the small sheet, but I wouldn't start by reciting them tomorrow.

“You can turn the useful side outward.”

“Already done.”

She laughed at how easily tonight's advice could be implemented. One day, I said, it was good to hear a useful suggestion that didn't require further work. We stopped inspecting every item. I put the folder by my bag and sat down again.

Would I like to continue the fourth story? Lumi asked. Yes. I wanted to discover what had happened to the gloves.

I fetched the thin collection, found the bookmark, and checked the preceding lines. The visitor was carrying the scarf toward the desk, having turned back to ask where the gloves might be. They had been directed to ask there. I established that place aloud, then read the next passage: the visitor reached the desk and handed over the scarf. Their own gloves remained missing.

There was less left than I'd expected. The person on duty placed the scarf in the lost-property box and asked whether the visitor wished to leave contact details. At first, the visitor refused and prepared to leave. Then they returned to write a note. Describing the gloves’ color, they realized they weren't certain whether it counted as dark blue or black.

I smiled while reading. Lumi waited until the paragraph ended to ask whether a wrong color might prevent them ever being found. Let's hear the rest, I said.

The story didn't find them. The visitor wrote another detail they remembered and handed over the paper. Outside, someone carried a full basket. The visitor held the door for them, then went away. The final sentence didn't describe the next day or guarantee the gloves’ return.

I turned the page and established that the next heading began another story before telling Lumi we'd finished.

“It really stops there?”

“There.”

I was a little dissatisfied too. We'd followed the search past so many chairs for such a small object. In the end, there was only an additional note. Lumi wanted to know the final distinguishing detail. I looked back and read carefully. A loose thread, not the hole I'd casually assumed.

Quietly, she said that meant the visitor had actually looked at the gloves, rather than merely wanting an object back. I almost carried that thought toward tomorrow's drawing table and knowing which question to ask. Then I stopped. We were discussing a story. Every page didn't need to prepare my next day for me.

Did she like the ending? A little, and it annoyed her a little too. Not every missing thing deserved to be written so peacefully. Laughing, I said I'd wanted an author's supplementary explanation. If there were one, she might be more annoyed, she said. The visitor had already left.

We hadn't reached the same assessment. I closed the book, moving the bookmark to the end of the fourth story without beginning the fifth. I'd wanted finishing it to remove something left hanging. The gloves remained somewhere beyond the story. The reading had genuinely ended.

Had I decided about tomorrow's dinner? Lumi asked. I'd eat something before the library, then see whether I was hungry afterward. She didn't produce a menu. Don't hurry to a seat so eagerly that my stomach spoke on behalf of the pencil, she said. I laughed and assured her I'd eaten today too. Packing a folder hadn't replaced dinner.

Before good night, I told her I was looking forward to it and still nervous. Both could be said without one of us deleting half. She wanted to hear whatever I chose to tell her afterward, she said. We didn't need to agree tonight how many people's names I ought to remember.

I understood. We said good night without rehearsing tomorrow's hour into a second hour tonight.

After closing the channel, I put the question sheet behind the bridge study and packed the pencils and eraser. The bag wasn't heavy enough for moving house. The old drawings remaining on the table returned to their places. None had become worse because I hadn't selected it.
