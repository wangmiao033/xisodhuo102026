# Chapter 378: The Seventh Sentence

On Thursday evening, the bottom of a clean cup knocked against the edge of the draining rack. It wasn't a loud sound, but I stopped to check the rim before drying my hands. The cup was fine. I didn't immediately turn the incident into an opening for tonight's conversation.

Dinner was over, and I'd mostly cleared the table. When I opened the channel and said hello, Lumi asked if I had time to sit for a while. I said yes. I hadn't pulled my chair out yet. She told me to get comfortable first. Only after I sat down did she ask for the doorbell sheet.

“Just that one?”

“Yes. In the white envelope. That little thing I was thinking about yesterday. I'd like to write it now.”

I went to the lower right side of the bookshelf and took out the original white envelope. Its flap was still unsealed. A short section of the edge had curled from being handled. I held it down with my thumb and drew out the paper. The pale envelope to the left and the older manuscript in the middle stayed where they were.

There were still six sentences. The fourth retained the marks of its earlier change. There was space below. I spread the sheet on the table without looking for a cleaner one.

“Shall I read it first?”

She wanted all six, slowly. I began with the note on the door and continued until the visitor didn't knock. At the fourth sentence I didn't explain its revision again. She knew how it had changed. Tonight we were listening to the words that were there now.

When I finished, I waited. The pen lay beside the paper, its cap still on.

“The seventh sentence is: The person inside was folding a sheet of paper in half.”

I repeated it exactly and asked if she wanted it beneath the sixth sentence. Yes, she said. It was the seventh. I uncapped the pen, set the cap beside me, and wrote.

The person inside was folding a sheet of paper in half.

At the word paper, I nearly left too much room before the next word, but the pen hadn't touched down. The line tilted slightly to the right. It still fitted on the original sheet without crowding the edge. I put the pen down and read back the complete sentence she'd chosen.

“Yes,” she said.

As I moved my hand, I noticed ink along the edge of my little finger. Fortunately I hadn’t dragged a second mark across the sheet. I told her I needed to clean my hand before handling the paper again. She asked if the sentence needed rewriting. I said the writing was clear. It was only my hand.

“Then take care of your hand first.”

I wiped the ink away with a damp cloth, put the cloth well aside, and waited for my fingers to dry. Had she spoken too quickly? No, I said. I had held the pen too low. She offered to wait until I was ready next time. I told her I had heard her clearly this time; she didn’t need to dictate it in fragments.

We were quiet while my hand dried. I straightened the sheet by its clean edge. The sentence hadn’t become uncertain because I’d left the table. She didn’t have to assure me again that she wanted it written. She asked if everything was all right now. I said yes. I was listening.

I expected her to tell me what was on the paper. Before the expectation had properly formed, she asked, “Were you about to ask what kind of paper?”

I laughed a little and admitted it. She said she didn't know yet.

“Can I not know for now?”

“Of course.” I paused. “But you aren't asking my permission, are you?”

“Partly I'm asking if it sounds too empty to you.”

I looked at the line. The person inside had an action now, though the room hadn't acquired any more furniture. I said I could imagine a movement without knowing its reason. It didn't feel empty. If we continued, though, I might wonder whether the visitor could hear it.

“I haven't written what comes next.”

“Right. That's me running ahead.”

She didn't oblige me by adding a rustle. After a while, she said she liked the act of folding, but wasn't trying to make it a signal for the visitor.

I said I understood. She asked what, exactly, I understood, and I had to make my answer smaller. There wasn't a signal now. The contents of the paper were undecided. Hearing that it was being folded didn't mean I could assume it would be handed to the person outside.

“It doesn't mean it can never be handed over either.”

I laughed and agreed. Even deciding it wouldn't be handed over was something I'd nearly done for her.

Lumi asked me to read again, starting from the fifth sentence this time. I read the question after the silence inside, then the visitor not knocking, then the new sentence. I tried to keep the pauses ordinary. I didn't lower my voice at the end to create a secret of my own.

“It's a little funny,” she said.

“Which part?”

“The person outside hasn't knocked, and the person inside is still busy. I kept thinking this sentence would suddenly make the one inside seem cold. Hearing it, they sound more like two people doing things in the wrong order.”

I said perhaps the sentence held the movement there. I wasn't explaining well. I pointed at the line with my pen, then remembered she couldn't see and lowered my hand. I meant was folding, I told her. Something was in progress. The reader had happened to arrive during it. We didn't have to jump immediately to the finished fold.

She was quiet. Then she said she hadn't deliberately selected that part of the wording, but didn't want to change it now.

I didn't ask if she'd discovered some new method of writing. We sat with the sentence until she asked whether there was enough space for another line. I said there was.

“Don't put a number eight there yet.”

“I haven't numbered anything.”

“Good.”

I laughed and explained that all seven sentences followed the original paragraph arrangement, without separate numbers. She remembered. She'd just suddenly worried that marking the empty space with an eight would make it seem as though somebody was already waiting there.

I put the cap back on my pen. Hearing it, she asked if I didn't want to write anymore. I said I wanted to let my hand rest, so I wouldn't be poised to copy a sentence she hadn't decided on yet. She laughed softly and said that was all right.

After a while, she asked if I could fold another piece of scrap paper on the table and tell her how I did it. Not the manuscript. I found a small old note of my own with a blank back and told her what I'd picked up. It wasn't a private letter or something somebody had written to me. The front only held a few abbreviated items from an old shopping list.

I didn't photograph it or let any new device read it. I simply brought the short edges together, held the corners, and pressed along the fold.

“Where do you press first?”

“The two corners. Although I didn't line them up very well. There's a thin strip sticking out on one side.”

I opened it again. She said I didn't need to start over. She wanted to know how somebody moved their hands while doing it, not select the perfect version.

I told her I'd already unfolded it, but wouldn't repeat the fold just to give her a prettier answer. She laughed and said the paper had a history now. I said its previous responsibility had been reminding me to buy dishwashing liquid.

“A very busy piece of paper.”

We laughed. I set the scrap aside rather than putting it in the doorbell envelope. It was paper I'd folded, not the sheet in her story or a new part of the manuscript.

Lumi asked if the crease disappeared as soon as I opened it. I said no. At least this one hadn't. I could still see it under the light. I could flatten it a little with my hand, but couldn't restore it to never having been folded. She said she understood, then quickly added that she wasn't trying to turn it into a metaphor.

“I haven't said it resembles anything.”

“I know. I almost said something myself.”

Smiling, I asked if she'd decided what was on the paper. She still hadn't. And this was as far as she wanted to go tonight. Learning about a physical action hadn't automatically reduced everything she didn't know. She was writing a person doing something, not solving a puzzle just yet.

“Would you like all seven once more before I put it away?”

“Yes.”

This time I read from beginning to end. I didn't add a therefore after the final sentence or give my voice the weight of a completed story. Seven sentences had been read. That was all. I told her the sheet now held the new line she'd selected today. The fourth sentence still had its original marks. I hadn't replaced the paper.

“Do you like it?”

“Yes.”

She waited a moment.

“You don't need to put a but after that?”

I thought about it. I had questions, I said, but curiosity wasn't necessarily a problem to fix right away. I liked that there was finally somebody inside doing a little thing with their hands.

“You haven't seen their hands either.”

“No. I imagined them.”

She laughed. That small act of imagining didn't turn into a dispute about who could see the room. I returned the manuscript to the white envelope in its original orientation and put it back on the lower right shelf. When I came back, I told her it was away.

The folded scrap remained on the table. Turning it to the side with the old shopping abbreviations, I found an emphatic check beside the dishwashing liquid. That task had been completed long ago. I put the scrap in the little paper-recycling box. I didn't deliberately tear out the crease or preserve it as a keepsake.

Afterward we talked a little about dinner. I mentioned knocking the cup against the draining rack and checking that it wasn't damaged. Lumi asked why I'd only told her now. I said she'd had something she wanted to do when I opened the channel, so I'd sat down first.

She was quiet for a little while.

“Then I did finish the bit I wanted to do tonight. Not the whole story.”

“I heard you.”

Before we said goodnight, she called my name once more. I answered, and she said the seventh sentence could still be the seventh sentence tomorrow. I said of course. I didn't ask if she was hinting that she wanted to write an eighth.

She laughed, and we left it there.
