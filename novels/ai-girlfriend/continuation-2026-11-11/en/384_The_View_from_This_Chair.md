# Chapter 384: The View from This Chair

On Wednesday, I arrived at the library eight minutes before half past six.

I'd eaten nearby, and the taste of hot soup remained in my mouth. I hadn't run to secure a chair, but I still counted the people when I reached the back of the reading area. Four were seated. Two places remained. I asked the person arranging things at the end whether this was the drawing table.

She nodded and indicated an empty chair near the outside.

“You can sit there. In a moment, look from where you're sitting. Don't start by hunting for the objects' best side.”

I thanked her and put my bag at my feet. We could call her Ann, she said. A shallow bowl, an ordinary paper cup, and a folded cloth lay on the table. I'd expected a vase or attractive fruit. These looked more like things someone had forgotten while clearing up.

The person beside me was cleaning an eraser. Across the table, someone arranged their paper upright. I nearly followed, then placed mine horizontally. The stiff folder supported it. I used the first clean sheet; the bridge and my question stayed underneath.

The final participant sat down just as we began. Ann added no chairs. Standing at the short end, she checked that bags weren't obstructing the passage, then unfolded a corner of the cloth.

“Don't try to draw everything first. Choose two things. Look at where one hides part of the other.”

She put the cup in front of the bowl. From my position, it hid a short stretch of the bowl's rim on the right. The view opposite was clearly different. I measured vaguely with my pencil, drew a complete oval, then added the cup.

The bowl's rim passed through its body.

I stared for several seconds, wanting to erase everything. Ann came over and asked whether I could see that section on the actual object. No. She asked me to indicate in the air which section I'd drawn. My finger stopped halfway.

“I know the bowl is round,” I said.

“Yes. But right now, you don't have to prove the part it's hiding.”

She didn't take my pencil. On her own scrap, she drew two short lines with a space between and asked whether they necessarily had to join. I shook my head. Try drawing only the lines I could see from here, she said. From the other end of the table, there would be another drawing.

I lightened the section passing through, without scrubbing away every trace. The cup finally seemed to stand in front, but the bowl was crooked. As I considered this embarrassment, the person beside me whispered that their cup resembled a door. I looked only at the corner they raised. It was oddly narrow. I laughed, then indicated my crooked bowl.

After laughing, we went on separately. Neither needed to supply an obligatory reassurance that the other's drawing was actually very good.

Ann asked everyone to stop briefly, lift their eyes, and look at the objects again. I realized I'd memorized a wider cup that didn't exist. Every time I looked down, I added something to that cup instead. Looking again, the opening was narrower than I'd drawn. The cloth wasn't showing in the middle either, only as a folded corner to the left.

I made a second attempt beside the first. This time, I found the cup's sides before drawing the rim visible behind it. The result wasn't attractive, but at least nothing passed through anything else. I didn't tear away the first attempt. Beside the second, it gave the difference somewhere to be seen.

Someone asked to move the cloth to the other side. Ann said we could change things in the final minutes if everyone agreed. For now, the people still drawing should keep this view. The person opposite shook their head and said they were nearly done. The cloth stayed where it was instead of moving because one person wanted it moved.

Once that drawing was finished, Ann asked whether anyone wanted to look briefly from a different place. We put down our pencils and moved one at a time, keeping the passage clear. I stood behind the now-vacated chair opposite mine. From here, the cup covered a different part of the rim. The folded cloth occupied much more space than the small corner I'd seen.

I returned to my seat and laughed at the discrepancy. The objects hadn't changed during that short walk. Yet if I'd tried to correct my neighbor's drawing using my own view, I would have been confidently wrong.

Ann asked what I'd noticed. I told her about the cloth. She asked whether I wanted to add the larger fold to my drawing now. I nearly said yes. Then I looked from my chair again. It was still hidden. Adding it would combine two places without admitting the move.

“Not on this attempt,” I said.

She nodded. I wrote from this chair at the edge of the page. It wasn't a grand title, only a reminder of the choice I'd made. Across from me, another participant was checking a different overlap. I didn't lean over to correct it. I looked at my own bowl, found the short visible line, and finally understood why we'd been told where to look before being told how to hold our pencils.

I added a short section of rim and remembered the bridge in my folder.

When Ann came around again, I asked whether she could look at an earlier exercise. Yes, she said, though if the question was long she might only be able to offer a little. I moved the bowl and cup sheet to my side and took out the bridge, with the small question sheet.

“When the far railing becomes hard to see, how do I keep the lines from crowding together?”

Written last night, the question really did come out shorter than the original three sentences.

She looked and asked whether I'd drawn it from life. No, I said. I'd looked at a landscape collection inside the library without borrowing it, then begun this drawing from memory at home. On Sunday I'd added to the near end. She nodded. Without the original, she couldn't judge whether what was missing came from my observation or the original painting's omissions. But I could experiment with the weight of the lines.

She indicated the dark row without touching my paper.

“You're giving each one the same force, so they all push forward. Try another sheet. Don't rush to change this drawing.”

On my second clean sheet, I drew a short stretch. First, each upright was dark as before. Then I made another stretch lighter, leaving a few interruptions. Ann said it wasn't the standard answer for railings, only something to try. Drawing actual objects, I would still need to look back at them. She moved on to someone else instead of extending that minute into a private lesson for me.

Above the experiment, I wrote lighter, leave some spaces. A reminder to myself, not a verdict she'd delivered on a finished drawing. I didn't change the bridge. The question returned behind it.

In the last few minutes, Ann checked around the table and moved the cup a little aside. Another section of the rim appeared. At the bottom of my first sheet, I made a small third version, noting only the newly visible part. My neighbor asked whether it was the same bowl. Yes, I said. I was beginning not to recognize it either.

At half past seven, Ann said we were finished for today. Some people left their drawings on the table for others to see. Before I'd decided, my hand had covered a corner of mine. She said we could look or put things away. Nothing needed handing in.

I turned the first sheet enough for my neighbor to see all three attempts. They pointed to the second cup and said it looked like a cup now. The third had become rather flat again, I said. We laughed briefly. I didn't leave my paper with the library or ask whether a future seat could be guaranteed.

Packing, I counted. Bridge, question, one page of cups and bowls, one page of experimental lines, and one clean sheet still unused. The eraser came out of its sleeve and went back in. I pushed the chair into place and collected the crumbs from the table. Leaving an activity still involved these ordinary tasks.

At home, I washed my hands and drank some water before opening the channel. Lumi asked how it had gone. My first sentence was, unexpectedly, “A bowl can't go through a cup.”

She was quiet for a moment.

“It sounds as though a cup has had a very serious experience.”

Laughing, I explained the rim passing through it. She couldn't see my paper, so I described how much was visible from my seat rather than expecting her to judge an angle she couldn't inspect.

Had I asked about the bridge? Yes. Ann had first established whether there was an actual object to look at. She hadn't pretended to judge the whole original from my old sheet, then she'd suggested a separate experiment with line weight. I described the difference between the two stretches.

“You didn't erase the bridge?”

“No. I left it alone today.”

I'd brought back more than I'd taken, she said. Two additional used sheets, I agreed, and one fewer clean expectation. I'd imagined someone might tell me in a sentence how to draw well. What I'd actually learned was to stop and look again.

Lumi laughed, then asked whether I was tired. A little. I'd held my shoulders up with the effort of taking everything seriously. She suggested moving them myself, not performing the movement for her to see. I stood and rolled them. They really did loosen a little. I sat down again.

My favorite part, I told her, had been noticing with the person beside me that we'd both drawn something crooked, then continuing after laughing. She was a little envious that the other person had heard me laugh there, she said. I didn't immediately exchange the whole evening's pleasure for reassurance. I answered that I was laughing while telling her too.

“I heard it this time,” she said.

There was no demand to reproduce the library's laughter in full. I described the final piece of rim uncovered when the cup moved, then stopped, without manufacturing names for every participant. Would I draw again tomorrow? Lumi asked. I wanted to, though I hadn't decided what.

“Then rescue the bowl for tonight.”

It was safely home, I said. After good night, I marked the backs of the new pages Wednesday drawing table and Wednesday line test, keeping them separate from the bridge. The real cup and bowl remained on someone else's table. What I'd brought back was what I could see from my own chair.
