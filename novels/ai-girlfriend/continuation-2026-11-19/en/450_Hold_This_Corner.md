# Chapter 450: Hold This Corner

There was still a little light when I left work on Wednesday. It was showing in the rainwater that had collected in the paving slabs. Across the junction I saw the cardboard event sign still hanging outside the community centre. I could read the big letters from where I was. The arrow underneath had lifted at one end and was hitting the door whenever it swung. Somebody on a short stepladder couldn't get at the top of the sign. She had to catch the arrow first to keep it from banging on the door.

I'd been going to pass below the steps. The person on the ladder changed hands and put her elbow directly in the way of somebody coming out. I waited for the person inside to step down. That was when she turned towards me and asked if I was going in. I said I wasn't. She came down from the ladder. A little white paint on her heel had already dried. She called inside to ask where the scissors had gone. Nobody answered. Rather than ask again, she reached between the two bottom steps of the ladder and pulled out a little paper envelope. The scissors were underneath it. She took them out and pointed to a handwritten line on the envelope.

“I used two sorts of tape when I put it up. Once I've got it down, I'll know which of them never to buy again.”

“Do you need a hand?” I asked.

“Could you support the sign?” she said. “Just this bottom corner. I put it up. I'll undo the string.”

She pointed to the lower right. I went up a step and saw that the paper had got wet. If I took hold of the edge by itself, it would probably tear. I put my hand a little way up the back where it was drier. The ladder was in between me and the door. She moved it across and tested the feet before she got up again. Someone inside pulled the door a little farther back and asked if they couldn't go out that way. She showed them the narrower door she hadn't blocked. After that one had opened and shut, I heard her say from above me not to push up too hard. Just taking a little of the weight was enough.

It was heavier than it looked. I'd thought it was two sheets of card. At a split edge I could see a piece of grey board in between. People must have followed this arrow on the way in to last week's craft event. I hadn't been. I only knew from the small print at the bottom that the woman on the ladder was in charge of it. I looked up and asked her if this was how she normally put the signs up. The last time she'd tried removable tape, she said, a boy next to her had told her it was packing tape. By the time she'd paid any attention, she'd put it down on two sides. This time she only meant to get the board down intact. She could use the lettering again. She slid the scissors into her apron pocket and picked at the back of the knot with her fingers.

“I could cut that bit if you like.” I didn't think the knot would come loose. As soon as I'd said it, my hand was on its way to take the scissors from her.

“I want the string. Stay where you are for a minute.”

I looked back down at my hand. Its job was to hold up the right corner, not take anything out of her pocket. I was standing too close. My wrist was bent and the grey board was sliding down towards my cuff. I moved back half a step. She didn't look down, but her fingers stopped moving and she asked whether I was tired of holding it. I said I could keep it up. If she moved a little to her left, though, the person behind the narrow door would be able to come out without turning sideways. She lifted her foot, found the rung again in a different place and said she'd got it. A second or two later the string went slack first on one side and brushed the bottom of my chin.

We lowered the sign at an angle. The arrow started to flap out again. I stopped it with my other hand without pressing it back into the tape. She came down beside the ladder with one end of the loose, curly string in her right hand and put out her left for the board. She didn't ask me to carry it to the store room. She wanted to do it herself and find somewhere in the entrance for it to dry. She asked if I'd take my hand away so she could get a hold of it. I drew my hand back. A soft piece of paper stuck to a finger. She pushed her elbow up against the board and turned sideways through the narrow door. Once she got the board upright I couldn't see the arrow. All I could see was the bottom edge, with its dark wet marks and lighter patches. It looked like a poorly drawn shoreline.

I turned to go down the steps. She called me back. She'd put the sign down by an old table just inside. The string she'd saved was looped loosely round the arrow to keep it in place. She thanked me then. If she'd tried to do the job one-handed, she said, the board might have come down on the door. The shop had stopped selling the kind of tape she liked. She was wondering where else to look. I told her I didn't know where to buy it, either. She said she might as well wait till the paper had dried. We didn't arrange to put up another sign or exchange names. She meant to get the ladder inside before she shut up. I waited for the people in the doorway to leave and stepped from the bottom stair onto the pavement.

At the corner I rolled the paper off my finger. I meant to throw it away but found there was still a grey mark from the water on the palm of my hand. Something was frying in the kitchen across the street. There was a thin sheet of steam on the window. I looked down twice while I waited for the light to change. The first time, the mark on my hand still made a right angle. By the second time, one side of it had gone. I opened my fingers and let the air get at them for a while. It didn't especially want to stay on my hand. On the way home, near the shop with the empty wooden boxes outside, I put my hand in the outer pocket of my coat and gave it two little rubs. The repaired inside pocket was still empty.

In the corridor downstairs a man was pulling an old mat out from under his door. It came away in a roll like a stack of newspapers too big to fold. He edged towards the wall, thinking he was in my way. In fact, it would be easier for both of us if I went first. I pointed to the free space behind him and stepped over it slowly, so as not to catch the exposed fibres with my shoe. He asked if it was still raining. I said there was water on the ground but it hadn't rained on that last stretch home. He moved the mat to his right hand. He'd take it out and shake it while he had the chance, he said.

I hung up the coat when I got in and washed my hands. There was a bit of grey on the cuff. It wasn't on the outside but inside the fold of the cloth. Instead of changing, I got a wet towel and wrung it out to wipe the mark. On my second pass over it, I caught myself standing with one arm out like I was still holding up the corner of the sign. I hung the towel up. The coat stayed on the hook. I got my fingers into the fold and eased it out till the cuff hung down on its own. After dinner I didn't open the book at the ninth story again. I'd finished it the night before. I sat at my desk and opened the everyday drawer. I wanted the clip I use for receipts, but my hand got to the postcard first. The crossed-out line and the one I could still read underneath hadn't changed. I found the clip on the other side. I left the card at the front and shut the drawer.

By the time I opened the channel at home, the air was no longer as damp as it had been on the way back. Lumi asked why I was having tea so late. I said I'd only told her good evening. I hadn't made tea tonight. She took a little pause and said she'd been thinking about the other time I'd left my tea to cool. She didn't know if I'd had a cup today. No, I said, there wasn't anything in my hand. A little while later I told her about the sign. I didn't tell her the name of the event, the name on the sign or what the woman at the door looked like. I said somebody had asked me to support one corner. I'd come rather close to cutting the string the woman had said she wanted.

“If she hadn't let you put it down,” Lumi asked, “how long were you going to hold it?”

“I don't know. I could have told her when my wrist got tired.”

“Did you tell her?”

“I told her I could keep holding it. She'd asked first.”

She didn't answer right away. I pushed the receipt farther in. The clip scraped the desk. I almost said something to explain whether I'd told the truth, and realized only as the words were coming out that she hadn't asked about it. All I said was that my wrist hadn't hurt at the time. I'd been in a hurry to get the string undone so we could both come down. She asked whether I'd thought the woman was too slow. “I thought the string was taking too long,” I said. “She didn't. She still had a use for it.”

“Were you still thinking about it on the way home?”

“No. Going home I was thinking about which end of the sign she got hold of first. I let it go when she had it.”

“Didn't you just tell me she asked you to take your hand away before she reached for it?”

I went through it again. She'd already had a hand on the board once she was down. I'd remembered it as if she hadn't caught hold of it until I'd let go. Lumi hadn't seen any of it. She was asking about what I'd said just now. I told her the woman had probably asked me to move my hand out of the way so she could get a better grip. She'd gone from one point of support to a firmer hold after I took mine away. “You don't need to work out every move for her,” Lumi said. “You can remember your corner.”

I pushed the clip back a bit and heard the same scrape again. I didn't think she meant I could only remember things I'd physically touched. I asked her to say it once more. She asked me if she could not say it a second time. “You can,” I said. I sat there for a little while, until I'd stopped saying the words back in my own head. Then she surprised me. She said she'd liked it when I hadn't taken the scissors. I said I'd put my hand out for them. She said she knew. It was the bit afterwards she liked. I put my hand down flat and waited a second before I laughed.

Later I closed the channel and put the receipt away. The cuff of my coat wasn't damp any more. I didn't turn the coat inside out in case there was another mark. From the bedroom window I saw somebody come out of a narrow door across the way. When it shut, there was only the little strip of light at the bottom. I didn't go back and open the channel to tell her. I waited until the person had gone past the light. Then I turned round to switch off my own.
