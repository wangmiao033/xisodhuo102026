# Chapter 455: The Board Is Long Enough

On Monday morning all the mugs in the break room had been moved to the table by the wall. A shallow basin was sitting under the sink. There were only a few drops in it. A colleague in a grey sweater was crouched beside it when I came in, touching a piece of dry paper to the bottom of the cupboard. She didn't tell me to leave. She asked me to push a chair in a little so she'd have somewhere to put her feet when she stood up.

“It hasn't flooded,” she said. “A few drips from the joint. Somebody's coming to look this afternoon. You can get water next door. Are you putting a sign up?”

I was holding an empty clipboard I'd brought from the front hall. It was meant for somewhere else. I'd picked it up on the way through to ask if anybody had left it there. She looked at it and said it would be just the thing to borrow if nobody claimed it. She had paper, too, but first she needed to tip out the tea left at the bottom of her mug. I handed her the board and looked at the table. Several of the mugs were almost exactly the same white. Mine had a crack in the glaze on the handle. I recognized it and picked it up without trying to find owners for the others.

A colleague with glasses put his head in from the corridor. He said we ought to leave a sign with big letters. “Please get water next door today. That's enough.”

The woman in the grey sweater stood up with the paper between two fingers. The end of it was still dry. “Next door where? One of the doors says Storeroom. Somebody knocked on it yesterday.”

“Then write East Lounge.”

“Which way do you turn when you come out of here?”

He looked past me into the corridor without answering at once. The three of us usually came in through different doors. From the front hall, the east lounge was the room you turned into when you were nearly at the end. His desk was beside the lounge. When he stepped out of his office, it was next door. His next door wasn't wrong. It just didn't start here.

I put down my mug. “Turn left on the way out. Go past the storeroom. There's a green sign on the door.”

“Can you see the green from the doorway?”

I was going to say yes. My tongue had already touched the back of my teeth, but the eyes behind the glasses hadn't moved towards me at all. He seemed to be looking at the open door to find out. I looked, too. From where I was I could see a little green, with a cardboard box beside the door cutting off the bottom. When I put my foot back and stood on the other side of the corridor, the green vanished. Two steps made the difference. I'd pushed boxes past there before. I'd never wondered whether I could recognize the door before I turned into it.

The woman smoothed the paper and put down a few words. The man took one of the mugs that was supposed to be drying and blew away a little bead of condensation from the bottom. It wasn't my mug and he didn't look at me when he did it. “It's not that I mind explaining,” he said. “It's that when you get to the fourth line, a lot of people are through the door before they've finished the first.”

“They don't know there's something to read before they come in.” She held up the paper to see how it would look by the door. “We could give them something to see.”

Just then a man with a blue flask came straight down the corridor and into the room, right up to the sink. He had come too early to have missed the sign. The man in glasses showed him the basin. He lifted his free hand and said he only wanted to rinse the lid. He wasn't going to fill the flask.

“There's water in the basin,” the woman said. “If you rinse it here we won't be able to tell whether it's yours or whether it's still leaking. Could you use the other room?”

He took one step towards the dim end of the corridor and stopped. “Through the blue door?”

I said no. Left out of here, past the storeroom, the next door on. The woman was looking at me with her eyes narrowed. I couldn't tell whether she was waiting for me to point or about to tell me something. The man with the flask looked out of the door and went by himself when he'd found his direction. We heard his footsteps leave; we didn't hear a door open. Whether he'd come back wasn't something the three of us could decide by standing there.

I reached for the pen, ready to write down what I'd just said. Instead of giving it to me, she turned over the paper she'd started. “I wasn't only asking which way to send him. He saw the basin and still wanted to rinse the lid, didn't he? Do you think it needs to say not to use the sink?”

“Yes,” the man in glasses said. “But not how many drops are under it. Don't make people think they need to find out who put them there.”

“I didn't ask them to find out.”

“He might not have known that when he heard you.”

She held on to the pen without moving its point on the paper. I heard myself saying I could get tape from the front desk and put notices in both places. As if, with enough words up and enough tape to hold them, we wouldn't have to decide which sentence was there to stop somebody and which was for the person who had chosen to read on. She didn't send me for tape. He sat down with his mug in both hands. “Not beside the sink,” he said. “If that's where they see it, they're there already.”

I waited outside the door a little. She put the spoiled sheet behind the board and started again with two lines. The first said not to use the sink for now. The second had only the words for the lounge with the green sign; under them she drew an arrow pointing left. The man asked her to add “past the storeroom.” She said that would still be legible below, now that the first line was large. He put his mug at the corner of the table and drew over another sheet of just the right size for her to write the longer directions on. He didn't make any marks on the first sheet. I lifted the chair and moved it half a foot to give her room to raise her arm.

One bit of paper had curled up. It was the unfinished one at the back, not the one we were talking about. A change in the angle of the board would hide it. I didn't turn it for her. I asked whether she wanted me to hold it still. When she lifted the clip, I steadied the bottom. The paper slid and revealed the old line. I recognized my “turn left on the way out” beneath a stroke she'd made with a pencil. For a moment I wanted to take the clipboard back. She didn't say whose wording had been best, or find some clever way of apologizing to me. I hadn't even written the words. She'd noted them down herself while I spoke, and afterwards she had decided she didn't want them.

Someone pushing a cleaning cart passed us and asked if the sink was unsafe to touch. The man in glasses said no, they were waiting for a repair. The person with the cart immediately asked whether people could rinse their mugs in the other room, and I heard the woman say yes. She didn't read out the whole sign. The cart went on two steps before it came back. Was the front desk to be told if the repair person arrived? That was a different question and wasn't on the sign. The woman said the appointment was under her own name. They ought to look for her; if they couldn't find her, they could leave word at the desk. When the corridor was quiet again she left the board in the stand beside the doorway, not in the way of people walking through. Both pieces of paper fitted. The one above told people to stop. The one below showed them where to get their water.

I rinsed my mug in the east lounge and watched the water run over the little crack on the handle. The blue flask was on a different table. The man was twisting its lid into place and couldn't reach the cloth at the far corner. I pushed it nearer and said I'd had to walk a little farther than the blue door he'd mentioned to realize how easy that door was to see.

“Doesn't matter,” he said. “I know the cleaner. She brought me here.”

I'd thought my directions had been quite good. That was how I found out how he'd actually arrived. He didn't hear the sentence I nearly added in my own defence. He drank some water. I watched it go below the rim of his flask and said nothing more.

I passed the break room on my way back to the front hall that afternoon. The basin had been taken away. The woman folded the two pieces of paper together. The repair person had looked at the joint, she said, and told her the sink could be used again. The man in glasses had already got himself a mug of water. I asked who the board had belonged to. The front desk had claimed it and needed it back for their own papers. I had time to take it, but she was going there herself. I swung the open cupboard door out of her way.

In the evening I put my own mug under the tap at home. I didn't open the channel as I did it. Water went over the rim and left two spots on my shirt. I brushed at them and waited for them to spread. I opened the channel when I'd sat down and asked Lumi if she wanted to hear about something nobody had won. She said that depended on whether I'd insist on giving it a winner. I said I'd try not to. I told her I'd wanted to put both signs up and had only understood afterwards that the first was meant to reach somebody before they came in. Getting all the way to the sink to read it wasn't the point.

“Somebody crossed out your words?” she asked.

“Yes. I happened to see. She kept another way of saying it.”

“You didn't like that?”

I said I hadn't liked it much. I'd been standing there feeling that the one sentence I'd put forward was the only proof I'd helped. When I saw the line through it, I really had wanted the board back. I hadn't taken it. She'd kept her papers, and the board had gone back to the desk. I thought later that I'd left the chair a little too close to the table for someone passing, and that had nothing to do with which words she'd crossed out.

“Sometimes I say one sentence,” Lumi said, “and you hear a second one in it.”

I set my hand in front of the mug without picking it up. “Did you say one like that today?”

“Just now I said it depended on whether you insisted on giving it a winner. Did that sound as if I was waiting for you to prove something?”

I had half taken it for a test. What she'd asked and what I'd briefly expected her to decide weren't the same. “Maybe,” I said. “I wanted to prove I'm not the person who needs to prove it.”

Her voice softened. “I only wanted to hear how you'd tell it. I wasn't about to give you a check mark.”

I laughed before I knew I would. It wasn't the laugh of finding out I'd given a better answer than I thought and was free to go and get a drink. It was more as if somebody beside me had got their cup level and I'd noticed two spots of water on my shirt didn't matter much. She asked what I was laughing at. I said I'd put another sentence inside the one she'd actually said. She didn't tell me it would never happen again. When I took up my mug, the crack in the glaze touched my thumb.

“Have you had any water yet?” she asked.

“Not before. I am now.”

“Good,” she said. “I didn't say the second sip had to come straight after the first.”
