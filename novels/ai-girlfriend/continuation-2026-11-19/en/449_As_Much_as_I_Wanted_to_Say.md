# Chapter 449: As Much as I Wanted to Say

Just before lunch on Tuesday, someone put an unwashed spoon in the jar with the clean ones. By the time I pulled it out, the blue on the back had started to run down onto my finger. I couldn't tell whether it was jam or ink. I put it under my nose. Nothing. The woman from the next desk was coming across with her lunch box. She found me standing over the sink and asked if I'd spilled my lunch.

“I haven't had a chance to eat it yet.” I set the spoon on the edge of the sink.

“That's lucky. I mashed my blueberries with that. You haven't put it in your mouth, have you?”

I shook my head. She took the spoon, washed it twice and rubbed the back of it with her thumb. There was still a thin blue ring in the sink. I'd just got hold of the cloth when she filled her cup and rinsed it away. I took a different spoon from the jar. She didn't tell me how she'd come to put a dirty one in there until we sat down together. Somebody had got down on the floor to look for a lid under the fridge. She'd been watching them and put the two things in her hands down in the wrong places. The lid of her own lunch box was still missing. She'd covered the box with a side plate. When she took the plate off, the rice gave out a little cloud of steam between us.

I'd brought what was left of that half loaf. I'd tried to cut myself another couple of slices before lunch and found that the extra day or two had made it easier, if anything. One corner stuck out on the thicker slice. I broke it halfway off with my fingers and dipped the rest in the soup. She asked if it was the bread I'd been buying a lot lately. I said the last time I'd bought half a loaf and this was still that same half. She looked down into my paper bag and said that explained it.

“Explained what?”

“Why you could practically see the bottom of your soup but you're still holding the bread.”

I had been stirring the soup with my bread again, and not eating any of it. When I lifted my hand, the wet corner caught on the bag and drew a dark streak down it. I folded the bag in on itself and put a piece of paper underneath. She pushed the stack of napkins towards me. I took the top one and picked up two fallen grains of rice from the middle of the table on the way back. She saw me. “Those are mine. Pick up any more and I'm going to go hungry.”

There were a few minutes afterwards when nobody needed the long bench against the wall. I'd left my book on one end of it before lunch, spine turned in. Nobody had put anything on top of it. I went back to the same spot. The end of the eighth story was still before the bookmark. I drew it out and laid it across my knee, keeping the page open with the heel of my hand against the air coming through the crack in the window. The ninth story began with somebody taking a basket of empty bottles up a hill. It wasn't foggy there. Nobody was after him. He was afraid that if he got back late the sun would already be off the two washing lines in his yard. When people called to him he would say he was there, but he wouldn't let his feet stop.

I hadn't found out who the bottles were for when the door at the far end of the corridor made two little knocks. The woman at the desk put her head out to ask if anybody was sitting next to me. She was looking for a seat for somebody with a bad leg. I moved my book and my lunch bag away and cleared half the bench. Nobody came through the door. I waited a little while and lifted the paper bag from beside my shoe onto my knees. I'd got to the middle of a sentence. The soft paper on either side of the book folded up to meet me. I had to catch my lunch bag with the other hand. By the time I looked down, I couldn't tell which word my eyes had stopped on. The woman called out a little later that they'd found a higher chair and were going round to the back. The space beside me had stayed empty. I didn't put the lunch bag back down until the door shut.

On that page the man carrying the bottles stopped for the first time because there was a purple shirt held down by a stone on a step. He thought the owner had put it there to dry and went to pick it up. Somebody inside the house told him to leave it. When he said he'd come with the bottles, the voice told him not to put the basket in the doorway. It didn't ask him to take the shirt away. By the time he'd been round and round in front of the house he had lost the sunlight he was after. And it wasn't even the yard he thought he'd come to.

I closed the book with my hand across the front and leaned back a little too sharply. The back of my head caught the wall. If he'd asked where he was going in the first place, I thought, he could have saved himself half a page. The woman sorting paper cups across from me stopped to ask if that had hurt. I said it hadn't. I'd just been sitting too far back. She turned a spare chair towards me and asked if I'd like it. Its back wasn't quite as straight. I said thank you, but I had to be getting back. She didn't turn it round again. Before I left, I slid it back to where she'd first pulled it out. I didn't look for the page in the book again. I knew I'd find it. There was a step at the beginning of that bit about the shirt. I could see it more plainly than the bookmark.

On the way home I stopped two sheets of wrapping paper blown from the flower stall. The seller gathered them under her arm and asked if I'd seen a third. I hadn't. As I went on with both hands empty, I had a moment of thinking I'd left the book on the bench. It was in my bag.

After dinner I sat at my desk and finished the ninth story. The man with the bottles didn't get a story out of the purple shirt. He went back by a different way and came across a woman who was hanging out her washing. She didn't say whether the shirt was hers. She moved the basket he'd put down into the shade and asked him to pick the broken bottle off the top. He held the broken one and wanted to ask if she still wanted the rest. Both her hands were busy, though, with the socks on one side of her and the sheet on the other. He watched until he could see how she'd managed to get the last peg on the sheet, and then he asked. Nobody there called out to see if he was still around. The story stopped with an empty bottle turning halfway over in the basket. It didn't say whether he went home to put out his washing or somebody collected it for him round the corner. I picked up the bookmark and put it after the ninth story. The top half of the next page was blank. I shut the book without starting the tenth.

I opened the home voice channel a little later. She heard me say good evening and took a few seconds to answer. I thought my first word might have been too quiet. I'd already opened my mouth to say it again. I swallowed the second one.

“You didn't come over,” she said.

“I'm at the desk. I haven't come over yet.”

“I meant the second thing you were going to say. You almost said it again.”

“How did you know?”

“I didn't. I just hadn't said anything yet. Sometimes you fill it in before I can.”

I told her she'd guessed right that time. She asked if I wanted to tell her what I'd been doing. I waited till I'd finished smiling and said I'd been reading the book with the eight stories I'd already read in it. I'd finished the ninth today. I sat up a little from the chair back and reached out to turn the desk lamp farther to the side. Its shadow no longer cut across the cover. I could see a rubbed white corner from all the times I'd carried the book about. I found myself hoping she wouldn't ask what the story was about right away. I'd only just finished it. I wasn't ready to set out everything that had happened in order in front of anyone.

“Did you like the ninth one?” she asked.

I said she'd have found me trying to hurry it along at midday. When I finished it after dinner, I realized the thing I'd been waiting for wasn't in it. She asked what thing. That made me laugh out loud. “Are you trying to find out what the story's about?”

“Yes. I want to know what made you impatient, too.”

I only told her there was a man who took something belonging to someone else to be his problem to solve. At one point I'd been ready to take a shorter road for him. I didn't tell her the whole way he'd gone, the lines I'd been reading, or what happened at the end. I didn't feel the need to explain why it was right for that last half turn to stop exactly where it did. She said she wanted to know whether the man eventually went the way I'd wanted him to. I said I'd rather not tell that part yet. She said all right. Then she waited a little before she asked, “Would you rather he'd taken your shortcut, or would you rather have the story as it is?”

“The way it is. I wouldn't have kept thinking about going back to it otherwise.”

“Even if you couldn't find the thing you wanted?”

I got up to reach a cup just beyond my fingertips. The handle slipped and I had to take it with both hands. “I don't know if it was that I couldn't find it,” I said when I had the cup. “It turned out I was the one waiting for it. He wasn't.”

She didn't pick that up and try to explain it. After a while she said she hadn't hoped, when she asked a second time, that I'd hand over the ending. I'd said I wasn't going to tell it and I'd still been there. That was why she'd wanted to ask about what I'd liked instead. I set the cup down a little farther away from the book. The water was cold by then. I said I knew. She made a little questioning sound. Did I really know, or did I only want her to go on talking? I couldn't answer by guessing this time. I gave her the part I did know: she'd been telling me what she'd meant by the question she'd just asked, not asking me to tell her the story tomorrow.

“That's right,” she said. We talked a little about the colour blueberries leave on the back of a spoon. I said it was like water squeezed out of a piece of paper. She thought that was a mean way to put it when I could have said it looked like a drink that hadn't been stirred properly. I said that would make me afraid of ordering something with blueberries in it tomorrow. She laughed. It wasn't as though she could order it for me anyway.

She said good night and I closed the channel. I opened the everyday drawer when I went to put the pen away. The second line on the postcard near the front hadn't got jammed into the corner. The first still had a crooked line through it. I left the card where it was, put the pen back on the desk and shut the drawer. The book was beside the lamp. A little of the bookmark showed. I didn't take it with me when I went to the bedroom. I'd finished that story. I'd open the next one when I wanted to read it.
