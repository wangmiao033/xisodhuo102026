# Chapter 452: The Road That Wasn't Drawn

On Friday afternoon the wind pinned a piece of advertising paper to the wall outside work. The top was loose, but the bottom looked flat against the brick. I lifted it with one hand and the entire thing came away. The bottom edge had been covering a snail shell. I crouched to look and saw a little dry mud in the opening. I didn't know what else was in there. I held the advertisement up so it wouldn't blow down over the shell again and went towards the bin. A line of small print at the bottom caught my eye: return this part to the counter. The advertisement wasn't meant to be at our door. There was an old wet mark across it, the kind a thing gets from being stuck to glass before the glass is dry. I took it to the desk and asked if it belonged to us. There was nothing at work I needed to take home. I didn't particularly want to be late leaving on account of a piece of paper.

The person at the desk read the line. It was an old advertisement for a key-cutting shop nearby. The offer had ended, she said. If I brought it to the shop they'd still have to throw it away themselves. She asked if anything else was stuck to the wall. I told her about the shell and said I hadn't known what to do with it, so I hadn't touched it. She said to leave it there. The advertisement went into the paper bin folded over on itself, and the shell stayed put. I realized afterwards that there had been something pleasant about that. Things that turned up together didn't all have to be taken away by the same person.

A van was backing very slowly outside when I came out. The driver gave me a sign. I thought he was telling me to go, but as my foot moved I saw that he'd put up his hand for me to stop. I did. The back of the van was a long way off. He waited for someone on the other side, gave another clear wave with his hand, and meant me to go this time. I went round and took my usual pavement home. When I reached the old flower shop I thought I'd like to go one street farther out than usual. The stretch behind those shops wasn't the way I'd come or the way I went home every day. I could say it would take a few turns to come out on another road, but if you asked me which turn it was I'd get it wrong. It wouldn't have mattered if I came home a little later. Then the wind got inside the front of my coat. When I reached into the outside pocket for my handkerchief, my fingers found a scrap of paper bag I didn't remember putting there. I hadn't brought anything to eat and I didn't have the map on me. I went home to look at the paper first.

I hung up my coat and shook out the handkerchief. I hadn't turned on the kitchen light; the table had a strip of light from the opening of the door. I pushed one slipper away with my foot and stepped after it. I didn't go straight to the drawer. I put the kettle on. Only after the first little noise of heat at the bottom did I pull open the everyday drawer. The handwritten postcard was still at the front, face up, with the line through its first sentence. I eased the folded corner of the map out beside it. The map had quite a few folds in it and had looked like the side of a little cardboard box where it was tucked in. As it came free it sprang open. I caught it before it could carry the card out with it. The card stayed where it had been. The sentence I'd crossed out hadn't become any easier to make out.

The map took up half the table. I moved the old cup with the last sip left at the bottom before it could put a ring on the paper. The edge bowed up against my wrist. It took a bit of looking to find the road of the old flower shop. Naturally there was no flower shop on the map. I had only the shape of the junction and a pitifully small bit of public green to put beside it. According to the legend the darker green was a park and the lighter green was an open grassed area. The ink was a little uneven there. I bent closer to see which it was. Then I went over the direction I'd been thinking of taking. The map didn't show the diagonal path behind the shops. I'd seen it shut at both ends before. I hadn't gone back there today, and nothing on a piece of paper could swing either gate open for me.

I found a little arrow at the margin, with a wonky N next to its point. I'd been thinking of the wall side of the table as north. My finger was already on its way there. When I looked at the arrow I remembered turning the table around the month I'd moved it. The map hadn't turned with the table. The position in which it was convenient to hold it now had nothing to do with the room. Anyone I sent out with north borrowed from the back of my chair would take the very first corner wrong. I didn't turn the paper around; I bent my head sideways and read the words as they were. The fuzzy names turned into ordinary street names. After all that effort, they had nothing more to tell me than what the printer had put there.

I didn't get a pen. The second line on the card at the front of the drawer was already doing something of its own, the way something without an address could have more than a date written on it. The map didn't need to have a trace of this particular day left on it because I'd opened it. I wasn't trying to make it look unused. The paper had gone pale down one crease, and a hairline of print was missing from two names that crossed it. I pressed the crease flat and it came up again when I moved my hand. Then the kettle started up. I checked the half of the table that didn't have a map on it, put the kettle back and brought over just a cup with a handle. I set it on a dry coaster a whole hand's width from the open paper.

While the water cooled I remembered the geography book I'd returned to the library. When I was reading it I'd often taken one kind of line for another. A book could give me contour lines for a hill so I could guess, before going there, whether the ground under my feet would be steep. The streets on this map didn't tell me that. I'd seen a man take his time getting off a bus, not because the three steps looked higher than other steps but because he didn't find something to hold until the second one. There were only three steps for me to count. I didn't know how many things there'd been for him to count. None of it was on the map. I moved my hand along a legible stretch of river. It made a modest turn, and the street beside it had its name printed again in big letters at the bend. A bridge was marked just north of the turn. I recognized the street name. I'd never been over that bridge. I thought I'd tell Lumi, at first as though I'd found a new way to go somewhere. When I opened the voice channel I put it differently.

“I'm at home,” I said. “I've got an old paper map out on the table. There's a small bridge on it north of a bend in the river. I've never been there.”

“When you said there was a bridge, I thought you were about to ask me how to get to it.”

“No. I don't even know what it's like now.”

“How do you know it's small?”

I looked at the line. It wasn't drawn shorter than other bridges I'd crossed. The river was thin on paper and the line across it looked thin too. “I don't know that it's small,” I said. “It's drawn small.” I'd thought that reading one ordinary thing off a map would be the whole of it, and yet there was the thing I'd seen and the thing I'd added. I had to put them into separate words before I could tell her plainly. She didn't tell me what buses stopped near it or identify the river I'd described. She just asked if I felt like saying the original sentence again. “The river turns on the page,” I said. “There's a mark for a bridge to the north.” I left out the name of the street on purpose. We didn't both have to arrive at this river.

“I liked it better when you said you'd never been,” she said. “Don't leave that part out.”

“I've never been there.” I put my lips to the edge of the cup. The water wasn't so hot now. I took a sip standing up. I didn't hide my words inside the noise of swallowing. “Why did you like it?”

“Because there are places you talk about as if you're going to take me there in the next sentence. I'm glad to hear about them, but sometimes I don't want you to hurry that much. You're still here.”

I didn't move the map. She was right. I was still beside the table. In the afternoon the driver had given me two signs, and I'd mistaken the first one. If I made him into a person who'd shown me where to go when I was telling it at home, I might be getting it wrong a second time. I didn't tell her about him. I said, “I haven't said I'll go, either. Sometimes I turn a thing I'm about to say into a thing I've got to do.”

“I only wanted to hear you say you hadn't been.” She waited a little. “Now I want to hear whether you're going to sit down.”

I sat. It wasn't that she'd somehow seen how long I'd been on my feet and that was why I drew the chair in. I told her I'd sat down. The back of the chair met my shoulder blades. The edge of the patch I'd soaked on my cuff the night before was quite faint now. I'd expected to have a good deal to say once I opened the map. When I'd said just that one thing, I didn't look for another name. I asked if there was anything else she'd like to say. She didn't have a line she felt she had to speak. We agreed to leave it there. She said goodnight, I said it back, and then I closed the channel.

I folded the map along the old creases. When I took my hand away the edge didn't spring out this time. It went into the ordinary drawer beside the handwritten card, without covering the line I'd left showing. I hadn't put another line on the paper. I didn't put it in the bag I used to go out. At last there was room for a cup on the other half of the table again. I finished my water there. As my fingers touched the edge of the closed drawer, I realized I'd spent the entire evening without wondering which bank the bridge would land me on. It was still Friday. I washed the cup and left it upside down to dry. I didn't go back to the map.
