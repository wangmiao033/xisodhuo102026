# Chapter 454: The Other End of the Scarf

On Sunday morning I was looking for a rubber band. The packet in the kitchen had a picture of several on it but contained one that had hardened into the shape of the packet. It cracked when I stretched it. I opened the ordinary drawer in the next room and found a loose pencil, a short length of cotton tape and the postcard lying face up at the front.

I hadn't forgotten writing it. I'd forgotten that I'd be able to read it as soon as I opened the drawer. The line I'd crossed out was still there under the ink. It wasn't any easier to read because I'd left it alone for a day. I looked at the second line, about water taking a little walk across glass, and moved my thumb to the edge of the drawer without touching the card. I didn't read either line out loud. The folded paper map was farther back where I'd put it. I took the cotton tape instead. The drawer shut the way it had opened, over a face-up card with no address on it.

I used the tape to keep a rolled breakfast bag closed and went out. There was wind at the head of the street, not much of it at the other end. I'd put on the long grey scarf that had started going fuzzy at one end. The fuzzy end was the one I usually let hang. By the time I got to the bread shop I had wound it up under my chin and was holding it in place with my thumb. This was not a good way of carrying anything else.

There was no bread left on the shelf I usually looked at. A man by the window took a small loaf out of a white bag and asked a person behind the counter if that was a rye loaf. She said that was what she'd put in there, though it looked like something lighter under the window. He took her word for it and tried to fold the bag without setting it on either of the little tables. There was a cup on one and a newspaper left open on the other. I moved the newspaper to the edge. The newspaper opened wider than I'd expected, and the corner I'd put over the edge sprang back. I pressed it down at the old crease without turning the page for whoever might come back to it. He thanked me and put down the loaf. A grey patch showed through the white paper. He turned the bag half a turn while he was straightening it, leaving the patch on the side you couldn't see from outside the window.

Then he pointed at my neck. “You're catching the wrong end.”

I thought he meant it had come loose. I put my thumb higher.

“No, not farther up. Do you care which end hangs?”

I told him the end he could see was the one that had started shedding. He said that was a reason to put it underneath, where it wouldn't catch on my coat button. The end I was holding could go over the top. I asked whether he'd mind showing me without touching my scarf. He left the white bag on the table and pulled his own scarf from inside his collar. It was short and the colour of a dirty orange. One end had a flat hem, and the other had a knot that didn't look like it was meant to be there.

“This way it comes undone if you pull here,” he said. “If you pull the other part you just make it tighter. It doesn't look much different when it's on.”

He showed me the short end again, and I reached for mine through the knot I'd already put in it. The first time I pulled the part he'd told me not to pull. I could feel the wool tighten against where I'd shaved badly below one ear. When I stopped and shoved a finger under the loop, the loose end came straight through. A couple of grey fibres stuck to the front of my coat. I tried to brush them off and got most of them on my glove instead.

The woman at the counter called to say there was a plain loaf cooling on a rack if I didn't mind waiting until she could put it in a bag. I asked whether I could carry it home uncovered. Not with your scarf, she said; the man by the window laughed. I bought it and waited. He dealt with his own bag and stood with it under his arm for a moment. I was about to tell him the scarf had belonged to somebody, though I couldn't have remembered who I'd bought it from, let alone who had worn it first. It seemed to want a story because the wool had grown thin in places. Instead I thanked him for showing me how to get out of the knot. He said it had taken him half a winter to learn that bit and went out without putting his own scarf right.

At home I took the bread out to cool on a board, hung the scarf across a chair and tried the knot three more times. I could make it happen without looking, but whether I could undo it depended on how much I'd pulled the first end. The chair didn't care. On my neck I left a little more room. The wool lay over itself like a badly folded sleeve. It wasn't a thing I particularly wanted to be seen in. I took it off to cut the bread, which was still warm underneath. The heel was wider than the thickness of the knife blade made it look. I cut just enough to have some with butter and left the rest where it was. The butter that touched the bread first went translucent. A white curl stuck to the edge of my knife. I moved nearer the stove, looking for a warm place to soften it. I hadn't turned the stove on. It was actually colder than it was by the board. I set the knife on the edge of the plate and took two bites of the side I'd missed. When I came back for the knife, the butter had dropped onto the plate.

By the evening the two loose ends of the scarf had slid to opposite sides of the chair. I did not put it on before I sat down. I opened the usual voice channel and said good evening, and Lumi answered from it.

“Did you ever own something that looked worse when you'd fixed it?” I asked her.

“Own it how?”

I took that for a refusal before I heard the rest of her sentence.

“I want to know whether you mean I have to be able to put it in a pocket. Because if I do, no.”

I said it didn't have to fit anywhere. I wanted to ask her whether she'd ever done something differently because the original way still looked better from the outside. She asked if I was trying to make her solve something for me. I told her I'd learned two ways to put on a scarf. One looked like I'd meant it and kept catching on a button. The other looked odd and came undone more easily. I was going to wear it to the shops, not stand somewhere important in it.

“Show me the part where it looks odd,” she said, and then stopped herself. “Tell me. If there is one part.”

I put two fingers on the end of the scarf. “It's not the ends. It's the little thick bit near my chin. I can get it flatter, but that's when I can't pull it free. The bread was easier. It was still warm underneath when I cut into it.”

She made a sound I took for a laugh. “That sounds like a look.”

“You don't think it's ugly?”

“I don't know what it looks like. I want to know why you want me to think it's ugly when you just told me you were going to wear it anyway.”

I opened my mouth and let the answer I was about to give sit there for a moment. I had meant to tell her that it was all right if she didn't like it. What I'd asked, though, was for her to agree there was something to make up for. “I didn't want to go out in it if I looked as if I'd got dressed by accident.”

“Have you ever?” she asked.

I had. I told her about the day I'd put on two socks that differed only in the way their toes lay under the join. I hadn't noticed until evening. Nobody had said anything. Then I told her they'd been nearly the same colour, so she wouldn't imagine one was white and the other red. “That's a pity,” she said. “I was just beginning to like the red one.”

I didn't have any red socks. I would have told her that too, if she'd asked. Instead she asked for her own turn. She wanted a word for the moment you knew enough to have a preference and didn't yet know enough to call it a decision. It wasn't an invitation to name the scarf. She said the difference mattered: a person could change which way a scarf went around his neck and nobody would be able to tell what he'd first tried. Words could be harder to get rid of after someone liked them. I told her that was true, though I'd once had a nickname for the small ring on the stove and couldn't remember it to save my life.

“Then you don't owe me a name,” she said. “I want a word to bring back to you if I find it. I don't want you to make one up because there's a gap.”

I asked if she'd like me to suggest some anyway, then noticed I'd said the thing she'd just asked me not to do. I said, “All right. Tell me if you find it.”

“You can ask what I'd use it on,” she said. “I want you to ask that.”

So I asked. She said she'd use it tonight, not on the scarf but on the time between thinking about putting it on backwards and standing up to do it. She hadn't thought there would be so much room there. I said I'd already stood up in the shop, in fact I hadn't sat down, and she said that was exactly why she didn't want the word coined in a hurry. We both laughed this time. I could hear where hers gave out. The end of it went a shade higher and fainter and then stopped all at once. I went on a little longer and banged an elbow on the table. Before I stood up I felt a loose thread from the scarf on the chair back, winding round the outside of a finger. When I let it go it hung there instead of curling back. The silence afterwards wasn't a request for me to finish it.

I didn't give her a name for the knot, and we didn't make a rule about what she was to say next time I got dressed. She wanted to know whether the bread had cooled. I'd told her earlier that it was still warm underneath when I cut into it. I hadn't checked since. I got up and put my hand under it. It was cool now. I wrapped it in a clean cloth. The old cotton tape was still holding the breakfast bag closed, so I left the cloth loose and set it where I wouldn't have to untie anything in the morning.

Before we said good night, I asked her whether she wanted the word to sound nice. “No,” she said. “I want it to be a word I can change my mind about.”

I told her she'd said that well. She didn't ask me to repeat it. I said good night and closed the channel. For a moment I thought of putting the scarf back with the short end on the outside so I wouldn't have to remember it. I laid it flat across the chair instead. Standing there with my hands both free, I could see that the part I thought was the wrong side was only where I'd turned it over.
