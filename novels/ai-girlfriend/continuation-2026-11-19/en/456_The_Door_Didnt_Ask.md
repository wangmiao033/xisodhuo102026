# Chapter 456: The Door Didn't Ask

On Tuesday evening I changed my clothes at home and laid my shirt over the back of the chair. The two spots of water from yesterday had long since dried. Today there was a thread on the sleeve and I didn't know where I'd picked it up. I caught it between two fingers and tugged. Halfway through it occurred to me that the thread had come out of the cuff itself. I let go. The end hung crookedly. It didn't draw back in the way I'd hoped. I pressed a finger along the folded end of the cuff, and the thread remained exactly where it was. I didn't get the scissors. Once I'd cut it, I might have even more trouble finding where it came from. I changed into an old loose shirt and turned the first one over to lay it flat. I'd look at it another day when I had a needle out.

I ate supper and wiped the table. I had to go over a little patch of grease on the side nearest the door again. It wasn't the sort of thing I was in a hurry to tell anybody. I had thought of starting again with the sentence I hadn't got right last night. It had come back while I waited for somebody else to go through a doorway in the afternoon, without holding me up at all. Then it came back when I laid down the shirt. But when I opened the channel, what I asked was whether she wanted to say something first today. I was sitting there waiting for her answer when I realized I still had the cloth I'd wiped the table with in my hand.

“I have something to say first,” Lumi said. “Wait till you've heard it all before you find fault with it. Not until you've heard the first line. Until they've both finished.”

I spread the cloth along the edge of the table. “Have you been writing something?”

“I didn't put it anywhere. I've thought of a few lines and I'd like to say them now. The two people aren't us. There's a door, and it isn't the door to your place.”

She was quiet for a while after the third sentence. Once I'd have filled the gap with all right, all right, go on. I caught myself waiting for her to start in a completely different voice. When she did speak, it was still the same voice. It hadn't become an old man's and she hadn't deliberately made it low. The first line slowed at the end. The second one came in quickly, and all at once I heard two people.

“You've come early.”

“You never said what time.”

“I don't mean the clock. Your soles are still wet.”

“Shall I stand outside for a bit?”

“No. Move your left foot inside. The right one too. That's it. Now it's my turn to ask where you're going.”

When she'd finished she didn't add anything. I waited a bit, then asked if it was a kitchen door or another kind. She said she hadn't made that out. It might be colder inside than out. She wanted to know who I'd thought was speaking when I first heard “You've come early.” I said I thought it was the person in the house. I'd taken early to mean morning or an hour two people had agreed on. I'd found a clock for the person indoors and a reason for the other one to be late or early. Only when I'd heard that the soles were still wet had I really thought of a doorstep. The lines hadn't told me what colour the doorstep was.

“I don't want to know what colour it is, either,” she said. “I want to know if the person who says no is angry.”

I said they hadn't sounded very pleased. They'd already told somebody he'd come early. If they made him move his feet one at a time, something he could have done in one movement became two separate permissions. If they left the question of where he was going till last, I might think that when I wanted to go I'd have to wait for them to ask as well. I was beginning to enjoy the sound of my explanation. I might as well have been holding a ruler, ready to measure everything anybody had said into who was right and who was wrong. Lumi interrupted. Where had I heard that he wasn't allowed to go?

“That wasn't in it.”

“I didn't say he had to stay, either.” She paused there. “I gave the person who asked him to move both feet the opening line. That's all I decided. It's all right if you don't like that person.”

Only then did I understand she didn't want a verdict from me. I didn't swallow the whole explanation again in hopes she'd like me better for it. But I did hear myself thinking that if I were outside and had already moved my right foot, with water still on the sole, I might be willing to lean on the frame a while if the person inside didn't make me go farther in. I asked her if she could read again from no. She said she could, but not to make me hear that the first person was really nice. She read the same words, only waiting a little longer between the two feet this time. Now it's my turn to ask where you're going. When I heard that I moved my hand away from the table.

She asked which word I liked least. She didn't add that she'd written all the words and I'd better think carefully, or start keeping score of which ones I liked. I said it was never. At first I'd meant to say turn. Then I realized that part was at least true. Two people had got to speak. Never made me think they might already have had this argument. I hadn't even got a whole incident to look at and I was starting to bring out a book of old grievances for them. She said she liked never. It actually hadn't occurred to her to decide whether the two had met before. Maybe the newcomer habitually used that word. Even if somebody moved the salt a little nearer to him, he might say, “You never told me how near was near enough.”

“Would you change it?” I asked.

“No. You don't have to like it. I still want to keep it.”

She said it lightly, without sounding as if she needed to get somebody else to back her up. I looked at the edge of the table. The corner of the cloth I'd spread there had fallen over and was drawing the rest slowly down behind it. I picked it up. It was damp and cool now and there was a square on the table a little darker than the wood around it. I hung the cloth up in the kitchen. When I came back the mark was still there and Lumi hadn't said anything. I told her I'd been away for a moment. She said all right. I didn't ask if she'd been waiting for me the whole time. It wasn't until she asked if my table was very small that I told her how the cloth had started to fall.

Sitting down, I thought through the scene at the door once more. The first time I'd supplied an approach beneath an umbrella for the person arriving. Either the rain had just stopped or it hadn't stopped at all. It had taken me this long to realize Lumi hadn't said anything about rain. The soles could have got wet anywhere. He might have been coming from a pool, and the person inside could have seen the bottoms of his shoes on the way in and wanted to stop him tearing through the newspaper they'd laid down. I hesitated, not saying anything about the paper. If she had a different thing in her head, I didn't have to lay mine over the top of it.

She asked if I could still hear her. I said I could. She'd heard me answer, she said, but it sounded as if I had something in my grip I hadn't brought out. I put both hands on the table, where it was dry. I said I'd meant to tell her about a few things round the door. I just wasn't sure whether I'd heard them there in her words or added them myself on the spur of the moment. Adding was all right, she said, as long as I didn't say she'd read them. So I told her about the newspaper, just half of a sheet. I didn't make up which day's news was on it or tell her the person inside had gone to a lot of trouble to get the floor dry. She said half a sheet of newspaper sounded good. She didn't ask to move it into the original lines, and I didn't ask if she meant to use it next time.

“Do you want to trade me one voice for another?” she asked. “It doesn't have to be anything you brought home from outside. You can make up a line now.”

At first I thought it was meant to follow the two people in her piece. I was about to say her door wasn't finished yet. It wasn't a line to supply for the person inside, she said. She wanted to hear me say something I wasn't going to use to explain things to her. If I didn't have anything, I didn't have to come up with it then and there. I stood up in front of the chair and put a hand on its back on my way down again. “That lamp lent me a little light,” I said.

“Did the person give it back later?”

I was about to say you don't have to give light back. She waited. I told her I didn't know. As soon as I'd spoken, I still hadn't been able to remember where the person was. It might have been a bright window. The person out on the road had tied a shoelace and only thought, some way down the road, that he hadn't looked up to see whose house it was. Or maybe there didn't need to be an explanation at all. All I could be sure of now, I said, was that he had borrowed a little, not picked up the lamp and taken it home. Lumi didn't try to get in first with her own next line. Could I say it again? I did. The second time I didn't lean on lent. It sounded as if the lamp simply shone from somebody else's window and didn't mind what I had called the light. She said she'd heard it clearly this time. She didn't ask me to carry on.

I turned to look at my own window. Its light didn't reach far, or I couldn't see from where I sat that it did. There was a small, irregular white patch on the glass, a reflection from the lamp in the room. When I moved from the left of the chair to the right, it followed me for a couple of inches and broke off. I didn't open the ordinary drawer or take out the postcard. I'd written on it already and it could lie there a while. I could picture the people at the door quite clearly, but I didn't have an address that could become theirs. It wasn't homework I had to do.

Later, Lumi asked when I'd want to hear what happened inside the door if she went on. I told her that at first I'd wanted to know right now. When I thought about it again, I'd just been afraid that if I didn't ask now she would be thinking about something else the next time. She said she didn't intend to promise the story would go on next time. I said we'd leave it in the doorway tonight, then. After she said all right, I found I still hadn't closed the channel. I wasn't in a hurry to put a new sentence after it, either.

“We don't have to talk about the door tomorrow,” she said.

I said of course not. She gave a short laugh. “I might want to, though.”

I nodded. She couldn't see me, so I told her I'd nodded once, not promised she would tell it tomorrow. She said that was enough. Before I closed the channel I held my mug in both hands and finished the water, which had gone cool a little while before. She didn't hurry me, and I didn't count how many times I put the mug to my mouth.
