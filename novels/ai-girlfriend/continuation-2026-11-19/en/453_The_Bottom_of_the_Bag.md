# Chapter 453: The Bottom of the Bag

I went to the community hall on Saturday to find a piece of paper long enough to wrap a book. The book wasn't a present. It was one of mine, a heavy hardback with a loose jacket, and I meant to put it away on a shelf where the sun came in sideways. The jacket had already gone pale along the spine. I could have turned the book around. I'd tried that with another book and spent half an afternoon looking straight at it without recognizing it.

The used-paper table was set up just inside the door. Someone had cut a detergent box down to hold the sheets that were too small to stack, and there was a saucer with three crayons in it. A sheet with rough brown edges lay lengthwise across a plastic chair under the table. When I went to pull it out, a man on the other side of the table said, “Hold it a minute, would you?”

I put it back. He had an old paper shopping bag upright between his shoes. There was a little pile of objects on the table in front of him, including a ball of string and a round tin with a bright butterfly painted on the lid. I could see where the underside of the bag had turned grey. I thought he was looking for another bag, but he was waiting to ask something about the piece of paper I'd just touched.

“Is that yours?” he asked.

“No. I was going to see if it was long enough for a book.”

He held the bag up by one handle. “Could I try it underneath this first? There's a split. I don't know how far it goes.”

The woman running the table was busy helping a girl roll two big sheets in opposite directions, and neither of us wanted to call her away. I took the piece of paper out again. The man put the bag on one end and sat down. When he folded the near side up, the brown paper made a sharp crack and a piece came away in his hand. It wasn't as strong as it looked. He turned the bag towards me so I could see the bottom. The split began at one corner and went in at a slant. A loose flap stuck out beneath his knuckles.

“I put a tin in it this morning,” he said. “That was fine. Then I put the tin down on the pavement and tried to fold the bag flat with everything else inside.”

He picked up the round tin and balanced it on his knee. In the better light I could see that the butterfly was made of two different greens. There was a worn patch next to one wing. I didn't ask what was in it. He turned the lid a little until it clicked, lifted it and took out a smaller tin. There were holes punched around the top. He put the bigger one back on the table and twisted the smaller one so I could see one edge. Around the holes somebody had put tiny rings of blue paint.

“My daughter made it for a school show,” he said. “She put a torch in it and said it was a lighthouse. The teacher called it a lantern. She wouldn't have that.”

“Was there a boat?” I asked.

“There were supposed to be three. One came on a plate and one didn't get made. I forgot the other one.”

He set the small tin back in the larger one with a scrap of thin cloth underneath so the rims wouldn't rub. He told me his daughter was grown and wasn't expecting him to bring the lighthouse today. She was moving between flats and had asked him to keep an eye out for a shoe box of old things on a high shelf. He'd found the box yesterday, but it had someone's tax papers in it. The tin had been behind the box. He was taking it across town because she had a shelf that wasn't packed away yet, and he wanted to ask if she'd rather put it there or send it back with him.

I asked if he wanted to put the tin in my cloth bag. I could carry it wherever he was going next, if he was only going round the corner. Before I'd finished saying that, he'd picked up the ball of string and was winding the loose end tight.

“I'm getting on a bus,” he said. “And I don't need you to take it. I just need to put it down once without it dropping through.”

I put my bag on the chair next to me. The seat sloped a little, and the bag slid out from under my hand. I caught it and hung it from a knob on the back. The man still had the string. He tried the old seam with a short fingernail, blowing a speck of paper away first. He wasn't looking for somewhere to make another tear. He was trying to get the lifted flap to lie down again. He held it for a bit and let go. It rose slowly, but not as straight as before. The woman at the table finished with the large sheets and came over. She had heard the bit about dropping through. She looked at the broken piece of brown paper and pointed out that it had probably got damp before they put it out. From the back of the table she took two pieces of card that had been the sides of a cereal box. The man tried one inside the bag and kept the other on the table. He could push the first piece flat with the heel of his hand. It didn't touch all the corners, but it went beyond the split. He picked the tin up by the base, put it in a bit off centre, and lifted the whole bag an inch. The corner stayed put.

The woman told him he'd want to keep a hand under the bag when he got on and off the bus. He said he already did that on buses. It was on the pavement where he'd got impatient. She rolled the second piece of card so he could tuck it in alongside the tin if he wanted it later. I thought it would get in his way, but he wanted it. He undid the string he'd just wound up and tied the spare card, then slipped it down at the back. The bag was still the same size. He picked it up, looked round once and waited for a child carrying a roll of paper to get past the door first.

The woman asked how large my book was. I showed her with my hands. She asked me to show her again, but without including the space I'd want at either end. It was hard to do. I knew the weight of the book better than I knew where its edges sat. She gave me a clean sheet from the bottom of a pile of old music. The printed side was blank except for a couple of bars and an almost illegible number in a corner. She said I could take another if it didn't fit, but I didn't want to open a roll out on the table just to be sure. I took it, thanked her and went home with the paper rolled loosely in my coat pocket.

It was too short by a finger's width. I put the book flat on the table and turned the paper a quarter turn, which left it short in a different direction. It took me three tries to admit that I didn't have to wrap the back if what I was trying to protect was the spine. I made a loose paper jacket instead. The loose cover underneath stayed in place and the seam of the new cover was still out of the sun. From the doorway I could have told you which book it was. The top of the old jacket showed a green stripe. I left it showing. A little patch of light at the foot of the window was climbing the bookcase. Once I'd settled the book in place, it crossed the green and moved onto the new edge I'd folded. Part of the fold was crooked. I reached to take it down again and stopped with my fingers on the shelf. I'd nudged a paperback. I straightened the paperback and let that be enough. The hardback hadn't been straight there before. Its old jacket had just been too busy for me to see it.

I hadn't planned to tell Lumi about the man at the paper table. The version that had formed in my head on the way home was about a man taking an old thing to his daughter. I couldn't hear him saying any of the sentences in that version. In the kitchen I washed one mug and put it upside down to drain. I left the other one on the table. Then I sat down and deliberately opened the usual voice channel.

“Do you have one you want to start with tonight?” I asked.

“I do,” Lumi said. “I want to ask it before you choose the subject. I might want to choose a different one after.”

“Go on.”

“If you tell me a story with something good at the beginning, can I ask to stay at the beginning for a while? You don't have to tell me the rest. I don't get to press rewind after you've said it.”

I had expected a different sort of question. I slid my hand up the handle of the mug and then back down, until I'd got most of my palm around it. “Yes. But I might just tell you one thing and stop.”

“You could.”

I told her I'd been at the paper table when somebody took a tin out of another tin. I told her about the two greens, and about the blue rings somebody had put around the holes. I said he'd told me it had been made for a school show. I didn't tell her where he was taking it or what he'd found it behind. I'd almost mentioned the box. Then I'd have wanted to explain whose things were in it, and that wasn't mine to tell.

“What noise was the little tin making?” she asked.

“It didn't. He had a bit of cloth underneath so they wouldn't rub.”

“I'm staying there,” she said.

I didn't have another sentence ready once she said that. It was pleasant to sit and feel my mouth go slack. At last she asked me if I thought the person who painted the rings had done them all the way round, even on the side that wouldn't be seen. I told her I hadn't seen the whole way round. I remembered where the rim had been when the little tin went back into the big one, but the cloth was already in front of several of the holes by then. I could turn it in my mind and give every hole a neat border without any trouble. I didn't tell her I'd seen that. The mug was still a little warm where the skin between my thumb and forefinger rested. I put my other hand there. She asked whether I wanted to make up one with her. Not the man or a daughter for him. Just a tin.

We gave ours a lid that turned a quarter of the way and stuck. She wanted six holes. I wanted a seventh she could cover with a thumb, though we had to go back and decide whether that made it seven or six if the light was on. Six on a wall, she decided, seven in the tin. I said somebody would get a sore thumb if they had to hold it for long. “Then they could take their hand away,” she said. “It's not their job to make it dark.”

When there was nothing else I wanted to put on the tin, I said so. She said she didn't need to keep it anywhere. We went on talking about the top of the mug being colder than the bottom, which she only knew because I said it. When I closed the channel, I took the book down once more to check whether the edge of the paper would catch as I pulled it out. It slid clear of its neighbour. I hadn't written the title on the new cover. I pushed it back into place without doing that.
