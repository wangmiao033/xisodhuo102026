# Chapter 447: Back in Its Place

On Sunday morning, while I was wiping the counter, a corner of the cloth dragged out from under the platter. I put a finger on it without looking and found a patch of crayon that had been pressed flat. The little dented ball I'd picked up beside the table the afternoon before rolled briefly through my mind. Then I bent down to see whose mark it might be. There was no telling. The child at the long paper in the common room had used several blues, and which of them had put a crayon down beside the platter didn't matter much in the end. There had always been cloth between the paper and the good side of the platter. The glaze was smooth except for a pale old mark at the rim. When I folded the cloth back, I left that mark alone on purpose. I hadn't put it there, and wiping it again wasn't going to make it any fainter.

The deli had been shut on Saturday. This morning its little exterior light was on. The door was only partway open, and a man with an apron string trailing around one arm was backing a cart over the sill. I stepped aside until the last wheel knocked down onto the pavement. He lifted his eyes and tipped his chin toward the entrance; I took that to mean I could go in. I held my bundle a little higher and pushed the door wide for myself.

Three people were standing inside. A woman at the far end of the counter opened her shopping bag and then set it back down. She said she'd ordered two small boxes, not one family size. The woman behind the glass untwisted a paper slip on the bag and turned it around for her to see. I recognized her: she'd been the one to tell me to take the platter home and come back whenever it was convenient. She didn't see me. I switched the bundle to my right arm so the scale on the customer side would be clear. It didn't weigh much, but I was holding it harder than I had outside. My elbow was working its way up toward my ribs.

“Put it down first,” someone said from the side door.

It was the man with the cart. He came in and lifted the curtain, showing a shelf wider at the bottom than at the top, with several plates leaning upright. He pointed to an empty box beside it, not to the shelf itself. I put my bundle there and asked whether that was enough or whether I ought to say which one I'd borrowed.

“You borrowed it from here?”

“Before Saturday. She gave it to me.”

I didn't point at the woman's face. I only turned a little along the counter. He said to let her look at it, then; I didn't need to unwrap it yet. Somebody squeezed in from the street with a pickup number pinched in his fist. The man shifted half a step to let him through, then turned back to me.

“If you're in a hurry, you can leave it. If you want to see her take it, wait till she's got a free hand. Doesn't mean everybody has to wait here.”

I almost said I wasn't in a hurry. Half-dry footprints led from the door to the display case. Beside the last two prints a streak of gray had come up from the floor. He'd already gone to get a rag when I realized that I was waiting for him to say something else so I could pick the answer that would make me look more considerate.

“I think I'd like to wait a bit,” I said.

He said all right over his shoulder, pulled the rag free, and didn't write down anywhere that I'd be waiting. Both my hands were empty now, and I suddenly didn't know where to put them. I looked along the glass at what hadn't sold yet. The woman had removed the wrong slip and taken two small boxes from beneath the cold case. She let the customer check the number against her own note. The customer didn't smile, but she held the bag open in a wide, steady circle. The next man gave his name. The woman turned two pages of a thick book before telling him that not all of his order was ready.

“There's somewhere to sit downstairs. Would you like to come back in a bit? I can't tear that tag off for you. There's still a box coming.”

By the time a clear stretch of counter opened up in front of me, the shop wasn't as crowded. I started to hand over the platter, but first the woman called behind the curtain, “How many of the big ones, in the end?” Then she came over and undid the bundle. She turned the platter once by the rim without paying any particular attention to the pale mark. She flipped it and put a finger beside a line of blue writing so faded I could hardly see it.

“That's ours. Thank you.”

She was about to take it away. “Was that the one you were counting?” I asked.

She stopped and glanced toward the shelves. “No. This one's not from today's stack. And we don't count on anybody bringing a plate back to make today's stack come out right.”

I let my thumb ease off the edge of the cloth. It had been pressed there much longer than I'd noticed. “I thought I might have left you short one.”

“Not this one. The big platters all went into the fridge next door with the same paper under them. My sister says she put six in, but when she went back she only took five off the bottom shelf. It's the paper we ought to count now. We're not working out who owes us a plate.”

She set the platter by the sink on the inside of the counter and returned my cloth. I tucked the blue corner into the fold. It was my own cloth, but for some reason I almost resented taking it back on its own, as if there ought to have been a receipt folded in with it that I could take home to show somebody. A few slips under an empty tin on the counter had begun to curl. The top one had a date printed in big type. I looked at it a moment, though it wasn't mine.

The line had begun to grow again. The woman held up the curtain and asked the man with the cart if he could come help. The person looking in the fridge had a sore hand. Would he steady the bottom of the pile when she lifted it? He took off his apron, the strings still not properly untangled, folded the clean side inward and went through. My toes had already turned that way. One hand was still resting on my cloth. What came into my head sounded perfectly reasonable: with everyone searching, I could stay and save somebody a trip. I could count paper closer to the counter, if nothing else. But I'd only wanted to see this platter taken in. I had seen it. It hadn't needed one of my hands to follow it behind the counter for it to be returned.

The woman with the bag was outside, moving one of the small boxes into her other hand. She nodded toward the door and asked whether I was going back in. I told her no and held it for her. She thanked me. Before I let go, I waited half a beat for the spring to catch. The first time I'd pulled that door I thought somebody must have to see it all the way home. In fact, if you carried it through the last inch, it was more inclined to bump your shoe.

It hadn't started raining. On the way there, balancing the platter, I'd kept my pace slower than usual. Now I was light enough to start straight toward the diagonal shortcut before remembering to look. At the distant junction, the west end was still blocked by a sign. I could only see this end. I didn't take out the map to check the other one. I went back to the main road and bought a pear at the little shop. The seller said I could feel how firm it was. I chose one that hadn't softened all the way. I didn't ask him to keep a better one for me next time.

At home there was a rectangle of a slightly different color on the empty counter. The cloth and platter had covered it; water had dried in a line around the edge. I washed the cloth. The crayon lightened but didn't come out completely. I hung the cloth above the sink and swept the drops into the basin without touching that corner again. The damp outline was gone; from the side you could still see where its edge had been. My hand hovered there for a moment, as if the counter had lost more than one thing. There was bread from the day before yesterday in the fridge. I cut a slice and turned the board sideways, and there was room to use it properly.

I cut up the pear, too. The end with the thin skin slipped as I started the first cut. I set a little saucer the other way at the edge of the board to catch the core. It was my own saucer, with most of its pattern faded from years of soup and water. I didn't need to carry it wrapped in anything. I'd just wash it when I was done.

It still wasn't quite dark when I finished. Later I opened Lumi's channel and asked if she wanted to hear something brief.

“I took the platter back. I chose to stay for a bit. I saw her take it in.”

“Did she ask you to wait?”

“No. I could have left it and gone. I wanted to see her recognize it.”

“Did she?”

“She looked at the writing underneath.”

I traced half a letter on the desk, a letter that hadn't been there. I told Lumi that I hadn't actually been able to read the line. They'd been counting a different set of platters. For a moment I thought I had to stay until all of them were accounted for. After I learned mine had nothing to do with it, I still wanted to help them look. I hadn't gone in. They hadn't asked me, either.

“Do you wish you'd gone?” she asked.

“I don't think so. Let me think a second.”

The pear was gone. There was a little water left on the saucer. I rubbed it with a fingertip without wiping it on my trousers. I've disliked jobs that take two people for a long time. Waiting for the other person's hand sometimes took longer than putting out my own. Every so often I mixed up being able to stop waiting with having to put out my hand.

“I want to know whether they found it,” I said. “That isn't quite the same as wanting to go find it for them.”

“I want to know, too.” She stopped for a second. “But if you went all the way back to ask them now, I'd think that was a long way to go.”

I laughed. “So would I.”

She didn't ask me to have her guess the color of the platter, and she didn't say they must have found it just to make me feel better. After a little while she asked if I could tell her again how the woman had held it when she read the writing. I needed a moment to see what I really remembered. The woman hadn't taken it in both hands and held it up to the light. One palm had supported the base while her other hand rolled the rim upward, all without moving it away from the edge of the counter. I told Lumi I could get that far, but I wasn't going to add anything I didn't remember.

“That's fine,” she said. “I just wanted to hear you say how she turned it over again.”

Before I closed the channel, she said that if I didn't want to wait another time, I didn't have to invent something to bring back for her to hear. I said we'd see the next time. I didn't promise to tell her about it. The little saucer I'd washed was dry by then, cool to the touch. There was a small place for it in my own cupboard, enough room beside two bowls.
