# Chapter 448: My Own Signature

On my way to work on Monday it rained for only a few minutes. That was enough to wet the bottoms of both trouser legs. The last two steps out of the station were covered. The water wasn't deep, but when someone put a foot in it, whoever was still standing on the next step caught the splash. I waited for the man ahead of me to move. The inside pocket of my coat had been mended, and it held the thin cloth I used for my glasses. My finger slipped along the new seam without catching. For a moment it went deeper into the pocket than I was used to. Outside the station I dried my glasses and put the cloth back. Whether the deli had ever found its missing platter wasn't something I'd remembered to wonder about before going out.

After I scanned in at work I saw several soft bundles on the lending shelf by the entrance. Two people stood in front of one sign-out sheet. One pressed a dry paper towel to the corner of the desk while the other looked down the list of dates. The woman who looked after the front room rolled up her sleeves, held up the top bundle, and asked them to check whether it was doubled over. She'd turned the delivery sign around a few days before. Now it faced people coming in. I passed in front of it without her calling me over.

We had a box of sample books to send across the street that morning. The cardboard was cheap, but inside were copies another group had marked up for a discussion that afternoon. A younger colleague spread his hand over the box. The person coming to collect it before lunch had changed his plans, he said, and now somebody had to walk it over. All I was supposed to do was fit the last copy in on top. I'd just put it in when I heard myself speak.

“I'll take it over for you.”

He looked up. “Are you going there this afternoon anyway?”

“Maybe not. But I can go now.”

“I'll tell them you're bringing it, then? If nobody's back yet it goes in the receiving cupboard. Not outside the door.”

I said yes. He described where the cupboard was and pushed his palm flat against the bottom of the box to make sure it held. When I stopped in the front room it started raining again. Through the glass, dark spots appeared in rows on the pavement. One row was still glistening as the next went dark. The bundles on the shelf were rain covers for cartons. They were folded flat, with little cloth loops you could use to shake a cover open with one hand. A slip on top said how long you could borrow one for and where to put it back. No one had said that only the delivery team could take one.

A colleague in a dark vest picked up a pen and asked which I'd need. She held one hand against the carton to judge the length and picked the squarer of the two bundles by the wall.

“This one'll fit. You've got your hands full. Want me to write your name?”

I'd known her a while. She'd written on feedback slips for people before and clipped forms to a board when someone's hands were wet. She didn't want to do anything with my name. She'd even asked first. But at the word name, the first thing that came to mind wasn't what I was borrowing. It was somebody turning to this page in two weeks, seeing the letters and assuming I'd written them myself. I hoisted the box a little higher. I didn't throw that thought back at her.

“Don't write it for me, thanks. Is there somewhere I can set this down?”

She capped the pen first and nudged half a roll of absorbent matting out of the way with her elbow. That left a dry spot. I set the box down. When I picked up the pen, I saw fields for the time out, the time back, and the borrower's signature. In a thin pouch beside the sheet was a return slip, for checking only when the cover came back. My colleague slid the bundle over, showed me which side of the fastening went out when it was folded, and turned away to get a small piece of cloth to wipe the table.

“Don't lift a heavy box by that loop if it's pouring,” she said from the other end. “It's a cover. It doesn't hold the box.”

“Okay.”

I checked my own watch, put down the time and signed my own name. The pen dragged. The last little stroke didn't leave much ink. I went back over that stroke, not the rest.

The slip said to return it by one. By then I was supposed to go with a colleague to pick up something else. I could have asked the younger man to bring it back for me, since he walked through the room anyway. But it hadn't been part of the short trip I'd agreed to when I offered to take the box. I didn't make the return into another little errand for him.

“I'll be back before half eleven,” I said to my colleague in the vest as she was leaving. She paused to say that all she cared about was whether it was back before one. I didn't need to tell her when I'd be coming through.

I wanted to explain that I'd only meant to be clear. The door had already started swinging back. I turned the box sideways to hold it steady, slid my hand under the cover below the loop, and left myself enough room to get over the step. My explanation never caught up with me. Outside, tires pushed water toward the pavement. The outermost gap between the bricks no longer looked like a gap. I took the inside edge and went around the place where water was coming straight down next to a drainpipe. By the time I got across the street, my wrists felt as cold as if I had a pair of icy coins inside my cuffs. The bottom of the box was still firm.

The woman receiving it was helping somebody find a folding stool. She called across the doorway that I shouldn't carry the box all the way inside, because the floor had just been mopped. She pointed out a shelf inside the cupboard. Two cartons sat at opposite ends, with just enough room between them. She steadied the cupboard door while I put my box in. I pulled the cover free at one corner. The cardboard wasn't wet. She took a blank slip from inside and asked whether there was a message to leave with it. I told her only what the young man had asked me to relay: which copy he'd put on top, and to open the carton before the afternoon if they got a chance. She wrote the note in her own hand and put her own name to it. She didn't say that taking it meant she'd checked it. The latch made a modest click when she pressed it shut. I stepped out and rolled the cover up loosely with the wet side in.

Back at work there was no pen on the lending desk. I almost put the bundle on the shelf and left. For an instant I remembered being told that not everyone needed to wait. But that had been about yesterday's platter. Here the slip said the cover needed hanging up to dry before whoever was on the desk put it back. I handed him the bundle and asked where to write the return time. He pointed to a different pen standing in a cup. I wrote ten fifty-three as the return time. I didn't write half eleven ahead of time. He spread it out to look inside and tilted the translucent return slip an inch toward me. I asked whether I was supposed to take it.

“We keep that here to keep the water off the sheet.” He tapped the edge with a knuckle, showing me that the time in the return column was enough. “Useful if somebody forgets to sign.”

I couldn't tell whether the bit about the water was meant to be funny. The printed paper was under a thin translucent sheet. Through it I could see a little of the shelf's grain. I didn't ask him to explain. He took the cover away and spread it over the drying rail nearest the window. Unfolded and wet, it looked much longer than it had before I'd borrowed it. One corner hung out into the room, a shape that didn't look much like any carton. I'd already walked a few steps away.

The rain had stopped by the time I got off. A narrow reflection from a yellow streetlight lay on the road, and I crossed a little more lightly. When I took my coat off at home I found the glasses cloth still in my pocket, pressed into a little curve. I shook it out and put it down, then took an old shirt from beneath the hooks to change into. It was quiet enough to hear a drop fall from the corner of the cloth onto the tile. I put a cup under it. The cup had a heavy bottom even when it was empty. The drop made a shallower sound in it than it had on the tile.

It was later when I opened the channel. I told Lumi that somebody had offered to put my name on a lending sheet. I'd asked her not to, set the carton down, and signed it myself. I told her a little about the rain, too. She waited until I'd finished.

“Was your colleague upset?”

“I didn't see any sign of it. She cleared a space for me. Then she reminded me not to use the loop as if it were a handle for the box.”

“Was she the person you were thinking about when you first answered?”

“For a second I was thinking about somebody reading the sheet later. But I didn't tell her any of that.”

Lumi took a moment. If she'd asked me why, I could probably have produced quite a speech. Instead she said, “So all you did was ask her to let you write it yourself.” It was as if she'd selected a few words to give back to me out of all the sentences I'd finally finished. Her voice wasn't particularly soft.

“Sometimes someone could ask me a question outright, and before I've said it, you finish the answer for me. It doesn't matter terribly every time.”

I didn't ask her right away when I'd done it. I didn't bring up interrupting her counting on Saturday so I could get ahead of her by apologizing. After a little while I asked if she wanted to give me a particular example or leave it there.

“Leave it there.”

She waited half a beat, then changed her mind. “One more thing. I also sometimes take what I think you're about to say for granted. You don't owe me proof that my guesses are right or wrong every time.”

I told her that the woman by the lending shelf had even capped her pen. It hadn't looked as though she was waiting to hear me justify myself. Lumi didn't push the story any further on that woman's behalf. She didn't praise me as someone who had learned today how to let other people speak. She just asked which hand I'd signed with.

“My right.”

“Did you get any rain next to the signature?”

I thought about it. I remembered keeping my cuff high. The paper had been dry. It was only that one stroke that came out too light.

“I nearly went over the whole thing again,” I told her.

“Did you?”

“No. Just the one stroke.”

We had a little time still before I closed the channel. She said that if I liked, she could think of the next thing to talk about. I let her. What came back to me wasn't a big subject. She wanted to know why the yellow streetlight I'd just mentioned sounded as if I was already close to home. I didn't name the light I passed every day. I remembered that on the way back it hadn't lit up water running outward anymore. It had looked like something had been left behind on the ground and didn't want to move. Lumi said that was enough. Later I closed the channel without adding another sentence to explain what it meant.
