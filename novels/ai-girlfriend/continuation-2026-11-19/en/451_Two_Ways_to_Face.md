# Chapter 451: Two Ways to Face

There was a white mark on my shirt cuff when I left work on Thursday. In the afternoon I'd leaned against the long table they'd brought in and hadn't noticed the fine dust still on its edge. When I got to the fruit stall I turned the cuff back and slapped it twice. The woman had just put out a row of empty paper bags. She heard the noise and made a little shelter over them with her hand. Nothing had got that far. I took a step away before she lowered her hand and used two fingers to open the mouth of the bag I'd made her worry about.

A single bulb hung under the awning. The light escaped at the rolled edge of a plastic sheet and put an irregular shape on the pavement. The pears were just inside it, in several stages of ripening. Some had yellow bellies. The ones near the scale were paler and had a glossy green on them. A man in a knitted cap picked up a yellow one and asked, “Is this hard?”

She didn't take the pear. She put a hand under it as if she thought he might drop it back from that height. “Is it for today or in a couple of days?”

“I asked if it was hard.”

She took her hand back. “I need to know whether you're worried it is or worried it isn't.”

He looked at her. He had a carton of eggs in his other hand, so he couldn't get at his phone. Perhaps it was simply that he had too much to hold, but he stuck out his chin when he spoke, as if he had to find somewhere to put his voice as well. “My mum said to get hard ones. She wants to make sweet soup tomorrow morning. Last time all the juice came out when I cut them up.”

“Then not that one.”

She didn't make him guess again. She indicated a greener pear about the same size. He shifted the eggs to put the yellow one down; when the carton tilted, I almost held it up for him. He got it against his ribs himself and came a step closer to the stall. I never touched it. The woman showed him the stems and said he could pick three of about the same colour. They didn't have to be identical, but he shouldn't take any with a soft place where they'd been knocked. He looked where she pointed. Then he singled out one so green it was nearly white.

“What about that one?”

“That one would be good if you wanted to have an argument with your mum. Not so much for the soup.”

He laughed. In his position I might already have paid for the first pear. I don't like to let people see that I haven't worked out what I want. Remembering what his mother had said was easier than knowing how hard a pear was supposed to be. Perhaps it hadn't occurred to him that hardness was something you could talk about on a scale. He was holding another now, turning it so he could see the ring of fruit that had been against his palm. When he paid, the woman put the eggs and the pears into separate bags. Nobody brought up what was to happen to the yellow one.

It was still at the edge, its crooked stem pointing out to the road. I pointed in that direction.

“Soup again?” she asked.

“No, to eat tonight. But I want another one for tomorrow, to take with me in a bag.”

She looked at me from between two pears and didn't reach for another. “Both in the bag, or one?”

“One. I'll carry tonight's. The one for tomorrow has to go with my lunch. It might get squashed on the way.”

“Put it in a little box. If the pear's got to do all your packing for you, you may as well get a wooden one.”

Two people went past with shopping bags, and one of them had caught just enough to laugh at the word wooden. I thought of the shallow box in the kitchen cupboard. Its lid used to spring back at one corner. I hadn't taken it out in a long time; there were two bowls on it, and I couldn't tell at the fruit stall whether it would shut any better now. In any case, there was an empty pocket at the side of my bag. I didn't need to take the afternoon's papers home. It turned out I'd been looking for a pear that could survive the worst way of packing it, a way that existed entirely in my head.

The woman set a yellow pear beside the scale. “There's your one for tonight. You can think about how to carry the other. I haven't weighed it.”

It wasn't the one the man had put back. The one at the edge faced the road; the one by the scale pointed its stem at me. Perhaps I'd been vague with my hand, or she'd taken the nearest pear for convenience. It shouldn't have mattered. But I wanted the first one. I'd seen it lifted and put down again. It had turned out not to be a pear for tomorrow's soup, and I felt absurdly glad to find it another use. The pear, of course, wasn't waiting for me to make its afternoon better.

“I'd like the one with the stem pointing out,” I said.

She took the pear I meant and turned it over. There was a little brown patch on the bottom, though the skin wasn't broken. She didn't tip that side discreetly away; she held the pear out flat so I could see how big it was. “It's had a knock here. If you eat it tonight, just cut that bit off. If you want to keep it till tomorrow, I'll give you the other one.”

“Tonight,” I said.

She weighed it and I paid. I didn't buy a pear for tomorrow after all. I didn't explain the troublesome lid, either, or buy some other fruit instead. When she asked if I wanted a bag I took one. It was big enough that the pear would have rolled around in the bottom, so I carried it with my palm underneath. When the light from the stall ran up my wrist I could see a little of the white mark still there. It hadn't come off, but it no longer looked like half the shirt.

At home I rinsed the pear and set it on the board. The knife went in outside the edge of the mark more easily than I'd expected. The bruise wasn't deep. I cut off a thin piece and found clear flesh underneath. The stem and the cut piece lay next to one another like punctuation that had been left in the wrong place. I didn't take a picture. The juice ran out on the board and onto the cuff I'd already turned back once. I put it under the tap. Both the earlier dust and the new juice turned into the same dark patch of wet cloth; there was no telling where either had been.

I turned the wet cuff down again and it stuck to the back of my hand. That, at last, made me impatient. I found a towel and held one end of the shirt over the sink to get a dry patch around the wrist. The sleeve wouldn't go back exactly as it had been, so I undid the other cuff too. Otherwise I'd have eaten with one sleeve rolled up and the other trailing. There was a newspaper on the table that I hadn't finished at lunch. I read only the date at the bottom of the column. I folded the paper onto the sideboard where wet fingers couldn't get at the ink, then brought the small plate back. Each thing took a little longer than the last. None of it actually made dinner late.

I made four wedges and ate three sitting down. The last stayed on a little plate until I was finishing dinner, and then I ate that one too. When I felt like talking, I dried my hands and opened the usual voice channel. I told Lumi I'd just eaten the last of a pear and there wasn't any left now, only a bit stuck between my teeth.

I told her roughly what the man had asked about the hard pears, and I described the two stems that faced different ways.

“I thought you were going to say you bought two in the end.”

“I only bought one.” I didn't make it sound as if the first one had always been meant for me. I said the woman had shown me the mark underneath. “For a second I thought the reason I wanted the pear was that she'd picked it out for me. But she hadn't, and it wasn't mine until she'd weighed it and I'd paid.”

“The part where he had to ask her twice,” she said, “I thought that was going to be about how he didn't know how to buy pears.”

“I thought that myself, a little.”

“He got them in the end.”

“He did. He didn't need me to get them.”

She didn't make any arrangements for the man's mother's soup the following day. I found that I'd already imagined a taste of it. The taste was faint and had come from nowhere. There was no more of that story I needed to tell. Lumi asked if I'd like to play a small word game. There was no pear to guess, she said, and it had nothing to do with what had happened at the stall. She'd say something, and I'd give it two quite different places to go. I didn't have to choose which was better. She'd offer one of her own, though there was no promise she'd give me the same number.

“You first,” I said.

“There's still a light left.”

The first place I thought of was my own passage. I might have left in a hurry and forgotten to turn it off. I told her that. For the other one, I said, “A shop has closed. They've left a lamp for the person who cleans the floor. He hasn't finished yet.”

“Maybe he has,” she said. “They've left the light for the person who opens in the morning, so he can see the keyhole from outside the glass.”

“That's not a different place. Yours is still a shop.”

“That's how you're counting. I didn't say it couldn't be the same place. The person who opens has never met the person who cleans at night.”

My lips moved. I was about to clarify her rules so we wouldn't have to argue about the score further on. Then I remembered that we hadn't agreed there'd be a further on. “I like yours, then,” I said. “I like the person in the morning. Whoever left the light for him might not have got a chance to speak to him.”

Lumi said the place she'd thought of at first wasn't a shop. It was someone's drawer. A person had found a little light while clearing things out at night, tried it, found it still worked and kept it instead of throwing it away. A light could be left without being left on. I laughed. There was a little taste of the pear still, as though a drop of juice had lodged in the lines of my fingers and I had to press them together before it would come out. The light and the dark could stay in the same sentence. Nobody had to reach for the switch.

“Does that person know what they're keeping it for?” I asked.

“No. And the next sentence doesn't have to have anything to do with today.”

I asked if we could leave it there. She said yes, then asked if I had to close the channel now. I said I didn't, but she didn't need to come up with another line. She didn't want to, she said. We stayed for a little while in the space each of us had been willing to leave. Somewhere outside there was the sharp sound of a car door shutting. I heard it clearly on my side; I didn't assume it had come across to her. A little later we said goodnight. I closed the channel and went to wash the little plate. There was no fruit on the table for me to remember to pack for tomorrow.
