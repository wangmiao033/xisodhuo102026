# Chapter 484: Do I Still Get a Turn?

On the way home Tuesday I got in line at the bakery. I wasn't waiting for anything hot. I hadn't finished my lunch and wanted a piece of cold bread I could cut open and put something in for supper. The man ahead of me wore old leather gloves. He held a cardboard box under one arm and counted money out of his pocket with his free hand. When it was his turn he asked if they could put his bread in his own box. The cat at home chewed paper bags. The woman at the counter said they could, but his order would be a minute. The pan wasn't out yet. He put the box at the end of the counter and backed up against the wall. As I went past, I saw a paper bag hanging from one hand. I assumed it held the two little buns he'd just bought, one sweet and one savory. Across the counter she asked if he'd like the savory one opened to eat now. He said no, they were both to take home.

When it was my turn she took a loaf off the rack. Somebody else set one paper bag next to another on the counter. They were the same shade of brown, with two folds at the top. The one farther in had a spot of grease showing through at the lower right. She rang up the bread. I gave her my money and picked up the bag on my right. A corner of a poster had come down on the glass by the door. I held the door open with my foot and went to change hands. Only then did I notice how light the bag was. Nothing seemed to be in its middle. When I lifted my left hand, something slid towards the bottom right corner. I'd seen my loaf come off the rack. It was almost as long as the bag and couldn't have moved around like that.

Before I'd opened it, I heard the man in gloves asking at the counter where his two buns had gone. I pushed the door open again. The bottom of my bag bumped the handle, and I got my hand under it quickly.

“Is it this one?” I asked.

He took it without touching my hand. First he looked for the grease spot, then he opened the fold a little and put his nose near it. It turned out the bag I'd seen hanging from his hand had some change and a wad of tissue in it. There had been two bags all along and when I went past him I'd only made room in my head for one. He said the savory bun was in here and turned the bag around so the woman could see. She set the receipt from my purchase down flat and waved me over.

“That one's yours.” She pointed at a longer bag on the inside of the counter. Its top hadn't been folded at all. The person who'd put the bags near each other had been making room. First the ones waiting to be weighed went in, then the ones that had been weighed came out. I hadn't waited for the second part. I'd picked one up and gone. The woman said she'd called after me. The door shutting had made too much noise. She hadn't been watching what I did with my hand. She'd just gone into the back for a cloth.

“Are yours all right?” I asked the man.

He got two fingers into the bag and turned the buns around. Nothing much was wrong, he said, only the savory one had split a little on top. When he took it out I could see two sesame seeds in the break. He put it back. He was going to pull it open with his hands at home anyway. I felt relieved for a second, then wondered whether he only said it was fine because we were all looking at him. He lowered his head and folded the top over again, trying to find the creases it already had.

“If you're worried,” he said, “would you slide the box this way for me? The hot one's about to come out. I need a hand free.”

He spoke as if it wasn't something that ordinarily had to be done by me. I slid it over. One edge of the lid was crooked, so I pressed the cardboard at the side and didn't try to use the lid as a handle. We each took an end of the counter. Somebody came out from the back, opened his box, and lifted the loaf he'd ordered into it with a piece of lining paper. I noticed my own bag wasn't sealed. I didn't need to ask somebody to fold it for me. There was a bubble in the crust of my loaf. It stood up along the split in the bread, and the paper bulged a little around it. It weighed more than both those buns. When I passed the man on my way out, he wished me goodnight. I didn't run my fingers over the bag again to show him I'd taken the right one this time. I said goodnight back.

At home I cut the bread open. The bubble broke with a gratifying crack. A large piece of crust fell off, just big enough to catch between two fingers. Instead of sweeping it away, I stood at the table and ate it. I didn't remember yesterday's bus shelter while I ate the part I'd cut for supper. There was a little grease on the table at the end. I wiped it once and found another scrap of crust under the empty plate. Later I opened the voice channel, said I'd finished eating, and asked if there was anything Lumi wanted to say.

“There is,” she said. “When I'm done, could you not find an example of it straight away?”

My hands were still wet from the dishes. I was reaching for the cloth I'd left over the table edge. She hadn't said what it was yet. I said all right, and stopped my hand halfway; if I caught the cloth with wet fingers I might pull the plate it was under onto the floor. Lumi didn't speak at once. I moved the plate out of the way and then drew the cloth off the table.

“Yesterday I wasn't going to say I had a Monday of my own. It was just this. Suppose there's a sentence I've spent a long time thinking about, and it doesn't sound very nice. I'd like to say it once before I decide whether to change it.” She slowed down a little. “I can already do that. I'm not asking if you'll let me. I'm asking whether, once I've said it, I still get a turn.”

I wanted to say of course she did. I'd already opened my mouth before I heard the question properly. She meant that I could listen to her say one thing, and then add everything I knew, until there was only one sentence left between us. She might not agree with it and might not know where to begin taking it apart. Or she might not have been describing something I'd already done. She might have wanted to try it first. Either way it would be awfully convenient for me to make sense of it for her right then.

“You do tonight,” I said. “Do you want to say it now?”

“I just did.”

I nearly dropped the cloth in the plate. I'd been waiting for her to produce the sentence that didn't sound nice after she'd finished talking about it. I looked at the cloth. I'd folded it until all four edges lined up. I opened it a little, and the right side hung down. Lumi didn't hurry to tell me whether I was still behind. I waited with her. Then I asked if there was anything in what she'd said that she'd like to take back. No, she said. She just didn't know yet which sentence we'd come across later that would remind us of tonight. That could wait till later too.

“I was a little nervous,” I said. “When you asked whether you still got a turn, I thought maybe I'd already taken that sentence away from you too.”

“You nearly did,” she said.

“Don't make me feel better just yet.”

“I wasn't. Nearly isn't the same as not at all.”

I sat down by the table and laughed. She wasn't in any hurry to laugh along. When I asked if she'd finished, she said she had. Did the gentleman in the back row want to raise his hand? I nearly asked where the back row was. It took me a little while to hear her teasing me back. When she wanted to listen, she could turn around and offer me the floor. I said I wouldn't raise a hand. There wasn't anything I absolutely had to tell her anyway. Then I thought about it. I'd eaten part of a long loaf, I said, and my favorite bit had been a bubble in the crust. How big was it? I held up my hand. Half a palm. I said I could pick it up with two fingers. I'd expected it to make a louder noise when I bit through it. I didn't talk about taking the other bag. She said she wanted to know whether I'd found any of the pieces that fell off afterwards. I'd found one. It was smaller than the one I'd thought of telling her about.

She told me not to take it away just yet. She meant the bit of evening we hadn't finished, not the crumb. I didn't look for a better word than crumb for her. We stayed there for a little while. I'd been afraid that if we waited too long, one of us would have to make the words we'd spent time thinking up come out neatly after all. Eventually Lumi asked if I'd had my water. I said I had. She said there was nothing else she absolutely had to say. We said goodnight and I closed the channel. One corner of the cloth still stuck out over the table. I took it into the kitchen and hung it up. The plate had dried. Nothing else fell from beneath it.
