# Chapter 481: The Shade Goes First

I went out for eggs around noon on Saturday and found part of the road closed. Two painted red frames stood in front of the crossing. Behind them were stripes of white paint that had just been put down. The woman who sold fruit on the far side had her crates out as usual. Now and then somebody would hold up a piece of fruit so she could call its price across. Other people followed the barrier looking for a way out, and after not many steps they squeezed round the foot of the frame. The first two had nothing in their hands and got through all right. Then a man with two shopping bags turned one shoulder toward it, and a bag nearly tore on the foot of the barrier. He backed away. My own hands were empty at that point and I was already trying to find a way home without running into anybody.

There wasn't even a light at the next turning. Just beyond it was a small paved area where you could sit with a drink, or leave a bicycle. A girl was sitting with a much older woman on the long bench. There was an empty end beside them. The sun was on the stone, and there was nothing under the bench. I didn't know the women and meant to pass far enough away not to get in their way. The younger one was holding on behind her and had got three or four bags where she couldn't reach them. She called to me and asked if I could hand her the farthest one. The mouth of the bag was knotted and the string on the left was shorter than the one on the right. I gave her both strings at once. What she'd pointed to was a bag. She hadn't asked me to stay where I was.

The older woman was holding a playing card that had seen a lot of use. She wasn't looking at either of us. She shifted a few inches in from the edge, then put the card away. The other woman was looking for their bus. It was one stop away. She wanted to take all the bags with her but worried that if they went over too early there'd be nowhere to sit. Would the other woman like to wait a bit longer? She was already picking up two of the bags as she asked. The older woman looked up. They didn't have to sit to wait, she said. Put everything down. Then they could work out which way to walk with their hands empty. As she said it she held down the dry end of a stalk in one of the gaps with the toe of her shoe. I heard a few more words. I'd got as far as the egg shop before they spoke again, and I couldn't make the words out.

There were two kinds of eggs in the shop, four rows arranged two and two in the same cardboard box. I pointed to the inside row and asked if I could buy them loose. Four or six, the man told me. Could I not have three? Of course I could, only he didn't have a box for three, and had I brought anything to carry them in? I had a thin cloth bag. He found three cups from a cut-up egg tray for me and wrapped a piece of brown paper, narrower than the bag, round the whole thing. I almost told him not to use that much paper. It would do nicely, I said instead, and took it. The eggs didn't cost much. He found two coins to give me in change and put them on the little board by the till. I took them one at a time. The strip of sunshine they'd covered came back off the board, and it was brighter to look at than either coin had been.

The bus had gone by the time I came out on the corner. The girl and the older woman were gone. A leaf pulled away from the wall by a tire was all there was to see by the bench. My bag wasn't stiff enough at the bottom. Anywhere I went, I had to put my hand under it, and up ahead people were hurrying so they could see the new crossing. I thought of going the other way. There was still a little shade behind the bench on the outside edge, and I went over and sat down. A man in work clothes came out of the lane we'd gone around before, walking a dog that hadn't much interest in walking. The dog was holding a ring in its mouth. He waited under a tree, moving the hand the trunk was hiding. Wait a little, he said. I don't have to throw you something every time. The dog didn't put the ring down. It turned once in the space it was in. When the shade moved with the wind it looked as though the dog had moved with it. The dog had only come to rest on its other leg.

Somebody turned a skateboard over and sat next to me. His elbow came farther out than the back of the bench. I moved a little with my hand between it and the eggs. When he saw me move he asked if I was waiting for anybody. No. I only didn't want anything knocking into the eggs. He said he'd sit on the outside, then. The wind would be the same either way. He shifted with his board between his feet. One wheel was still on a sloping piece of the pavement. I didn't move the wheel for him. He tried with his heel and found a way to put it down. We sat for a few minutes like that. He looked at his part of the street. I watched to see whether the white car could get round the corner with only one lane left. Neither of us took anything out of a bag to show the other.

It was easier going back than it had been coming over. The barriers were still there. What had been one line of people was two now; somebody had put the second frame a little farther left. The person painting the crossing stood on the far side, bent down to peel up a piece of tape. The shade from the tree got there before he did. Nobody was sitting on the bench anymore. The shadow went over the stripe he'd painted without stopping on the next one. I waited until he pulled up the strip in front of him. Then he waved a hand, and I went over at the end where the egg shop was. I didn't use the gap that had opened. Nobody asked me for an opinion, and I didn't tell the person behind me to come after me.

At home I peeled back the paper along the seam and lifted the three eggs out of the tray. None of them had cracked. I'd been holding up the bundle from underneath all the way home. Only a moment after I put it down, my wrist made as if to lift it again. It wasn't going to slip away anymore. I pushed the paper to one side, shook out my hand and went to turn on the tap. I could keep three eggs till Monday, or have one now. Nobody had asked me to count what I'd want that far ahead. I chose the smallest and cracked it lightly on a bowl. The yolk was whole. The shell hadn't split in the long line I'd expected. I put the other two in the fridge and left a space by the temperature dial, big enough for something else to move into if I wanted. It didn't have to be a third egg. Later I wiped up a drop of water from beside the table leg, the one that had missed my shoe, with a corner of the dishcloth. I didn't put the two coins in the same place: one went in the dish; the other was still in my pocket. What I'd be buying next wasn't up to them yet.
