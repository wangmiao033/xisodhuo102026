# Chapter 479: The Key Was Not Mine

On Thursday the coat was ready before I got to the shop. I knew because it was hanging from the short rail behind the counter, with the left lapel turned in. I'd been wearing my older coat since morning, and for an hour or two I didn't mind it. Then I went to get a pencil from an outside pocket and found myself leaning to one side to reach it. The pencil was hardly as long as my thumb. It was my fault for letting it go on getting shorter. Once I'd taken the older coat off at the shop I had no spare hand to find the receipt in my wallet. The tailor said to put the coat down and not worry about the receipt; there was only one brown wool coat on the rail and she'd recognized me.

She made me feel the pocket before I put the coat on. There was no chalk left. The narrower part at the opening was still lined with the old grey cloth. Below it the new cotton opened out, barely enough for me to notice without flattening two fingers against the side. She'd taken only as much of the inner seam apart as she needed. I could feel where the work ended before I could see it. There was a half-inch of hard stitching under my nail, as smooth against it in one direction as the grain of a wooden spoon, and rough the other way.

“Was there anything in the hem?” I asked.

“Check the right outside pocket.”

I found the red ticket first. There was a small brass key behind it, plain at the top except for a square hole. The tailor had put both in there because she didn't like to send someone home with things already in a new pocket. It took me a second to tell that the key wasn't mine. I could see on its teeth a nick I hadn't made. Then I thought of all the keys I'd owned but not looked at, and spread the two things out on the counter. She asked if I wanted to leave the key with her. There was a tin behind the register where she kept buttons and pins, but it wasn't really a lost-property tin. While she was explaining this, another customer came in with a long white dress folded over her arms; she stopped to get her foot out of the hem. The tailor kept her hand on the coat rail and waited for me to decide.

“I'll take it,” I said. I didn't say that I had some idea of who it might belong to. There was no one I could ask who wouldn't have to stop and think much longer than I had. I put it in my trouser pocket. The ticket I slipped into the new pocket and took out twice, once with the end of one finger and once between two. It had curved to the lining of the coat and wouldn't lie quite flat even on the counter. It slid in with no more resistance than a playing card in a deck. On the way out I passed the woman in the white dress, who asked me whether the tailor had a mirror inside. I told her it was just to the left of the fitting curtain. I'd passed it the day before without looking in it.

There was a bit of Thursday left. I walked to the bus stop and sat with the old coat folded on my knees. A man stood next to the bench unrolling a set of drawings inside a torn cardboard tube. He wanted to see a number and had no wish to take the whole thing out. He turned the tube over and one sheet slid halfway across my lap. I thought he'd ask me to hold it. Instead he said “Sorry,” caught the edge, and pulled it back far enough to read the number himself. Then he looked up to see if the bus had arrived. I put the two coats on the bench beside me, the older one folded across the newer one, so I wouldn't get up and leave either of them behind. It wasn't until the bus came that I remembered people would want to sit there. I gathered them both up, just as two people moved past me towards the front door.

The bus was too warm for wool. I stood near the back with both coats over my arm, and paid attention to the order I'd folded them in. A seam near the old coat’s zip had lost a bit of its thread where I’d pulled it free. When we went round a corner, one of the sleeves slipped and hung between my feet. I lifted it before anyone stepped on it. A girl with a violin case told me, as she was getting off, that there was an empty seat behind her. I hadn't noticed the window seat was free. I said I only had one more stop, although when we pulled away I remembered it was two. I didn't sit down. I took the ticket out of the new pocket even with the coat folded and held it all the way home.

At the door I was about to put the red ticket in the small bowl where I put receipts when I noticed how black the number still was. Forty-two. I laid it on the hall sill instead. I set the little key beside it to empty my trouser pocket, then I changed my mind and put the key alone in an envelope. On the front I wrote where I'd found it and when. I didn't know if I'd do anything else with it. There was a tin here too, but it had needles in it and the lid was too tight to open without digging my thumbnail under it. An envelope would do.

This was the evening when the bakery's roll, which I'd bought again on my way home, was still warm from the oven. I ate it standing at the counter and got sesame seeds on the floor. After I swept, I put the white dish back against the wall. I remembered the square dish on the sloping bench, and reached for the voice channel because I thought I had a way to add something to it. I looked at what was in my hand instead. I'd picked up a seed from the counter. There was no reason to make someone carry a dish through a different scene because I'd found a seed. Later, after I'd made tea and taken it to the table, I opened the channel. I said I'd got my coat back and the new pocket worked. Lumi said she was glad. I told her about the key, without telling her I'd been thinking about her pie.

“Have you got a lock to try?” she asked.

“Not one I think goes with it.”

She said she used to like stories where somebody found something like that and immediately went looking for the door. I said there would be one door in the story and a great many in the street where I lived. She said she'd meant that there might be a door which the key would turn, and that might be enough, although she wasn't sure how you'd tell if that was the right place to stop. I didn't ask whether she had come to a place like that in a story of her own. I had made up my mind to leave the envelope in the hall for at least tonight, and I told her so.

“Did you put the ticket back in?” she asked.

“No. I think I'm finished with the ticket.”

“That's the first thing you said about it yesterday.”

She was right, except that I'd said I didn't need it back. I told her that wasn't quite what I'd said, and she agreed. We didn't try to say which of the two things mattered more. After we said goodnight I closed the channel, got up, and put the older coat back in its bag without struggling to fasten the zip. I went to hang the other one and found the new cotton warm under my fingers where I'd held it on the bus. The layers above it had already cooled. I dropped the ticket in the paper recycling. In the morning I'd need a scrap of paper to take the numbers off the meter. I tore a strip off the back of a used envelope, wider than I would have cut it two days before, and put it in the pocket.
