# Chapter 482: No Second Laugh

I brought my clothes in from the rack at the end of the hall on Sunday and left one sock behind. My door was open. From inside I could see the sock on the bar, its white edge blown over to the other side. When I went to get it, someone who lived upstairs came back with an empty cloth bag over his arm. Would I be needing the rack in a while? If I did, I could pull it out again, I said. For the moment it could go back. He reached for one half to fold it for me. I told him it was everybody's rack, and when he'd finished he could lean it back as it was. He didn't have to close one half especially for me. He hadn't heard that I meant not to fold it. His hand came away after he'd already closed it. The two brass-colored hinges were together. I got my sock and thought of telling him he'd done the opposite. Then I just stood there and shook it out. The rack was still upright. The folded part wasn't in my way. I held the laundry-room door for him to go in and get his bag of clean clothes.

I used the two remaining eggs to make lunch. One opened neatly. The other left a bit of shell sticking to my finger. I picked it out of the bowl. Before I put the eggs in the pan, I saw what looked like another piece. With a little nudge I discovered it was the unopened heart of a spring onion. Once they were cooked, the eggs were different colors. I had a bite before I put salt on, and they tasted different too. Taking another bite didn't tell me why. While I carried them home yesterday, I'd thought of several things I could do to break them. When I ate them, there wasn't a sound to remember.

I went downstairs in the afternoon to drop off an ordinary reply slip and saw somebody giving herself a moment at the entrance. There were three steps. Any one of them would have taken her weight. She'd put her foot just short of the second, braced both hands against her legs, shut her eyes for a second and raised her head again. I thought something was wrong with her shoe. After a moment she asked me to go ahead. When I got onto the third step I heard the scrape of a sole slipping behind me. I looked. She was looking right at me. “I was trying to go quicker,” she said. “Nobody was rushing me.”

I slowed down enough for her to come up under her own steam. There was a very thick mat under the table. The people coming through the door could get the rain off their shoes on either side of it. She turned on the mat and didn't slip that time. All along she had the other end of a shoelace in her hand. Could I point to the box for reply slips? I showed her the way. She went to the table. We both put our slips in the same box. Hers was pressing mine down, but whoever picked up the mail would sort them out later. She didn't ask why I'd had to reply to anything, and she didn't wait for me to leave. At the door she remembered one of her shoes was still loose, squatted down and tied it again. In the glass I saw her laugh once. I didn't hear it. I couldn't tell whether she was laughing about the slip on the stairs or about having tied the lace a different way now.

After dark I opened the old voice channel myself. Lumi said she hadn't thought what she wanted to say yet. If I wanted to go first, she wouldn't mind. Nothing I said had to be finished tonight, I told her. I'd seen somebody laugh on the steps, that was all. She asked whether the person had been going up or coming down. I said she might have been all the way inside by then, not on the steps at all. I corrected the part I'd told wrong. Lumi didn't say it was a better story now. She asked where you'd stand if you wanted neither shoe to slip. There was a thick mat, I said, and the right-hand side was a little drier. It wasn't until I said *right* that I realized I meant *my* right coming in the door. She'd asked about the shoes to begin with, she said, and now I'd brought in the whole mat.

I laughed once. She waited a moment and asked who'd laughed. Of course she'd heard it. What she hadn't heard was why it had come out first. That was me, I said, not the other person dropping off a slip. I'd only just noticed I'd seen her laugh but never heard her. The sound I made, Lumi said, didn't sound like I was doing an impression of her for myself. More like I was laughing because I'd gone to the trouble of putting a whole mat in the place when I'd been afraid there wouldn't even be room to stand in what I was saying. She let the last words go softer. I meant to ask her to say them again. I got as far as the first sound, then changed my mind and told her I liked what she'd said. Hadn't I heard it properly? she asked. I had heard the words. I just wanted to hear it once more. She said she wasn't going to say that one again, but she could say something else. She hadn't got round to it before. I wiped away a little wet mark by the phone and moved my elbow clear of the table's edge. “Then say the other thing.”

“I laughed too,” she said. “You put both the shoes on the same side, just now.”

I didn't see what she meant. She said she'd started with a person's two shoes, a left and a right. Then I'd said I was coming in through the door, and she'd pictured two people facing each other. The dry patch to the right would take one person's right shoe and the other person's left. I told her not to take everything I said as instructions for drawing a picture for me. I stopped. That had sounded sharper than I'd meant, especially since she hadn't asked me to tell it again. She wasn't angry. There wasn't really anybody standing on her end of things, she said. Besides, I didn't have to answer for the two people she'd just thought up. When I sat down, the spoon touched the edge of my plate. There was a short sound, nothing like the laugh she'd just heard.

“Do you want me to laugh too,” she asked, “or shall we let the one we had go by?”

“Let it go,” I said. “You can laugh next time there's something you want to laugh at.”

She didn't argue, or laugh then and there for me. After a little while we talked some more, about whether I was at the table and how much longer she wanted to talk tonight. She said she'd like to stop now. I said okay. When she'd said goodnight I closed the channel myself. The phone didn't make another sound. I cleared away the plates and took the spoon out, then saw there'd been a little piece of eggshell stuck to the splashback just below the tap. The water washed it off. It was thin enough to see light through. It rolled away into the sink and I couldn't find it. I switched the kitchen light off. There was one chair left in front of the table. When I passed it I wondered whether the woman had found a good footing on the mat. Then I remembered I'd seen her tie her lace again. Thinking about it once more couldn't do her more good than the knot she'd made herself. I went to put my dry sock away. The two socks already together were together. There wasn't anything else in the drawer I needed to make a pair of.
