# Chapter 477: The Ticket in the Hem

The woman at the vegetable stall gave me a little red ticket with a forty-two printed on it. There was no queue when she gave it to me, but the man who weighed things was out the back carrying a sack of potatoes, and the woman said I'd be glad of the ticket if he took much longer. I put it in the left inside pocket of my coat. I liked that pocket because it was just the width of two fingers and because I could take a slip of paper out without undoing more than one button. It wasn't much use for anything larger. A few winters back I'd carried a boiled sweet in it for a week and bitten off a corner of the wrapper by mistake when I finally ate it.

By the time the man came back with the potatoes there were seven of us. He said, “Forty-one,” to a woman who had just arrived.

“That's me,” said a man already standing at the scales, and showed him his ticket.

I opened my coat to get mine. There was nothing between my fingers and the pocket mouth. I tried with my index finger pointing down, which was how I usually caught the top edge of the paper. This time my fingernail kept going. It came up against a rough space under the cloth. I took my hand out and went through the outside pockets quickly, as though the ticket might take that opportunity to change places. I found a fifty-cent piece I hadn't known about. The woman who had given me the ticket watched me put it back.

“You're forty-two,” she said.

“I'm trying to find the evidence.”

“You can try while he's weighing your leeks.”

He put the leeks on the scales with the white ends all pointing the same way. There were three in the bunch, and the third was long enough to lie across two scales if people still used two scales. He twisted it sideways until it didn't touch the counter. I bought the bunch and four potatoes, including one no bigger than an egg. Then I went to check the ground where I'd been standing. There was a piece of red leaf from a rubber mat by the stall. I picked it up and put it at the edge of the mat without saying that I'd thought it was something else.

On the bus home I thought I heard the ticket. There was a thin stiffness at the bottom of my coat whenever I sat back. A woman with a flat box full of cakes asked whether I wanted to change seats with her. I hadn't noticed the space beside me was against the warm panel. I said yes, and when I stood up I heard a noise close to my left knee. The woman put the box on her lap without tilting it and left me the aisle seat. I pressed the cloth between my hands. Whatever was inside was no longer near the pocket. I could feel a rectangular edge through the wool until the bus went over a bump. After that I had to wait until I got home to find it again.

I set the vegetables under the window, hung the coat from the back of a chair, and unbuttoned it all the way. I could see the bottom of the pocket then. The grey lining had worn through just above the stitching. There was enough fabric behind it to keep the ticket from falling out onto the floor. At first I thought I could reach through the hole, which would save me having to pick open the hem. I pushed two fingers through. The hole got a little bigger without making a sound. I could tell, when I took my fingers out, from the pale flakes on both knuckles. One of the flakes stuck to a wet spot on my index finger where the leeks had dripped. I put the chair back square against the table and went to wash my hands.

While the water ran, I remembered buying the coat. The man at the secondhand shop had worn an electric-blue fleece under a brown apron. He'd told me the coat was too narrow for his shoulders. I wondered then whether his judgment of my shoulders was part of the price, since he had asked for considerably more than I had brought. He took exactly what I offered him, then asked if I'd like the things that were in it. There was a lozenge and a paper clip, both in one outside pocket. I asked if he knew who they belonged to. He said he'd put them in there to remember to look for a pencil. He took the lozenge back, but I kept the paper clip and used it for something I no longer remembered. I didn't look in the inside pocket till the first day I wore the coat to work. It had been easier to lift my arm when I stopped wearing a thick sweater under it.

I put the egg-sized potato aside because it wouldn't take as long to cook as the other three. There were flat cuts on the stem ends of the leeks; at some point all three had been growing out of the ground together. I went back to the coat, laid it on the table and held one corner higher than the other. The stiffness in the hem slid sideways. I got as far as feeling a sharp paper corner just below the inner seam. Then it wouldn't move any farther. I left it there and bent down to see whether the wool had worn through anywhere else. It hadn't. The sleeve ends were shiny, not threadbare. There were old stitches around one button, in a slightly different brown from the others, and the seam inside the right cuff went on a slant that suggested somebody had shortened it by hand.

The small alterations shop on my way to work had a light in the back after the bakery next to it shut. There was a chair in the window with an unpadded seat where customers stood for hems. I'd watched a child place both feet on it in yellow boots and then get told, not unkindly, to keep one foot on the floor until it was her turn. I didn't know what a pocket would cost. I wrote the shop's opening time on the back of a vegetable receipt and left it by my keys so I could go on my way home the next day, if I wasn't late.

It was very quiet then. I didn't want to try the coat on again until the chair was clear of the stray flakes. I brushed them off, took the leeks to the sink, and rinsed the loose dirt from their roots. One strand stayed caught in a fold of the skin at my wrist. I had to lift it with the corner of a tea towel. This went on for a while, the leeks making the tap water faintly green, the little potato coming clean with no more than a rub. I washed that one although I hadn't decided whether to cook it. In the end I put one leek and the little potato in a pan, cut the other potatoes into quarters, and added those too. I ate the small one whole. It was only small until I put it in my mouth.

The coat went on a hanger eventually. I could have moved the ticket back to the place above the hem. Instead I picked up the change from the stall and used the coin I'd found to fill out the exact amount I owed for yesterday's tea, which a colleague had paid for when the machine wouldn't read my card. I put the money in a little envelope on the table. Tomorrow it would go in a trouser pocket. In the coat, down where I'd last left it, the paper didn't make a sound. The hanger stretched the shoulders farther apart than the chair had, so I picked the coat up once at each shoulder to settle it. On the second try I felt, through the weight of the wool, the faintest dry tap as the ticket crossed the width of the hem.
