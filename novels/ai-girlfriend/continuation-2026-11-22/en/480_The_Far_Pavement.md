# Chapter 480: The Far Pavement

On Friday after work, Meg and I walked as far as the bus stop together. She had an errand in the other direction but decided to part company with me after the first set of lights. The signal across from us was tipped a little downward. I couldn't make out whether the little green man had begun to flash. Meg wouldn't decide for me. Look at the cars, she said. When I looked, a long truck was stopped behind the line, blocking my view of the second lane. Neither of us stepped out. By the time the lights went round again, the mud on the truck's tires had dried gray.

At the junction there was a shop that sold lights. Usually all I saw were the hanging glass shades. Today somebody at the door was taking down the posters from the month before. When she took one away, I caught a small street scene in the corner behind it. The road was folded out of gray paper, and the buildings were made of pieces of thin wood with faded squares stuck on the windows. On the left two eaves leaned so close together nobody could have walked between them. There was a pavement on the right, two pencil marks with a bend in them that was sharper on paper than I could have walked in the street.

“Is that the old reading room you go to?” Meg asked.

I recognized a post at the corner. It could have belonged to any corner in town. I said I'd need to go around and look from the far pavement. She took a look at the time and said she'd better go the other way now. If I found out, I didn't have to go to the trouble of telling her. We said goodbye. I stayed to look a little longer without crossing. The glass reflected a bus that had drawn up behind me into the paper street. It floated over the roofs. They were nowhere near big enough for it. After the bus moved on, I still couldn't see which bit Meg had meant.

The woman taking down the posters rolled one up and put a rubber band round it. She had a gray shop apron on with a little white thread showing at the pocket. The poster had left some glue behind. She tried getting her nail underneath it twice without lifting it. I meant to go in and ask who had made the model. It wasn't until I had reached the door handle that I realized I hadn't thought of what to say when I got inside. She came out the other door with a wet cloth and asked if I'd like to look at the lights. No, I said, I was only looking at the model. She didn't try to get me in. She took half a step away from the window and pointed with the cloth, just clear of the glue. “Try from here. You won't be looking through the blurry bit.”

I bent down a little. At the corner there was a road that didn't go all the way round. A piece of thick gray paper sloped through a gap about as wide as my finger. There wasn't anything underneath. The reason the two buildings crowded each other wasn't that the street was narrow. The one on the right had been moved, exposing a little hole that would ordinarily have been covered. She watched me looking at it. The maker had left it that way so bits could be lifted out for repairs later, she told me. She didn't know if there had been another corner there before, or if it was based on an actual street. “I only wipe the outside. They don't want me touching anything small in there. I couldn't reach it if I tried.”

I stood up. For a moment I wanted to find a smudge under one of the lamps as proof there was somewhere she could reach. She hadn't said anything at all that needed proving. A customer called from inside. The woman transferred the wet cloth to her other hand. She said I could go on looking from out there; it wasn't necessarily any clearer if I went in. I stood aside and left a way to the door. She drew a box for a lightbulb off a shelf with one gloved hand. Under the edge of the roof on the right there was a tiny mark, the shape of a letter. I suddenly remembered there was no post by the reading room windows. It wasn't the place we'd thought, or perhaps it hadn't been any place in particular.

I crossed the street anyway. On that side a man was studying a map by the curb. The top edge had folded over. He hadn't found anything to hold it open. I didn't ask what road he was looking for. I went a couple of steps toward the stop, then turned back. From over here the glass did indeed cover the model in a reflection. The entire block pressed up against the window. Nothing of the paper houses showed through. There was a row of narrow numbers beside the lighting-shop door. In a gap between two trams I finally read them. I'd been past the address more times than I remembered; I'd never needed to know the number before.

The man with the map looked up and asked if I knew which way it was to the car park. I said I couldn't swear to it. There was a blue marking on a white sign, but I hadn't come along this side yesterday. Which way was the writing facing, he asked. I moved so he could look past my shoulder. Then he folded his map. From where I stood it wasn't much of an arrow. Ordinarily I'd have gone and made sure, and come back to show him the right way. My bus was nearly there. I said if he went that way he'd be able to take a look at the front of the sign. He looked at the bus and said all right, thanks. When he went on by himself, he didn't put the map back into his bag.

I got on. Not until it was time to get off did I discover there was a banana in the bag that I hadn't eaten that morning. I'd been feeling it lightly against my side since just after the bridge. It hadn't been the handrail pushing through my coat. I ate it under a streetlamp. There was a line down the middle that had gone soft. On either side it tasted the way it was supposed to. Nothing at home needed me right that minute. I put the walnut-colored peel into the bin and noticed a string of it sticking to my hand. I washed it away first. While I stood under the kitchen light I thought of trying to tell someone about the street inside the shop window. Only a person who had come near enough would believe that corner was there.

I remembered that Meg hadn't wanted me to report back. She hadn't said she would never be interested in what I'd found, either. The next time we saw each other, if we came round to it, I could tell her I hadn't recognized it. Getting my phone out just to tell her now would be like sending her a map and asking her to walk home over again. I didn't want to do that, and I didn't. The lamp threw plenty of light over the table. In the corner of the window there was a piece of glass I hadn't cleaned properly. The blur there was old enough I couldn't tell how wide the finger had been. I thought of getting a cloth. After I'd stood a moment, I saw it would stop being in my way if I stood somewhere else. I went one step sideways.

After supper I carried the bowl to the kitchen and knocked the handle of my spoon on the door. It made a noise on the side I thought I wouldn't touch. When I came back, at either end of my table the paper street was equally far away. I hooked my shoes out a little from under my chair and turned off the lamp. A car passed in the street. The window brightened and went dark. The people on the second floor hadn't drawn their blinds. I couldn't see inside from where I stood, only a mouthful of light they'd left in the channel beneath the window. I stayed until it was no longer moving. Then I remembered I hadn't brought my water. Before I went into the kitchen I had one last look. I hadn't changed places. The light hadn't come any nearer on that account.
