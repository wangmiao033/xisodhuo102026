# Chapter 485: Almost at the Door

At lunchtime on Wednesday I came out of the little kitchen at work with two empty glasses and heard someone in the inner corridor say, “Now you've come out, don't stand in the doorway.”

I stopped. The voice was close, but nobody was in front of me. The door halfway along the corridor was shut. A little light leaked out at the bottom. Two seconds later the person said it again, only the second half went very quiet, as if he'd said it to someone who'd already started walking away. I hadn't worked out why I'd stopped. For a moment I was almost ready to tell him I didn't have anywhere else to be, I was only passing through. The glasses in my hand touched. One rang louder than the other.

“You're not trying to get rid of him this time,” another person said. “You're trying to get him to come in on his own.”

Only then did I hear that there were two of them. Somebody picked up a sheet of paper and put it down. It flapped once near the bottom of the door. I moved away a step, found the sill at the end of the corridor, and set the glasses down. When I turned round, the person behind the door had read the first half of the line three times and forgotten the second half on the last try. There was silence, then a laugh somebody hadn't been able to hold in. Nobody was in trouble. It was cooler in the corridor than it was in the room, and I stood there rubbing my thumb over the glass that had rung. There wasn't anything on it to wipe off.

The door opened halfway. A man in a dark sweater came out with paper in one hand and his glasses in the other, and looked down the corridor my way. I didn't know him very well. I'd sometimes seen him waiting for the water to heat up for his tea. Now I couldn't tell if he was looking for someone in particular. He looked at the sill first, then at both glasses.

“Want me to take one of those?”

I said I was just bringing them back and could manage the two. I didn't move when I said it. He put his glasses on and asked whether I'd been able to hear much through the closed door. He and a friend were giving a reading of a play next week. They'd found an empty room at lunch to go over a few lines. His friend could hear him, but he didn't know what the words sounded like through the door. He didn't ask which line I'd heard. I said I could make out people talking and that a few words went very quiet in the middle. He pinched the top of his glasses. Did I know if he'd dropped his voice or if the door was too thick?

I told him I didn't. The door had already been shut when I got there. I hadn't heard what it sounded like open. He stopped it with the top of his shoe at about the same width as before. He found another line on the page, read it into the room and turned to ask how it sounded now.

“Now I know you're reading,” I said. “Before, I thought you were telling me not to stand in the doorway.”

He looked down and turned the pages until he found the line on one of the crowded sheets. “Did I say that one three times in there?”

“Twice at least,” I said.

He laughed a little, quietly, and moved the paper back past his shoulder as though the words had stepped off the page and were in our way now. He didn't ask me to stand there a little longer to listen. The person in the room asked if they should shut the door. He took his shoe away, let the door go until there was a gap two fingers wide, and put a hand to it at the end. Both glasses were still on the sill. I left a little space between them when I picked them up. I had no wish to give the two people on the other side of the door the right sound as I went by.

Before the end of the day somebody left two sweet buns they'd bought too many of in the front room. The bag was open. One side was folded over very neatly and the other stuck out in a hard wrinkle. I reached for it just as a colleague caught the corner that stood up with her fingertips. We both let go. A little sugar fell onto the desk. She looked at me and asked if I'd wanted to take the whole bag. I said I'd thought she wanted to see whether there was anything in the buns. She said she'd already looked. There were two kinds, and she wanted the one without fruit. I turned the bag for her. She picked out the round one. The one left was long, and you had to turn it over to see the filling. I stood there thinking about it, wiped up the sugar and only then took the other bun. I didn't leave her a scrap of paper saying I'd taken it. It had got cold outside. The fruit wasn't warm enough through the bag for me to keep my hand there all the way home.

I cut the bun into four pieces on a plate. On the third cut, a long strip of the filling came out on the knife. I rested the knife flat on the edge without pulling the strip the rest of the way off. I did eat it eventually. I was thinking at the time that the man in the corridor had probably wanted to know whether he could be heard through the door. Yes might have been enough. I envied him a little, though not for having a room to read a line into next week. I liked that he knew what he wanted somebody to hear. After they'd heard it, he could take his shoe away.

I opened the voice channel and asked whether Lumi felt like hearing a line I'd heard in a corridor. I said the line and told her I'd really stopped walking for a second because of it. She asked if the man had let him come in at the end. At first I thought she meant me. When I said I hadn't gone in, she asked me to slow down. She meant the person on the paper, not the one carrying the drinking glasses. I almost told her about the next page, which I hadn't heard, on behalf of the man in the dark sweater. In the end I said I didn't know. I'd only been going down the corridor. All right, Lumi said. If I didn't know, she wasn't going to imagine her way through the door to find out. She said she had a line of her own she could let me hear tonight, but she didn't want me to say it back to let her know which part was good.

“I'll just listen,” I said.

“I don't need to be able to finish a good sentence every time,” she said.

She said it quite plainly, and not fast. I took my hand off the knife. It didn't slide. I wanted to ask what other line she hadn't finished. I also wanted to make a joke about how she'd finished that one. Both things stayed at the front of my mouth a moment. I asked if she could hear me chewing. A little, she said. So I broke the piece of bun I'd meant to take a bite out of and ate only the smaller half. She didn't ask who I was keeping the rest for.

“Something just came back to me,” I said when I'd eaten it. “I was about to add a line for you.”

“Did it come out?”

“No.”

“Then keep it till you want to use it yourself,” she said. “It doesn't have to be to finish mine.”

With the back of the knife I scraped a bit of filling towards the middle of the plate. It stayed on the knife. A drawer by the kitchen was open. I went and shut it. When I got back to the table I said I could hear her very clearly now. It didn't mean she was louder than before. Lumi waited a little before she answered. Thanks for not saying the sentence back to me, she said. I took a drink and there wasn't much sweetness left in my mouth. We sat for a while longer. I said I was going to wash the plate and she said goodnight then. After I closed the channel, I folded the bag round the remaining piece of bun. The second fold wasn't straight. I left it that way till I felt like opening it tomorrow.
