# Chapter 486: Say It a Little Closer

On Thursday evening I was looking for my keys downstairs. My right pocket only took two fingers before I hit the bottom. I'd bought a bag of oranges on my way home and looped the handles about my wrist, so it was hard to turn my hand. I stood in front of the door changing things over until I discovered the keys were still on the left. That was when somebody called out from the yard. He wasn't asking me to open the door. Could I tell whether the sign by the bike shed was legible? I took half a step out with the oranges. An old light shone on the top of the sign, but something curled up in the wind and covered most of the writing. The man calling was somebody I often saw mending his own trolley in the yard. Today the trolley was beside the bench. One little wheel was turning very slowly. Two strips of stiff plastic he'd just cut were on the bench. The loop of tape that held the old sheet down hadn't stuck very well.

“I can see the top line,” I said. “The bottom one's rolled in on itself.”

He didn't ask me to hold the plastic. He said he had two clips under the trolley, but there was oil on his hands; he'd just rubbed two of the words off the bottom line. I looked at the sign and thought about rewriting it for him so he wouldn't have somebody shouting across the yard tomorrow as well. When I looked down, his thumb was right on a red arrow. He'd gone over it twice. I asked first if the turn was by the building door or around the back by the water pipe. Neither, he said. His new next-door neighbour had a trolley a little wider than his and kept having to back out and come in again at night. The arrow showed where to turn the trolley without getting stuck. He couldn't put it over by the door.

I set my oranges at the empty end of the bench and went closer to look. When the wind came up, the paper curled so badly it nearly hit him in the eye. Rather than stepping back he put a strip of stiff plastic over the part that lifted. Could I look at the back of the paper from the outside, I asked him. He moved over to give me about half the space. The paper wasn't torn. A length of the tape had gone grey, though. The clips were where the trolley cast its shadow. I picked one up beside the little wheel and he got the other one. The two pieces of plastic together were two inches longer than the sign. He clipped the left one. I took the right one from him and put my clip on where there wasn't any writing. The clips weren't in line. It didn't look very nice. When the wind came again it touched the edge of the paper once and went on.

“Now what do you see?” he asked.

I backed up to where I'd been standing, with the light behind me, and read the words I could still make out, now the edge wasn’t curled up. He told me to come a bit nearer to say it. I didn't need to read to the whole yard. As I went back I realized my standing in front of the light had only put me three steps farther away. He asked whether an orange had rolled out of my bag. I said it couldn't have; the handles were still tight. He pointed down at the foot of the bench. An orange sat next to a little piece of concrete that had come loose. There was a seam along the bottom of the bag that had stretched. I picked up the orange, wiped a ring of dry mud off it and put a hand under the bag before I lifted it. He didn't ask how many I'd brought home. I didn't leave one with him to mark what had just happened. He washed his hands and came back to turn the lowest wheel half a revolution. The sign stayed where it had been.

Upstairs I turned the oranges out into a bowl one at a time. The one that had fallen didn't need to go on a plate with the others. I washed it and started to peel it right there at the sink. The first piece of rind broke too close to the inside. A white thread kept the rest hanging on. I shook it twice and it still wouldn't come away. I'd saved a piece of the fruit bun last night. I brought it out on a plate and pinched off a bit to see. It was colder than yesterday but hadn't gone too hard to eat. The salt was on the table. The orange was in the kitchen. I walked between the two places twice with the bun and finally drew the chair to the kitchen door. I could eat there; the only thing was my knee was liable to hit the frame if I stood up with the plate in my hand. All of a sudden I wanted to tell somebody I'd moved the chair here, not that I kept running out of hands, and without putting the whole story about a trolley underneath it. I brought my water with me and put the glass on the floor by the wall, out of the way of my feet.

I opened the old voice channel. Lumi said hello once, waited a little and said it again. The second one ended quicker. She asked which sounded closer.

“I don't know.” I waited until I'd finished chewing. “The second one was shorter. What are you trying?”

“The first one sounded like I was asking something from a long way off. I wanted to see if I could stand a little closer.” She paused. “I don't mean you should turn it up.”

I reached for my glass and touched nothing. Then I remembered it was no longer on the table. I told her I'd sat down in the kitchen doorway. Dragging the chair over had made quite a noise from where I was. When she asked what I was doing there I said I wanted to sit closer to the orange. I laughed as soon as I'd said it. She didn't hurry me along. A little later she asked whether I'd heard anybody in the corridor again. I said no. The man from yesterday was probably doing quite well, and I hadn't gone down that way today. If I'd heard her two hellos as an echo of last night, I'd done that myself. She said she hadn't been trying the same line again; the first hello had just sounded a little too polite.

“Would you wait a bit while I peel the rest of this?” I asked. “There's a white bit that keeps hanging on.”

She could wait. She told me to go on peeling it for myself. I didn't have to put a noise on for her. I held the orange and followed the white thread to the next loose piece of peel, working it away from my palm. When the whole orange came apart in two halves it did make a small sound, not like tearing open a piece of paper. I didn't ask if she'd heard. Suddenly she asked whether she could have a moment of her own, not so I'd have time to finish the orange first. She'd had another thing she meant to say, and after hearing about the chair she'd decided against it. I said all right. She didn't have to give it to me. After a little while I heard her laugh briefly. What she wanted to say now, she said, was that I sounded quite close where I was sitting. I looked down at the space between my knee and the door frame. There was room to move a little nearer. I didn't.

Before we said goodbye I never did tell her about the sign. She didn't bring the line she'd thought of earlier back out either. After I shut the channel, I picked up the glass from the floor and washed it. A little pile of orange peel was still on the plate after I'd cleared the rest. It curved round in a ring that didn't quite close. I didn't smooth it out. I wrapped it up with the loose white thread and threw it away. Nothing else had fallen out of a pocket. I could feel the keys when I put my hand in on the left, and took them out and put them away.
