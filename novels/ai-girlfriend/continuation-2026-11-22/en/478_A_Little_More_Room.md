# Chapter 478: A Little More Room

The first thing the woman in the alterations shop asked me to do was put the coat back on. I'd taken it off before the bell above the door had finished ringing. I had both sleeves hooked over one forearm, and was about to turn the whole thing inside out on her counter. There was a pair of white trousers lying there with one leg rolled up. She put her hand on the folded leg to keep me from brushing against it.

“If it pulls, I need to see where,” she said.

“It doesn't pull. Things fall through.”

“Then I need to see how you put them in.”

She watched me unbutton the topmost button that was actually fastened, which was the second one from the top, and slide my hand in from above. The third button stayed done. I said I usually stopped with two fingers halfway into the pocket. She asked me to stop where I would if I had something in my hand. In the position I ended up in, my little finger caught on a buttonhole, and she pushed the wool aside with a fingertip so I could finish. I'd been drawing the tip of the paper down against the far edge of the pocket to get it going in the right direction. That was exactly where the cloth had worn through. I didn't want the opening moved farther towards my chest. I liked being able to reach it with the rest of the coat done up.

“Let's not move it, then,” she said. “But I can give you a little more room below the opening if I replace the bag.”

I reached for the outside pocket. She said the bag was the pouch on the inside, not a separate thing I had to buy. From a drawer she brought out two pieces of cotton, one black and one the colour of unbleached flour. The black was finer; she said the pale one would stand up better to a key, if I was in the habit of carrying one. I told her what I'd had in there yesterday. “Then I don't need to build you a pocket for a key,” she said, and let me hold the dark piece. It felt thin compared with my coat, but it had a faint tooth and no shine. I asked how deep it could go before I wouldn't be able to get a ticket back out. She didn't answer with a measurement. She held the cotton behind the torn lining and had me put my fingers in again; when I drew them halfway up, she pinched the fabric at the length we'd agreed to keep. The extra room was at the sides, out of sight.

At the desk I paid what she asked. It was less than I'd feared and more than the vegetables. The man behind me needed two new buttons on a pair of shorts. He had the old ones in his hand and put them down where the tailor could see the better preserved of the two. I was putting my receipt away when I remembered to mention the red ticket. I'd forgotten to mention it because I'd concentrated so hard on what kind of pocket I wanted. The tailor made no promise to recover it. The less I put in the pockets of the coat before leaving it with her, she said, the fewer places she would have to look for something that didn't belong to her.

I emptied the outside pockets onto my palm: keys, a square torn from a tissue, and a cap from a pen I didn't have with me. I had paid my colleague for the tea that morning, and the little envelope had gone into the wastepaper basket in the break room. The tailor took the coat by both shoulders, like somebody getting a child settled before cutting their hair, then laid it flat. It was cold enough outside that I nearly went back and asked whether I could bring it in the next day instead. She had already chalked two little bars on the lining. I had an older coat at home. I pulled my sweater down over my hands and went into the bakery to get warm.

The bakery had finished doing sandwiches. A boy was cleaning the crumbs out of the slotted board with a loose bristle brush. When I asked for one of the rolls by the glass, he pointed to the row behind them. The ones in front were wholemeal. I asked for wholemeal anyway. He set the brush aside and got a roll with tongs. It was not hot, not even in the middle, but I ate it in slow pieces outside the shop, where a draft slipped between the buildings and turned the bag inside out around what was left. I got to the corner with more flour on my sweater than I'd had all day wearing the coat.

At home I took three clothes pegs out of an earthenware jar and clipped the sweater to the drying rack. I thought the flour would be easier to get off when the sweater had aired a little. I didn't actually need the third peg. I used it to keep the other two from spreading apart on a sagging length of wire. The older coat was in a bag at the back of the wardrobe. Before looking for it, I opened the voice channel and told Lumi I'd been trying to take out a ticket from a pocket that had a hole in it.

“You don't need it back now, do you?” she asked.

“No. It was for getting served at a stall.”

“I thought it was for going somewhere.”

I told her which stall, and what I'd bought. She said I'd made it sound as if this was the first leek I'd ever bought, but it wasn't. I had mentioned leeks to her before; she didn't say when. I caught myself about to tell her that the ticket was the interesting part. Instead I told her how warm the panel on the bus had been, and that all the cakes in the box next to me had stayed level through the change of seats. I didn't know what kind they were. There wasn't a clear patch in the top of the box to see in.

“Can I tell you what I was going to say about the place you were going?” Lumi asked.

“Yes,” I said. I thought she was going to tell me which direction she'd imagined I'd taken.

“I'd pictured a school play,” she said. “You were sitting on a little bench waiting to go in. But it was the pie with the hole in it, not the pocket.”

It took me a moment to work out that we were no longer talking about the trip I'd taken. I asked where the pie came from. She said somebody on the bench had it in a square dish, and she didn't think he should have balanced it where the bench sloped. There wasn't any more of the scene, at least not any she wanted to tell. I was pleased the dish was square. I didn't tell her that my mother had had one like it, because the one that came to mind was the blue enamel roasting tin the woman upstairs had once lent me, and I could no longer remember its shape.

We spoke a little longer about whether a cold roll ought to be finished outdoors. Lumi said if I was already halfway through it she'd probably stop me from going back inside. She didn't like having to push past people to end up exactly where she'd been. I said I hadn't gone back in. Then I had to think how long I'd actually stayed in the doorway. I had stood there till the boy took the slotted board away, about half the roll, and walked the rest of the way with the bag open.

After goodnight I closed the channel and got the older coat down. There was a long thread caught in the zip, which I pulled out between my nails. Its pockets were large but set low; I had to bend my elbows almost straight to get my hands to the bottoms. I put it by the door. The paper receipt for the alterations was in my wallet, behind the window where I normally kept a bus ticket. The shop would have my coat back the next afternoon. Before I went to bed I shook the flour out of the sweater over the kitchen sink. Most of it went right down the plughole. A single piece of crust had caught in the knitted cuff. I picked that off with my fingers and ate it.
