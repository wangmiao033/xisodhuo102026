# Chapter 483: Which Monday?

A bus timetable blew past the stop sign on Monday morning. Before I could stoop for it, a woman in a blue apron caught it with the toe of her shoe. The wind had sent it halfway out of the shelter, and another inch would have put it in the gutter. She freed a hand to pass it to me. Only then did I see our route number and MONDAY TO FRIDAY printed across the top.

“Can you tell me if the seven forty-two still comes?” she asked.

I looked at the timetable behind glass. Same route, same Monday to Friday. In the seven o'clock row were only twenty-eight and fifty-six. The handle of her steel lunch tin wouldn't lie flat; she'd used a rubber band to fasten it to the lid. As she turned the tin in her hand it covered forty-two. She turned it back.

“Don't let that side get wet,” I said. “We've got two different times here.”

“That's why I asked you which one was Monday.”

She'd said it so straight I thought she was making fun of me. Then she took the paper back and raised the bottom edge flat. There was a little line of print in the fold. It said effective Monday the 12th. There was a line on the one behind the glass too: effective Monday the 19th. Neither had an end date. I ought to have known which date began this week. The night before, an email had come about an invoice next week, and all that was left in my head was next week and Monday. I unlocked my phone. The top said nineteen. I showed her. She ran a fingernail across the twelve on the paper.

“This one's from last week,” I said.

“Friday, this was what we had in the box and behind the glass.” She lifted her chin towards a little metal dispenser on the side of the shelter. There was a split in its lid. It used to hold timetables you could take home; there was nothing in it now. Ten minutes ago she'd been looking at the numbers behind the glass herself, she said. She'd stood out of the way for somebody asking for directions before she saw the old one under her feet. She didn't blame me for not knowing them apart. Whenever the buses changed, she had to warn somebody she'd be late.

I asked if she was due somewhere at a particular time. She looked at her lunch tin. She was opening the side door of a breakfast stall for somebody, and the key they needed today was with her. The other person had gone ahead by bicycle to get supplies. He couldn't wait until he found himself outside the locked door to ring her. She patted her pocket. Through the apron the key made a dull little noise.

“So we wait for fifty-six?” she asked.

I nearly said yes. A delivery van was backing into the lane behind us, its lights going on and off, rubbing out half of the fifty-six in the glass every time they came on. I was only going to work. Ten minutes either way wouldn't matter much. She had a locked door at the other end of her fifty-six. It wasn't enough for me to wait it out with her. I read the timetable in the glass from the beginning. The column on the left was departures from this stop. The one on the right was arrivals at Central Street. She was standing to my left and had thought I was reading her the right-hand column. I moved over so she could see the line between them. She pointed her tin towards the left. She could see it properly now.

The first thing to open on the public bus page on my phone was the live map. There wasn't a single bus on the route. She asked if that meant it had been canceled. I didn't know; I hadn't even worked out which direction the map was showing. When I reached the notices, there was a pinned one. During road work, the first two departures would leave from outside the east market gate. The temporary stop was round the corner on the same street. The notice about the move wasn't up in the shelter. On the little map under the notice, the two dots were practically on top of each other. She told me not to stare at the dots, just to point out where we were going. I stepped out of the shelter and pointed across the junction towards the market gate. I could see the blue roof over the entrance, but not a bus sign under it.

“I'll go over and wave back if I see it,” I said.

“Don't wave. I can't see that well.” She picked up the lunch tin and handed me the old timetable. “I didn't say I couldn't walk. Let's both go.”

I checked the time once before we reached the crossing. Eight minutes left. She didn't ask how many. When the light changed she lifted her tin so it wouldn't get in the way of somebody pushing a cleaning cart. There were two electric bikes parked under the blue roof at the market's east entrance. One was under a pink cover. We still hadn't seen a sign when a man leaned out from behind the cover and asked if we were waiting for a bus. He had two empty crates at his feet and had been sent over to work the temporary stop. He pointed at a post a little farther past the market gate. There was a yellow board strapped to it, yellow side facing the direction the bus would come from. We'd come up behind it and all we'd seen was gray.

Fifty-six was the next departure from there, he said. The first two buses listed on the timetable left from here, and after two stops they'd be back on their usual route. She asked about forty-two. He thought for a second. Last week it had been forty-two. This week it was fifty-six. She nodded and moved the key into the little upper pocket on her apron. While she got her phone out to call the stall, I showed him the timetable we'd found on the ground and asked if I could throw it away. Just give it to me, he said. That's fine. I shook the little bit of grit off the bottom edge and handed it over. Instead of dropping it in a crate by the bicycles, he folded it once and tucked it in his folder. A white edge was sticking out. You couldn't see the line that said Monday anymore.

While we waited, the woman said there was somebody at the next stall who'd lent the person expecting her a stool from inside. She'd gone ahead and asked if he wanted anything to eat. Don't buy anything, he'd said. You've got a meal in your hand. She put her phone away and stretched her arm a little. The tin was all right. It was the rubber band that had been working itself up for some time, and it snapped off one side of the handle. I made room for her to put it back. When the bus turned in, she didn't look at me. She put out a hand to make sure the driver saw her. I got on after her. Neither of us mentioned the two timetables.

After dinner I washed my plate and opened the voice channel. I told Lumi I'd run into two kinds of Monday. She asked if one was the morning one and the other hadn't got out of bed. They'd both turned out to be morning ones, I told her, at the same bus stop, one for today and the other for last week. I told her what the man at the temporary stop had said without trying to read her the little map. I didn't tell her I'd know at a glance which copy to trust next time.

“What if they hadn't changed the one behind the glass either?” she asked.

“I wouldn't stand there waiting for it to change itself.”

After I said it, I thought of the man folding the sheet into his folder instead of throwing it out. I'd thought handing it to him was the end of it. Now I could see how it might be useful to show it to whoever had changed the timetable. Or it might just be a bit of waste paper he'd put away. I hadn't seen any of that happen, so I didn't give it to her as the end of the story.

Lumi asked if the woman was still in a hurry afterwards. I said she'd sat at the very front so she would have two fewer rows to get past at her stop. I'd got off early. I didn't know when she'd reached the side door. When I finished, Lumi didn't supply the stops I didn't know about. There was something she wanted to say, she said. She thought for a while. She wouldn't say what it was, just that she didn't want to put it after this thing that had happened today. All right, I said. A little later I asked, if both Mondays were real, what name would she give it?

“Don't Step the Paper into the Water,” she said.

That was when I laughed. There wasn't any rain against the window. I heard her laugh too, and then I closed the channel.
