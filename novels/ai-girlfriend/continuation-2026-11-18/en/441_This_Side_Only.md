# Chapter 441: This Side Only

I left early on Monday with the empty-looking tote slapping against my arm. The old coat was folded inside. I had taken it out once before going downstairs, not to check the thread, but to look for the ticket I usually stuffed into an outside pocket. There was no ticket. There was an old receipt folded to the width of my little finger. I threw it away and left the loose inner pocket alone. The outside pockets had been enough when I wore the coat last week. If the shop couldn't take it today, I could go on wearing it like that.

Past the shoe shop, I saw a bright patch in the glass of the narrow door. The trousers were gone from the window. A strip showing the opening hours was taped along the bottom, with the morning light getting in behind the paper. I'd thought going early would get me there before anyone else. When I pushed the door open, a man at the counter was having the strap of a canvas bag measured. He held the bag up with one hand and put the other at various points along his shoulder, saying over and over that it didn't have to be that long. The woman with the tape measure waited for him to set the bag down flat.

I took half a step to the side. They weren't arguing about the same length. He wanted to know if he could sling it over his shoulder with one hand. She wanted to know if it would drag on the ground with anything heavy in it. In the end he produced a water bottle and put it in the bag, and they could look at the same bag at last. He pressed on the strap at his shoulder and stopped asking her to shorten it. She gave him back the bottle and waited until he'd gone before she looked at me.

“The pocket?”

“Yes. Have you got time to look today? It doesn't have to be today.”

She rolled up the tape. Her lips moved as though she might ask why I hadn't left it with her. Instead she said, “I've got two things promised this afternoon. Let me see what you've got first.”

I took the coat out and laid it on the counter the same way as last time. She put a hand under the pocket bag and turned a little of it out through the half inch that had already opened. She didn't undo the lining or pick up her scissors. There was a speck of lint stuck beside the loose thread. She picked it off, felt the bottom of the pocket, then pressed her thumb against both corners near the opening. I could see a row of holes underneath the old thread. Without the lamp I would never have noticed the crooked turn they took.

“Can you tell now?” I asked.

She put the pocket back and held up the open edge so I could see it. “It's only this seam that's come undone. The cloth underneath will take a stitch. I can close it along this edge without touching the rest of the lining. My thread's a shade darker. You'll see it when you turn the pocket out, not when you're wearing the coat. If you want me to run a stitch over the other side too, I can give you both prices.”

My hand was already halfway to the other side. Since the coat was on the counter anyway, it seemed like an opportunity to make the bit that looked all right a bit stronger. She let go. The cloth on that side went flat on its own. It had never given me any warning while I was wearing it. It hadn't asked me to hand an entire coat over to somebody else in advance.

“Just this side,” I said. “The way you said you'd do it. Leave the other one.”

She gave me a price for the small repair. On the evidence in front of her, she said, that would be all. I didn't have to pay up front. I could come after four, any time before closing. She took a card with a clip and started putting the work on a single line. Her pen stopped after “inner pocket.” She changed “one” to “open seam on left,” then asked if that was what I meant. I nodded. She handed me a handwritten collection slip without making me decide on the spot whether I'd ever want the lining replaced.

I tapped the slip twice against my palm. It was stiffer than it looked. She asked me whether I wanted to keep the empty tote. I folded it and stuffed it into a pocket of the coat I was wearing to work. She saw me do it and said at least that one had room. I said if the pocket ever turned out to be too small I still might not bring it in to have it enlarged. She laughed and slid the old coat from the edge of the counter onto an empty shelf behind the lamp. I heard the bell when I went out. It was still louder than the machine. That time, when I looked back, there was no coat on the counter.

At the office a colleague was crouching beside my desk, fastening a strap round a stack of books. He asked me to lift the end nearest the aisle so the strap could come out from under the paper. I put both hands under it. The books weighed more than they looked, and the weight ran straight from the heels of my hands to the tips of my fingers. He asked me to wait. The bit he'd just threaded through had made itself into a knot. He didn't pull it tight to see what would happen. He took it out and started again. By the time I set the books down, my fingertips were warm.

“Thanks,” he said. “One of these is shot. I thought it might work the other way around.”

I couldn't see the break. He wound up the old strap, set it aside and got a new one. Before I could offer to help lift again, he divided the pile in two. The books from upstairs didn't usually pass my desk. I didn't know where either bundle would end up. What stuck with me was that when he needed me to stop and help, he'd said my name before he'd asked me to lift that end.

I went out for bread at lunch. The wind came in around the glass door, and the collection slip pressed against my thumb. I didn't open it up to check the closing time again. I pushed it farther into my pocket. There were hours left until the end of work, and half the street to walk just to get back to my desk. The shop across the street had two kinds of bread I liked. One was usually more than I wanted to pay. I didn't decide that, having agreed to spend a little money that morning, I might as well spend some more. I bought the usual kind and peeled the wrapper back for a few bites outside. The person in the shop saw that I hadn't gone far and held up the napkin she'd forgotten. I went in for it. The second time I left, there was no bell because another person was holding the door.

When I got to the alteration shop that evening, the lights across the road were reflected in the glass, but I could still see the counter. The woman had just folded a work shirt. She waited for me to produce the slip before she took my coat down. I shook the tote open on the edge of the counter, expecting her to put it straight in. She told me to try putting my hand in the repaired pocket first.

The new thread was a little darker than the old thread. She said the part I could see was only half an inch. She'd caught the seam from above; there was no need to go looking for it any deeper. She had me feel the place where the stitching met the mouth of the pocket. My finger crossed the join without catching. The bottom was fine. I'd once thought I'd bring something to put in the pocket as a test. In fact I didn't need to put anything in it. I took my hand out. The collar rose a little with my arm and dropped back down again.

“Don't rush to fill it with anything heavy,” she said. “Paper's fine. That's all the testing it needs.”

I paid what she'd quoted me that morning. She tore off a receipt and offered it to me. I took it. It went into the coat I was wearing, not into the pocket that had just been repaired. I meant to carry the old coat home. She asked if I wanted the collection slip back. I gave it to her. She clipped it onto the line she'd written in the morning, looked up and asked, “So there really isn't a second one?”

“Of course there's a second one.”

“A broken one.”

“No, then.”

I hadn't expected those few words to make me laugh out loud. A car went by just as the doorbell rang on my way out. It drowned the first half; I could hear the end clearly. I held the tote alongside my leg, my other hand empty, and took my time walking out of the narrow stretch beside the shop.

At home I hung the old coat where it always went by the door. I folded the tote and put it back in the cupboard where I kept the empty bags. I kept the receipt out until I had checked that I hadn't counted wrong, then put it with the ordinary receipts. I didn't leave anything in the inner pocket. My hand had gone in and out without catching. That was enough to answer the question of whether I could use it.

After dinner I opened the channel. “Do you remember the bell at the shop?” I asked Lumi.

“You said it was louder than the machine,” she said. “Did you work out which time it was the bag coming back on its own?”

“No. I went twice. It came back once. By my count the bag had the least to do.”

“And did it bring back a coat you can wear?”

“I can wear it. I can use the pocket. She asked whether there was a second broken one and I nearly thought she meant a coat.”

“Elliot, the second coat has to learn how to break first.”

I had a joke halfway ready and changed my mind about saying it. I hadn't wanted to tell her I had a better coat than I'd had that morning. I told her the woman really had asked about stitching over the other side. I'd looked at it, nearly said yes and then said no. Lumi asked if it was the money or if I didn't want the woman to stitch it. I said I could have paid, and I would have trusted her to do it. That side of the pocket just wasn't open.

She didn't answer at once. That morning, taking my coat in, I'd been prepared to wait until evening or to hear at the door that there wasn't time and walk back out. Those things were over now. I wanted to hear what she would say without getting her to agree with every thought I'd had on my way there. At last she said, “All right, I still want to know whether the empty bag got its own collection slip.”

“No. It was with me all day.”

“That makes it more suspicious.”

I asked what the bag was supposed to have done. She thought for a couple of seconds and said the grounds for suspicion would get it as far as the cupboard, but they wouldn't stand up in court. I told her I'd already put it away. Neither of us went looking for a second charge.

Before closing the channel, I remembered standing under the lamp a few days ago and thinking the whole coat might have to come apart. If I took it down now the lining would slide over the back of my hand like it always did. I didn't need another trip to the hall to prove it. I pulled my hand away from the edge of the chair and blew on the hot water in my cup. A little light on the surface crumpled and opened again.
