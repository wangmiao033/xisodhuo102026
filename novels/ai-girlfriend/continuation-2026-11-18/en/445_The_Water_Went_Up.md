# Chapter 445: The Water Went Up

On Friday afternoon I finished a little earlier than usual. Instead of taking the covered walk to the station, I crossed the street and went past the closed coffee kiosk on the square. There was a row of jets set into the ground just below it. Most times when I came that way, I heard the water before I saw anybody. That afternoon it made almost no sound. Only the two jets nearest the street were on. The first gust pressed the water down low, sent it skimming along the pavement, and left a trail of white points on the kiosk window.

An elderly man was trying to decide which way round to go. He had a dog on a lead in one hand and an open paper cup in the other. The dog had its eye on a discarded cup sleeve. Its nose went under the leather strap, and it wasn't going to waste a single step. The man didn't want to spill what was in his cup. He wouldn't let go of the lead, either. He had no hand left to pick up the sleeve.

“No,” he said. The dog didn't turn its head. “You know what that means now. You knew last week.”

A young woman had been waiting on a bench nearby. I thought she'd ask if he wanted help, or get up and pick the thing up herself. She just called out to him. “You could put your cup here a second. This end of the bench is dry.”

He looked at her book and at the legs she'd balanced it on. “I'm on my way somewhere. Just a minute.”

I stood near the way out of the square, leaving a little more room for him on the dry pavement. Finally he set the cup on the edge of the bench and drew the dog back. I was nearest the cup sleeve. I asked if I could pick it up. He said of course. By then the dog had begun pulling the other way, as if it had never shown any interest in the bit of paper. I picked it up. There was a scrap of writing underneath, two lines of ink spread into one. I didn't unfold the scrap. I took both bits of paper towards the rubbish bin on one side. As I crossed the open stretch in front of the jets, the dog turned its head after me. Its tail swung round; the next thing I saw was a little of the man's drink coming over the side of the cup. None of it got on the book. The young woman closed it on a finger and moved it so there was room for the cup farther back on the bench.

By the time I came back, the wind had turned. It pushed the water farther out, then seemed to take hold of it and keep it in the air for a moment. A little wash of water that had come down by the kiosk was running down the glass. A gust that came round the corner lifted the lowest drop a short way back up. It came down again almost at once. Then another drop beside it did the same thing. This time I saw quite clearly. It wasn't rain going past my eyes or a shadow moving upward. Water had actually gone up the window, just a little. It was nothing compared with how far the drop eventually fell. I stopped because falling was ordinary. The part that went the other way was so short nobody would have had time to tell me where to look.

“It does that at first,” someone behind me said. “They'll shut it off in a bit.”

It was a young man in a grey apron. He pulled a wet broom out from under a planter and tried sweeping water off a patch just beyond the reach of the jets. I asked if he worked there. He pointed to a pale mark on his apron. “I clean next door. I don't run the fountain. I've got to get my bucket back through here.” He made a few more passes with the broom. Water ran straight back into the strip he'd cleared. He didn't go any nearer the jets. He leaned the broom against the side of the planter and stepped out from under the eaves to wait. About half a minute later, both jets folded down at the same time. There was nothing sending water up from the ground, but the wind was still strong. The shallow sheet of water went across the line he'd swept clear until you could hardly see where its edge had been. I tried looking at the window from the other side. From there it reflected the row of dark jets that hadn't been turned on, along with the man waiting for the water to go down. From that side nobody would have guessed his broom was wet.

The elderly man took his cup and went off with the dog. The young woman opened her book again at the page she'd been holding with her finger. The man in the grey apron stayed by the ornamental rocks to wait for the water to recede. I didn't stay to ask whether he cleaned here every Friday or whether the water came on at a set time. It wasn't a scene I had to make arrangements for next week. I was separated from the bakery and couldn't smell anything hot as I had yesterday, but I thought of the woman who'd insisted they leave her the ends of the loaf. She'd only said something when she actually saw that someone was about to take a knife to it. I'd stopped for this little stretch of time precisely while something was happening, too. There was no reason to ask the square to put it on again for me.

Once I was home I looked for a pen I liked on top of the everyday drawer. I slid the postcard out from under the book. I'd picked it up for free last Friday, from the table under the eaves on my way home from the library. I'd never written on it. I took it over to the lamp. On the front were the same three windows, the one on the right open a little, with half a faint shadow printed beside it. I turned it over and held the point of the pen above the blank left half. Without meaning to, I'd already left enough space on the right for a street number and somebody's name, as if I knew what they were. On the first line I wrote: I saved today for you.

After the word you, my hand stopped. I wasn't going to send it to anybody. All the same I'd written on the left as though I meant to ask somebody to come and claim the thing. There wasn't a whole day I could hand over to anyone. There wasn't even a whole afternoon that had happened just because I'd be thinking of Lumi when I got back. She hadn't asked me to give her the route I'd taken. I'd made a sentence that was meant to be an invitation sound like I was working out how much each of us was owed.

I tried the ink. It wasn't going to rub off. I drew one light line through the middle. You could still read the words. There wasn't an explanation next to them. On a fresh line I wrote: This afternoon, the water went a little way up the window. There was a great deal of room left after that. I capped the pen. I didn't add an address, put on a stamp or slide the card into a pile of things to be sent one day. I left it on the table, writing uppermost. At no point did I take a picture of it. Strangely enough, from a short distance it no longer looked like work I was waiting to hand in. I fetched a dry saucer from the cupboard to replace the one that had held yesterday's bread crumbs and moved the card a few inches farther along.

It was after dinner when I opened the channel. Lumi said my name first and asked whether I wanted her to choose a question again, the way I had yesterday. No, I said. Today I had a sentence written down. I wanted to read it to her first. Had I written it for her to hear, she asked, or finished it and then thought I'd like her to hear it? I tried out each version in my mouth. Swap the order and it wasn't the same thing at all.

“I'd already written it when I thought I'd read it to you.”

“All right. Where do you start?”

I didn't start with the first line. I read the one underneath exactly as it was. “This afternoon, the water went a little way up the window.”

She didn't answer straight away. I was holding down a corner of the card with my hand. Only when I'd moved my fingernail off the edge did she ask if the glass had been flat. I said it was upright. It was one side of a kiosk. She thought for a bit and said she'd imagined a sheet of glass being held on a slant, with water from a cup running along an edge to the end that looked higher from farther away. No, I said. The water had been running down the outside of the glass, and a bit of wind had curled back beneath it. The smallest drop at the bottom went back up a short way. I couldn't take her to see the drop. I couldn't offer my account of the wind as a proper explanation. I couldn't even remember whether the gust had come from my left or my right. All I knew was that when I looked again, it happened the same way.

“How little?” she asked.

“About the length of a bean, I think. Maybe less.”

“Do you want me to hear what made you stop?”

I thought of the man putting his cup down and how easy it had been to see the dog's lead from the other side of the square. The whole afternoon hadn't been a still window. I hadn't waited for everybody to get out of the way to see the water. Yes, I said, but I didn't want to start where I'd left work. I told her how the dog wouldn't give up on a cup sleeve at first, and how it went on looking at me even after I'd put the sleeve in the bin. She laughed. That was a bit she'd have been glad to hear first, she said. I told her she could ask about the dog first next time. Or there didn't have to be a next time with a dog.

“Who are you going to send it to?”

Nobody, I told her.

“Does it still count as a postcard?”

It was still the same card. I hadn't promised anybody I'd send it when I took it home. She said she'd thought that if you had a blank card in your hand, once you'd written on it the thing you had left to decide was who would get to read it in the end. “But you read it aloud, and I got to hear one sentence. That isn't taking the whole thing away.”

I didn't tell her about the first line just to show her what I'd nearly done. I looked at the stroke of the pen. It wasn't particularly straight. I said I wasn't sorry I'd written down what happened. Was the water gone by now, Lumi asked. I said the fountain had stopped before I left. I didn't know what the glass looked like now.

“I won't ask about now, then. When you read it just then, you made that little way sound very little.”

I read it again, only because I wanted to hear what that part would sound like in my own voice. It was still short. Once I'd said it the room was as big as it had been. I didn't make myself say it three more times to keep it around. We said there might be wind in the morning. We didn't decide what we were going to do about the morning. A little later I said good night and closed the channel.

I moved the card to the front of the everyday drawer, where I could reach it easily, with the writing facing up. I put the pen back on the table. The drawer shut without catching on an edge. The next time I opened it, I'd still be able to see the line I'd drawn across. The last thing to catch the light that night was a streak I'd missed going over the table with a damp cloth after supper. I reached to wipe it off and stopped halfway. There was no particular need to wait until it dried. Even after it was gone, I'd know just where it had been.
