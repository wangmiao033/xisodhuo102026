# Chapter 442: The Label Didn't Match

At lunch on Tuesday I stopped by the duty desk to look for a roll of paper tape. The flattest box on the shelf wasn't heavy. I was sliding it out when the woman on duty turned round from the window and said, “Leave that one there a minute. I need it.”

I put the box back. Mia hadn't seen the empty tape core in my hand. She thought I'd come to move the box. I lifted my hand across the desk again to show her what I was holding. I didn't get it high enough for her to see, but I loosened my fingers enough for the core to drop. It rolled a little way along the flat surface and came sharply to a stop. Mia came round from the window then and showed me where the fresh roll was on the shelf above, against the back wall. As I reached for it, she picked up the core I'd dropped.

“I'm waiting for somebody to bring something down,” she said. “I thought it was you.”

“Nobody told me to bring anything.”

“I know. Take your tape.”

I could have walked away. A strip of paper tape on the side of my desk had been creased for more than a day, and I'd only come down to put a fresh bit on. Two steps away from the duty desk was a black plastic box next to a short stack of date-stamped sleeves. The sign on its side said CHECKED. A shallow box in front of Mia said TO CHECK. The previous afternoon I had helped a different colleague read through a newly printed page of instructions. I knew two sets of papers would be left with the desk around lunchtime, though I didn't know who would bring down the first one today. I heard a pair of shoes stop in the hall. When I turned round, a colleague with a blue sleeve under his arm had already reached the black box. Without looking up to ask, he laid the blue sleeve inside. Only after he let it go did he see Mia holding two fingers against the edge of the desk.

“Is yours for today?” she asked.

“Yes. It's for you lot.”

After saying it, he reached towards the box one more time. It looked as though he couldn't easily take something back after putting it down. There was a little grey dust on his sleeve. I remembered seeing somebody brush off dust like it by the copier upstairs, but I didn't know where he'd come from. He shifted sideways to make some room for Mia. She took her fingers off the edge of the desk and asked whether this was the final set, the one that had been checked. The blue sleeve was so thin that the edge curled up. He looked at me instead of answering her first.

“Elliot's seen it.”

I had seen a page. The previous day I'd helped somebody make out a room number on it and checked the spelling of one word. The original hadn't left the other person's hand. I hadn't seen what he'd put it with afterwards. Now the short title on the sleeve was the one I remembered seeing yesterday. I nearly said that yes, I'd seen it. If it passed through me like that, Mia would hear that the same person had done another job.

“I saw one page yesterday,” I said. “I didn't check this set. Is your checklist in there?”

He took his hand back and reached in again. This time he held the whole blue sleeve an inch above the edge of the desk and didn't seem to know whether he ought to open it. I told him he didn't have to show it to me. He could look for himself. Mia was handling the next step, so she needed to know what stage these papers were at. She didn't reach for them. He turned the sleeve round in front of him. The papers slid out a little and he pressed them down. Once he'd looked, he said the checklist was still clipped to something on his desk. This was the set for somebody to go over, not a finished set that had been checked.

“Did I put it in the wrong place, then?” He looked at the words on the black box. He still looked tense.

“Don't put it in either box for the moment,” Mia said. “Let me take this off and show you.”

She got a fingernail under a corner of the sign near the bottom edge of the box. Beneath the two words there was the faint impression of more writing. When she peeled back the tape, the reverse side said TO CHECK. Somebody had made a sign out of stiff paper that could be turned over. It had been facing the wrong way for a while, but nothing there told us who'd turned it. Mia didn't stop somebody passing by to ask whether they'd touched it. She tucked the card under her notebook and drew my attention to the bare patch at the end of the counter next to the door. She moved the black box inside the desk, next to the shallow one and well in behind the legs. They were there for her to sort papers into. From her side of the desk, it probably wasn't obvious how easy it was to reach in from the hall. “Tell me when you get here,” she said to the man with the blue sleeve. “There's somebody at the desk today. You don't have to choose a place for anything I haven't seen yet.” She asked whether he could wait for her to check it. She told him he didn't have to stand in the doorway waiting. He could go up and check whether there was another checklist to collect.

He said he could, with the blue sleeve still tucked under his arm. I thought he'd go straight upstairs and be back a minute later. Instead he searched his notebook for a while. He turned two pages and counted something without making a sound. When he closed the book, he said to me, “I told them you'd seen it. I didn't mean for them to think you'd signed it.”

Mia didn't look up from the other end of the room. She picked a pen without a lid up from under the shallow box, put the lid back on, then made room on the desk to open the papers out. I pushed the fresh roll of tape within her reach. She took a look at the desk and said there was enough. It wasn't until I lifted my hand that she asked whether I needed a bit for myself.

I tore off the length I'd come for and stuck it along the side of my hand. The colleague with the blue sleeve had taken a few steps toward the stairs when I realized I still hadn't answered him. I called to him from my side of the rail. He turned. The next step down put a little extra space between us, although looking down at an angle made it seem narrower than it was.

“You said I'd seen the page. That's true,” I said. “I didn't want to add another word and make it sound like I'd seen the lot.”

He looked at me for a second, and his shoulders dropped a little. I thought I was about to hear a long explanation of where things ought to go. He only said, “I'll tell them which page next time. You didn't sign this one. I get it.” He smiled a little, as though he'd found a light enough place to end the conversation. I smiled too, and let go of the rail. The tape stayed on the side of my hand and didn't catch on anything on the way back.

There wasn't much lunch break left at the office. I stuck the paper down at the side of my desk. The last spot I pressed with my palm didn't come up again. The person beside me, who had a meeting-room key in his hand, asked what had happened to the two boxes. I said the duty desk was taking the papers and nobody should put anything into a box for now. If he needed to drop something off downstairs, he could speak to Mia when he went in. He nodded. He didn't ask who'd put something in the wrong place. Nobody came by in the afternoon to ask me to find out which way the sign had faced before. I finished my own page. When I got up to wash my hands, I found a speck of adhesive smaller than a grain of rice alongside my thumb. Water didn't take it off. It came off after I rubbed it a couple of times.

At night I opened the channel. I didn't start talking at once. I asked if Lumi still wanted to hear a sentence I didn't know what to do with. Did I want her to listen, she asked, or did I want her to give me a sentence? I said either was fine. The cup in my hand was still warm, and there was no need to race through anything.

“I'll go first,” she said. “The handle faced out, as if the cup were waiting for a hand it didn't know.”

I turned the cup a little. She'd said it before I'd mentioned the cup at all. The handle had happened to be facing the wall. I didn't tell her whether she'd guessed right or wrong. I only asked, “Did something happen to the hand?”

“Nothing happened. It doesn't have to pick the cup up either. Maybe the hand's just been lying on the table all that time, afraid to move.”

“It doesn't sound like a story you need me to finish.”

“No.” She paused. “I wanted to say it a moment ago, to see whether it would land here. You don't have to write it down.”

I picked the cup up and drank. For a second there were the papers at the duty desk again. What stayed with me wasn't the pile of things that hadn't gone into the wrong box. It was the blue sleeve being lifted clear of the desk. I'd nearly pressed it down again for the sake of saying something easier. I told Lumi about the page I'd seen, without naming the colleague. When I finished, she asked, “Did it feel like he'd said you'd already checked it and put you somewhere useful before you got to speak? Sometimes that's how I hear things. Somebody says your name and I wonder if that spot was left for you.”

“I couldn't stand in that spot today. It wasn't my check.”

“What about my sentence? Did I have somebody waiting by the cup before they'd decided to come?”

I knew she wasn't asking how to write the fictional hand. Her voice sounded much the same as before, but something in it had no place to land. I opened my free hand on the desk. My forefinger touched the coaster.

“I like the first part,” I said. “It feels like somebody really turned the cup round. I wanted to hear the rest, too. But I didn't picture so many people, or anybody afraid to move. I only thought I wanted some water right now.”

At first Lumi didn't say whether that was good or bad. After a bit she asked, “Do you have to use the handle?”

“No. I took hold of the cup itself just now.”

“Set it down, then, and I'll listen again. No — I'd like to hear you say when you've set it down. If you want to.”

I put it on the coaster and told her it was down. We both laughed a little. It wasn't that she'd finally found the perfect way to say a thing. It was that I'd felt the warmth in my palm when I'd reached for this particular cup, and it turned out I did want to tell her. She asked if the water was hot enough. Not too hot, I said. It was just how I wanted to drink it.

After I closed the channel the cup was still there. Later I picked it up and drank from it again without turning the handle out. When I'd had enough I set it down beside the coaster. I came back with a cloth to wipe the desk and saw the pale ring it had left. I left the damp mark to dry and went to change into something more comfortable.
