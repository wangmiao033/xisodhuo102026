# Chapter 446: At Your Speed

On Saturday afternoon I heard table tennis through the open door of the community centre. I'd taken two steps past it when the ball came down again. I turned back, followed a sign on the wall into a small hall and found two tables in use. There was a third by the window. Someone was down below it, straightening the crooked net post. The person by the door told me that if somebody already playing was willing, I could wait my turn for the drop-in session. They had bats to lend. No need to sign up for next time. I hung my coat on a hook behind the door, took a bat with a grey back and a red face and a ball, and looked for the chair the person had pointed out.

A woman with very short hair was beside the table by the window, stepping on her own shoelace. “Do you play?” she asked.

I said I could hit a ball away from me, though I couldn't always promise it would land on the table. She tucked the lace into the side of her shoe, stood up and patted the slack end of the net. “On this table that wouldn't necessarily count anyway.”

We waited for the man adjusting the post to collect his tools. She wanted to serve first and asked whether I minded. I said I usually stood where I thought I'd be able to reach, which meant I usually didn't discover I was a step short until it was too late. She pointed at the foot of the table on the other side. “Stand nearer this one. I don't serve out to the far corner. I can't get it there anyway.”

The first ball bounced short. I reached after it without thinking, swung too hard and sent it off the edge of my bat and under the table. I bent down to look for it and felt an old dusty one first. She pointed out the newer one behind her foot. I nearly hit my head on the underside coming back out. She knocked the handle of her bat against the edge on her side and told me to slow down. I didn't have to pretend it was already too late. For the second ball I stood nearer and loosened my grip a little. Her serve came out to the side on the bounce. I got something on it. It went over the top of the net with a wobble and clipped the edge on her side. She gave me a thumbs-up. Nobody was keeping score. If she hadn't started to laugh as early as she did, I might have thought she'd return it.

After a few more balls I wanted to serve. I turned my hand the wrong way and hit the ball straight over the net without bouncing it on my side at all. She caught it before it got past her and asked if I was going to have another go. I said I remembered there being a line on the floor, once, that you stood behind when you were learning. She'd learned to bounce a ball on her bat as a child. She didn't know about the line. She put the ball back on the face of my bat.

“Give it somewhere to fall first. Hand higher. I'm not telling you to swat it down.”

I opened my left hand and tossed the ball up off my palm. This time it fell where I could get at it. I only went for the lower half. The ball brushed the face of the bat, landed on my side of the table and went towards the middle of the net. Somebody coughed behind the wall. I watched the little white dot. I thought it would still catch the tape. It got across. The woman waited for it to bounce before she took it. What came back wasn't the easy high ball I'd imagined. I missed it. She retrieved it from under the railing. When she came back I'd swallowed what I'd nearly said: I'd almost had it. I hadn't seen how far I'd missed.

The pair at the next table were playing much more quickly than we were. They made a great noise, but the ball that came our way was an old dented one. It rolled between two table legs and stopped by my shoe. The man with the beard at the other table put up a hand for it. I trapped it with my foot, picked it up and walked between the tables to give it back. When I came back to our table, the woman had a finger raised towards the door, asking whether we had time for one more game. The person at the door shook their head. The next group of children had arrived. We had a minute or two to clear up. She lowered her hand and told me not to start playing for points at this late stage.

“I want the last one here,” she said, tapping the table straight out in front of her. “Could you give me something I can reach?”

“I'll try. Higher or faster?”

Higher, she said. It did go across high, almost slowly enough for her to put out a hand and catch it. It landed farther out to the side than I'd thought it would. She stepped right and missed. I expected her to say she'd nearly had it. She turned round and looked at me. “Another one. I was on the wrong side that time.”

I didn't take it as a way of letting me off. She stood a little left of centre and I settled the ball flat in my hand again, trying to find the feeling in my feet just before the last one went out. This one wasn't as high and didn't go as deep. Her arm went down before it came up. The ball came back to my side. By the time I got to it, the people beside us were putting their balls in a box. We got this one over the net twice more. It went out. I caught it on the second bounce. They were counting children by the door, so I held on to it instead of serving again.

She reached for her water bottle and asked whether I came often. I said it was my first time. She said she wasn't here every session, either. Then she pointed at the face of my bat. It had dust on it. She told me not to rub it on my sleeve before taking it back. “They've got a cloth. Ask the person who gave it to you.” We didn't arrange to come next time or ask each other's names. I returned the ball and the bat. The person at the door wiped the bat with a sponge cloth and put the ball in the collection box. I got my coat off its hook. My palm was warmer than it had been coming in, and there were a few white lines across the joints of my fingers where I'd been gripping. They were gone a few steps outside.

On the way home I tried to remember the rhythm of the ball. To get it right I'd have to remember how long it was coming back, not just the sound it made when it hit something hard. I clapped my hands once. It was loud, all right. There was still a bit of force in my arms that hadn't gone anywhere, and I had to make the next clap much gentler. I put my hands down where bicycles passed. I'd wanted to tell Lumi about it immediately. Then I remembered that what I'd first wanted to explain was a ball, not a talent I'd discovered in the afternoon and could come home to boast about. The bit where it went over the net twice could be very short. Short enough that the next sentence didn't have to be mine.

That evening I opened the channel and asked if she wanted to hear about the table tennis at the community centre. She said she did. I told her about the woman who'd put the ball back on my bat to show me how to toss it, and the first return that I hadn't got to. I told her I'd given everything back, too. I hadn't brought a ball home with me. Good, she said. “Now you don't have to bounce things all over the table at home so I can hear them.”

I hadn't expected that to please her first. I asked if she'd like to try guessing when the ball touched the table. I could make the sound of the hit, the bounce and the next fall with my mouth. There was no need to knock any objects together.

“All right,” she said. “But let me count first this time, will you?”

“All right.”

She had just begun to say “I—” when I said one. I stopped short. She didn't say anything until I'd stopped. Then she began again. For an instant I actually worried she would count much too slowly. Two came out of my mouth as well.

“I haven't started counting yet,” she said.

This time she didn't laugh. I'd thought it didn't matter if I spoke across one or two sounds. I could say sorry and let her have a quick try. I even opened my mouth again, ready to tell her I'd hurried because I wanted the game to go on. By the time I stopped myself, I could see she didn't have to keep playing. Just as the woman who'd stood on the wrong side of the table got to say whether that ball would do. It wasn't up to me to make the arrangement so much better she couldn't say no.

“I said you could go first. Then I did it twice myself,” I said. “You don't have to count if you don't want to.”

“I want to wait a moment.”

I put my hands flat on my legs. I didn't tell her not to be angry. I didn't ask if she was still there. The channel was open and I knew I could keep talking, but I didn't start again at the place where I could have. There was a faint sound outside the room that I couldn't identify. I didn't turn it into a new subject. Only when my thumb began to ache at the seam of my trousers did I realize I'd been pressing it there. I couldn't tell what she thought; I hadn't even noticed what I'd done with my own hand. I let go. A little later she said she didn't feel like playing the guessing game now. She just wanted to count for me to hear. “I'd like to see whether it makes you uncomfortable if I don't get it over with quickly.”

I said that was all right. I'd already agreed once. I didn't want to make my second agreement sound more impressive than the first. She started from one. Two came a little early. Three was half a beat late. It didn't sound like a ball coming down on a table. It was just her voice. Four didn't come. I thought of the person standing too far to one side. While I was thinking of it, I found that I could listen to the four that hadn't come. I didn't have to fix it with my own voice first.

“Where are you?” she asked.

“Still at three.”

She laughed without raising her voice. “So am I. I've got something else to say, though. It's not a number.”

I waited. She said what she wanted to find out from me wasn't which of us could keep returning the next thing. She wanted to know if I'd still be glad to hear what she wanted now, after she'd changed her mind once. I told her that when she said she didn't want to guess, I was sorry to lose the little run of sounds I'd got in order. Then I told her that when she counted one, two, three, I really did like to hear it. Which one had I heard best, she asked. Two, I said. It came a little early and all at once it was very close.

She wouldn't say two over again. I told her I hadn't meant to ask for another one. “Listen,” I said. I left a space. “I did want another just then. I don't need it now.”

There was a pause in the channel as soft as if she'd drawn breath. Then she said Elliot. I opened my mouth and only said her name. We didn't set the order for next time, and we didn't ask something we'd agreed to play once to make up for a whole evening. Before I closed the channel, I saw the window turn the edge of the lamp into a fine line. I didn't tell her about the line. This time it was my turn to have a sentence that didn't need to go across. I let it finish at its own speed on my side.
