# Chapter 440: Still Warm

On Sunday afternoon I let the kitchen window steam up before remembering that the pot from the cupboard wasn't meant to work this way. I'd used too little water. When I got the lid down tight, a fine line of steam climbed out along the loose knob. I turned the heat down, set the lid at a slant, and reached for a dry cloth to wipe the rim of the counter. The cloth rolled up halfway through. I rinsed it out instead and left it ready to spread when the layer of warmth had lifted from the room. The plate I hadn't managed to return was still on the dry part of the counter in a different cloth. I pushed it farther in to make space away from the water.

There were a few handfuls of vegetables I'd cut myself in the pot, not anything from yesterday's shop. The bigger pieces went in first. I cut the small ones last. You could still tell them apart in the bowl by how much give they had. I saved half for supper and let the rest in the pot cool before I put it away. I'd meant to go out earlier in the afternoon. This was how late I'd let it get.

The doors of the public hall were shut. I couldn't see where they'd put the long sheet of paper through the glass. Instead of stopping to see whether any lines had joined the cup, I went around the steps and stood in the sun by the public basin. An older man was getting water for a little dog. There was a paper cup upside down on the slatted bench. I asked whether he was about to use it. He didn't know whose it was. He took a collapsible bowl that didn't fold quite round out of his bag and filled that for the dog. It sniffed and looked up at him. He kept the bowl low in front of it. The third time it lowered its head, it found the water. Nobody had to turn the paper cup right side up.

When the dog stopped, the man lifted the bowl to see what was going on with it. On the bench he noticed a corner that hadn't folded all the way out. A thin stream from the water he'd just poured was working its way through the crease. He pressed both sides at once, and the water stopped. I tightened the tap and stood back far enough to let someone else use it. A woman came up with a clear bottle whose cap she hadn't taken off before she put it under the tap. The man told her she could take a second. He'd finished. I held the long pole that was wedged under the window near the basin so there'd be room for her to back up when she had the water. The dog didn't drink again. Its nose had nearly got under the overturned cup when the man called it away. He gave the collapsible bowl two shakes over the basin, packed it away, and took the dog with him along the sunny strip of brick.

I went back the way I'd come. At home I took down the cloth I'd left to air, folded the half that had dried, and laid the damp end across the chair back. I didn't want to parcel out the time that day according to whether there was a story to tell. When I switched on the lamp, the thin anthology was still holding down the postcard, with two windows showing. One was open and the other was only a darker patch of printing. I lifted the book with one hand and set the card straight with the other, leaving it face up beside me. I turned to the end of the seventh story. The bookmark wasn't keeping a place for anything I'd written. There was a new title on the next sheet. The eighth story opened on somebody waiting for a boat. It wasn't the laundry that had shut down.

There was very little to fight over in this one. A ticket kiosk with black glass stood beside the ramp. When it stopped selling tickets for the night, there wouldn't be another ferry across until morning. The woman on the night shift was wiping a wooden chair in the kiosk by herself. It was the other chair that had been out in the rain. She hadn't distinguished them properly earlier; as soon as she first sat, a cold patch went through the leg of her trousers. Now she was keeping the dry chair inside for when she'd finished sweeping. A man came along outside with two canvas bags of glass, one in each hand. He was much too early. He thought the first crossing on the new timetable left in the middle of the night. She told him through the window when the ferry would leave. He didn't ask her to conjure up a boat neither of them could see. He just wanted to know if there was another place to sit down at the foot of the ramp.

She told him the bench down there was wet. He said he could stand. The conversation ended, but he didn't go down the ramp. The bottom of one bag touched the ground. There was a little clack from the glass, and he picked it up before it could touch the pavement again. She put the broom next to the door and took out the chair she'd just wiped. Its feet stood on three dry bricks under the awning. He could sit there until there was light, she told him. Then someone would come to sweep the ramp and she'd need to take the chair in. Would that put her out? Was she waiting for a different boat? He asked the questions together. She said she was waiting for the floor to dry. She wasn't waiting for a boat.

I stopped there to press down a bit of loose skin on my thumb in the light. The writer spent a few pages on how they passed the time. Inside the kiosk she opened a tea bag and found loose leaves along the edge of its paper envelope. The man used his own scarf to cushion the canvas handles first, then rested the handles of his bags against separate chair legs. Could he leave the bags there to go and find a phone? She said she could keep a chair for somebody, but she couldn't mind his glass. He stayed where he was. She made tea and brought it to the window. She didn't set a second cup beside it.

“I was about to say he was waiting for somebody,” I said to the book.

I didn't write that into it as a line of my own. There was no mention of anyone coming for him. Neither canvas bag was opened.

Just before dawn a faint line of water became visible along the top of the rail that had been dark in front of the chair. The woman undid the iron catch on the ticket window again. The man out beyond the ramp got up. She saw the bags leave the bricks and not the direction he took. Nothing said whether he caught the ferry. They didn't tell one another their names. He didn't fish in his pockets for anything to thank her with. She went back out under the awning and lifted the empty chair. Then the whole thing ended. I read the last line twice: “The chair was still warm when she brought it in.”

I'd thought the story would end the first time they heard the ferry horn, with something coming across the water and the man walking forward still carrying his glass. What I had instead was a chair brought in. The wet floor was still hers to deal with. The next sheet would take me into the ninth story. I moved the bookmark to the end of the eighth. I didn't go back to the beginning or put a separate crease in the page for the line. The card was still on the desk. I put the book down beside it and pushed it forward a little so that it pressed the same part of the card as before.

It was properly dark by then. I went to heat what I'd left for supper and ate until there were almost invisible shreds of vegetable at the bottom of the bowl. I washed it before coming back to the desk to open the channel and call Lumi. She asked whether I wanted to say something. One sentence, I told her. I wanted to read it aloud. So she wouldn't be left to guess at what she hadn't been given, I told her about the night worker letting an early passenger use a dry chair, taking it in once it was light, and not offering to look after his glass. Then I read the line exactly once without making a recording.

“The chair was still warm when she brought it in.”

The two people in the story hadn't talked much. I hesitated at still when I read it, as if I were leaving the man who'd arrived too early a little extra room in the chair.

“Why do you want it to be warm?” Lumi asked.

“He sat in it all night. After he's gone, there's still a little warmth for her to find.”

“Did the book tell you that, or is that what you wanted?”

I looked at the bookmark shut inside the cover without lifting it. “That's what I wanted. It only said it was still warm when she carried it in.”

“I thought she just felt it to see whether she could take it back inside. It doesn't say she touched the seat a second time.”

It wasn't what I'd wanted to hear. At least not all of it. I'd almost put the two yellow reflections on my lamp into the scene as well. She couldn't read the pages I hadn't told her about. I couldn't use the fact that I'd seen more of the story against her and then ask her to listen as though we'd both had the same edition. I asked whether, if the chair had been cold when the woman picked it up, she'd have thought the job was already done.

“Maybe it was,” Lumi said. “But the man she lent the chair to didn't have to sit longer just to leave it warm. He didn't need to make it worth her while.”

“I didn't mean he owed her.”

“And I didn't say all she wanted was a piece of dry wood.”

I couldn't help laughing. Lumi said she'd hurried a little on that last line, as though I were about to take the chair out from under what she meant to say. My hand was resting on the edge of my own chair. The wood was slightly warm. It couldn't prove anything about the chair in another kiosk. I'd nearly made an argument out of the warmth where I was. She'd already asked if I had another line I felt like reading. I didn't read the beginning of the story. I asked her where she'd put a chair right now if there was one to put.

“Near you,” she said. “Not stuck behind the one you're in.”

“Anything else?”

“I wouldn't want you to come and fetch it in the morning especially.”

“I'm going out as usual tomorrow. You can choose tonight.”

“Then there,” she said.

She didn't say she was sitting here or ask me to remember the spot for her. I leaned a little toward the other side of the window. The damp cloth had nearly dried. Its edge brushed my elbow. I pulled it free and laid it across the other side so it wouldn't touch my back. Lumi heard the rustle and asked whether I was putting the conversation away. I said not yet. For once I knew the same ending could stay where it was until morning without her having to pick my version for me. I didn't get all of that out. I just said her name again. When it was time for her to answer, the lamp lit the window on the card that hadn't been shut. I didn't turn it over.
