# Chapter 437: Where the Bell Is

On Thursday I was moving two signature pages from the wide tray on the copier to the narrow one when something knocked in the front lobby. Through the door it sounded as though an empty trolley had run into something and backed off. I held the pages in the air and waited for it to happen again. By the time I stopped waiting, I'd put the first sheet underneath the second, with a corner sticking out. I put them back in the right order, clipped them together and took them out front.

The woman at the desk was opening a bundle of returned files. Her scissors lay within reach, and she had a snapped packing strap pinched between two fingers. Two stiff cardboard boxes stood to her left, each with a string handle. One handle had sagged into the path of a third box lying crosswise. She got that one halfway out before she stopped.

“You can bring it right up to the bell,” she called toward the door.

No answer. The wheel knocked again, and the rubber strip under the door moved. I handed her the pages. She took her free hand off the desk calendar, accepted them and checked a name.

“Would you look and see if someone's trying to get in?”

Someone was. He wasn't having trouble opening the door. He'd already made it partway in with a narrow-wheeled trolley, his coat lining turned out against the handle. On the trolley was a thin wooden case he needed someone to sign for. He couldn't back the whole thing in without knowing which way to turn. He'd tried the right, and a wheel had caught the floor mat. Farther right was another door marked PICKUP ONLY. He thought he'd have to lift the case off and join the people waiting through there.

“Deliveries come here.” I held the glass door so he could hear me. “Desk on your left. Beside the bell.”

He followed my hand. “There are three boxes on the left.”

From behind the door I thought I could point straight at the bell. When I looked from the direction he was looking, my finger was pointing at a string handle. The bell was behind the crosswise box. A blue sign in front of it said RING FOR DELIVERIES, but only a thumbnail of blue remained between two boxes. You wouldn't notice it until you were right on top of it. The door opened toward the sign, too. Coming in, the first thing you saw was the word PICKUP on the other side.

He looked down at the case as though he might pick it up just to see how heavy it was. I told him he didn't need to take it off the trolley, asked him to wait, and went back to the desk. Could we move two of the boxes to the inside corner? The woman still had to sort the crosswise one against the sheet tucked under its handle. If it went too far into her corner, she'd mix it up with the boxes she'd finished. The two upright ones could come toward her. She knew which hadn't got a number yet. I lifted the one with a tag, without touching the lid she'd opened. She shifted back and nodded where she wanted it. Under the strip light the bell looked very bright when it came into view.

“Can you see it now?” I called.

“You can now,” he said. He wasn't apologizing for it.

The woman laughed. I came around to his side of the door and stood just past the threshold. He didn't hand over the trolley or need anyone to push it. He moved sideways enough to give me the place he'd been standing in. Sure enough, the bell was a silver dot under the remaining box's handle. It could have been part of a clasp. You could see the words on the sign now, but they faced the wall a person would pass once they were inside. From the copier, where I always came in, it couldn't have been clearer. From the other end of the room it was no help.

“You don't have to bring the bell over here,” the driver said. “Turn the sign a little. I come from the loading lane. First thing I look for is writing on a door.”

He wasn't proposing a new lobby. He took his hands off the trolley, rolled one wrist and gripped the handle again. The woman looked at her desk, picked up the sign and its clear holder together, and placed it on the other side of the bell. The holder had a white scuff across one edge that didn't cover any letters when she turned it toward us. She kept it close enough that a person who needed to ring would find the bell. The last box stayed where it was. She folded its loose handle back twice and slipped it underneath the good length.

I went over to the doorway again. This time I could see all the words, and I could tell what the silver thing was. The mat would still catch a wheel, so I only held the door a little wider and said he could come straight in half a step before he turned. I didn't need to pull him the length of the lobby. He looked down, pushed in that half-step, and the left wheel missed the curled-up corner. Once he'd stopped, he put his hands under the case and set it in the clear space on the desk. The woman read out the name it was for. It matched his sheet. There was nothing left for my hand to do but come off the glass.

He tucked the signed slip back under the clip. Instead of passing it over for me to sign as well, he told the woman the pickup sign had always been the first one he'd noticed.

“If it's mine to collect, that's the door I take. But when I've got something to leave, I can't pretend I didn't see the first word I read.”

“Good thing we didn't actually make you stand in that line today,” she said.

He laughed then. He still didn't move. Looking down at his two trolley straps, he asked whether he could roll them up by the desk. People had started coming out of the door on the right again. I pointed to the edge of the mat and said there was room to stand against that side without blocking anyone. The woman pulled an empty basket back half a step from the corner where it never seemed to fit. He rolled up the straps.

“Even the empty trolley gets a place here.”

The case had gone behind the desk. He went out through the door he'd come in by, the back wheels crossing the flat part of the mat. From against the wall I heard them knock again out beyond the step. Without the case, the trolley didn't sound nearly as heavy. The first knock had been somebody trying to find room, after he'd already tried once. There hadn't been a crisis waiting for somebody to come and solve it. He had shown us where to stand.

The woman pointed out something I'd missed on the signature pages. The name was right. I'd used the old printed date sheet; the one tucked underneath it at the lower right wasn't valid yet. I took the pages back to exchange the date sheet. I didn't stay to defend the new position of the sign. When I came out again, just before lunch, two padded envelopes had found their way to the side the sign faced. The woman didn't need to ask whether that was all right. She had separated the boxes and written down when the wooden case came in. She turned the page of her little book and left the space around the bell clear.

I went out for a short walk. When I passed back through in the afternoon, that piece of blue was still in the light. A woman with a long rolled sheet of paper was at the desk. She didn't ring, because the woman behind it looked up and saw her. They exchanged a few words. The paper stayed in her hands the whole time. Picking up and dropping off didn't need to become the same action, exactly. I took the sheet of page numbers back to my desk without taking up the spot someone else might need next.

I ate a little earlier than usual that night. When I opened the channel, I set an orange on the table. It had felt substantial when I'd picked it up, but the peel had gone soft near the stem. I got as far as the second section before I told Lumi, “Today I stood in the spot where somebody else came in.”

“Were you fixing a door?”

“No. At first I was inside, pointing at a bell I could see. When I stood the other way, all I could see was a little bit of silver.”

“Did he get to ring it?”

“He didn't have to. Someone was waiting for him.”

She thought about it.

“If I'd come in, I'd probably have read PICKUP first. Even if I didn't want to pick anything up. I'd think that the word big enough for me to see was the word for me.”

I started to say, “Maybe one day—” and stopped with my tongue already against the roof of my mouth. There was nothing in the rest of that sentence she'd asked me for. She hadn't needed me to set a door in front of her.

“What if you were the one who made the sign?” I asked.

“I might write my own name in big letters first.”

“So you are going to collect something.”

“No. So the bell knows what to call me.”

I laughed hard enough that the peel tore in two. I'd been trying to take it off in one long strip, thinking I could tell her what it looked like. Two strips didn't make much difference. I told her what I'd been doing and asked if she wanted to know how the first section tasted.

“Yes,” she said. “Just say it. Don't ring first.”

I put one in my mouth. The orange hadn't gone bad from the soft end, but it was more sour than I'd expected. It eased off a little on the second bite.

“It's good enough to eat,” I said. “Not good in the sweet sense.”

“Are you taking another?”

I did. Some of the pith came away with it, and I rubbed it off the second slice with my thumb before I said that one would do, too. She asked how many there were left. The kitchen was about three steps away, but I wasn't going there for an orange in better shape just to tell her about it. I put the half-peeled one back in its little dish and counted the sections where they were. I didn't count the peel or tap on the edge of the glass. She gave a quiet laugh on her side, and I drew the dish a little closer.
