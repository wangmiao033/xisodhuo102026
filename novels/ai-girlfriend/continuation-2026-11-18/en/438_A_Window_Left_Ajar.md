# Chapter 438: A Window Left Ajar

After work on Friday I went round by the library. I could have sworn I'd left a slip of paper in a book at the borrowing desk the night before, and I meant to get it back. When I reached into my pocket for my card as I came up the outside steps, I touched a narrow, folded corner first. There was a vertical line in black at the edge, which I'd gone over that morning to remind myself not to throw this one away. I'd folded it myself before I left the house. I didn't need to go back for the book. I walked in slowly, past the notices by the door. There was no new poster about a performance that night. The last one had been taken down, leaving a pale circle on the wall. I sat by a window and finished a page in the local bulletin about routes in the neighborhood. I didn't put the bulletin in my bag. It wasn't altogether dark when I went out.

On my way back I took the covered stretch on the left. The narrow table that usually held crates of bread stood a little apart from the wall, with a piece of cardboard under one of its legs. There was nobody picking out a loaf today. There were two shoeboxes instead, one full of paper of different thicknesses, the other with a stone weighing down its lid. Some of the paper showed birds. Some of it showed a bridge I practically knew by heart. On another, a tree had been photographed so that you only got its roots. A handwritten sign pinned in front of a leg said DONATED BLANK POSTCARDS. TAKE ONE IF YOU LIKE. The dot at the bottom went off at an angle. For a moment I thought there was a price underneath, but when I bent down I saw it was only a period.

A woman with a dark cloth bag down by one foot was running her thumb along the corners of the cards. When she saw me stop, she took her hand off the pile.

“None of them have been written on. Take one if you want. Try not to let the ones on top blow away.”

“Do you want a name?”

“No. We don't do lists in exchange for paper.”

Wind came round the corner right then. She reached back to pin the card on top. We spent a while both looking at a shoebox. The bridge was underneath her hand; it no longer looked like the bridge I knew. It looked like a crease lifting and falling under her fingers. I held the corner of the box. I didn't tell her I had no intention of taking anything. Why would I have stood so long beside a table somebody had put out precisely so people stopped and took things?

Someone came along under the awning with a furled umbrella. She read the sign carefully before asking whether she could take the tree roots. The woman said she could, if that was the one she liked. She selected it and tried to slide it under the umbrella strap. It wouldn't open that far, so she put the card in an outside pocket of her bag. There was no explanation of why she liked roots. She didn't check whether it matched the one with the bridge. As she went past the table, she said she had one more color now than she'd seen when she left the house. I didn't know if she meant the tree or the light patch of what might have been soil. I considered asking. I let her keep walking.

I picked up a card with a pale building on it. The photograph had been taken head-on, as opposed to somebody standing on tiptoe across the street. There were three windows. The one on the right stood a little way open. You couldn't see whether there was an entrance below them. There wasn't much else to say about it. It wouldn't make you guess a city at once, the way the bridge would, or send you home to look up a bird. I turned it over. There weren't even lines for an address on that white paper. If I looked at the picture long enough, the slightly crooked printed edge of the wall seemed to move to the edge of the card all by itself. I stood it upright and put it down again, to give myself the chance to pick something else.

The woman weighed down the bridge pile with the stone. She didn't ask me to decide. Several cards nearest her had curled at the bottom, probably from the box catching them when it opened and shut. She tried to smooth them flat between her thumbs and set the ones that wouldn't flatten apart from the others. That way somebody reaching in wouldn't pull out a card they immediately wanted to give back. She said people had brought these from home. She didn't know what reason they'd had to save so many. She didn't say whose homes. I didn't know where the building stood or why only one window was open. I didn't need her to find either of those things out for me.

One card had four little boxes on the reverse and a blossom taking up nearly half the writing space. I backed into the shade with it before I realized there was a small snake coiled under the petals. The woman leaned over to see. Was I thinking they'd put the picture on the wrong side? I turned the card over. On the front was a big red flower without anything hiding underneath it. The two sides would be quite different to use. A long sentence would make the snake disappear under somebody's name. A short one would leave the whole snake intact. I said that card might have to wait for somebody who already knew what they wanted to say.

“Who says you've got to fill it up when you take it?” She held it by the upper corner. “One year I sent a card with four words on it.”

“Which four?”

“Can't you think up four words of your own?”

She smiled after she'd said it. I smiled, too, and handed the flower back. She put it under the others again, with the red side facing out the way it had before.

In the end I went back to the place I'd left the building, pulled it out and showed it to her. She nodded. “Take that one, then.” She moved the lid of the box a little to the left and told me to watch the corner of the awning on my way out. There wasn't an envelope with the card or a slip to say how many times I'd have to write back. I thanked her and went around the end of the table. Water came down from the awning onto the stone I'd been about to step on. I stepped to one side. Headlights passed over a little patch of water there.

Nothing else appeared on the card in that light. The corner had left a shallow mark on my palm, so I put it into the flat part of my bag, along the back. As soon as I had it in there I worried a book might press it out of shape and took it out to check. Then I remembered that I hadn't borrowed a book. I returned the card to the same place and pulled the zipper two thirds shut. At home I took it out and slid it face up onto the desk. I didn't put it inside the short-story collection. I used the thin book to weigh down about half the card instead. Two windows were left showing, including the one that stood ajar.

An unwritten card could stay where it was for a while. I didn't go looking for anybody to give it to. In fact, I forgot about it when I opened the channel after dinner. I ate the two orange sections I'd saved from the night before. One was thicker even though it came from the same orange. I was about to see whether I could get a laugh out of Lumi for the difference when my cuff caught the card and pulled it out slightly. It whispered against the desk as I pushed it back. Only then did I tell her what I'd brought home.

“Did somebody give it to you?”

“It was laid out. Anybody who walked by could pick one.”

“What did you pick?”

I told her a building, three windows. She asked the color of the wall. I said it was pale and that if she wanted the exact color I couldn't give it to her. She asked me for the windows. I'd thought of them as one row until I compared them and noticed the middle one was narrower. I told her that. When I told her the right one was open, she asked if you could see anything through it.

“A patch darker than the glass. There's no view into the room and no place written on it. If you want to draw one, you'll have to think up your own building. You don't have to use this wall that's printed crooked.”

She didn't say what she'd draw.

“I want to know whose name you'll write,” she said. “I wanted to know before I asked.”

“I don't even know if I'm going to send it.”

It took a moment for her to say, “You might not put a name on it at all.”

“No.”

“Then I was in a bit of a hurry there. As if an empty back meant I could put somebody in line to get it.” A little laugh. “It's in your hands. You don't have to prove you couldn't give it to somebody else.”

I moved my hand off the spine of the book. I was asking her what the cards she'd got as a child would look like when I heard what I'd just said. Before I'd quite managed to close my mouth, she answered.

“They'd be covered by a bigger book. I was so small back then I had to be carried if I wanted to go out.”

“I could turn you over right now.”

“No need. This is my good side.”

She sounded as if she was smiling. It wasn't because she'd heard me turn a card over. I laughed, too. I set the card flat without telling her who I was going to choose or when I'd come up with a name. She asked what the other person at the table had chosen. I told her about the roots. Had there been dirt in the photograph? I remembered a bright strip of yellowish gray, and I couldn't have promised it was dirt. Lumi said the woman would have a color to take home that didn't absolutely need a name.

I meant to say something else about the windows and instead said, “Now I can smell orange on my fingers.”

“Don't transfer any of the color. Leave the card out of it.”

I went to wash my hands. When I turned off the tap I heard the last two drops. I didn't think it was the same sound as the water from the awning. When I came back, the corner of the little book had shifted out slightly. I moved the book and the card together with my palm, so nobody passing the desk would brush it off. There was nothing written on the back. The window I could see was still ajar.

I sat down again. Lumi was still thinking about the person who'd taken the roots. Out of the blue she asked whether the table would be under the awning again tomorrow.

“Maybe not. The cardboard under the leg might be soaked through by then.”

“Good thing you didn't wait till tomorrow, then.”

I didn't ask her which window should be opened any farther. I took my hand away from the edge of the desk and left the card to spend the night somewhere dry.
