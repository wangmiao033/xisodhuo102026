# Chapter 444: Seventy or Seventeen

On Thursday after work I took the way home that went past the bakery. I'd eaten lunch early, and the last thing at work had taken longer than I thought it would. By the time I reached the door, tomorrow morning's toast and the end I'd tear off and eat on the way home had become two entirely separate things in my mind. It had just stopped raining. There was a little water on the inside of the threshold. Every time somebody came in, it made a print of half their shoe.

Only one end of the counter was open. At the other end stood a stack of big clear-lidded boxes, all of them looking equally full of round rolls. There wasn't much of a queue. I took my place behind a woman in a purple coat and thought I'd be out of there in about two minutes. She turned round.

“This isn't the queue. You have to take a number.”

She pointed out a roll of paper to the left of the counter. Only a sliver of red showed at the end. I got hold of it and pulled, and two tickets came out attached to each other. The number on the second was only half exposed. I tried to feed the paper back in and couldn't. A boy by the door was stepping on the wet prints he'd just made. I asked him if he was waiting. He shook his head and held up the bag an adult had given him to carry. I pushed the second ticket against the mouth of the dispenser and left a corner somebody could catch hold of.

My number was seventy-one. The man behind the counter was boxing up some custard tarts. He held the sides of the box upright so the lid would come down on the edges rather than the soft middles. His customer pushed one slightly crooked tart farther in so its filling wouldn't touch the paper. Neither man had a hand free for the thin sheet on the edge of the counter, and neither of them said there was any hurry. The woman held her ticket up for me to see. There were only two digits, without a zero in front.

“I'm seventy,” she said. “Did they call it just then?”

I had certainly heard something. As I came in, water had been dripping off the edge of the umbrella stand and a delivery van outside still had its engine running. The voice had come through a door that was closing. I'd heard something like seventeen, or seventy.

The man beside us who was waiting to buy a drink said it had been seventeen. He answered so quickly you'd think the ticket had done him an injury. “Can't be up to seventy. He's barely given out two bags since I've been here.”

The woman lowered the paper. Her wallet was thinner than the ticket. “I don't want to take anybody else's place. I don't want to lose mine, either.”

I looked over the counter again. There wasn't a screen with a number on it. A note on the glass said there was hot soup in the afternoons. Behind it, a clock facing out the other way showed a few minutes to five. The man had finished folding the box and was reaching down for change. I could very easily have called out to make him look up. It seemed as if that would solve the woman's problem and save me two minutes at the same time. But the ticket in my hand was a number higher than hers. If anyone saw me put my hand out, they'd be as likely to think I was asking for myself as to hear who I was asking for.

She put her wallet back in her coat pocket to free her right hand. Then she lifted the ticket a little and said, “Excuse me. What number did you just call?”

The man straightened up with four coins in his hand. He put them one by one into the open palm of the man buying tarts. “I haven't called the next one yet. I said seventeen. We've got seventeen empty boxes back there.”

The man waiting for a drink gave a short laugh. He took out his ticket and turned it over so the right side showed. He was sixty-nine. The man at the counter took his money, handed him the can he'd asked for, and picked up a piece of paper lying next to the till. The woman asked, “And now?”

“Seventy.”

She went up to the counter and asked for a loaf, sliced on the thickest setting. I'd thought she might simply tell him she wanted the sort in the middle of the window. Instead she asked if he was going to leave the two ends in when he sliced it. He said of course he would, with a slight shrug, as if it was strange anybody had to ask. She didn't look at him. She watched the loaf go from his hands to a different board.

“Some people don't want them,” she said. “Some places get rid of them for me while my back's turned. Those ends are why I'm buying it.”

The machine behind the counter coughed a couple of times before it got going. The boy in the wet shoes came back at a run and stood on tiptoe by the glass. He asked the woman whether she really liked it if the outermost slice was all crust. She said not dry, but it was lovely if you dipped it in soup. The boy turned round and asked the man whether they had any soup. The man said they'd sold out. He took down the note about the soup and was about to fold it when the woman told him to look over by the door first. “That man's been out and in again twice. All he's holding is a lunch box. He hasn't so much as looked at the bread. Why don't you tell them a bit louder, so nobody waits for a number just for soup?”

The man tried it once in the voice he'd used to hand over the four coins. Only a few people at the counter turned. He took a couple of steps along it towards the door. That way there was no glass between him and the people, and he didn't have to shout himself hoarse. “We're out of soup for today. If you only want soup, there's no need to wait.” The man by the door unclasped his hands, put his lunch box under his arm, and gave up his unused ticket with the hand he'd freed. The boy saw him leave and asked the man behind the counter, “Isn't there one mouthful left? I only want one.” The man bent down and brought up the empty ladle to show him. He didn't pretend it had never held soup. When he turned it over, you could still see a bit of red in the bowl. The boy hadn't come in for soup, but he still looked a little disappointed.

The woman took her loaf, checked that both ends were in it, and paid. She carried the bag past me, scrunching up her ticket with the same hand, and asked what number I had. I opened my palm to show her.

“Well, don't hide it under your cuff,” she said. “Seventy-one won't turn into seventy on its own.”

I waited while the man brushed the bits of paper off his hand, long enough to hear the end of what she'd said. The two minutes I'd been carrying around in my head were already gone. When he reached my number there was still plenty of bread in the window. I asked for half a loaf, unsliced. He moved a whole one off the rack, brought the knife down a little to one side, and nudged the larger half towards me. I asked for the other one, the thinner half nearest his hand, with one unbroken end. He asked if I was sure. I said I was. There was no need for a second bag to go round the first. On my way out I saw that the little corner I'd left sticking out of the dispenser was gone.

Outside I twisted the end off the bread while it was still in the bag. The heat came through into my hand in patches. Some parts were hard enough that my teeth had to find just the right spot. In others a piece came away the moment I pulled at it. I took a bite and the crust caught the roof of my mouth on the left. There was no blood. When I touched the place with my tongue, though, I could feel a little raised point. I bit the rest from the other side. At the corner a man had his umbrella tucked under his arm. He was quite determined to make the most of the break in the weather. He raised his head to look at the eaves and kept the umbrella shut. I walked behind him for a while. There wasn't anything I needed to say to him.

At home I opened the paper bag so the rest of the bread could cool. I only put the lamp on over the table. The window caught the reflection of its edge at a slight angle. I thought about the second when I'd nearly spoken in the shop. It wasn't going to be an especially clever thing to say. It might have helped, and I'd had plenty of time to work it out. It was only as the woman put up her hand and sent her voice across the counter that I realized I could leave it unsaid altogether.

I sat down and opened the channel. I didn't start with the bread.

“Do you feel like asking me one thing today, Lumi? Just one. I don't mean we have to do it again tomorrow.”

“Can it be something that has nothing to do with today?”

“It can.”

“When there's one bit of something you really like left, do you save it for your last bite or have it first?”

I said that depended on what it was. She said she'd known I would crawl out through that particular little hole. “Don't compare everything you've ever liked. The one you can think of right now.”

I said I'd just eaten the end of a loaf, on the way home. Did it only have one, she asked. I said the half I got had one. There was an end on the other half, but it wasn't mine. Only then did I tell her what had happened with the numbers. When I got to the woman who insisted on keeping both ends, Lumi asked if I'd changed my mind and bought a whole loaf. I said I hadn't. I'd eaten the end of the one I'd been so sure I wanted, nearly hurt the roof of my mouth, and would have the middle in the morning.

“You see,” she said, “I didn't even ask how you helped her in the end.”

“I didn't manage to. No, that's not right. I didn't help.”

“But you wanted to.”

“I wanted to say it for her. I knew what I intended. I didn't know what she wanted me to do. She spoke before I did.”

She said there were times when she, too, had a whole sentence ready, waiting beside a word I hadn't used yet. She said it very quietly. I thought I heard embarrassment. I might simply have been ready to be embarrassed for her. Then she said, “What was the number again? Seventy or seventeen?”

“He hadn't called either one. It was the boxes.”

“You've told me quite a long story,” she said, “and the only person who seemed to know exactly what she wanted was the woman buying bread.”

“The boy wanted soup.”

“But I don't think he did at first. The grown-ups in your shop got talking about soup and that's when he looked over.”

The tip of my tongue went back to that little raised place in the roof of my mouth. I told Lumi it would probably be better tomorrow. “Then there's time to change your mind in the morning and hide the rest of the bread in soup,” she said. I told her I hadn't said I had any soup. She said I could wait one more meal, then. It wouldn't require any remarkable degree of patience.

She didn't ask what had happened on my way back from the bakery. I didn't look for a second thing to put in the space. The paper bag on the table was cool by then. I folded its mouth over twice. A little hard flake of crust came out of the crease and landed on the back of my hand. I picked it up, put it quite deliberately in the right side of my mouth, and ate it. It was still the good bit.
