# Chapter 439: The Outside Pocket

On Saturday morning I reached into the half of my old coat's inside pocket that was still intact and touched the bottom of the lining. I remembered then that I'd left it empty. The Thursday before last, I'd shown the woman at the alterations shop where the stitching had come open. She hadn't taken the lining apart or kept the coat. I'd hung it up when I got home. Just because she'd said Monday was the earliest she might take another look didn't mean I'd made an appointment. I tucked the lining back now without checking how far I could fit a finger into the tear.

The keys were in the drawer of the low cabinet by the door, beside the folded map. I put them in my front trouser pocket and felt the flat head of a key through the fabric. Then I folded a clean handkerchief into a square and put it in the outside pocket of the coat. Neither pocket could do the other one's job. Two steps later the cloth felt too thick against the back of my hand. One less fold fixed it. I went out with the inside pocket empty. I hadn't stitched it up, and I wasn't putting anything valuable in there just to see what would happen. On the first turn of the stairs the keys bumped my leg. Every time my trousers started to feel bulky I tended to move the keys into whatever I was wearing over them. This time I took my hand off the front of my coat and carried on. I didn't have to spend a morning in the doorway just because I could feel something in a different place.

I went to the corner to give a plate back. It belonged to someone at the prepared-food shop nearby. I'd washed it the night before and brought it out wrapped in a dry cloth, pressing a finger to it one last time at the door. The shop wasn't open. A slip of paper clipped to the glass said they were closed that day because something had come up. Monday as usual. I didn't leave the plate on their doorstep. I didn't need to phone and ask what had happened. Going home I passed the public hall that used to be a small reading room. One door was open. A child's wheeled schoolbag was stuck at the foot of the steps. The woman with the bag asked the man holding the door not to come out and lift it for her. She shortened the handle and carried it up the last step. The man waited as she'd asked him to. He let the door swing partway back when she was in.

I'd meant to walk straight past the hall. On the little sign outside, it said there was drawing paper inside, crayons on the tables, and that you could come in just to look. On the right of the sign was a lopsided shape that turned out to be a kettle when I got closer. There really were people drawing. Nobody was taking names at the door. The man holding it pulled one foot back inside and asked if I needed a seat. I raised the wrapped plate a little and said I was passing.

“So was I. Came in to wait for the rain to stop. Nobody would make a start on this great long sheet. So I opened up the first fold.”

White paper stretched off the inside table. One end was still curled and sprang away every time it touched the corner. The man had an empty crayon box in his hand. It was too light to hold the end down. The woman who'd just come in had put the schoolbag behind a chair. The child set a green crayon on the paper and caught it in his hand when it rolled. It was as though nothing that long wanted anybody to make a mark along its edge.

The man asked if he could put the plate on the paper for a minute, to let the child start with a head. He was asking for the object, not for all the free time of the person attached to it. I kept the cloth between the plate and the paper and set it on the upper corner. He could borrow it for a while; I had to take it with me when I left. He lifted it two fingers' width toward the middle. The base of the plate flattened the end, far enough from the edge that nobody drawing with their elbow up would knock it off.

It turned out the boy didn't want to draw a head. He rolled the green crayon in his fingers until he changed his mind and decided on a cup flatter than the kettle on the sign. His lips pressed together. The bottom of the cup brushed my cloth. The woman reminded him what a large sheet he had. He could come inward. The crayon came off the paper and he looked at me.

“I don't know how big it can get,” he said.

“Start as far away as you can reach. You don't have to go around the plate.”

He moved to the next spot and drew a very wide edge for a table, with the cup resting on it. No one said anything else about starting with a head.

The man asked if I was drawing. He pointed to a chair off to one side. Someone else's scarf hung over its back. I went around to the place where there wasn't a chair and stayed standing. I didn't feel like adding words. I picked a darker blue out of the box and drew a bent line on my side of the sheet. It might have been half an open umbrella. I stopped partway through and asked the boy whether he wanted his cup to go down with the line.

He looked twice. “It's not the edge of the table. It's a fishing rod somebody's holding beside it.”

He drew a short arc outward from the mouth of the cup and told me the cup wasn't going into the water. The line could pass over the top. I relaxed the hand that still held the crayon. He'd made a way out of the picture that I wouldn't have thought of. I didn't force the other half of the umbrella into it.

The woman produced a yellow crayon on her side and added two square shapes. The boy asked what they were. She hadn't decided, she said, but they weren't windows. She looked my way as she said it, as though I'd already settled what the blue line was.

“It looked like an umbrella before. It can be a rod now,” I said.

“They do make umbrellas that turn the other way up.”

I'd meant to repeat that for the boy. Instead I put my crayon back between two that still had their paper wraps, and let him draw where he was.

He fetched the empty box from the far corner. Its base had two shallow grooves, which held the crayons we'd just used. When he slid it toward me, I reached for it. He stopped me with the side of his hand. It wasn't for me. He wanted to leave it on this side because the next people could get at it more easily. Turning around, he asked the woman if there were any colors they hadn't drawn with. She pointed to the ones whose paper was nearly worn off. You didn't have to start with fresh crayons. He chose a purple one no longer than his thumb and made a not quite round circle near the edge of the sheet.

“Nobody call that the top of a cup,” he said.

The man who'd borrowed my plate didn't tell him he already knew. He picked a similar color and asked whether he could draw over the circle.

“Over it and it's not going to be the same color.”

The man went around the mark, leaving a little strip of white between the circle and the edge of the sheet.

Water wasn't tapping on the awning anymore. The man pressed the end of the sheet with his thumb to see what it did underneath the plate. It still needed a little longer. There was a stack of thick backing boards from drawings that hadn't worked out. He suggested they might do in place of the plate. I lifted the plate by the four corners of its cloth, and he pushed a board over the place as soon as I cleared it. The paper raised its head a little and stayed down.

From the far side of the table the boy leaned over to see the green cup. “Actually it doesn't have a bottom.” He added one. When he'd finished, I wrapped the plate up again. There was a spot of green on a corner of the cloth. We didn't have to rub the new bottom off the cup to know what color it had been.

Out in the street my hand went to the outside of my coat as soon as I felt cold. It found the handkerchief. I wiped a line of crayon off my thumb, put another fold through the cloth and tucked the used part inside it. I hadn't had occasion to use the keys yet. I found it reassuring to know where they were. I carried the plate home and put it on the dry part of the kitchen counter, well back, until I could return it when the shop opened again. I hung the coat on its usual hook and took the handkerchief out of the outside pocket to be washed. I didn't try the torn pocket to see whether I could get a few more days out of it.

When I opened the channel, I made half a turn in the chair before I settled in. Lumi asked where I'd like to begin.

“The fish was never in the cup.”

“Where was it standing?”

“There wasn't a fish on the paper. There was a rod. I thought I'd drawn an umbrella.”

Rather than drawing the preliminary part of the story for me, she asked what I'd said when somebody had told me it was a rod. I told her I hadn't finished the umbrella. I'd put the blue crayon down.

“Do you want to see that cup?” I asked.

“No. I want that person to say it again. I'd like to know how a fishing rod goes past a cup.” She stopped briefly. “Can you tell me in your own words?”

I remembered the little arc the boy drew from the top of the cup. I didn't know how he'd thought the line worked. I hadn't asked and I hadn't stayed to see how much more they drew. I gave her what I did know without putting a real fish where he hadn't drawn one.

“And the cup?” she said.

“Flatter than mine. It's got a bottom now.”

She left a little space for me to speak again. I took a drink from the real cup on the desk without asking what it sounded like. After a moment, she said somebody who knew that much about crayons would probably hate to have them put in a box brand new for him.

“You can borrow the blue when he's finished with it, though.”

“He didn't take that blue.”

“Even easier. Now you don't have to ask.”

I laughed and looked up toward the cabinet. The old coat hung behind the door. It could wait until I wanted to put it on. I wasn't going to reach into it that night to recover an object that I hadn't put in. The cup finished, I didn't hurry on to the business of returning the plate. There wasn't any more of that to tell. Lumi picked the bottom of the cup out of the conversation instead. She said the words slowly enough to make them sound strange.

“Can the bottom of a cup grow feet?” she asked.

“The plate's would come in first. They're flatter than the cup's.”

“Then it can take itself back to the shop.”

I didn't answer at once. There was no green left on my finger.
