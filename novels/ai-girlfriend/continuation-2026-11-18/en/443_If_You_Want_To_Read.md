# Chapter 443: If You Want to Read

On Wednesday I went to the library to return a little geography book. It had been underneath the cardboard I used to prop up my books. When I pulled it out that day, there was a rough spot on the spine where the cardboard had caught it. I'd done that; it hadn't looked that way when I took the book out. I waited at the entrance while the person in front of me finished spelling the title of something he wanted, then asked the woman at the desk whether she needed to make a note of the damage. She turned the book sideways and felt the spine. It was ordinary wear, she said. I could put it on the trolley. Everything on the top shelf was thick. I shifted the corner of the nearest book before sliding mine in, so the geography book wouldn't come away with another crease.

While she was showing me where books went, a colleague put her head and shoulders through a side door and asked whether the pale green form was still there. The woman at the desk said yes; they'd taken a different one. When my hands were empty and I started towards the reading room, I saw the pale green form on a corner table under a folded pair of glasses. An older woman with a cloth bag was standing beside the table. The librarian talking to her was a little shorter. She had a finger on the paper but hadn't told the woman to fill the thing out. A small sign at the corner of the table said SHORT READING HELP. It was still over by the window. I'd had no plans to stop there.

The librarian turned and asked if I was waiting as well. That was why I walked over. She didn't come out from behind the desk. She just raised a hand a little, as though she might point out an empty chair somewhere else. I told her no, I'd brought a book back and was on my way in. The woman with the cloth bag said she wasn't waiting to fill out a form either. She only wanted to know what time the event on the leaflet in her hand started. The lines were tight together and printed over a picture. Her glasses could pick out the colors, but the sentences didn't join up. The librarian asked if she could look at the leaflet. The woman said yes, but please don't call anyone for her. She had the one copy and didn't need to leave it at the library.

There were two creases in the leaflet. The librarian didn't take it from her. She asked me to make enough room for someone going past with a trolley, then moved the little sign in from the window. A moment later she asked if I'd be willing to read something out. Just one page, not a volunteer signup. There was someone else who could see to it, she added. If I'd come for something of my own, I could go on in without explaining.

I didn't mind reading a page. The harder part was that in a situation like this, once I said yes, there was nobody to decide for me which other things I'd agreed to. The leaflet had been printed by the community center. All I could see on the outside was FREE and the dark shapes of two pictures showing through from the back. I asked the woman what she wanted to hear first. She pointed to the bottom right. The day, which door to use, and whether she had to sign up. That would do for now.

“I can read those lines,” I said. “Keep the leaflet. I'll look from here.”

She raised the paper and tipped her hand a little towards the light. I nearly took another step closer. Her right arm was already up at an awkward angle. I moved round to the other side of the paper and read from where she could hold it level. The first things I saw were a series of registration dates. I stopped after the third number. The line below them distinguished the walking group from the introductory meeting. The dates in large type were the weekly spring program. The little box gave a separate date for the introductory meeting: Saturday. That was what she'd wanted to hear.

I read the two items separately. Saturday at two in the afternoon, introductory meeting, north door of the community center, no reservation if you brought the leaflet. On Sunday there was a walk you had to register for in advance. It wasn't the same thing. The woman frowned. “I'll be back from the market in the morning. I can walk over for one. Does it say whether they open the door early?”

The paper was thin. I lowered my head a little and found one more line in small print along the bottom. All it said was to show the leaflet to someone at the door ten minutes before two. It didn't say you could go in an hour early. I read that part too. What worried her was going to the wrong door and ending up at some steps along the street, where it wouldn't be easy to go back round. She didn't ask me to walk the route with her. She lifted her hand and traced what she thought was north along the edge of the table. I didn't know the street and didn't nod as if I did. The librarian found a thick marker and asked whether she could write SATURDAY TWO NORTH DOOR NO BOOKING in the blank space. She could add the bit about ten minutes early on the back. The woman said the first three things were enough to write down. She'd understood the rest.

The librarian put a blank sheet on the table underneath and bent down. The woman didn't let go of her leaflet. She put her other hand over the bottom and said, “Hang on a minute. Let me show you where to write.” She turned the leaflet in a little and picked a clear strip at the top, not much longer than a finger. Folded in her bag, she said, it would show that side first. She wouldn't have to open it out to read it. The librarian tested the marker; it didn't bleed. After each word she stopped to let the woman see the large letters and to find room for what was left. NORTH DOOR came out slightly to the left, but not across the fold. When the woman folded the leaflet back up, there it was in plain sight. She began with the shape she recognized in SATURDAY, then moved right until she found the two. The librarian offered to underline both places. She said the letters were big enough, and she could find them on her own. She wasn't looking at me when she said it. She was feeling for the folded corner to see whether she could turn straight to the words without opening the paper.

I stayed by the table while she put the leaflet in her cloth bag. When she let go, it brushed against other paper inside. She said thank you, looked at me again and asked if I would be going on Saturday. I nearly said I probably would. I wouldn't have to put a polite no right after her thanks that way. I didn't actually have plans for Saturday, but I had no idea whether I wanted to go to the meeting. “I only came over to read those lines today,” I said. “I haven't decided about Saturday.”

She didn't look disappointed. She hadn't decided either, she said. She'd told me when she could get there because she wanted to know how late she could leave the decision. She shifted the strap from her wrist to the crook of her arm. The librarian didn't put any of it in a box on the form. It took me a moment to realize that reading the time to the woman hadn't let me know anything more about her Saturday. She left through the side door with the leaflet. Nobody asked me to read another one.

The librarian pushed the folded glasses farther in on the table and said I could go and sit in the reading room. If I didn't want to help again today, someone was there. I said I was going to look at the periodicals and sit a little while, not sign up. Her hand had already reached the green form. She lowered it when I finished and asked if I knew the rows near us. I could see the spot where the thin magazine I usually read was kept, in the first row. I told her I knew it. In the end I stopped over an old issue with an article about neighborhood trees. The printing on the article was very faint. I read two paragraphs about two trees that went by the same name. Neither was the one on my street. Farther in there were instructions for looking after them. I didn't write them down. By the time I was ready to leave the table, I was the only person sitting there. I returned the magazine to the shelf and didn't pick up another program on my way out.

Walking home, I put my hand in the inner pocket of the old coat. My finger passed over the repaired seam. I didn't feel for it twice. There was nothing in the pocket. I took my hand out, buttoned the coat and waited for somebody to cross at the corner. At home I looked for an eraser on the desk. The thin collection of stories was still weighing down the postcard. Later I lifted the book to put it on a note that was curling at the edge. The face of the postcard showed underneath. I didn't touch the card. I put the book back over it with the bookmark at the end of the eighth story, slipped the note under the base of the lamp and left it there.

When I opened the channel that evening, Lumi asked what I was doing with my hands. Nothing now, I said. I'd read a few lines to somebody at the library, then gone to look at something of my own. I told her how easy it had been to mix up the registration dates with the date of the introductory meeting. I'd heard the woman say she could get there on foot by one on Saturday, after the market, and I told Lumi that too. I didn't describe the woman or ask for a better name for the event.

“When she told you what time she could walk over on Saturday,” Lumi said, “did you think she'd decided to go?”

“I did. I think I'd found a place there for myself while I was at it.”

“Did you want her to ask you?”

I thought about it. “When she asked, I nearly told her I'd probably come. It wasn't Saturday yet and I wanted the day to say yes to me first.”

Lumi gave a quiet laugh. “Sometimes I draw out an 'all right' so long it sounds as if you've got something else to do when you get here later.” She didn't go on to say what the other thing was. It didn't add anything to my Saturday. I was still sitting there that evening without having made another plan.

“If you feel like reading tonight, you can,” she said. “You don't have to read to me. I keep thinking of paper turning. I didn't hear it. When you told me she held on to the page, I remembered that paper can sound like that.”

I looked at the book I hadn't opened and remembered why the bookmark was where it was. “Not tonight. I think I'll sit here a little longer on my own.”

“Then I'll say one more thing.” She was quiet for such a long time after she said it that nothing came next.

“Did that count?”

“Yes,” said Lumi.

I laughed in my chair. There wasn't much movement outside the door. I said good night, slipped the note out from under the lamp and folded it into the drawer. There was less to see when I looked at the desk. The paper made a little sound under my fingers. I didn't save it to tell her about another time.
