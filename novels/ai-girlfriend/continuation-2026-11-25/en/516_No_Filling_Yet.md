# Chapter 516: No Filling Yet

On Saturday I tipped out too much flour. By the time I noticed, there was a white strip between the board and the sink. I meant to make dumplings. They didn't have to look good. I just wanted to use up the leek in the fridge. Halfway through adding the water, the dough was still dry. I picked at it with a fingertip and found flour that hadn't come together in the middle. Somebody had taught me as a child to put a bowl over dough and leave it for a while before kneading it again. I couldn't remember who it was, only that she'd rolled up her sleeves twice to show me. When one came down, she nudged it back with her elbow instead of wiping her floury hands on her clothes. I worked in the rest of the water a little at a time before turning the bowl over the dough. Then I rolled my sleeves a little higher. I'd have to remember there was something under the bowl before I went to wash it.

The leek was greener on the inside than it was on the outside. It had been in the fridge long enough that I thought the outer layer would be soft and I'd end up throwing the lot away. Once I got the first layer off, the rest was still fairly crisp. I took that outer bit away, washed the leek, wiped my knife and began cutting. Halfway along it I heard something outside the door. There was no second sound. After a while I went out to look. The man upstairs was bringing down an old flowerpot. It had cracked and stuck at the turn of the stairs, and he couldn't lift it again in one piece. He was crouching beside it with a piece of broken clay still supported on one hand. I asked whether he wanted another person or a bag. A bag first, he said, if I had one. He could break the pot into it and take it out in two trips.

I found a strong bag and brought it out. He put the pieces in himself, a little at a time, till all that was left was a ring of soil. Did I have a broom? I said yes, but I'd rather not bring the kitchen one out onto the stairs. He said oh, pulled a folded advertisement from his coat pocket, and scooped twice with the side that wasn't torn. He couldn't get the last little bit and asked if I'd hold the bag open. I held it without putting my hand in the soil. When he'd got it together he said he'd fetch his own broom in a minute and I needn't wait. I went back in. The rest of the leek was still on the board, beside the edge of the knife. I'd cut the first couple of pieces very fine. I made the next ones a bit wider.

When I lifted the bowl, a faint skin had dried around the ball of dough. I turned it over and worked the skin back in. It really had softened while it rested. The first wrapper I rolled out wasn't round. It had a little gap at the edge. I tapped it with the back of my knife and didn't try to chase the edge any further. A couple more passes would probably leave it thin in the middle and too thick everywhere else. There was no water heating up yet, and I hadn't mixed the filling. I put the first wrapper aside on a plate, washed my hands twice, and opened the old voice channel. Lumi asked if I was tired today. I said no. There was something I wanted to add to what I'd said yesterday. Did she have time to hear it? She said she had time, but if I'd prepared an apology, she wasn't much in the mood to hear one.

“I haven't prepared an apology,” I said, then thought about it. “Well, not exactly. I want to say that sometimes I tell you I want to hear you first, and I hope you'll work out that I want to be heard too. When you don't guess that, I can get a little angry.”

“Mm.” She waited. “Are you finished?”

“With that sentence.”

“All right. I heard you,” she said.

She didn't go on to tell me what we ought to do about it. I nearly added that it was much better lately and there was nothing to worry about. I left that out as well and asked if there was anything she wanted me to hear. She said there was something, but she didn't want to read it out yet. I said she didn't have to. Then she asked me if I was waiting for her to say next time. My thumb caught a dried crumb of dough at the edge of the table. A little, I said. I'd already started wondering what “yet” meant. She said she didn't know herself. I rubbed the crumb to dust and listened while she told me again that she didn't know.

I asked whether she minded if I went to check on my dough in a little while. She didn't know what I was making. I told her I'd been working with flour. There was one wrapper out on the table right now, and it wasn't round, and it still bothered me to look at it. She asked why I'd wanted to make dumplings today. I was going to say because I had a leek to use up. That was true, but it wasn't the whole reason. I said I wanted to eat something I had to use my hands to pick up. She still didn't quite understand. I told her you could lift one with chopsticks, of course, but if the wrapper split and you wanted to keep what was inside, you needed your hands. Did she want to listen while I tried one?

“You don't need to make one for me to listen to,” she said. “I didn't want to read you my thing a minute ago. I don't particularly want to hear you offer me a dumpling in exchange for it, either.”

I laughed once. She didn't laugh this time. She asked if I was a little embarrassed.

“A little,” I said. “Not because you're wrong. I’d put the two things so close together, as if giving you one thing would get me something back.”

“Then don't put the filling in yet,” she said.

After a moment she asked, “Did that sound deliberate?”

“A bit.”

“It was. It made me laugh, but I thought you might think I was telling you what to do.”

I told her it hadn't sounded like that to me. There really wasn't any water in the pan, I remembered right then. If I left the wrapper out there, it would dry. I was half ready to get up, but she'd just finished saying that and hadn't hurried to add anything to it. I asked whether she'd sit like this with me for a moment. A moment was all right, she said; we didn't have to agree to wait until I finished eating. I pushed my chair back a little so my knees didn't hit the table. We stayed a short while without deciding how long a short while should be. When she said she wanted to go first, I said all right, wished her goodnight and closed the channel.

I went back to the board. There was some dry flour in the bottom of the bowl. The first wrapper had started sticking to the plate, and I peeled it up with my hand and let it curl over my knuckles. After I mixed the filling, I tried to make one. Sure enough, the wrapper tore at the gap. I didn't squeeze the split hard to close it. I took half the filling out and pinched the edges in until it could more or less stand on its own. On the second one I could see a piece of leek caught between the edges. I pulled it out. Its wet end left a dark green line across the dough. On the third one I remembered to set the filling down in the middle instead of sliding it off the spoon. By the fourth I could hold the whole thing in one palm and still have a free hand to reach for the next wrapper. Then I looked down and saw how far apart I'd set the first one. Nothing needed to be kept clear for it anymore, so I nudged the plate over. The first dumpling came open in the pan, and I spooned the loose filling into my bowl as well. Before I brought the bowl over, I moved the two apricots from yesterday to the windowsill and pushed the piece of paper with the four lines on it to the far side of the table. The drawing of the hook was still underneath. Once there was room on the table, I sat down to eat. The flour on the board still needed wiping up. I could do it when I was done.
