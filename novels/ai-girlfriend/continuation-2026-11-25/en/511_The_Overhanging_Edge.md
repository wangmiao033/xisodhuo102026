# Chapter 511: The Overhanging Edge

On Monday morning somebody downstairs called to say the man with the cover samples had arrived. I stood by the phone for a moment before I remembered they meant the new batch coming in, not the one we were sending out. I'd turned half the collar of my coat in when I came out of the film at the community hall yesterday and forgotten to turn it back. On my way down to meet the man, one side kept catching against my neck. I undid a button, which gave him room to lean the bottom of the box against my chest and get a hand free for me to sign. Did I want him to carry it upstairs? Here was fine, I said. The door up there wasn't open yet.

I took the box to the workroom myself. Mia pinched the end of the tape with her thumb and pulled a long strip off the top. There was wrapping underneath, then another layer before we got to a stack of cream-colored samples. The ends meant to be trimmed faced out. Whoever packed them had tucked some cushioning in at the sides so they wouldn't shift. Mia picked one up, ran a finger down the edge, and asked me to check the design number. The number was right, I said, but there was too much on this end. When I laid it on the master, a fuzzy white line stuck out between the two sheets. I thought it was a piece of the wrapping that had frayed. When I tried to pick it off, both sheets moved. Mia leaned in, her shoulder making a sound against the cardboard lid. She turned the master all the way round. The line was still there.

“Are all six like this?” she asked.

I took two from the middle, careful not to touch their faces, and lined up the edges against the master. Then I checked the rest against it one by one. All the same. The people at the factory had left extra stock for their tag. It had to come off before the samples could go in the display stand, or they'd stick up at six different angles. Mia lifted her shoulder and the lid sprang open. The delivery van would be downstairs in another half hour, she said. The bindery two streets over needed all six for a color check. Couldn't we send them as they were? We could clip a note on with the right size.

“Yes,” I said. I was still looking at that bit of white. It took me another second to hear exactly what I'd agreed to. I picked up the dispatch sheet she'd left at the edge of the table. “I can cut two before the driver gets here. Put down two for the delivery. Don't mark the others as dispatched today.”

“Sign for the whole batch,” she said. “They can make out their paperwork ahead of time. It'll save you doing it twice.”

I knew she'd already been looking for this box three times that morning. She wanted to give the bindery a job number so they could book the work in, and she really did mean to save me a little time. She turned the clipboard toward me. Her finger was still next to the box that said all six were present. I took hold of the board but not the pen she held out.

“If the other four aren't on the morning van, who are they supposed to ask about the missing samples?” I asked.

“They can ask me.”

“It's my name on the sheet. I don't want you explaining why I signed for six.”

Outside, somebody pushed a low trolley over the joints in the tiles. Mia lifted the clipboard out of its way. The pen slid down and she caught it with the heel of her hand. When could I cut the other four today? I nearly said before lunch. There was already something on my desk a colleague had to check by noon. I'd set it apart as soon as I came in so I wouldn't give him the wrong color. I said I could finish at three. There wasn't a second van on Mondays and I wasn't taking the samples to the shop myself.

“I thought you went that way after work.”

“Sometimes.” It only took a few minutes longer to go that way. But as soon as she asked, I pictured a day I didn't go, with somebody standing in a doorway waiting for a box that, according to the sheet, had already left. “Not this time. They can go on the van tomorrow.”

Mia crossed out the six and wrote two. With the tip of her pen she started another line for the four the next morning. Would she tell the bindery they'd only have the first two colors this morning, so they didn't have to clear their table for all six? She said she'd phone. I got the wooden ruler from my drawer. There was a piece of stiff paper left over from last week that was thick enough to work on underneath. She pushed the top two samples toward me. Those colors were hard to mistake for each other once you had them side by side. I pressed the extra edge of the first one outward. The blade dragged at the last inch. When I stopped I saw my left hand was pressing too far down. I moved it and finished the cut. A strip so narrow it barely showed above the work surface curled slowly out from under the ruler. When both were cut I held them under the light. No fibers had turned back over the face.

Mia stopped me on my way downstairs. She was holding the blue sample from last Thursday with the faint impression in the cloth. There was no name on it; half the number was still visible on the back of the good sample. Somebody had asked for a piece of board to put at the bottom of a shipping carton and nearly taken the blue one. She put it back by the window with the mark facing in so people passing wouldn't think it was for the bin. “The apprentices are coming on Thursday. It'll be here. We'll just turn it around.”

“The time they gave us hasn't changed?”

“Not yet. Our label is still there, too. Turn it a little.” She turned two fingers together to show me, then asked at the door whether I wanted a hand or if she should come down with me. Two samples didn't weigh much, I said. I could do it. Both workroom doors were open. I nudged the second one farther with my elbow and got downstairs without scraping the piece of paper pasted across the top of the box.

The driver was a few minutes early. He looked at my clipboard and pointed to the new two underneath the six. Which was it for this trip? I said two; the other four were for tomorrow morning. He asked me to say it again and put a big 2 on his copy, so large it covered the next box. I started to explain why they were going separately. He'd already turned the clipboard over. No rush, he said. They had the same sheet at the other end. Once the box was in the van, it slid a little along the floor. He pulled a strap out from beside the back door and drew it tight. The box stayed put.

I cut the other four that afternoon. Mia was on the phone in the workroom when I finished. She held up a pen for me to wait. I put the four on my own desk instead, with the white tape running out from under the lid where it would be easy to find in the morning. The ruler went back in its drawer and the frayed edges went in the wastepaper box. Going down the stairs, I felt my collar again and turned the folded half out at last. The glass door downstairs had fogged up. As I pushed it open, somebody on the street was coming in with a clipboard tucked between his arms. I stepped out of his way. His board knocked against me, but his pen didn't fall.

I didn't open the old voice channel that evening. The rice on the stove had dried a little. I added water and pressed apart the clump stuck against the pan. After eating I undid both buttons on the coat and hung it beside the hat. The four lines were still on the table. I picked up the cloth I'd put under my bowl and took it away to hang. Water ran off one corner into the dish. I thought there might be another drop and stood there a little longer. The dish made no sound. I washed my hands and switched off the kitchen light.
