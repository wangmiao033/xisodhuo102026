# Chapter 508: Keep That End Still

After work on Friday I went the long way across the little square. The white tent where I'd patched my bicycle tyre the previous Sunday wasn't up. There was a row of long tables against the brick wall instead. A paper sign held down by two bricks said REPAIR TABLE, OPEN TILL SIX. Whoever had written it was afraid the brick would cover the six, so they'd drawn a bent line from the word and written it over again in the margin. A man was picking wood shavings off the bottoms of his trousers. He hurried over to tell the people waiting by the wall that there was another table behind the glass doors. If they wanted to see a cupboard door rehung, now was the time to go in.

I hadn't brought anything that needed fixing. I'd looked at a hook in the secondhand shop on Wednesday and hadn't even bought the screws to go with it. The drawing of it was still on the table at home. Going through the door, I patted my pocket anyway, as though I ought to be able to produce something that gave me a reason for being there. A woman near the door made a little room for me. “Come in a bit,” she said. “You'll see better.” She took a step the other way herself to let a child in behind her.

On the table was a small green cupboard door. The paint had dried unevenly and you could see the colour of the wood at the edge. The man doing the demonstration had hung it loosely and didn’t shut it straightaway. He wanted us to look at the two hinges first. One was screwed in tight. Beneath the other was a piece of card folded in three. He slipped the card out and asked the person nearest to hold the edge while he showed us where the corner caught. Everybody looked towards the sound. Then he put the card back and the corner came up a little. Someone in a work apron asked if the card was supposed to stay there for ever. It could, he said, but he'd look for a thin shim that wouldn't swell when it got damp. There was no point getting the door straight today if you had to move it again in two days. The door closed without a sound. It was quieter than it had been while he was talking. The child, who'd been waiting for the knock, reached for the gap and the woman by the door said his name gently.

At another table further back, a man had set a narrow drawer between two felt pads. The handle was missing and there were two dark circles where it had been. He tried pushing one corner back and the other side came up. He put his elbow down on it and nearly got his cuff caught under a strip of wood. I knew him by sight from our row of buildings but couldn't have told you his name. He never let the door of his letterbox bang. He hooked a finger round it until it shut. I'd already started towards the cupboard door again. After two steps I went back. “Would you like me to hold one side?”

He looked at me and said no at first. There were clamps on the table. He tried two. One didn't reach far enough and the other had lost the rubber pad off its foot. Putting the second one down, he asked if I'd got a bus to catch. I said I hadn't. I was walking home.

“Hold this end for me, then,” he said. “Keep it down on the felt. If the sides go crooked, leave them for me to put right.”

I put my hands where he'd pointed. He told me to move them out a little. Otherwise he wouldn't know if the strip of wood was sitting straight. I moved my hands. There was an old rounded edge under my left palm. The paint had come off in three places that I could see, but there wasn't a rough bit anywhere I touched. He bent to look from the inside and brought the joint together with both hands. Once the gap closed, I thought that was it. I'd just lifted my left thumb when he said, “Not quite yet. You've got it in a good place.” He didn't sound cross. I felt caught out all the same and pressed harder. He glanced at my hands and laughed. “You don't have to flatten the wood.”

Someone behind me asked which table the drawer belonged to and if she could take a photo to show the people at the entrance. I was about to turn sideways when she took a step closer. The owner of the drawer looked up. “Let me try it once. You can do the cupboard door if they'll let you. I'd like to leave this one where it is for now.” By the time she reached the other table the man had opened the door again to show the lower hinge. I heard somebody ask if a photo was all right and didn't follow what happened after that. I looked down at the grain by my fingers and waited for him to tell me what he needed. He shifted his head to see the joint from the other side. His finger stopped at the corner block. “All right, ease up a little. Not all the way.” I took off a little of the pressure. Both pieces of felt were still where he'd put them. The drawer didn't slide or tip. Then he said it was all right, and I took both hands away.

He used the drawer for tea towels, he said. There were always too many of them in it. He'd still have to find a way to fix the corner after the pieces fitted together. He could do part of the job today; there was no point in getting home quicker if he was going to fill the drawer right up again. Somebody passed him his paper bag. He opened it to show me he'd brought none of the tea towels with him. He'd spread them by the stove and forgotten to check underneath for the one at the bottom before he left. He'd have something to look for when he got back. I asked if you could tell at a glance that such a narrow drawer was missing from the cupboard. He said you could. Open the drawer above it and you could look right down at the dust on the floor. He wasn't especially troubled by that. He lifted the empty thing a little and made room for me to get past the table.

As I was leaving, the woman by the door turned over a clipboard. She held it out and asked if I wanted to leave my name. They could message me before the next session if I wanted to help out. At the words “leave your name” I put a hand in my left pocket. There was just a receipt in there. I could reach right to the bottom without putting my hand through the lining. She brought the clipboard closer. The first person had circled their telephone number and drawn a little flower after it.

“I only came to see the door today,” I said.

She hadn't drawn back the clipboard. “Oh, you don't need to write anything, then. Did you see what they did at the top?”

I said I had. There'd been a big difference between the sound the first time and the sound the second time it closed. She said getting it to make no sound was the hard part. Anybody could give a screwdriver a couple of turns. She took the clipboard with her to ask for help bringing in the bricks. Pushing my chair towards the table, I saw the drawer still lying sideways on its paper bag, its owner waiting for someone who knew woodwork to become free. I didn't wait for him to take it home. I went down the two steps from the square, kicked a strip of wet leaf off the side of my heel, and walked back the usual way.

My hat was still on the rack at home. It caught my shoulder and the brim turned towards the wall. I straightened it a little and went to the table to look at the drawing. The man who'd said to keep that end still didn't know I'd got the outline of a grey hook on a piece of paper, the screw holes drawn in one by one. It wasn't what he'd needed either. I couldn't see a way of fastening those two things together. I put the paper flat where it had been and didn't take it over to the door for another look. When I put the water on for dinner, the kettle made one sound early, before it was really boiling. I let it finish, poured the water and turned off the heat. The old voice channel stayed closed all evening.
