# Chapter 510: When the Lights Come Back

It was nearly five on Sunday and the little sign that said COME IN was still hanging on the glass door of the community hall. The bottom half had been reglazed. You could see a ribbon of white sealant that hadn't set evenly along the edge. The door was heavy, just as I'd been told. I only managed to open a crack on my first push. Someone inside thought I might be knocking before I went away and asked through the gap who I'd come to see. Nobody, I said. I'd come to watch the film. Then he got hold of the door on his side and pulled it a little further in to let me through.

I didn't see the man with the drawer and didn't stop in the doorway to look for him. There were about twenty people. None of the chairs seemed to go together except the ones in the back row, which were all the same colour. I chose a spot I could hear the front from and found, sitting down, that two of the rubber feet made a terrible noise. I picked the chair up and moved it an inch so it wouldn't do it again when I got up. The wall clock was yellow and we had a little time before five. The man putting the film on said the picture might jump and the sound might not keep up. If we really couldn't hear it, we should put a hand up. There was no need to come over to the projector. A little girl put her hand up to ask what she should do if she recognised somebody. He asked if the person would mind being called out. Her grandmother wasn't here yet, the girl said. She'd wait and let her say for herself. Two people at the back laughed and the girl put her hand down.

The film opened on a patch of grass. Shoes had worn the mud along an iron fence into something like a path. The next shot was in the morning. Somebody was sweeping leaves and a barrow came behind and pulled a few of them out again. A woman in front and to my right knew the fence. She told the person beside her she'd still come round that side the previous year, but the new gate was too close to the tree and usually locked these days. She wasn't speaking loudly. I only heard “the gate.” A little later, in a scene where people were moving paving stones, the sound dropped. There was a word I couldn't make out. I leaned forward. I thought perhaps the woman had caught it. Her head was still turned towards her companion and the next thing she said cut across the voices on the film. I got impatient. My mouth was quicker than usual. “Could you wait a second?”

She didn't finish what she was saying. When I sat back I realised I hadn't told her whether I couldn't hear the film or simply didn't want to hear her. Her companion glanced at me. I looked down and saw my foot resting on the rubber foot of the chair in front and pulled it back. The film was still going. The stones had been carried away. A man in a dark raincoat was using a wooden rule on a patch of raised soil. Whoever had been talking was out of the shot. I'd said something like “wait a second” in a cinema before. I'd been a long way back and the people in front hadn't heard. I spent the rest of the film thinking I must never have worked up the nerve to say it out loud. This time I'd said it, but I still couldn't have told you who I wanted to wait.

A little later the picture went white and the sound cut out. It wasn't the end. The machine couldn't read the bit that came next. The man went round to have a look and asked us to give him a moment. He put two of the lights back on. One person went to the toilet and a few others rubbed their eyes. The woman in front turned round. “What did you mean when you said ‘wait’?” she said. She didn't sound intent on finding out what I'd done wrong. When she'd finished asking, she tilted her head as though she was worried she'd remembered the words wrong. While the film was off I'd been thinking I could pretend I hadn't said anything. I looked up instead and told her there'd been a word I couldn't make out. I'd thought her voice had covered it up.

“I didn't hear it either,” she said. “I was talking about the old gate.”

“I heard ‘gate.’ I spoke a bit sharply.”

She thought about that, said it was all right, then held up a hand to show me something on the wall. When she was little she'd pushed her skateboard through the old gate. You didn't have to go round to get downhill. Some of the film was out of order. The iron fence and the wheelbarrow in the later shot hadn't been filmed at the same time. The person next to her said if she recognised the gate she didn't have to tell only one person about it. She said she hadn't seen whether it came down later. Just then the man at the front asked whether we'd like him to go back a little. The woman looked round at me. I said if it wasn't too much trouble, I'd like to see the bit with the paving stones again. Then I said it again to the man so he could hear it. He asked if a little way back would do. She and I both said yes at the same time and didn't say anything over one another after that.

The two lights went out again. The woman’s companion moved a drink can from between their feet to the wall. I heard the word on the second go. The man carrying a paving stone said “bedding sand,” not the name of a place as I'd thought. The edge of the stone dropped at one corner and the camera dropped with it. Then somebody took the broom they'd used on the leaves and swept the sand into the gaps. The stone covered up the sounds again. I stopped leaning on my knees and let my hands turn over in my lap. At the end of the film people came onto the grass from all directions with balls of different kinds. Nobody went out of the way to look at the camera. Two of them stepped over a strip of newly planted grass. I thought we'd get a shot then that would tell us what had happened to the old gate. The man put the lights on. There wasn't another shot.

When it was time to leave, the woman didn't hurry to get up. She said she still couldn't tell what year the gate had come down. I asked if the path along the wall was still there. It was, she said. Don't go looking for a gate. Follow the bricks and they'll take you through. I told her I'd come out of the yard by the other side today and the bricks there looked much the same. She laughed a little. I already knew how to find it, then. We didn't make plans to go down the path together. She stayed sitting to get her arms into her sleeves. I pushed the heavy door and felt the person behind me get a hand on it before I let go. Outside there was still no sign of my neighbour. I did see somebody run back empty-handed, calling through the door to ask the people inside not to clear away his unfinished drink. He'd only been out to put a bit of paper in the bin. I took two steps to the side and let him past.

It wasn't raining when I got home, but the house smelled as though something wet had just been hung up in it. I took my hat off and put it on the old rack by the door. The paper with the drawing of the hook was still on the table. I turned the light on above it and went to the kitchen to cut half a pear. I wanted to talk about the film, only I'd already said a few things today that I hadn't known how to say until afterwards. I wanted to taste what I was holding first. The knife caught for half a second by the core. I turned the pear over and finished the cut from the other side. I didn't open the old home voice channel. Before I'd finished eating, I didn't go back for the paper either.
