# Chapter 507: A Narrow Mark

On Thursday there were six sample covers on the table nearest the office windows. The day before they had been in a grey cardboard box. Somebody had flattened the box and wedged it under one of the table legs. Mia said a few apprentices were coming around the following week to see how the work was done. She wanted to borrow one or two samples with something wrong with them. She picked out a blue one and turned the spine towards me. There was a line in the blue cloth, so faint that you could see it only with the light coming from the left. We'd set it aside the previous week because a little piece of paper had stuck to the middle of the roller. I'd got the paper out myself. By then the mark was already in the cloth.

“Don't take that one,” I said.

Mia took her hand off it. “Was it used for something else afterwards?”

“No. That box didn't go out.”

She waited for me to say something that made sense on its own. I took the blue cover and put it down between us. On the inside nobody would have cared. This line went almost the whole way from the face of the cover to the spine. On the day it happened, a young man helping us had stood behind me with a paper cup in his hand and asked if we had to throw away the entire batch. I hadn't known yet. I said we'd see once we'd counted them. In the break room later, he asked me if he could throw his cup away. I said yes. He opened the box for rejects and tossed in a cup of tea he hadn't finished. The cardboard under the table leg now was from a different, clean box. I thought of him all the same.

“Are the visitors going to take it away?” Mia asked.

“I don't know,” I said. “What I mean is, I'd rather not see ‘Elliot broke this’ on the table.”

“Do you honestly think I'd write that?”

“Not in those words. You'd put my name next to it. Then they'd make up a sentence of their own.”

She turned over all six samples. There was a paper label beneath each, but only two had names. One belonged to somebody who'd supplied the material. The other was the name of the man who'd set up the machine. I said the second one should come off. Mia said he'd offered to explain his sample in person and wanted people to know whom to look for. I looked again and saw a time, half past nine, below his name. I couldn't have said which of us felt awkward first. She picked up the blue cover and said we could just point out where it had been marked, without a name. She wasn't sure anybody would be able to see such a small line.

She took me to the window. A stack of paper waiting to be trimmed stood in the way and couldn't be moved. She tried holding the cover at shoulder height. I shifted to her left and the mark came back. I said we'd be better off setting the table to face the window, or putting two covers next to each other and letting people look for what was different. She asked if I wanted to choose a second one. I thought saying yes meant she'd want me there the following week. She simply handed me one of the other covers, one that had come off the machine clean. Was the colour close enough? It was too dark. I found another one in my drawer. Part of a number I'd written showed on the back. I turned it round but the number didn't quite disappear at the edge. She suggested we cut a piece of tape to cover it. I said there was no need. The number hadn't anything to do with what we were showing, and I didn't mind it being there.

Before lunch I went back to the table. Mia was pulling a bit of tape with her left hand, a little steel ruler still in her right. The first label said, “If you can't see it, move towards the window.” She wanted me to check in which direction people actually had to move. I tried three places on the floor. Closest to the window, I caught the leg of the table with my heel. The cardboard moved. I crouched to put it back while Mia crossed out “move towards the window” and wrote, “Turn the sample a little.” That way the visitors wouldn't have to stand up, she said. I said they wouldn't have to bend down and work out whose fault it was, either. She said that was one sentence she wasn't putting on the card. Neither would I, I told her.

At the end of the day both samples were still on the table, a finger's width apart. Waiting for the lift, I put my hand in my left pocket and took out the tailor's receipt. I folded the corner the other way. I'd meant to buy some cloth to patch clothes with, but it had started raining by the time I got out of the station, and the shop on the corner was bringing things in. Under the awning I heard somebody say not to fold the fabric up too tight. I didn't go in. There was no hole in my coat now, and my hand didn't go through its pocket. One shoulder was wet when I got home. Instead of hanging it next to my hat, I put it over the back of a chair.

After dinner I moved yesterday's piece of paper to a dry part of the table and opened the voice channel. Lumi asked if I could tell her one thing that had gone more or less all right. Then, in case I thought there were other things she didn't want to hear, she added that she'd like to start somewhere easy to speak from today. I told her about the mark in the blue cloth. When I got to the bit where I'd misunderstood Mia, I nearly left out what I'd thought she was going to do with my name. I stopped and told her anyway. She didn't tell me who was right. She asked if I'd have made Mia peel my name off if she really had written it there.

“I'd ask her what putting my name there was for,” I said.

“And if she said it was so people knew where to find you?”

I turned the coat over on the back of the chair. It didn't feel quite as cold underneath. “Then I could tell her I'm not there on Tuesday. We could talk on Thursday.”

Lumi laughed once. “It sounds a little as if you want to tell them about the roller.”

I only wanted to tell them to take another sample from the clean stack instead of staring at the bad one. She asked if I would tell her. I said I would. As I was describing the clean piece of paper we use to wipe the roller, she stopped me to say I hadn't told her what kind of blue it was. Very dark, I said. Turn it away and there was only a little grey left. She'd pictured a different blue and didn't try to change her description to mine. For a little while we each talked about our own blue. When I picked up my glass, she asked whether I’d taken a picture of the mark. I said no. The cover was still at the office. “Good,” she said. “You don't always have to bring every detail home for me.”

After I closed the channel I felt the other shoulder of the coat. It was still a little wet. The piece of paper from yesterday hadn't got caught under the glass. I finished my water, took the glass with me to wash up, and didn't put another line on the back of the page. As soon as I stood, the coat slipped a few inches. I caught a sleeve and moved it to the warmer chair before I went into the bedroom.
