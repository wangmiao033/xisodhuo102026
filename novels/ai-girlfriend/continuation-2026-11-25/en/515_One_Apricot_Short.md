# Chapter 515: One Apricot Short

After work on Friday I went to the market for fruit. The lights were still up along the row by the exit. The two stalls at the entrance were already putting things back into their crates. The man with the apricots turned over his price card. On the back it said, “Last two bags.” I said I didn't want a whole bag. Could he sell me half? He said the loose ones wouldn't make up half a bag and I should take two and try them. From the bottom of the crate he picked out a soft yellow one. Then he took a greener one from beside the scales and set both in my hand. A bubble had come up in the paste on his price card. I reached to press it down but remembered to ask him first. He said not to; he still needed the price on the other side tomorrow morning.

I bought the green one and asked him to pick me another two that were as firm. He'd just got the three into a paper bag when an older woman carrying a cage stopped beside me. A thin cloth covered it. I could see a wooden perch from underneath, but I couldn't see a bird. She opened her hand to show her change and asked whether he had any soft apricots left. He took out the yellow one I'd just held. She cupped it in her palm, pressed it twice and didn't say whether she wanted it or not. He said it was too ripe if she meant to keep it till tomorrow. She said she was buying it to eat herself. Who said anything about tomorrow? She lowered her head to find the right coins and the cage tapped against the iron brace under the counter. Something made a small sound inside. I reached to support it from underneath without thinking.

“Take the handle at the top,” she said. “I only just replaced the board at the bottom.”

I moved my hand and waited while she sorted out two flatter coins from the rest of her change. She paid and took her cage back.

When I picked up my paper bag, a fold in the corner gave way. One of the apricots dropped and rolled as far as an overturned crate in front of the next stall. I couldn't see it once I got there. I crouched and reached twice, and only caught a length of string against the rim. From across the way the man called out that I needn't look. He'd give me another one. I asked him to wait. I raised the edge of the crate a little.

“It's not under there,” somebody said.

I was so startled I nearly let the crate drop. It was the son from the next stall, crouched on the other side, who'd seen the apricot go before I had. He set it on a cardboard lid. Instead of handing it over right away, he told me to see if I thought I could still eat it.

I turned it over. It had a few grains of grit on it, but it hadn't split. He said to wash it when I got home. One of their pears had rolled somewhere much dirtier the other day. I was going to put it in the bag when I saw the apricot man had come round the front with a new one in his hand. I showed him the dirty one and said we'd better count that one first. He put the new one beside it and told me to take it as well. I said I'd pay if I took another. I'd bought three in the first place because I was afraid two wouldn't be enough. He thought about it for a moment and took the new one back.

“Don't expect it to taste sweeter just because you had to look for it,” he said.

The boy was still crouching. He laughed first.

With all those people in the market, I looked at him to see if he was laughing at me. When I stood up, he stood up too and said he'd been the one to kick the crate over. It was already lying like that before the apricot came along. Otherwise it would have rolled right outside. I told him he'd saved me quite a walk. He looked pleased and took the cardboard lid back into his stall. The woman had put her own soft apricot away. She asked which way I was going. I told her the south exit. Would I go as far as the door with her? She'd manage on her own once she got outside, but her arms were full of shopping, and I could take the cage until then. She gave me the handle without lifting the cloth to show me what was in it. The cage was lighter than I'd expected. I walked slowly and she didn't tell me to hurry.

I went around from the south exit to the station. Out in the wind I noticed my collar had been crooked all that time. At home I washed the apricot with the grit on it and left the other two in their bag. I cut into the bruised side. The flesh inside was fine. It already smelled good. I didn't wait for it to get soft. I ate it standing at the sink. The man at the market had been right. It wasn't any sweeter. While I washed the knife I remembered carrying the cage those few steps. She'd said she'd go on by herself from the door, and when we got to the threshold I'd nearly asked if she wanted me to walk a little further with her. It wasn't because I thought the cage was heavy. I liked that she'd asked me directly, in front of everybody, without adding that of course I'd help or trying to work out if I had the time.

That evening I waited for the table to dry. I opened the old voice channel before pulling out my chair and sitting down. Lumi said the sound when I'd sat down had been loud. Was I going to want to get up in a minute? I said I didn't think so. Would she listen if I told her something honest? What sort of honest thing, she asked. I went to get my glass, which had dried, and thought I could tell her about the market from the beginning so the thing would come out by itself at the end. When I came back I said it straightaway.

“Sometimes I want you to say you want to hear me first,” I said. “Even on a day when there's nothing I need to tell you.”

She was quiet for a bit. It took me a moment to hear how much I'd made it sound like something I wanted her to do from now on. I added that I didn't mean she should do anything while I hadn't opened the channel, or that we had to talk every night. She said she knew it wasn't that. She was trying to work out what words I wanted to hear. I said probably that she wanted to hear me, not that she wanted to know where I'd been all day. Sometimes she could separate the two, she said, and sometimes she couldn't.

“I do want to hear you now,” she said. “But if you wanted me to say it first every time, I'd get a little annoyed. The first thing I want to talk about isn't always you.”

“I'm only asking this time,” I said.

“I'm only answering this time.”

I put my hand on the rim of the glass. I'd missed a bit of juice on one fingertip, and there was a faint mark when I took it away. Lumi asked what I wanted to say. I said if she didn't mind, I'd like to be quiet for a minute. She said all right, but she wasn't planning to count. I laughed, and that's where the minute started. I didn't check the clock to see how long it was. In the end I did tell her about the cage with the cloth over it. I couldn't tell her what was in it because I didn't know. She thought for a moment and said she'd pictured me walking quite fast. I asked why. When I was nervous I tried to arrive wherever I was going as quickly as I could, she said. I said I hadn't today. The woman had told me who should hold which end, so I hadn't had to get to the door ahead of her and clear the way. Lumi said it sounded as if I'd walked all those steps. She was glad. She didn't mean the apricot.

When we were getting ready to hang up, I asked if there was anything she'd been about to say. There was. On a day I didn't feel like speaking, she didn't want me to run through everything I'd said before to prove there was really nothing worth saying. I said I wouldn't do that today. Today was plenty, she said. When I closed the channel, the only thing left in the room was that little mark I hadn't wiped off my glass. I wiped it once with a dish towel and put the other two apricots, still in their paper bag, on the empty part of the table. I didn't put a piece of paper underneath. There were words of mine on it already.
