# Chapter 514: The Light Comes Across

On Thursday morning, I heard somebody in the break room asking where we kept the scissors. He spoke carefully, as though he might be asking the wrong question. I put my head around the door. He'd already found them. He was holding two stiff boards with sheets clipped to them and couldn't decide which of the boards to put down first. I said there was no need to cut anything yet. He could lean the boards against the wall, and Mia had left a sheet for the visitors on the counter. He looked down at the badge on his chest and asked how I'd known he'd come to see the work.

“Our scissors are hard to find,” I said. “After the third visit you start carrying your own.”

His name was Tom. By the time I got there with my tea, he was at the table by the window, looking at the two blue covers with two other apprentices. Mia was talking to their group leader about the line on the floor by the machinery at the other end. My name wasn't on the list of people doing demonstrations. I'd only meant to pass the corner of the table. The stack of paper by the window was still there, not noticeably lower than it had been the week before. There were only four of the six samples out now, but the two blue ones still had a finger's width between them. The little card said, “Turn the sample a little.” My name wasn't under it. Somebody had wiped the table recently, and there was a short damp mark next to the edge of the card.

One of the apprentices, a girl with her hair tied back, pushed both samples towards the window. Tom said she was supposed to turn them, not send them over there. She said in that case he could turn one for her. He tried lifting the one in his left hand. A short bit of the pale mark showed up behind his thumb. She leaned over and his hand got in the way of the light again. They were quiet for a moment. Neither said whether they'd seen it. I went around to my side of the table and put my cup well out of reach. Tom recognised me and held the blue cover up.

“Did the fold there make this line come out?”

I looked where he was pointing. The fold was something the sample was meant to have, down by the spine. When the mark reached it, it seemed to join a line that was already there. I was about to say no and take the thing out of his hands. It would have been quicker. I'd had enough tries the week before that I wouldn't have had to stand there in front of everybody working out where to put my fingers. I asked him to bring it a little closer in without letting go. He drew his forearm back. The light came across under his wrist. I could see the mark now, but the people at the other end still couldn't.

“Tilt it until the fold is out of the light,” I said. “Can you still find the pale mark?”

He turned the cover a little further. Mia had come back by then. She didn't crowd in beside him. She gave the other blue one, the one without the mark, to the girl with her hair tied back. She asked her to hold it at about the same height and at the same angle to the window. After a minute the girl said hers had the same fold, but there was nothing extra where the light caught it. Tom looked down at his thumb. The pale line had gone out right beneath it. He tried lowering his wrist and lifting his shoulder. When he found it this time, he called the girl by name before he said anything else. I took a step out of the way. He didn't try to hold his sample up against hers. He just asked, “Can you see it now?”

I told him the extra mark was from a little piece of paper that had stuck to the roller. We'd stopped the machine and taken off the paper, but the mark had stayed. We had to put fresh material through to see whether we'd got the roller clean. He asked whether that made it a sample of a broken machine or not. I said I could tell him what had happened to that cover. Other things could go wrong with the machine, and they didn't all come out looking like that. He nodded without reaching for anything to write on. When he put the sample back on the table, he held his open hand above it for a moment, as though setting it down too quickly might change what he'd just seen.

The group leader came over and asked if one of us could show the people behind him what we'd just tried. Tom looked at me. I didn't offer him up straightaway, but I didn't turn and look at the wall either.

“I can try it again,” he said. “Don't stand behind me. You can't see from there.”

Mia asked whether he needed somebody else to hold the good one. The girl said she still had it and began lowering both hands. Mia pulled out a clean sheet to make a place where she could set the cover down. The group leader changed his clipboard to his other hand. Two people at the back came forward and stooped a little. There was a space by the window now. Tom turned the sample towards it.

I didn't stay and hear him do the whole thing from the beginning. When I got back to my desk, the drawer wasn't shut properly. I nudged it with my knee, and the wooden ruler made a sound inside. It was only when I took a second swallow of tea that I noticed how tight the joint in my thumb felt. I'd reached out once at the table. I hadn't taken anything, but my hand seemed to remember trying. I loosened it around the cup. Behind me, someone at the table laughed and said they'd finally seen it. I couldn't tell who. Later, Tom came to put the scissors back. He told me he'd thought they were going to take the marked cover away. He opened the drawer in the break room where the shared things belonged and laid the scissors in with the points facing inwards.

I met Mia at the lift. She asked whether we should leave the card out. I said yes. We didn't have to add what I'd said earlier. She asked which thing I meant. Then I remembered she hadn't been next to me when I'd mentioned the light on the fold. I told her exactly what I'd said. She said if the next group asked, she'd have them look at the fold first as well. The doors opened. There was somebody inside with a box of used cups, and Mia put out her hand to keep the doors open while they went by. There was a piece of unused tape in her other hand, the same kind as last week. I didn't ask what she was saving it for.

At home I cut a piece of cheese and straightened my coat on the rack by the door. The hat was still hanging at the end, brim down without covering the place for the coat. I sat before I opened the old voice channel and told Lumi about the table. She asked why I hadn't waited while he finished.

“Did you want to know how he did at the end?”

“A little,” I said. “I wanted my tea more.”

She laughed once. “I thought you were going to say so he could tell it himself.”

“He already was. He'd have been telling it whether I sat there or not.”

She asked if the mark really was as fine as that. I said it was. When he held it above his shoulder, there hadn't been one time I could see it all. She said that sounded a little different from what she'd heard last week. Last week I was the one holding it. I'd been able to see every bit. This time it was in somebody else's hands. Half of each thing I said had to wait for him to move first. She asked if I wanted to go back to the office and look at it again. I said no, I'd had enough of it for today. Was there anything else she'd like to talk about?

She asked what had knocked a moment ago. The plate, I said. I’d cut myself some cheese. She wanted to hear what it was like. I said it was dry, with crumbs along the edge, not quite the colour it was in the middle.

“I didn't have to work very hard to picture that,” she said. “I've got it already.”

I asked what colour she'd got. She wouldn't tell me. I ate a piece and pushed the dish a little further towards the middle of the table. There was nothing else I needed to show her, though there was still a goodnight to say. We each said it once. I closed the channel and stood up with my dish. The hand I'd been holding the cup with was loose by then.
