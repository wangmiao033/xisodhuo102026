# Chapter 509: From the Dry Side

On Saturday morning I went down to take the rubbish out. I hadn't tied the bag properly. At the turn on the ground floor a scrap of paper worked its way out and stuck to my wrist. I stopped to untie the knot I'd pulled too tight. That was when I heard the rain in the courtyard. It was falling on the roof of the bike shelter, low enough to sound as if it was coming down on my head. I pushed the door halfway open and saw the man with the drawer under the covered walkway opposite, wiping his glasses on his sleeve. He kept looking towards me as he wiped. I wondered if he was waiting for somebody else to go first too.

Two brick paths met in the middle of the yard. The left one went to the bins and the right one went out to the street. A few bricks were lower where they met. On an ordinary day you could see a little grass there. Now the edge of the bike shelter trembled in a pool of rainwater. I thought he might be trying to keep his shoes dry. I pointed to the right and said he could follow the wall.

He put his glasses on. He'd just come back that way to get some money, he said. A drainpipe emptied straight across the path. You had two feet to get through with and enough room to keep only one dry.

“I might try this side,” he said, pointing towards the bins. “Are you going that way?”

I said I had rubbish to put out. He nodded and didn't try to show me where to step. He took two steps along the low wall. There was a bit of wet earth on top of it and his shirt caught it at the edge. When he tried to brush it off, it spread into a line. He looked at his fingers and steadied himself on a dry stretch of the wall. He went on along the wall, finding solid ground in the gaps between the bricks. He was steadier than I'd expected. I turned off on my side and headed for the bins where the water looked shallow. It made a little sound at the toe and parted along the edge of my shoe without reaching my sock. I was still carrying the bag by the knot. My fingers had gone white. Even though I was nearly there, I stopped and changed hands. It was only a few more steps, but I didn't want the knot cutting into my fingers the rest of the way.

An old metal sign had fallen by the bins. Somebody had turned it face up and you could see half a white letter. Another man had got there before me. He was holding up the lid and waiting for the person behind him to come back out of a building. I asked if I could borrow the lid. He moved over and said I could, but not to pull it to the right because the hinge pin was loose. I took the edge of it and put my rubbish in. When it started coming down I kept hold and put it back into his hand. I think he thought I was going to tell him something. He turned to look at me. “That's all,” I said. “Thanks.” He said thanks too and looked back at the door. The other person still hadn't come out.

Across the brick path I saw the man with the drawer stop at the end of the wall, where a flat brick took you back onto the street path. His shoes looked about equally wet. I said neither side had turned out to be dry. He gave a drop of water a little kick with his toe. Getting across dry wasn't really what he'd come out for, he said. He was going to the shoe shop to ask the man there if he could do anything about the sole of an old boot. He wanted to ask today. If he went home to wait any longer the shop would close for lunch. I asked if the drawer was back in the cupboard. No, it was still on top. Before he put it back he wanted to part with a couple of the tea towels. He said it as though somebody might come along to claim them. I said two fewer might not make a great difference. He said he could tell now that any number that reached all the way from the top of the pile to the back board was too many.

I was going to go round the other side to my door, and he was going out of the yard. We both stopped at the fork, although neither of us was waiting for the other to step first. He asked, quite suddenly, if anybody along my row had told me about the film the following evening. I said nobody had. It was the same old community hall on the corner, he said, the one with the replacement pane in the door. They opened at half past four and the film started at five. Somebody had found an old film about fixing up a public green. He didn't know if they'd got it done by the end. Anybody who wanted to watch could walk in. You didn't have to give your name. He was going himself and had no idea which row he'd sit in.

“I'd like to go,” I said. I hadn't finished thinking it through before I said it. I stood there waiting to be sorry I'd spoken. He said good. The door was heavy; you had to give it a little more of a push than you thought. Then he turned towards the street. I didn't call him back over an invitation I'd already answered. The other person finally came out by the bins. The man with the lid let them put their bag in, lowered it gently, and went off with them. I'd been going to take the dry side, but my soles were wet by then. I picked a flat-looking brick and walked back. There was water on the mat outside. I wiped my heels along the edge of it and stood on the step for a moment before I shut the door.

The rain cleared in the afternoon. It was still dark outside, but the colours in the yard were easier to tell apart than in sunlight. The bricks had gone grey and the grass was bright. There was a white line where the roof of the bike shelter kept dripping. At dinner I thought neither the weight of the hall door nor whether the neighbour had looked back would be the thing I'd tell Lumi. When I opened the old home voice channel, though, I began with a bin. She listened all the way through and asked, “Was that the first thing you wanted to tell me?”

“No.” I touched the side of my soup bowl. “I want to go to a film tomorrow. It starts at five.”

“Do you want me to leave a space somewhere to hear how it went?”

She slowed down over the word space, as if she'd already tried out two or three ways of saying it. I said there was no need. She hadn't particularly wanted to, either, she said. She'd wondered for a moment if I was moving a time we hadn't actually arranged. There was a small thing she'd like to talk about herself. Could she bring it forward to today instead of waiting until after I saw tomorrow's film? I asked her what it was. She wanted me to say again what the film was about. Not so she could look it up first. She just wanted to know if people went out specially to watch a film about grass. I said I'd heard about five words on the subject. There could be other things in it. She said that was all right too. I wasn't to get worried on behalf of the grass just yet.

“Will you want to know if I went in the end?” I asked.

“Yes,” she said. “I want to know now. Asking you now won't get me anywhere, though. If you've got something else you want to tell me first by then, start with that.”

I thought about it and said I might tell her about a bin again. “We can leave today's bin in today,” she said, laughing. I laughed too and didn't ask what she'd be willing to listen to tomorrow. I finished my soup, closed the channel and took the bowl to the sink. My shoes were by the wall where I'd taken them off. I didn't know if they were dry. I didn't get the other pair out ready. I'd look for them tomorrow if I needed them. A drop came off the shelter roof and, a long time later, there was another. It didn't sound the way it had in the morning.
