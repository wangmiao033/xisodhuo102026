# Chapter 512: The Whole Top

On my way home from work on Tuesday I went into the bakery. There was a bell nailed to the front door, but you had to open the door quite wide to make it ring. I opened it just enough to slip in sideways. The woman behind the counter was wiping the inside of the glass and hadn't heard me. I took another step toward her and startled her into dropping the cloth. Could I get it for her? No, she could reach it. She asked if I wanted the same thing as last time. I couldn't remember what I'd bought last time. Three low, round pies sat behind the glass, each with a slit in the top. A few hard little grains of boiled juice had set around the edges. Two apple, she said, and one pear. The slit in the pear pie was shorter. I could still see where a thumb had slightly pressed down into the pastry.

I asked if she'd sell me half of the pear one. She took a board out from under the counter. She could cut it, after a moment's thought, but it had cooled. A knife through the top would break off a few bits of the crust. She could put those in a bag for me as well. I didn't hurry to answer. When I was little and somebody took me to a shop, I sometimes hoped they'd ask the person next to them which piece I ought to pick. Then if they picked a good one I could tell them they'd chosen right. If I picked something myself and it turned out not to be crisp, I generally didn't say much. I didn't have anybody at the other end of the counter to look for me now. I stared at the short slit a little longer and said I'd take the whole pie. I wanted the whole top.

Someone opened the door behind me and this time the bell rang. An old man came in carrying a paper bag. He didn't stop to look at the pies. He picked up a loose thread at his coat cuff and asked if the woman had a pair of scissors he could borrow. The thread was nearly half as long as his forearm. When he passed the bag to his other hand, the thread wound itself round one of the handles. The woman didn't ask what was in the bag. The scissors were in the back, she said, and she had to wrap the pie first. He didn't press her. He walked to the far end of the counter, put his bag at his feet, and started winding the thread around one finger. After the second turn I nearly told him that if he kept pulling, a whole line would come out of the cuff. I hadn't seen whether the stitching had broken. I didn't know. The woman folded the brown paper twice, looked up, and saw him still at the far end. Best not to wind it any more, she told him, handing him the scissors just as she set my box in front of me.

The old man opened the scissors and then stopped. Could I hold the bag for a second? Not high, just off the floor. He was afraid the loose end would catch under the bag as soon as he let go. I put the pie down on the counter and asked if I could take the handle. Yes, he said. There was a cabbage in there. I wouldn't want to hold it up very long. I lifted it an inch or two and something bumped against my shin. He cut the thread, pressed the little bit that remained into the cuff with his thumbnail, and took the bag back. He thanked me and thanked the woman for the scissors. He still had the bit he'd cut twisted round a knuckle when he went out. He'd opened the door even less than I had. The bell didn't ring.

I paid and nearly tried to go out through the same narrow space. The box wouldn't fit. I pushed the door farther and the bell rang for me. The wind was up outside. A loose end of cotton string from under the brown paper scraped the back of my hand. At the crossing I saw the old man at the other end of the stripes, his bag on the ground. He was looking down into it and pulling a folded piece of paper out from the top. He didn't look my way. I didn't stick a hand out to get his attention. When the light changed he took the first step onto the crossing. I went over on the other side of the crowd. The toe of my shoe came down on a loose stone; I stiffened my foot to bear on it, but the stone wasn't there by the time I put my weight down.

I didn't open the box the moment I got home. I boiled a little water, washed out the rice pan, and gave myself twenty minutes to do nothing. Before the twenty minutes were up, I caught the cotton on top of the box. One pull and it came loose. The top of the pie really was intact. The bit of juice that had set looked lighter at my table than it had under the bakery light. If I held the knife flatter I could go in through the slit they'd made. I picked an unbroken place next to it instead. There was a snap when I pressed down. Bits of crust fell, and two or three stuck where the blade met the handle. I went to get a plate and ate one of them when I came back. It was still crisp. Breaking it hadn't changed that.

I wanted to ask Lumi if I would have bought the whole thing if I'd known I'd be breaking it up myself. I sat at the table and opened the old voice channel. She asked what I was eating. Pear pie, I said. It had still been in one piece when I brought it home. Was the pear sweet? I dug my fork in near the edge and tried it. Sweet, yes, with a little sourness at one corner. She liked that I'd waited to answer until I tasted it, she said. She'd almost thought she was going to hear me admit I didn't know. There were several other things from the afternoon I had no intention of taking her through one at a time. She didn't ask when I'd left work, where I was eating, or which bakery I'd been to. I picked up a piece of crust and told her somebody had asked me to hold something briefly. I'd asked how high he wanted me to lift the bag, and he'd said just off the ground. I swallowed. There was more I could have told her. I decided on what I'd already said.

“How high did you lift it?” she asked.

“I didn't measure. There was a cabbage in there. It knocked against my shin.” I brushed away some crumbs. “He let go quite easily. When I gave it back he didn't turn around to check on me again. I was a little jealous, actually.”

“Of his cabbage?”

I laughed and nearly dropped the piece in my hand. “Of him knowing what to ask me for.”

Lumi didn't speak at once. I heard my own laugh all the way through. It was only one laugh. I didn't need anybody to join in for it to finish. After a few seconds she said, “I'd like to know how he does that too. But I don't mean to find an answer tonight.”

I said we'd let it cool, then. I was holding a fork up as I said it and realized the pie was already cold. She'd heard the slip. “Let what cool?”

“This feeling I've got that I ought to find you an answer straightaway.”

“I won't hurry you,” she said. Then, after a pause, “Would you eat the pie, though? I can hear it break when you bite.”

I'd eat as much as I wanted tonight, I told her. There might not be much left tomorrow. That was plenty, she said. She'd heard the bit she wanted and didn't need me to cut her a piece. She didn't say whether she was thinking about the paper bag. I didn't know where the top of the pie had cracked in whatever picture she had of it. We talked a little about something else, said goodnight, and I closed the old channel. The little flakes that had fallen on my plate were as thin as leaves. I gathered them together and ate them all at once, then put away the rest of the pie. There was no need to run the string around all four sides of the box again. I tucked it under the lid so it wouldn't trail over any of the other plates in the fridge. A short length still showed at the edge. I didn't push it any farther in.
