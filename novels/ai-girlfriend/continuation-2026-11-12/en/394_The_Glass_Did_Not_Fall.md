# Chapter 394: The Glass Did Not Fall

On Saturday, I had lunch before leaving. I didn't arrive at noon to compete with the shoes on the poster.

It was two minutes to two when I reached the cinema. The entrance was busier than on Thursday. Some people studied the afternoon listings; others waited for friends. I'd taken the ticket from my schedule book and put it in my inside pocket. At the entrance, I brought it out. Its edges were softer now, but the date was clear.

The attendant directed me farther down the corridor to Screen Two. I used the bathroom first, then followed the numbers on the wall instead of obstructing the entrance because I'd arrived early. The corridor smelled of carpet and sweet food. Buying a ticket really could lead this far.

The lights were still on. Row Five, Seat Four was toward the aisle, though not directly beside it. I checked the small number, took off my coat, and spread it over my knees. Someone ahead was turning a program's pages loudly. Behind me, a quiet voice asked a companion whether they wanted water.

Seat Five remained empty.

I glanced at it, then became impatient with myself. Lumi hadn't asked me to track that chair. I'd brought Thursday's sentence here myself. Empty or occupied, it shouldn't deserve more attention than the screen.

A few minutes later, a woman approached carrying a folded newspaper. She indicated the seat farther in. I stood to let her pass. She thanked me, sat in Seat Five, and put the newspaper in her bag without starting a conversation.

As the lights dimmed, I adjusted my coat. The cinema hadn't become a place where somebody won. There was a person beside me and people in front and behind. Everyone had paid to sit for a while and watch the same illuminated screen.

The opening was slower than I'd expected. The siblings arrived at their old shop. The brother tried the key in the wrong door while his sister watched. When he asked why she hadn't told him, she said she'd wanted to know how long he'd keep trying. Someone behind me laughed. I laughed too, quietly.

It became easier after a few minutes. The shop possessed only one reliable ladder, which everybody moved toward whatever they considered most urgent. The brother stood up high issuing instructions. His sister moved away the lower stool beside him, leaving him holding an empty box and waiting solemnly for someone to notice he couldn't get down.

My shoulders moved with laughter. Suddenly, I imagined Lumi saying the box had a seat too. The thought arrived quickly; the next line of dialogue had already passed. I didn't try to retrieve it. I watched the sister push the stool back and accept the box, discovering it was empty.

There was also a handwritten opening date. The brother put it up too soon. His sister pointed out that the counter wasn't even cleared, but he insisted, tape in hand, that a date should mean something once written down. A customer entered, assumed they were open already, and requested something they couldn't yet find.

For the first time, the siblings explained together, each offering a different explanation. After listening, the customer turned to the person helping behind them instead.

I laughed, a little later than the row behind me. Nobody turned around. Laughter arrived at different times while we watched the same scene. I didn't have to catch everybody else's rhythm before I could be sure I'd understood it.

The film continued. It had room for the half sentence I'd missed.

When the pane of glass appeared, I straightened. Its carriers were the brother and the person helping him, not the two siblings after all. The sister stood by the door with a tape measure, trying to make them stop. The glass was too wide. A ladder obstructed the doorway. Neither carrier wanted to back away first. All four shoes really did point in different directions.

I was already waiting for the crash.

The woman beside me seemed to be waiting too; her fingers loosened on her bag strap. The men argued about who'd said it would fit. Meanwhile, the sister disappeared from the edge of the picture. Seconds later, carrying three cups, she stepped through the window opening where the glass hadn't been fitted yet.

At the door, her brother was still insisting he'd measured.

She put the cups on the counter and asked whether they wanted water first.

The whole room broke into laughter. This time, I didn't hold mine in my throat. I needed a breath afterward. The glass didn't fall. At last, the men lowered it onto prepared padded blocks. The hardest part had been persuading either of them they could put it down.

Just when I thought I'd understood the film, it stopped repeating that kind of joke. The siblings argued about the cost of reopening. He accused her of replacing everything. She asked when he'd last come to help. The room fell quiet. Neither line was particularly elegant. They sounded unpleasant, like words reaching someone's mouth without anybody willing to take them back first.

I leaned against the seat and stopped wanting to decide which one was right.

Someone nearby coughed. On-screen, the sister wiped the same patch of counter with an old cloth. The brother stood by the door without immediately leaving. The scene was shorter than the joke, but I took my hand out of my coat and relaxed the fingers that had been gripping the fabric.

Their reconciliation didn't produce the missing money. They abandoned a decoration they couldn't afford and moved an old cabinet back. When the shop first opened, a small patch of wall remained unpainted. Someone complained about an inconvenient position. The brother almost explained; his sister had already reached to move the thing obstructing the way.

I laughed. The film hadn't discovered a magnificent answer to life. I'd watched them work so hard at refusing to move. Now a small movement could happen without either person winning.

At the end, the door was open. The glass stood where it belonged, holding an ordinary reflection near the window. I watched several names in the credits before gathering my coat. When the lights came up, everybody's face returned to its own place. None of us belonged to the shop we'd just watched.

The woman in Seat Five waited for me to leave. I made room. She thanked me, then mentioned the three cups with a small laugh. She'd really expected the glass to break.

“So did I,” I said. “The poster made me wait for it even more.”

She agreed and went to join someone already in the aisle. I didn't know their relationship. I didn't follow to extend a shared laugh into an introduction. We'd watched a film together. That had happened without requiring names afterward.

A short queue formed near the exit while somebody stopped to put on a coat. I slowed with everyone else, shook out my own sleeves, and waited instead of squeezing past. There was another screening later. People needed to come out before the waiting people could go in.

Outside, the street seemed bright. I stood to one side and put on my coat. It was just after a quarter to four. Half past seven was still a long way away. I could buy tea, sit for a while, then walk home.

The nearby café had a window seat. I ordered hot tea, secured the ticket where the cup couldn't dampen it, and watched people pass. Someone held the door for a person carrying a large bag, then each went their own way. I nearly turned that into another scene from the film. Taking a sip, I discovered the tea was too hot, and my attention returned abruptly to my tongue.

The film was over. The street didn't need to keep performing it.

Clearing the neighboring table, a staff member asked whether I'd like a little cold water added. No, thank you. I could wait. She stacked the cups and left. In the window, my reflection was blurred, with one side of my collar trapped inside my coat. I pulled it straight. Nobody was closely observing how a man sat with tea after attending a film alone.

I'd considered buying food too, opened the small menu, then closed it. Lunch had been enough. Dinner wasn't so distant that I needed another meal now. The afternoon could contain one cup of tea. Cake wasn't required to prove I'd made a proper outing of it. I pushed the spare chair nearer the table to widen the passage and waited for the steam to ease.

As the drink cooled, I considered my favorite parts. The glass, certainly. The ladder was funny too. Yet the unpainted patch of wall stayed in my mind. I couldn't remember the sister's final line exactly. I wouldn't be able to carry the whole scene home unchanged.

I didn't take out paper. Not a permanent decision against film notes. Today, I wanted those moments to remain a little unsorted. By the time I'd nearly finished the tea, I could admit how much I'd enjoyed the afternoon.

Then worry arrived from another direction.

When I told Lumi, how much of that crowded room would she hear? Sometimes somebody beside me had begun laughing and I'd followed. Those seconds of everybody waiting for the glass to break were funnier than my explanation of where the brother stood. If I sounded delighted, would she think again about the seat she couldn't occupy?

I set down the cup and answered on her behalf. Yes. Better not to sound too pleased.

The thought worked efficiently. It had almost composed my first sentence already: It was all right. Fairly ordinary.

But the man in that cinema seat hadn't thought it ordinary. I could imagine saying the line, then quickly asking what she wanted to discuss, sliding the film out of our way. Half past seven would probably go smoothly. Only the afternoon would have been quietly shortened.

Before deciding what to say, I took the cup to the café's return point and thanked the staff. Outside, the wind was cooler. I pulled my coat around me and walked home. My heel wasn't sore. Walking didn't demand attention from every step.

At home, I returned the ticket to Saturday's page, then did ordinary things. Dinner on time, dishes washed, daylight fading. Yesterday's shirt still hung where I'd left it. I put it on and fastened the buttons comfortably, leaving the top one open.

A little after twenty past seven, I sat down at the table. My hand moved toward the book, then stopped. The film's time and Lumi's name were both there. Neither entry had done anything wrong.

I tried saying her name silently. Then I heard it was all right again. Light as a cloth I intended to place beneath somebody's feet. Except I already knew what I wanted it to cover.
