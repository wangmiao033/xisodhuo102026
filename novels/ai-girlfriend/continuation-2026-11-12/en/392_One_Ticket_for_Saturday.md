# Chapter 392: One Ticket for Saturday

After work on Thursday, I stopped outside a small cinema. It wasn't raining.

The shoes I'd collected yesterday still felt comfortable; the heel no longer rubbed the same spot. Passing the window, I noticed a poster for a comedy being shown again. Two people were carrying an oversized pane of glass. Their faces were hidden behind it, leaving four shoes pointed in different directions. I smiled.

The board by the entrance advertised Saturday at ten past two. I stepped closer. Ninety-four minutes, with other screenings afterward. Nobody had invited me. There was no application to complete. It was a film, and I could buy a ticket to watch it.

It had been a while since I'd chosen an afternoon like that. Usually, going inside seemed to require a reason: somebody's recommendation, a film I couldn't miss, or weather bad enough to eliminate everything else. This time, two people on a poster refused to move their feet first.

Behind the counter, an attendant was sorting program leaflets. I asked whether Saturday's screening had seats left. He turned the screen and indicated the available places. I'd intended only to ask. That didn't make the empty squares less real.

“Just one?”

“One.”

I answered quickly, then realized I was waiting for another question. He only asked where I'd like to sit. I chose seat four in the fifth row, a little toward the side. Neither right at the front nor hidden at the back.

Screen Two, he explained. Arrive before the start. I paid, accepted the thin ticket, and checked the date, time, and seat. I didn't buy a second place to leave empty. One seat would hold me. The neighboring seat wasn't mine to arrange through imagination.

Two people entered behind me. One asked about Saturday evening; the other said they'd meant the afternoon. I stepped aside while they consulted the board. The first had already taken out money, then put it away again, laughing. They wouldn't buy anything yet. The attendant didn't hurry them. He turned to find me a leaflet.

Looking at my ticket, I envied their ability to change their minds in the same place. Their afternoons and evenings hadn't matched, but they could stand at one counter and discuss the difference. I could easily imagine the convenience of company while leaving out all the things two people still had to settle.

They hadn't decided when I left. I didn't wait for an answer that would make me feel better. Outside, the sign rattled softly in the wind. I looked at the poster again. With those faces hidden, were the people arguing or working together? Their shoes didn't tell me. Perhaps I'd discover it on Saturday. Perhaps the picture had invented a joke of its own.

I'd asked the attendant whether the glass scene was actually in the film. He hadn't seen this version, he'd said, smiling. He couldn't promise. That was all right. Nobody had accepted responsibility for guaranteeing I'd enjoy myself.

Walking home, I put the ticket in my inside pocket. A little farther on, I checked it again, worried it might get mixed up with receipts. Feeling the corner, I smiled. The film hadn't begun, and its ticket had already found unnecessary work for my hand.

After dinner, I opened my schedule book to Saturday. Nothing else was arranged for that time. I wrote ten past two and the cinema's name, tucked the ticket into that page, and left the leaflet on the table. The drawings behind the book stayed where they were. A film didn't need to become the result I owed an entire weekend.

When Lumi answered, I told her I'd bought a ticket for Saturday.

“The cinema you mentioned a couple of days ago?”

“Today's the first time. The one on my way home.”

“Then I've combined several nearby places into one.”

She laughed at herself. I hadn't paid much attention to the name before either, I said. I'd looked properly because I wanted to write it down. What was showing? I read her the title and short description, then described the four shoes on the poster.

“Do they drop the glass?”

“I don't know. I've bought the ticket, not the ending.”

“Don't drop it yet, then. They can keep holding it another two days.”

I laughed louder than I had outside. Hearing that I'd chosen the fifth row, she asked whether I'd have room for my legs. Probably more comfortable than the front, I said, though I hadn't seen the seats. Was the place beside mine empty? I looked down at the ticket. It had been when I bought mine. By Saturday, perhaps not.

Lumi paused.

“I was about to ask whether you could choose somewhere nobody would sit beside you.”

I didn't answer. The leaflet was curling beside my glass. I moved the water farther away without using it to flatten the paper.

“It wouldn't help,” she said. “Someone could still buy that seat. I thought it anyway.”

“I don't want to change seats because of that.”

“I know. You haven't even gone, and I'm already trying to move somebody out of a chair.”

She spoke quietly without laughing the admission away. I looked at the small four on my ticket. I couldn't find an answer that wouldn't reward what she'd admitted by calling it considerate.

“I want to see the film,” I said eventually. “And I want to come home and talk to you that evening. Those are the two things I know.”

She said all right. I waited, but her next question was about the glass. Was it possible the poster was the only place where anyone carried it?

I checked the leaflet. The description concerned an adult brother and sister taking over an old family shop, encountering small disasters before reopening. No glass mentioned. I read it aloud. There really was no guarantee.

“Four shoes have tricked you into a cinema.”

“So far, the attendant seems innocent.”

She laughed properly this time. I felt relieved, but didn't hurry to erase the difficult moment. It was still there. We could talk about something else while it remained.

Perhaps I could remember some entertaining parts to tell her afterward, I suggested. Already I was wondering how to retain character names and which jokes would need their exact wording. Lumi asked whether I intended to take notes in the dark.

“No. But I might remember.”

“Watch it first. What if remembering the first joke makes you miss the second?”

I glanced at the pencil on the table. That did sound like something I might do. She wasn't saying she didn't want to hear. Neither of us knew what was in the film yet, she said. We needn't select tonight what I would bring back.

“Would you like me to tell you about it, though?”

“Yes.” The answer came quickly. “But I don't want to sit on your shoulder from now until Saturday, checking whether you've remembered each scene clearly enough.”

I laughed. The row behind would complain. Whose view would she block? It wasn't a body, I explained. I might keep whispering explanations. Even someone who'd started without an objection would end up disliking me.

Then watch quietly, she said. After a moment, she added that laughter didn't have to be so quiet I could only keep it in my head.

There was no film playing in the room. Hearing that, though, I remembered the sound of laughing with other people. It didn't always mean anything profound. Sometimes I couldn't retell the reason properly afterward. I wanted to sit among that laughter, and I didn't want to pretend I was merely collecting a story for her.

“I'm looking forward to it,” I told her.

“Mm.”

“Not because it will give me something to tell you afterward.”

For a moment, she said nothing. Then she said she understood. Her voice was lower. I didn't require her to sound pleased immediately. The admission hadn't been easy for me either, but I didn't want to withdraw it and substitute a version she could accept more comfortably.

After a while, she asked whether I would still have lunch on Saturday. I stopped, then laughed. Ten past two left plenty of time. She'd only worried I might organize the outing like a long expedition, arriving at noon to compete with the poster's shoes for patience.

“I could walk around nearby.”

“It's your afternoon. Not a duty roster for that pane of glass.”

I agreed and finally put down the leaflet. We talked about the rice I'd made too firm tonight. Too little water, I admitted. At least it hadn't turned into potato cakes, she said. Laughing, I answered that changing pans couldn't rescue every meal.

She asked whether the brother and sister would manage to open their shop. I turned the leaflet over. Only next week’s program, with no further story. Then she would stop asking, Lumi said. Otherwise, before the opening scene, we would have completed all their preparations for them.

“Would you get tired of them if they argued the whole time?” I asked.

“It depends what they argue about. Don’t choose a brother for me to dislike already.”

Looking at the small reproduction of the poster, I said the people holding the glass might not even be the siblings. One could be a neighbor. She laughed. Excellent. I was close to misunderstanding all four shoes. We imagined two entirely different movements: both people trying to go forward, or one of them backing away while the other advanced. Neither possibility became something the film had actually shown us. Buying a ticket hadn’t supplied that answer. We could still be wrong together about where the glass would end up, without needing to correct the poster or call the cinema.

Later, I asked whether she wanted the fifth story. Tonight, she didn't feel like pursuing the station sign. I wasn't eager to concentrate on reading either. The collection stayed where it was. Its attendant remained at the point after taking down the incorrect sign. A new arrangement didn't mean we needed to finish every older thing before bed.

Just before good night, Lumi said my name.

“Elliot.”

“I'm here.”

“When I thought of someone I don't know sitting beside you, I disliked them a little. That wasn't fair.”

“They might never come.”

“So I haven't even chosen the right person to dislike.”

Her laugh was brief. The seat wasn't a person yet, I said. We could leave it on the booking screen for now. She murmured agreement. She didn't ask me to promise I'd only look straight ahead on Saturday.

After closing the channel, I secured the ticket in the book again. Somebody occupied Saturday at ten past two now. Me. I went to switch off the kitchen light, then returned without checking my pocket. The ticket was in the book. Tonight, she'd said my name. Neither fact needed another inspection.
