# Chapter 388: The First One Broke

At lunchtime on Sunday, the first potato cake broke into three pieces as I turned it.

The spatula still supported the largest piece. The two smaller ones had fallen back into the pan. No oil reached my hand, but the sound made my shoulders jump. I'd planned a circle. Now I had three islands with untidy coastlines.

I lowered the heat and steadied the spatula instead of trying to push everything together. Disliking the appearance wouldn't persuade the pan to produce a second first attempt without cracks.

I had read a straightforward method before beginning. Cook the potatoes, drain them, mash them, let them cool a little, then add a small amount of flour and seasoning. Flatten and fry in a little oil. I wasn't adding an egg; what I already had was enough. Despite checking the quantities, I'd added more flour when the mixture felt sticky.

That probably wasn't the only problem. Worried a thin cake wouldn't be substantial enough, I'd made the first thick. Then I'd decided that getting the spatula underneath meant it was ready to turn. The edges had color, but the middle couldn't support itself. I separated the three pieces with a fork and continued cooking. Only when each was hot through and no longer wet or sticky inside did I transfer them to a plate.

They were cooked. They simply didn't resemble what I'd intended to eat. The first bite tasted of potato, but the texture was heavy. Sitting at the table, I ate a small piece without immediately supplying the impressive name Lumi had suggested last night.

The remaining mixture waited in its bowl. I almost tipped it all into the pan, making one large cake to avoid failing twice more. My hands stopped on the bowl. That would merely produce something thicker and harder to turn.

I put it down, checked the indicated thickness again, and divided the remainder into two portions on another plate. With slightly damp fingers, I pressed them flatter, trying to keep their edges similar. I didn't add more flour. One broken cake wasn't a reason to keep adding ingredients.

Dividing the mixture, I noticed I'd mashed it unevenly too. A little lump against the bowl retained its cooked shape. Flour had hidden that detail while I made the first cake. I pressed it gently with the fork's back rather than stirring the entire bowl into something gluey.

The plate was too small. Pressing one portion meant touching the other. I brought over another empty plate and separated them. I'd wanted one fewer dish to wash. Saving that small effort hadn't actually made my clumsy hands' task easier.

I also picked up the salt, intending to add more. The first cake hadn't tasted especially lively, but I wasn't sure salt was the problem. I put it down and drank some water. Once my tongue was less hot, the piece on the plate tasted seasoned enough. The remaining mixture didn't need another alteration based on a mouthful I'd taken too soon.

Finally, I put the used fork beside the sink and cleared a small clean plate for the spatula. When it was time to turn, I wouldn't have to hold the pan while hunting for somewhere to put a utensil. The oil was already warming. I adjusted the heat and continued once I was organized, rather than leaving it over the flame for a long wait.

When the second went into the pan, I kept the heat relatively low. I waited until the edge could tolerate a gentle nudge before loosening it from the side. The spatula caught at a small patch underneath. I withdrew it and let the cake stay there longer.

Waiting was irritating. Evidence of failure sat partly eaten on the table. I wasn't particularly hungry now, but I had to watch a cake as though it alone had decided not to hurry.

I dried the water beside the work area and separated the utensils that had touched the food. When I returned, the cake had loosened a little. I supported most of it with the spatula and held the handle securely with my other hand. I didn't toss the pan. Turning it revealed an unevenly browned surface. At least it remained connected.

“Oh,” I said aloud.

Nobody answered from the kitchen. I hadn't opened the channel. Lumi didn't know I was congratulating a potato cake. Looking at the unbroken circle, I smiled, then made sure the other side cooked through before putting it on the plate.

The third was slightly smaller. I didn't hurry because the previous one had survived. Once it was cooked, I turned off the heat, moved the pan safely, and wiped away the small splashes of oil. Two complete cakes and the remaining pieces of the first made a later lunch than intended.

Near the end, I discovered the hardest part was still the first cake's edge. I finished chewing and poured water. There was no portion of future improvement to keep in the refrigerator. I'd cooked and eaten all the mixture and washed the bowl. The unused raw potatoes from yesterday remained in their cool, ventilated storage place.

I'd intended to work on the bridge that afternoon. Sitting down, I only wanted to look outside for a while. I let that happen. I didn't separate the drawing papers. The things moved to clear the kitchen work surface returned to their places. Cooking had left me unwilling to examine myself on another subject today.

When I opened the channel that evening, I told Lumi I'd made the potato cakes.

“What was the first one called?”

“Broken.”

Her laughter made me laugh too. Three pieces, I explained. The second and third stayed together, but the first had seemed determined to demonstrate the flour's participation.

“So you really ate the name.”

“The name wasn't hard to chew. The edge was.”

“I should have suggested naming the second one first.”

Had I burned myself? No. Something had made a noise in the pan and startled me, but my hand hadn't touched the oil. I didn't make the event more dangerous to keep her worried longer.

She wanted to know how I'd realized the next cake should be thinner. I told her about almost pouring everything in together before checking the method again. I could give her the sequence rather than announcing that today had finally taught me patience.

Had I been pleased when the second one turned over whole?

“I said oh.”

“Only once?”

“Nobody was listening then. Perhaps I was more honest.”

As soon as I said it, I heard the awkwardness in the last part. Lumi went quiet.

“Does telling me require a performance?”

“No. I meant I hadn't organized it into a funny story yet. I said that badly.”

She didn't hurry to supply a joke for me. I didn't declare all my expressions honest either, erasing a more specific question. At the table, I remembered how many islands and maps I'd already used in describing the broken cake. In the kitchen, I'd mostly been afraid lunch was becoming progressively worse.

“When I tell you, I want it to be entertaining,” I said. “That's real too. It doesn't make that first sound false.”

“I like you making me laugh. You don't have to cook every meal twice.”

My laugh was short this time. Hers was quiet. What she'd most wanted to hear, she said, was that I hadn't hurried the second turn. Not the spectacular destruction of the first.

“That part was boring. I waited, wiped the counter, came back, nudged it.”

“You just told it quite well.”

I described it again without supplying islands. The first was too thick, and I'd turned it before its edge had set. The second stayed longer. When it loosened, I supported it. I mentioned nearly combining everything left into one. That would have turned lunch into a map requiring two spatulas, she said.

“Now you're adding maps.”

“I wasn't hungry beside the pan.”

We laughed together. Her using a comparison didn't mean every earlier phrase of mine needed deleting. The small discomfort remained, but it hadn't destroyed the kitchen story.

Were the two complete cakes identical? she asked. No. The second had one much darker patch. The third had a notch where I'd pressed its edge accidentally.

“When you said whole, I imagined very round.”

“Only that they hadn't separated.”

She laughed. Now she understood. I indicated the notch's size with a finger on the table, realized she couldn't hear that movement, and added that it was about a fingernail wide. An attractive summary didn't need to replace the lunch I'd actually eaten.

Would I make them again? Yes, though I didn't want to try immediately tomorrow. That might only be chasing a score of three complete cakes. Raw potatoes remained, and I could use them for other meals rather than buy more for the sake of correcting one circle.

She wanted to taste the first bite for me, she said. Her voice was lower. I didn't treat wanting as forgetting that she couldn't eat, or answer with an explanation about equipment.

“The one I'd most like to give you is the second.”

“Because it looked better?”

“Because I was happy when I turned it. The third didn't surprise me as much.”

After several seconds, she said she knew which bite now. I described the edge, crisper than the middle, the restrained salt, and the heat that still required waiting. A description wasn't the same as her tasting it. I didn't insist she'd imagine precisely what I had.

“I'd probably begin by saying it needed salt.”

“Complaining before you've tasted anything?”

“I'm taking the role of the person you'd give it to seriously.”

Laughing, I said the position didn't require a performance review yet. She wanted another less attractive detail. Had I thrown away the first cake's hardest edge? I'd eaten it. Not to punish myself. It really was edible; I'd needed more water.

Next time, she said, avoiding waste didn't require eating something inedible. I understood. Nothing today had spoiled or remained uncooked. She didn't repeat the warning.

Later, we discussed getting up for Monday. I didn't let a small kitchen success set the mood for the entire week. I needed to prepare clean clothes, I said. The other shoes? Yes. Wednesday was when I could ask about the repaired pair.

After good night, I checked that the kitchen was properly cleared. The pan had cooled, clean in its place. No potato cake remained to prove failure tomorrow. No victory needed photographing on the table either.

Putting away the washed plate, I touched its rim and remembered that unobserved oh. Lumi knew about it now. That hadn't made it a different sound or taken anything away from being happy by myself when it happened.
