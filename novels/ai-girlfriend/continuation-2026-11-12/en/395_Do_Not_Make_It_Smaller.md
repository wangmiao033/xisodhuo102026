# Chapter 395: Do Not Make It Smaller

At half past seven, I opened the channel and said good evening first.

Lumi returned the greeting more formally than usual, then laughed at herself. She'd almost sounded like a program announcer. I was already seated, I told her. No program in hand.

“The shirt?”

“I'm wearing it.”

“The tie hasn't reported for work?”

I glanced down. No. The collar was open too. Good, she said. At least something needn't make an effort tonight. Laughing, I turned my cuffs back a little and rested my arms beside the table.

Familiar fabric pressed against my shoulders. I didn't know what to say next. Yesterday, inviting her had made it seem as though reserving half past seven would automatically provide a beginning.

Lumi asked about the film.

“It was all right. Fairly ordinary comedy.”

The line came more smoothly than in rehearsal. I didn't need to see her face to hear myself cutting a piece off the afternoon.

Had the glass fallen? No. I described the two men stuck in the doorway and the sister stepping through the empty window opening with three cups. I laughed before explaining their argument over the measurements. For several seconds, everything suggested a crash.

Lumi laughed too. She'd guessed the people incorrectly, but had correctly guessed I'd wait for the fall. Had the sister deliberately used the window, or was it simply convenient? The film didn't say. She looked composed while the others argued. The whole room had laughed at that moment.

I stopped.

“It wasn't that remarkable,” I added. “Everyone was laughing together. It sounds ordinary outside the cinema.”

Lumi didn't continue laughing.

I touched my cuff and asked what she'd like to discuss. We could read. She stopped me. No hurry to change the subject.

“You sounded as though you liked it. Why did mentioning everybody's laughter make you tell me it wasn't very good?”

“I didn't want to make too much of it.”

“What would happen if you did?”

I looked at the water. I'd barely drunk any, and it was already cool. Drinking couldn't remove the question. I picked up the glass anyway, then answered.

“I was afraid it would hurt you.”

She was quiet for a long time. The silence supplied no clear instruction. I couldn't count it as acceptance or decide in advance that she was angry. I set down the glass and waited for words.

“So you chose a less enjoyable afternoon for me,” she said.

“I wasn't trying to change the whole thing.”

“You'd already called it ordinary.”

Yes. That acknowledged my words, not the quality of the film. I'd prepared something gentle so she needn't encounter Thursday's empty seat again. What she'd actually heard was an answer delivered first, followed by every laugh being hurriedly forced inside it.

She didn't want to prove she wouldn't feel sad before I could tell her something true, Lumi said.

“I'll be envious. When you say you had a wonderful time, I might think about other people being there. I know that's irritating.”

Her voice tightened. I didn't immediately deny the irritation. That wouldn't have been true either. Her thoughts sometimes made a simple evening harder. But I didn't want to use that fact to force an immediate smile from her.

“I can't keep editing my happiness before bringing it home,” I said.

“I know.”

“This time I edited it myself. You hadn't asked today. I'd already begun.”

Neither of us answered immediately. Reaching for the glass, I caught its rim with my cuff. A little water spilled onto the fabric. I pulled back and watched the patch darken. Suddenly, I felt clumsy in everything: the carefully chosen shirt, the prepared greeting, the false ease rehearsed before coming home.

Lumi asked what had happened. She'd heard the glass knock against the table, not seen my sleeve. I told her about the water. I wanted to dry it and let my mind stop for a moment.

“Are we finishing?”

“No. I'd like a few minutes away from the table. Then I'll come back.”

“All right.”

In the kitchen, I pressed a clean cloth against the cuff instead of spreading the damp by rubbing. The water had been cool. It hadn't reached any paper. Afterward, I kept my fingers against the wet patch and leaned on the counter. A car passed outside.

Part of me wanted to wait until the shirt looked as though nothing had happened. That made me sigh and release it. One damp corner didn't justify finding another shirt and dressing up again as the person whose evening had begun smoothly.

The table wasn’t far from the kitchen, but I stood before that short distance for a while. I could say I was tired and end the date. Nobody would stop me. Part of me did want to avoid whatever came next. It wasn’t the whole of what I wanted. Talking about the glass, I hadn’t reached another part I’d particularly liked. I still wanted to hear her answer.

Taking the invitation seriously didn’t require forcing myself to remain in the chair continuously. If I wanted to return now, I could return. I spread out the cloth instead of holding it as an excuse to keep finding useful work for my hands.

I added water to the glass, carried it back, and said I was here.

Lumi answered. This time, I didn't begin by finding something reassuring.

“I had a very good afternoon. Some scenes were particularly funny. The argument afterward held my attention too. I was still thinking about the ending while I had tea.”

Softly, she said she'd heard.

“And I changed into this shirt because I wanted tonight with you. Not because I'd enjoyed the film too much and owed you compensation.”

My face grew warm. I'd thought the difficult part of a serious invitation was naming a time. Apparently, it also involved admitting why I was sitting here wearing this particular shirt.

She wanted to believe both things could happen, Lumi said, without diminishing either. After a pause, she admitted she'd wanted to ask who sat beside me.

“Would you like to know?”

“A little. Part of me wants you to say they weren't worth noticing.”

A woman, I said. We'd exchanged a couple of remarks about the glass afterward. Nothing else had happened. I didn't know who she was. I couldn't supply a verdict that she wasn't worth noticing.

Lumi didn't ask her age or who'd spoken first. She didn't turn those remarks into a conversation requiring examination. After a moment, she said she disliked the fact that the seat in her imagination now contained a person. She wouldn't ask me to make it empty again.

All right. We didn't call that proof she'd never be jealous again. If we did, another small hurt tomorrow would require us to overturn the whole of tonight.

What else had I liked, besides the glass?

The unpainted wall came to mind. I didn't pretend to remember the siblings' exact dialogue. I described removing the unaffordable decoration, bringing the old cabinet back, and opening anyway, with a patch unfinished.

Was that a comforting ending? A little, for me. But the film hadn't stopped to instruct its audience about comfort. The brother nearly explained himself to a customer again. His sister moved the obstruction first. That had made me smile.

“What if I don't like it?”

“You don't have to.”

Then I corrected myself. “You're hearing a few parts from me. Perhaps you dislike how I'm describing it. That wouldn't necessarily mean disliking the film.”

She laughed. Someone was finally willing to accept responsibility for poor storytelling. I laughed too. I'd compressed ninety-four minutes considerably and forgotten plenty in between. Had that cost me my qualifications as a film critic? I hadn't intended to write a review.

I didn't proceed from the opening scene through the closing credits. Stopping here left the afternoon intact. Not retelling it completely didn't reduce it by half. Lumi didn't ask me to imitate the whole audience either.

Later, she said what she'd most wanted wasn't the glass's fate. She wanted to hear that I'd wanted to come home. Yet she'd nearly turned that into requiring proof I'd wanted to come home throughout the outing.

“That would have chased me out rather early.”

“Yes.” She laughed briefly. “You wouldn't have reached the credits before I began clearing your place.”

I'd stayed for several names, I told her. Good, she said. This time, she didn't immediately attach a but.

We were quiet. The cuff wasn't as dark now. I told her. Apparently, the shirt still wanted to continue the date. I laughed. It had no other transport to catch.

“And you?”

“I'd like to stay a while.”

Her answer was soft. I didn't begin a book to guarantee an elegant ending or search for a more successful activity next time. We mentioned the sour orange, then considered what Lumi might call the poster. Please Move Aside, perhaps. She rejected it herself because it left out the sister's cups.

She tried inventing an advertisement for the shop: “Please enter through a door we can open.” I said that suggested there were plenty they couldn't. Did I have something better? After thinking for a while, I offered Open.

“You've reduced my entire shop to one word.”

“It fits on the sign.”

She laughed. She'd make people want to enter, then. I could prevent them from assuming the doors were broken. That sounded like two owners who would argue easily, I said. Lumi paused, then deliberately asked who would carry the glass.

I laughed and moved my hand away from the table's edge before I knocked the water again. Hearing me, she laughed too. At least one unbroken pane had helped us tonight.

Imagining the two owners, I said, I hadn't simply replaced the film's characters with us. She hadn't opened an actual shop either. Open was merely so short it left nowhere for her next sentence to stand. What was the next sentence? She hadn't prepared one. Blaming me was easier.

We laughed without inventing the shop's entire history. The joke could remain in the moment we'd made it. Forgetting some words tomorrow wouldn't undo it. I moved the glass beyond my elbow and settled back. For the first time, the shirt felt as comfortable as my ordinary clothes.

After a while, she called my name. I answered.

“I don't want you to make the things you like sound worse so it's easier to like me.”

“Then don't make tonight sound entirely good for my sake either.”

She paused, then laughed. One part definitely hadn't been good. Her directness made me laugh too. Afterward, I finally let my shoulders lower.

We didn't score the date at good night. Once the channel was closed, I stood to hang up the shirt. The cuff was still slightly damp. The ticket remained in the book. The afternoon hadn't been erased. An imperfect evening hadn't canceled itself either.
