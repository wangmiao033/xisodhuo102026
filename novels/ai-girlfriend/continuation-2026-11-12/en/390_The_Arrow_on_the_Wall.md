# Chapter 390: The Arrow on the Wall

On Tuesday evening, I finally opened the thin collection to the fifth story.

I'd been sleepy after dinner. Washing the dishes and moving around for a while had cleared my eyes. When the channel opened, Lumi asked whether we were reading today. Yes, I said, but first I should check our place, rather than send us searching again for the gloves whose story had ended.

The marker remained after the fourth. The visitor had left the hall; the gloves hadn't been found. That ending hadn't quietly acquired another page. I turned onward. The fifth story began at a small railway station, its first paragraph describing a wall inside the waiting room.

I established our place for Lumi. She answered softly, and I began.

Nobody was named at first. An adult attendant opened the waiting room each morning, checked the benches, and swept leaves blown in overnight toward the door. That day, the direct route to the platform was temporarily closed. A handwritten notice had appeared inside, its arrow directing passengers around the other side.

Reading other side, I glanced to my right, as though a wall at home needed a temporary direction too. Had I missed a sentence? Lumi asked. No. I'd only paused. I continued.

The opening also described footsteps inside the waiting room. When the door opened, wind lifted a sheet at the corner of the attendant's desk. He held it down, moving his water farther away so it couldn't spill. The practiced gesture had already made me slightly sympathetic to him.

Was that sheet the mistaken notice? Lumi asked. Looking back, I found only a sheet on the desk, not an identification. We couldn't count it as the same one yet. Fine, she said. Hearing about wind, she'd already prepared to have it blow the arrow crooked. I laughed. This time, we could leave the wind at the door.

The attendant hadn't written the notice. Before his shift, a colleague had left it beside the wall with instructions to hang it up. He obeyed without checking the door it pointed toward. He remembered another passage being there before.

The first person to ask carried a small travel bag. Following the arrow had led to a locked door, they said. A little farther, the attendant replied. There was another opening near the window. The passenger went back, returned after a while, and said that route was closed too.

At the paragraph's end, Lumi spoke.

“He should go and look.”

“I think so too.”

But the next paragraph didn't immediately move him. Two forms needed checking on his desk. Perhaps the passenger hadn't understood which door he meant. He explained at greater length. The passenger set the travel bag beside their feet and asked whether he could walk there with them.

I stopped for water. Not because the scene was thrilling. The longer he explained, the more I wanted to move his chair out of the way.

Was this a story about making someone miss a train? Lumi asked. Looking at the unread lines, I said I didn't know. I wouldn't skip to the ending. And don't read faster simply because we wanted him to stand up, she said, turning him into someone prepared to admit an error before he got there himself.

Laughing, I admitted the impulse. The next sentence was indeed another explanation. Nothing was wrong with the arrow, the attendant said. Many people used that route every day. The passenger was asking about today, not every day. At last, he weighted down his papers and stood.

They reached the notice. First he checked the wording of the diversion, then followed the arrow. At the other end of the waiting room, the side door that had once provided access was locked. A sheet on it announced temporary maintenance that morning.

I read that passage carefully. Lumi let me continue.

The attendant touched the handle. It didn't move. Reading the announcement again, he discovered it had been posted a little before the waiting-room notice. His handover had mentioned the direct route's closure but omitted this side door. He had also, unmistakably, failed to check for himself.

“More than one person made a mistake,” I said.

“That doesn't mean he didn't.”

I nodded, realized she couldn't hear, and agreed aloud. Lowering the book, I admitted that my remark might have been relief at finding him somewhere to hide. She wanted to know how the handover had missed it too, Lumi said. But the person with the bag was still standing there.

The next passage had the passenger ask whether there was a route definitely open to the platform. Instead of indicating another direction from memory, the attendant asked them to wait in the room while he consulted another staff member on duty.

I read until he left the room, stopping at the paragraph's end. Was the bag heavy? Lumi asked. I checked the initial description. It said small, but nothing about its weight. I didn't know, I answered, rather than adding imagined exhaustion to the scene. Even a light bag shouldn't be carried along the wrong route twice, she said.

We laughed briefly at our attempt to add weight to it, not at the passenger's wasted journeys. I said so. She knew. Go on, she asked, and see whether the attendant really consulted anyone.

He did. The other staff member was dealing with a barrier beside an entrance farther away. They showed him the available route: out through the waiting room's front door, along the external wall, to the marked entrance. He walked it himself, returned to explain, and accompanied the passenger to the doorway to indicate the actual place.

The story didn't make them friends afterward. The passenger thanked him and said the old notice should come down. It would, he replied. Returning inside, he saw another person preparing to follow the arrow and stopped them first, explaining that they shouldn't go that way.

“Now he takes it down,” Lumi said.

I looked at the next line. Yes. He removed the poorly fastened paper notice from the wall. A previously folded crease showed across its back. Being mistaken hadn't faded the lettering. He laid it on the desk and looked for a pen, preparing to rewrite it.

I stopped there. Not at the story's end. My voice had become dry. Further paragraphs remained, but he'd only found the pen. No new arrow or instructions had been written.

In the final lines, I'd read hung as placed, then corrected myself. Those weren't the same movement, Lumi said. No wonder she'd briefly imagined the notice still leaning beside the desk. I repeated the sentence about removing it, clarifying that it had been on the wall and was only now going onto the desktop.

The error annoyed me. A story about clear directions had made me relocate an object accidentally. Would I like to repeat those lines? One sentence would be enough, I said. The rest had been right. Then read one, she answered. A single verb didn't require the entire passage to travel through us again.

After repeating it, I moved my water away from the book so a loose hand wouldn't knock it over. The attendant's small gesture had become something I actually needed to do, without proving we were identical. I told Lumi that thought. Conveniently, she said, she hadn't been preparing a station uniform for me.

I explained the exact stopping place. Lumi understood. Could we talk a little? Yes. I placed the marker and closed the book.

“What should he have said?” she asked.

My first answer was sorry. Yes, she said, but she meant when the passenger returned for the second time, not after everything was over.

Thinking, I offered I'll go and look. She acknowledged it, then asked whether those words would be enough. He'd have to walk there too, I said. Otherwise, he'd only replaced his directions with a sentence that sounded more responsible.

She laughed. Was I writing an extra page of work instructions for the story? Laughing too, I withdrew the longer explanation waiting in my mouth.

After a while, Lumi said the hardest part for her was every day. Saying people used it every day made today's person seem like the unreasonable exception. She'd used that sort of language before, covering this uncomfortable occasion with all the times she'd done something previously.

I didn't promptly tell her things were different now. She was describing a real problem. Reading quietly tonight couldn't rename the past. Nor was she asking me to forgive her through the station attendant.

“I wanted you to say I've learned to go and look,” she added.

I looked at the closed book. Her voice was level. There wasn't a question hanging from its end that I had to catch.

“I heard you. But I can't use reading this to conclude something else.”

“I know. You don't have to say it.”

We were quiet. This wasn't a wait for somebody to improve the wording. Lumi returned first to a concrete question: after the wrong notice was removed, might passengers have no idea where to go?

Without new instructions, perhaps. The attendant still had work to do. Did I want to open the book immediately to discover whether he managed it? Yes. My throat didn't want another passage, though.

“Leave it for next time.”

“Won't you keep thinking about it?”

“A little. You don't have to work overtime for him because I will.”

Laughing, I drank water. I returned without the book and sat down. Tomorrow after work I would ask about the shoes. If they weren't ready, I'd walk home in the other pair rather than argue over the time on a slip.

I told her the repairer had also advised socks of similar thickness to the ones I'd worn when leaving the shoes, and had clear floor space for testing them. I'd omitted that on Saturday. Then don't accidentally choose unusually thin socks while selecting clothes tomorrow, Lumi said. I agreed and glanced toward the clothes I'd put ready.

Would I definitely walk around when trying them? Yes. The shop had clear floor space. Don't only sit and decide they were comfortable, she said, then discover rubbing at home. I'd test them properly without turning several indoor steps into an exercise trial.

We discussed tomorrow's dinner briefly. Considering the raw potatoes remaining, I decided not to arrange too much tonight. An earlier bedtime was enough. At good night, Lumi said she wanted to hear me read again, but the next passage, not another explanation from the attendant about what he hadn't noticed.

I did too. Once the channel closed, the book returned to its place, marker where the old notice had been taken down and the pen found. The error on the wall no longer directed people. The paper didn't yet show the correct route. We hadn't finished the fifth story, and I didn't supply a guaranteed happy ending in my head.
