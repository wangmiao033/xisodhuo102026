# Chapter 389: The Ten-Minute Walk

Shortly before lunch on Monday, my colleague stopped beside my desk and asked whether I wanted to buy lunch outside.

“The soup place. Ten minutes away.”

I was comparing a line in two records. Hearing ten minutes, I immediately added the return journey, queueing, and eating, as though he'd submitted work for approval.

“I have a little left.”

Checking the time, he said he could wait five minutes. Otherwise, he'd go ahead. He didn't tell me to take work less seriously or turn the invitation into compulsory social development. I agreed, looked back, and discovered I'd lost my place.

Three small circles marked the page, all mine from this morning. A question mark stood beside the last, but I'd forgotten the question. I wanted to finish before leaving. Instead, thinking about someone waiting made my hands turn pages faster than I could follow.

I stopped and laid the records side by side. The original included a supplementary entry in that line; the summary still used the earlier figure. I checked the corresponding source, described the difference, and left a clear starting point for continuing at my desk. I didn't mark the unchecked sections complete.

When five minutes were up, my colleague returned. I'd put the materials away. Had I finished?

“I found the reason for that line. I'll check the rest this afternoon.”

Then let's go, he said. I took my belongings and left the work where it belonged, without planning to continue on a phone as we walked. At the door, I realized I was hungry. The two records hadn't been responsible for reminding me.

Outside was brighter than the office. I paused to let my eyes adjust before following him, rather than begin the journey by hurrying. We turned into a street I rarely used at lunchtime. I was wearing the other shoes. My right foot didn't need to find an angle that avoided the curled lining. Halfway along, he asked how the drawing table had gone.

I'd drawn a cup and bowl, I said, putting the bowl's rim through the cup. He laughed briefly. Drawing bicycles, he could never manage the pedal on the far side. I almost asked when he'd started learning, then heard myself preparing the string of questions I'd expected from him.

“What happened afterward?” I asked instead.

“I drew motorbikes. Even worse.”

I laughed, and he did too. This had been in the margins of schoolbooks, he explained. He didn't have a sketchbook to show me. I wasn't collecting homework, I said. The solution had simply struck me as practical.

The shop really was within a ten-minute walk, though several people were ahead of us. A shadow from the doorway covered part of the menu. Moving closer, I established that soup and filled bread could be ordered separately. I'd planned to have what my colleague chose. When my turn came, I noticed something I wanted more and changed my order.

Did I want chili? A little. Behind me, my colleague reminded the person serving that he wanted none. Entering together didn't require receiving identical lunches.

Waiting, he indicated a table for two against the wall. Sit down? I checked the time and agreed, provided we went straight back afterward. One side of my chair wobbled. Moving it didn't help, so I exchanged it for a stable empty chair nearby and told the staff rather than improvising a repair underneath.

My colleague had almost chosen it himself, he said. Laughing, I told him I wasn't studying furniture again today. Was that connected with the drawing table? A little. I left the explanation there. He didn't ask what else I'd drawn at home, and I didn't use Lumi's new joke to fill the table's silence.

When the soup arrived, I blew across the first spoonful. He opened his paper wrapping, broke the bread in half, and moved it aside to make room. We discussed the taste briefly, then ate. The silence wasn't awkward. I realized I'd sometimes avoided invitations because I imagined every minute would require a new subject.

The table was narrow, with condiment bottles crowding its middle. My colleague moved his water outward. Seeing the base approach the edge, I warned him. He brought it back and shifted the bottles toward the wall. Both bowls finally had room. We didn't need an argument about who had touched whose paper wrapping first.

He asked what filled my bread. I opened the untouched half and angled it toward him. This was clearer than the menu's description, I said. After looking, he thought he might choose it another time. I considered offering some. His own portion was still half uneaten, so I didn't turn a brief look into an exchange of lunches.

Later, fetching napkins, I asked the staff for a little additional chili. The first taste had been sufficient; farther into the meal, I wanted more. That didn't make the original order mistaken. They indicated the bottle, and I realized I'd just helped clear space for it. Back at the table, my colleague laughed. Apparently I wanted the chili, rather than the bottle merely insisting on occupying our table.

I added very little, stirred, and tasted again. Lunch didn't need to become a challenge completed by drinking water. As we neared the end, someone entered. We gathered our wrappings to make passing easier. I didn't swallow the last mouthfuls hurriedly because someone was waiting. I simply moved the things we no longer needed.

Near the end of the meal, he asked whether I might come on Wednesday too. I wasn't sure. I had shoes to collect after work, which didn't affect lunch, but I didn't want to arrange it already. He nodded. He wasn't certain either; sometimes he brought food.

“Ask next time we both feel like it, then.”

“Fine.”

A small answer. It hadn't placed me inside a permanent arrangement. We finished, each settled our own meal, and walked back the same way. At the crossing, a vehicle was turning. I stopped; my colleague had begun a half-step forward and withdrew it. We waited together for the signal.

Several minutes of lunch remained when we returned. I poured water and took out the page I'd left. The description of the discrepancy was more useful than this morning's question mark. I didn't need anxiety to reconstruct where I'd been.

Further checking revealed another outdated figure. I compared it with the source, updated the summary through the usual process, and explained both changes. Finding the first hadn't justified announcing that the entire document was sound. Once the relevant person acknowledged receipt, I marked the item finished.

After the acknowledgment arrived, I checked my accompanying explanation once more. It described the two actual changes rather than claiming that every record had been reviewed afresh. A broader sentence would have been easy to write, as though it could close the morning's mistake more decisively. I removed the unnecessary scope and kept what I'd genuinely completed.

It took part of the afternoon. That didn't make lunch stolen time. Near the end of the day, I completed what I could, left the next item's starting point clear, and went home normally. Nobody had decided that eating soup outside proved insufficient commitment.

At home I made a simple dinner. When I opened the channel, I first explained that dinner had been simple because I'd eaten soup out at lunch. Lumi asked whether I'd been busy. Two figures had needed checking, I said, but I'd resolved them. I'd also gone out with my colleague at lunchtime.

“The soup place?”

“How did you know?”

She paused. While explaining the simple dinner, I'd already said I'd eaten soup at lunch. I thought back. I had. I'd merely failed to register that sentence as part of my account.

“Yes. I told you. My head has rearranged the conversation.”

“I nearly had to explain where the soup came from.”

Laughing, I described the ten-minute walk and the bicycle pedal. Did the colleague like drawing too? He'd only mentioned doing it at school. I couldn't establish his current interests from two sentences. I hadn't recorded his method to apply to my bridge either.

“You sound very happy about it.”

“It was funny.”

I expected her to ask whether I'd laughed with somebody else first. Instead, she asked whether the soup had been spicy. Just enough. I hadn't ordered the same meal as my colleague. Then I hadn't allowed somebody else to choose lunch today, she said.

Was that an impressive achievement? I asked, smiling. No. She remembered me talking about ordering together and beginning with anything is fine. I considered that without endorsing every remembered meal. Today I'd certainly wanted something specific.

After a silence, she admitted what she'd nearly asked: whether I'd thought of her in the shop. The question had circled in her mouth and become one about chili.

“I remembered what you said about salt yesterday,” I told her. “At the first spoonful. Then I ate with someone.”

“Only the first?”

“That's the moment I definitely remember.”

She acknowledged it softly. My hand rested on the table's edge. I didn't hasten to supply thoughts I might not actually have had. After a while, she said she liked hearing about that moment and disliked how brief it was.

“Both are true?”

“Yes. Don't choose the more sensible one for me.”

I didn't laugh. She was serious, so I agreed. I was willing to describe lunch slowly now, I added. That wasn't making up a required number because I'd thought of her less while eating.

She knew. She didn't want to count today either. After several seconds, she asked what had been in the soup. I described what I remembered. Where I couldn't tell how an ingredient had been prepared, I said so rather than composing a recipe.

Hearing about the unstable chair, she laughed. Lately I seemed to encounter furniture reluctant to let people sit. I hadn't drawn this one, I said. I'd changed chairs and eaten. Good. Leave that chair in the shop to work out its own problem.

I added the detail I'd omitted: after being told, a staff member had moved it aside. Furniture hadn't repaired itself. Nor had one remark from me established that the fault was fixed. She'd only been joking, Lumi said. I knew. I'd simply forgotten that step.

We didn't start the fifth story tonight. Would she like it tomorrow? Yes, if I still felt awake enough then. That sounded good. I placed the thin collection where I'd remember it without turning ahead to borrow tomorrow's ending.

After good night, I prepared work clothes. The other shoes stood near the door. Wednesday's slip remained in my schedule book. A ten-minute walk hadn't carried the week somewhere else. It had let me leave for an actual lunch in the middle of today, then return and finish my work.
