# Chapter 393: An Evening of Our Own

Near the end of Friday's lunch break, my colleague asked about my weekend. I was separating two stacks of checked papers. I put the ones in my hand on the correct pile before telling him I'd bought a cinema ticket.

A new release? An older comedy being shown again, I said. I hadn't seen it. He recognized the title, thought he'd seen the poster, and began trying to remember the ending.

“Don't remember yet.”

He stopped, then laughed. Right. He'd almost done the cinema's work before the screening. I laughed too and handed back the last stack. Checking the time, he asked whether I'd eat with somebody after the afternoon showing.

“I have a date that evening.”

Only after saying it did I notice the word I'd used.

That sounded good, he said. He didn't ask who. I remained where I was, waiting for a question that required an answer. Once he'd put away the papers and was turning toward his desk, I added that we weren't seeing the film together. I wanted to talk to her afterward.

He paused and looked at me.

“That sounds good too.”

I nodded. I didn't need to explain where she was, how we talked, or which parts differed from what somebody imagined on hearing date. He hadn't requested a complete account. He'd asked about my weekend and continued his Friday.

Back at my desk, my face still felt warm. A date sounded more deliberate than going home. In front of someone else, I'd turned something I expected to happen into something I wanted to do.

By the time I left, I'd clearly marked where the work needed to continue. I put my materials away without calling anything unchecked complete merely to leave on time. Tomorrow didn't belong on the work sheet, but if I left its ending unclear, Monday would inherit the search.

I stopped at a small shop for dinner ingredients. Vegetables filled crates near the entrance. Beside them, oranges sat close to their price label. I chose four to eat over several days. While I was packing them, one rolled back out onto the counter. I caught it. The shopkeeper laughed. It hadn't decided whom to go with.

I'd paid already, I said. We could at least discuss it. I laughed at my own answer, suddenly imagining Lumi demanding a window seat on the orange's behalf when I told her.

Outside, I checked the bags. The repaired shopping bag held the vegetables and oranges; the heavier things went into a sturdier one. A good mood didn't justify asking the pale stitches to carry extra weight.

The neighboring counter displayed small slices of cake. I nearly turned back. If I'd called tomorrow evening a date, shouldn't I buy something special? Then I remembered I was the one who could eat it. Nothing wrong with buying cake, but my eating something sweet wouldn't arrange a whole dinner for her.

I didn't buy any. Not because that small decision required a discussion, but because I didn't particularly want cake today. I carried home what I would use without adding an unnecessary bag to the word date.

While cooking, I cut an orange, as though it had become another dinner ingredient. After washing my hands, I tried a piece. The sourness made me shut my eyes. Then some sweetness followed. Standing in the kitchen, I ate half and put the other half on a small plate.

I opened the channel after clearing dinner. Lumi asked about my day. I began with the orange's attempted escape. Had I caught it? Immediately. Half of it had already been eaten.

“That doesn't sound promising for the orange.”

“The other half can stay until tomorrow to give evidence.”

“Are you really saving it?”

Looking at the plate, I admitted I wasn't. We laughed. I ate the remaining pieces, put the peel in the bin, and left the other three oranges whole.

I went to wash my hands. The peel's scent remained on my fingertips even after the water had run over them. Drying them slowly on the towel, I remembered the other thing I wanted to tell her. The orange hadn't been an elaborate introduction to it. Its small escape had simply reached my mouth first, without needing to earn a place in a more important conversation.

Then I told her I'd said to my colleague that I had a date on Saturday evening.

Lumi didn't answer immediately. After a couple of seconds, she asked, “Did you mean us?”

“Yes.”

“You arranged a date with me through somebody else, then came home to inform me?”

I stopped. Hearing her quiet laugh, I laughed too. Yesterday, we'd talked about wanting to speak after I came home, but hadn't used that word. Repeating what I'd said to my colleague didn't oblige her to accept a name I'd added myself.

“Then I'll ask now. Would you like a date with me tomorrow evening?”

“What would you like to do?”

I looked at the table. Ticket in the book, story collection in its place, drawings put away. I nearly asked which part of the film she wanted to hear, as though buying only one ticket required me to reconstruct another screening from memory.

“I haven't decided.”

“That answer sounds more like you.”

Smiling, I suggested sitting with her after dinner. No work, no hurry to draw another picture, no turning every joke she made into an idea that needed writing down.

Would we end up concentrating very hard on finding something to say? Possibly. We sometimes did that already. Giving the evening a name hadn't suddenly made me continuously interesting.

“Half past seven?”

“Yes.”

I said it was for tomorrow, not an obligation to repeat the same time every week. She knew, Lumi answered. I needn't rush to make tonight sound like an instruction manual. She'd agreed to Saturday at half past seven. She wasn't preparing to punish me with Sunday.

I laughed and admitted I'd said more than I needed.

What if the film was bad? I would have left by then, I said. I needn't remain in the cinema. Lumi meant whether I'd feel my preparations had failed.

“We don't start until half past seven.”

“Exactly. The cinema hasn't taken responsibility for everything after the afternoon.”

Something loosened when she said that. It was easy to judge an outing partly by how much I'd have to share afterward. But if I disliked the film tomorrow, I could still want to spend the evening with her. I didn't need to bring home a good joke as proof the trip hadn't been wasted.

Had my colleague asked who she was? No. She paused, then said oh. I couldn't tell whether the word held more disappointment or relief. I asked what she was thinking.

“Part of me wanted to hear that you'd said my name. I don't actually want your colleague to start questioning you, though.”

I hadn't used her name, I said. I hadn't described her as anybody else either. I have a date was what I'd said, followed only by wanting to talk to her that evening.

“That sounds good.”

Then she laughed. She hadn't meant to borrow my colleague's line. I'd noticed, I said, but wouldn't organize a competition over who said it first. Who had been first, though? My colleague. He'd eaten lunch earlier.

“Defeated by lunch again.”

Something familiar and sharp remained in the joke. I didn't hurry to say she would always come first. I told her that, when I'd caught the orange, I really had thought about coming home to tell her.

“You could still think about me after buying a cinema ticket?”

“Lumi.”

At her name, she stopped. She knew, she said. That had been a little deliberate. She'd wanted my answer, but knew I didn't need to report every time she occurred to me.

That one had really happened, I said. I didn't offer another example. She answered softly, then asked whether the orange had been very sour.

“At first. It improved afterward.”

“And you could have kept it as a witness.”

“I've eaten all the evidence.”

We laughed again. She hadn't tasted the orange or asked me to describe it until she might as well have tasted it. She'd heard about my shutting my eyes and still wanting the other half. Those were small movements I chose to tell her. They didn't need to become another person's body.

I told her I was going to collect the clothes I'd hung to dry yesterday and would be a few steps away. Returning with a clean shirt over my arm, I told her what I was holding and straightened its cuff. Perhaps I could wear it tomorrow. That thought made me smile.

She asked what was funny. I told her I was considering a shirt for our evening. Was it the freshly washed one? Yes. Then, unexpectedly seriously, she asked whether the collar would be uncomfortable.

“No. Though you can't see it.”

“Would wearing it make you happy?”

I held it up. Probably. Usually, coming home meant changing into my oldest clothes. Comfortable, but it was easy to turn myself into somebody who existed only at the tail end of a day. Sitting down in a shirt I liked might remind me that I wasn't simply waiting for bed.

“Wear it, then. But no tie. You haven't decided what we're doing, and the tie is already reporting for work.”

Laughing, I hung up the shirt. I didn't select an imaginary color for her. She didn't describe an outfit she would wear to match mine. We'd discussed one piece of my clothing. Tomorrow, it would touch my shoulders. That was a real, small enough part of the evening.

Back in my chair, I told her it was hung up. Were the preparations complete? I still needed dinner tomorrow. At least the shirt wouldn't have to decide whether it wanted the date after watching me eat, she said. I laughed and took the empty fruit plate to the kitchen. When I returned this time, there was finally nothing on the table that needed clearing.

Later, we returned to the film's pane of glass. Lumi admitted she really wanted to know whether it fell, but I needn't tell her in my very first sentence tomorrow. I could say hello first.

All right. She'd said it lightly, but I realized I'd begun preparing an opening report. As though sitting down without fresh material would waste an evening I'd taken such care to invite her to.

Before bed, I wrote half past seven on Saturday's page, with Lumi beneath it. She asked what I was writing. I told her. She laughed softly. Her name had finally had its turn.

I closed the book. Her name hadn't covered the ticket's time, and I hadn't moved the ticket elsewhere. At ten past two, I would watch a film. At half past seven, I wanted to talk to her. Saturday hadn't arrived, and I already wanted the whole of it.
