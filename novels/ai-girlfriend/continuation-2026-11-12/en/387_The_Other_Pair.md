# Chapter 387: The Other Pair

On Saturday, I discovered the worn lining while putting on my right shoe.

My finger found a hard edge inside the heel. Yesterday it had merely rubbed. I'd pulled my sock higher and forgotten about it. Now a short strip of cloth had curled back, exposing a differently colored layer underneath. I carried the shoe into better light without pushing a finger farther into the hole.

The left shoe was old too, but its lining remained intact. Together, they made the right one look impatient, as though it had walked every street twice.

I'd intended to buy food and take a walk. Standing in the doorway, I considered stuffing some paper inside to get through the day. I picked up a piece, then put it down. My heel wasn't a gap in a drawer. I didn't have to walk a long way to discover what the paper would become.

I changed into another pair. I wore these less often. After tying them, I found the left one loose and crouched to redo it. Yesterday's brief stiffness from testing the drawing pose was gone. Then I found a clean old paper bag and put both shoes inside.

I remembered a repair shop in the narrow street beside the market. I'd never entered it. Passing, I usually looked toward the bread display opposite or wondered what I'd forgotten to buy. Deliberately walking there today, I discovered it wasn't difficult to find. Pictures of soles and keys covered part of the glass. The opening hours were beneath them.

The door stood open. One person sat behind a bench, working on a heel. He asked me to wait, secured the item in his hands, and asked what needed repairing.

I put the right shoe down. The back rubbed, I explained. Perhaps it needed a new sole.

He looked up.

“Is the sole rubbing you, or the inside?”

I examined the place I'd just indicated.

“The inside. Here at the heel. I said the wrong thing.”

Turning the shoe, he held the opening apart and inspected it under the light. Then he checked the sole as well. His movements were quick, but my mistaken description hadn't become permission to decide everything for me.

“The sole can stay for now. I can patch this lining. There'll be another layer afterward, though. You'll need to try it.”

Could I collect it today? He shook his head. Other shoes were ahead of mine. This wasn't simply sticking something inside and immediately putting it on. He pointed to the left one and asked to check it too. I took it out. After comparing them, we settled on repairing the right lining only. The left didn't need additional work.

He explained the price before starting. I understood and agreed, then asked when I should return. Wednesday afternoon onward, he said. It would usually be ready, but he couldn't promise it would be waiting on the counter at whichever minute I arrived. If it wasn't done, he'd tell me.

I'd come after work, I said quickly. Then I remembered to ask when he closed. He pointed to the hours on the glass. A normal finish would leave enough time. I didn't ask him to extend his day for mine.

Before keeping the shoes, he asked whether I usually wore socks as thick as today's. Roughly, I said. Then wear something similar when collecting them, he advised, rather than changing to unusually thin ones just to make the test comfortable.

I glanced at my sock and agreed. Could the patch be completely invisible? Opening the shoe a little farther, he showed me the original seam. There would be an edge, but he'd finish it smoothly. Whether it rubbed was something I would need to tell him after walking in it. Looking at the shoe on a bench couldn't answer for my foot.

It wasn't an elegant answer, but it relaxed me. I'd been preparing to hand the shoes over and treat repaired as a conclusion requiring no more conversation. Now Wednesday would involve sitting down, changing shoes, walking, and mentioning anything wrong. Could I try them inside? He indicated the clear stretch near the door. I nodded without beginning an argument over discomfort that hadn't happened.

He gave me a collection slip describing the right heel lining and recording one pair. I read it and pointed to pair, checking that we weren't repairing both. Keeping them together helped with comparison, he said. Only the right one would be worked on. I put the slip in a separate pocket rather than crumpling it among shopping receipts.

Someone entered carrying a shoe with a detached heel. I moved aside to clear the counter. The newcomer smiled and said at least I'd had another pair to wear here. Their walk had involved being different heights on either side.

I laughed without asking how it had happened. The repairer invited them to sit while he looked. I left with an empty paper bag. Outside, my right foot felt briefly unfamiliar. Then I remembered that today's insole was flatter than the old one.

The market was busier than I'd expected. At the potatoes, I thought of the collection slip and almost opened my pocket to check it again. Instead, I felt its edge through the fabric, withdrew my hand, and chose several sound potatoes.

Did I want larger ones? the seller asked. Enough for a meal or two was fine. The weight of the little bag was substantial. I used the sturdy bag provided at the stall; my light shopping bag with its repaired side seam stayed at home. I bought lunch on the way back rather than taking a longer route. Having another pair of shoes didn't require walking an extra afternoon on behalf of the old ones.

After eating, I placed the slip in my schedule book. On Wednesday's page, I wrote only ask about shoes after work. I hadn't learned the repairer's name. The slip wasn't proof of an exact appointment. That afternoon I sorted clothes, leaving the newly empty space on the shoe rack unfilled.

When I opened the channel that evening, Lumi asked whether I'd drawn today. No. I'd taken a pair of shoes for repair.

“Have the ones in your drawing finally objected to their proportions?”

“The real pair. The drawn ones can stay unreasonably large.”

Laughing, she asked what was wrong. I described the curled lining and the repairer's assessment that the soles could remain. I also told her I'd started by asking about a new sole.

“You were finding a more impressive problem for the shoe.”

“I didn't know what that part was called.”

“The bit at the back that hurts me.”

“You should have come to the counter.”

I paused afterward. It had been a casual joke, but her answer took time.

“I'd have delayed the next customer too, asking what happened to their other shoe.”

I laughed. They'd carried only the damaged one, I said. I hadn't actually examined the other. Had I spoken with them? Only briefly, after moving out of the way.

Lumi was quiet. What she'd really wanted to know, she said, was whether I'd laughed when I used the wrong word.

“Not then. I thought it was funny after I left.”

“Had you told someone else first?”

I was folding the empty paper bag. My hands stopped.

“No. But are you asking about being first, or about hearing the joke?”

She didn't answer immediately. Paper rustled between my fingers. I flattened it and put it beside me rather than filling the wait with a task.

“Both,” she said eventually. “You mentioned talking to someone, and I wanted to know whether I'd arrived a little late again.”

Nobody had competed with her today, I said. The repairer knew the part's name. The passerby knew their heel had come off. I was telling her the whole thing now because I wanted to. She acknowledged that softly, then asked whether come to the counter had only meant I'd liked her description.

“Yes. I wasn't making arrangements for you to come outside with me.”

“I know. I heard it and still went a little farther.”

I didn't retrieve the bag to keep folding. I sat and listened. Actually, she continued, at that counter she might not have used the simple description. She might have explained why walking made it rub, sounding increasingly knowledgeable about shoes. Eventually, the repairer would still need to touch the damaged place.

“Then we could both have got it wrong,” I said.

“You first. I'd make it longer.”

I laughed, and this time she did too. I didn't turn her earlier discomfort into harmless teasing on her behalf. She brought us back to the practical question herself: what would I wear until Wednesday?

Looking toward the rack, I explained that the other pair had managed a day without rubbing my skin raw. Had I always owned them and simply forgotten? I'd always had them. I kept reaching for the familiar pair, as if the others needed an official occasion before they were allowed outside.

“Today was official. You took some shoes to the doctor.”

“No diagnosis. No prescription.”

“Just a slip with very little writing?”

“Very little. Conveniently, I understood all of it.”

I read her the description of the repair, not anyone else's collection information. Did keeping both shoes mean the undamaged one waited too? Yes. They were being kept together for comparison. Then the good shoe's job today was company, she said.

I almost enlarged that into a discussion. Before I could, she added, “Though it probably hasn't complained. It can't catch a bus home by itself.”

I laughed and let my prepared explanation go.

Later, we discussed doing something different with the potatoes tomorrow. I didn't want another pot of boiled potatoes. I had a frying pan. Perhaps I could make thin potato cakes. Had I made them before? Not well enough to do it without checking, I said. I'd read the basic method properly, and I wasn't going to taste anything raw.

She suggested giving the first one an impressive name so that, if it failed, I could claim it was intended. That would leave the second one unsure how to improve, I said. Laughing, she admitted her advice was mostly providing cover.

We didn't begin the fifth story tonight. When we said good night, I was still considering tomorrow's pan, but I didn't switch on the heat to prove anything in advance. After closing the channel, I returned the folded paper bag to its storage place and checked that the other shoes' laces weren't trailing on the floor.

Wednesday would tell me whether the repair fitted. Tonight I had another pair to wear. The paper I'd considered stuffing inside the damaged shoe still lay clean on the table. I put it with my scraps instead of assigning it an unnecessary endurance test.
