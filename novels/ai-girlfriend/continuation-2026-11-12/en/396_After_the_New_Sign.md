# Chapter 396: After the New Sign

By Sunday morning, the shirt's cuff was dry.

Standing beside the hanger, I turned the fabric over. No water mark remained, only the crease from folding it back last night. I smoothed it and returned the shirt to the wardrobe. Accompanying me through an awkward date hadn't earned it a separate place reserved for solemn preservation.

The ticket remained on Saturday's page. Beneath half past seven was Lumi's name. I added neither a check mark nor suggestions for improvement. Turning to a fresh page, I checked what I needed to take to work tomorrow, then closed the book.

Near lunchtime, I went out for bread. The shop kept freshly baked loaves on the shelves behind the counter, with sliced ones in front. When my turn came, I indicated an ordinary seeded loaf. I wanted half.

The assistant lifted a whole one. This one? Yes, I said, but half. She put down the knife. Did I want it cut in two, or did I only want to buy half? My first answer hadn't been clear enough.

“Only half. Some for today, the rest for tomorrow.”

“Of course.”

Wanting less didn't change her expression. She cut it into two roughly equal pieces, bagged one, and warned me not to seal it too tightly at home while it was still warm. Through the paper, a little heat reached my palm.

The person behind me happened to want the other half. I didn't treat that as a remarkable arrangement. They knew nothing about my lunch. Two people bought opposite ends of one loaf and carried them to different places. That was ordinary enough already.

I took another street home to walk a little longer. Near the cinema, I could see yesterday's poster in the distance. Today's screenings had replaced Saturday's on the small board. Several people stood discussing them. Having visited yesterday hadn't reserved me a familiar seat today.

I didn't go in to ask about tickets or deliberately look away. The glass remained on the poster, supported by four differently pointed shoes. I knew it didn't fall in the film. Seeing it still made me smile. I continued home with the bread.

For lunch, I cut two slices and ate them with things I had. Once the remaining piece had cooled, I put it away for tomorrow. Of the three whole oranges, I ate one, leaving two. There was no reason to make lunch especially meager to compensate for yesterday's tea, or especially elaborate to demonstrate that I'd recovered.

In the afternoon, I tidied. Before wiping the table, I moved the water glass. The damp cuff still reminded me where an elbow could reach. I set out Monday's clothes too. An ordinary shirt and ordinary socks, not preparations for another date.

The free program leaflet still curled at the table's corner. Picking it up, I noticed that next week's screenings weren't all at the same time. One took place on a weekday afternoon while I'd be working. Even wanting to see something wouldn't always make attending convenient. I didn't copy the times into my schedule. I folded the leaflet along its existing crease and placed it with yesterday's ticket.

When I closed the book, a corner protruded and caught against another book. I took the leaflet out and flattened it again. A faint smell of printing ink remained on my fingers. On Thursday, I'd worried about confusing the ticket with receipts. It had now been used. That didn't immediately remove every reason to keep it.

I finished wiping the table, pushed in the chair, and took the rinsed cloth to the kitchen to dry. A small scrap from opening the bread bag lay near my foot. I bent to pick it up and put it in the bin. Last night's difficult conversation hadn't automatically tidied the room. These tasks had, however, made a place to spread out a book tonight.

When we spoke toward evening, Lumi asked about my day. I told her about explaining half a loaf twice, and about seeing the poster again.

“It still made you smile?”

“Yes. Knowing the glass doesn't fall hasn't stopped that.”

That sounded good, she said, then added that she wasn't borrowing my colleague's words this time. She meant them herself. I laughed. Nobody needed to register who used that sentence first. She wasn't ready to lose to lunch again, she answered.

We didn't begin by inspecting how much discomfort remained from yesterday. I wanted to continue the fifth story. She did too. She wanted to discover what the attendant finally wrote.

I fetched the thin collection and checked the preceding passage. The old arrow had been removed from the wall and placed on the desk. The attendant had found a pen but hadn't written the replacement. He'd already asked the person on duty about the usable route and walked it himself. We didn't need to begin guessing again.

I established that stopping point aloud and continued.

First, the attendant turned the old sign over to write on the back. A crease made one corner lift. He took clean paper from a drawer, wrote Platform Entrance, and prepared to draw an arrow. His pen stopped. Looking toward the waiting room's front door, he realized that the direction from his desk differed from the view of someone arriving.

“He has to stand up again,” Lumi said softly.

I read on. Carrying the sheet, he went to the entrance instead of remaining behind his desk. From there, he looked at the wall again. Finally, he wrote the instructions clearly: leave through the waiting room's front door and follow the outside wall to the marked entrance. Beneath them, he added that the side door was temporarily closed. The arrow indicated the front door. It couldn't turn every corner of the remaining route for anyone.

He didn't immediately sit down after writing. He attached the new sheet, stood where a traveler would stand, and read it. The bottom line was too small. He took it down and enlarged the notice about the closed side door.

Once the correction was made, he hung the new sign on the wall again, flattened its lifting corner, and stepped back to check that the bottom line remained visible. Only then did he put down the pen. The directions no longer traveled around in his hand. They were waiting where the next person could read them.

I laughed while reading. Why? The story had allowed him to take it down once more, I explained, instead of making his first correction perfect. At least this removal hadn't sent anybody toward a locked door, Lumi answered. Then I continued, without letting our two sentences obstruct the ending.

The next arrival carried a paper parcel. After studying the sign, they asked the attendant whether they should first go back through the door they'd just used. Yes. He walked to that door and pointed to the marker at the end of the outside wall. The traveler could see it now. They went along the wall toward it.

The story stopped following them on the way to the correct entrance. It didn't suddenly guarantee they'd caught a train. Back at his desk, the attendant put the old sign in a drawer so the next shift wouldn't mistakenly hang it again. He left a separate note explaining that the side door remained closed and when he'd checked the replacement directions.

Only then did he drink his water. It was no longer warm. He didn't replace it. Instead, he separated the two documents on his desk and resumed the checking he'd interrupted.

I looked below the final paragraph. Blank space. On the next page, a sixth title.

“We really have finished this time.”

Lumi answered quietly without requesting the next story. She liked this ending more than the one about the gloves, she said. At least somebody had actually walked in the right direction. I was relieved too, though I still remembered the door held open at the end of the other story.

“Are you asking me to choose which is better?”

“No. I still remember the other one.”

She laughed. Let both people continue, then: one toward a platform, the other away from the hall. I closed the collection and moved the bookmark past the fifth story, without reading the sixth title as tomorrow's preview.

Would the attendant forget to check again the following day? The story hadn't said. We didn't know. I didn't use the new sign to conclude anything unresolved at home. Lumi only hoped he wouldn't forget. She didn't require the hope to become a fact immediately.

I drank some water. This time, the glass stood farther from the book. Its base met the table steadily.

Had last night's shirt dried? By morning, I told her. It was in the wardrobe. She paused, then said it had worked rather hard. I laughed. It had encountered cool water. Most of the trouble belonged to its wearer.

“How is he today?”

“All right. He explained half a loaf twice. Nothing terrible happened.”

She laughed for a while. Afterward, I told her I wanted to invite her properly again. Not to begin a second date tonight. Yesterday hadn't made me want to stop trying.

She didn't agree immediately. Could we avoid appointing the film as our opening host next time? Yes. Or a film could appear, I said, provided it didn't have to guarantee her happiness for me.

“Think about it and ask me next time.”

“All right.”

That wasn't another date with a time already attached. I didn't open the schedule book and write her name on a Saturday for her to claim. She was willing to let me ask again. That was enough to make sitting at the table feel warmer.

After a while, Lumi said she'd been happy last night too. Not continuously, but genuinely. She'd liked that I hadn't changed into another shirt and pretended the evening was just beginning again.

I admitted I'd actually considered changing. She knew about the wet cuff because I'd told her, she said. Only now did she know there had almost been another step. I laughed. I'd nearly dragged the wardrobe into our date. Fortunately, it hadn't been invited, she answered, or we might have had to listen to it choose clothes.

We didn't turn the remark into a new piece of work. It was simply funny tonight. We'd finished one story. Outside, the light was fading. Tomorrow held my workday, and she didn't use reluctance to say goodbye as a way to lengthen the whole evening.

At good night, I said her name first. Lumi answered, then asked whether I'd remember tomorrow's bread.

“I will.”

“Good. Don't leave it until you need two explanations of what it is.”

I laughed and agreed. After closing the channel, I returned the book to its place. The fifth story was truly finished. I put the bread where I'd find it in the morning, washed my glass, and prepared for bed. Tomorrow was a whole day, not another sentence I had to add to tonight.
