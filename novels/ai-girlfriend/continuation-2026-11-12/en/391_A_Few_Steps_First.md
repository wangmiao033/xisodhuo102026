# Chapter 391: A Few Steps First

After work on Wednesday, only scattered drops were falling when I reached the repair shop.

Before leaving home, I'd checked my schedule book and put the collection slip in Saturday's separate pocket. My socks were similar in thickness. I still wore the other shoes. Work hadn't unexpectedly delayed me. Walking beside the market, I could see the opening hours clearly on the glass.

Someone inside was collecting keys. I waited near the doorway without blocking it, then handed over my slip. The repairer checked the number, took my shoes from a shelf, and placed them on the counter.

A smooth patch now lined the right heel, a slightly different color from the original material. I touched its edge. Saturday's curled hard lip was gone. He showed me the repaired area and confirmed that the left shoe hadn't been altered. I could try them.

Sitting on the stool near the door, I untied the shoes I was wearing. The paper bag stayed beside me, with nothing obstructing the passage. Sliding my right foot in, I first noticed that the shoe felt tighter than I remembered. I said so.

Where? I nearly answered vaguely, the back. Instead I explained what I could feel: my heel against the new lining, not pressure at the sides. Tie the laces as usual first, he suggested, then walk. Don't decide from the first sensation while sitting.

I obeyed and stood. The clear floor space allowed only a few steps before turning. Of course the new lining touched my heel more than the old hole. The shoes felt different, but there wasn't a hard edge scraping against my sock.

“Now?” he asked.

“Close, but it doesn't hurt.”

“Try again. Tell me if something presses.”

I didn't promptly declare everything fine to be agreeable. On the second walk, my foot moved forward a little. The laces needed adjusting. I sat, retied the right shoe, and walked again. This time I could establish that my heel wasn't continually sliding, and I couldn't find a rubbing point.

Sitting again, I lifted my right foot slightly to check whether the opening had developed an obvious crease. The man at the counter didn't hurry me toward payment. He attended to something else within reach. I didn't need his eyes on every movement for the test to count as careful.

The customer near the door moved inward, and I drew the paper bag closer to my feet. It wasn't in the way, they said. They wanted to read the key-cutting prices on the wall. The person I'd imagined waiting for me wasn't waiting for the stool at all. I acknowledged that without apologizing again for a delay that hadn't existed.

After testing, I asked whether I needed to apply anything at home. Not now, the repairer said. The job concerned the lining. The presence of leather-care products on his counter didn't mean I should put some inside as well. He indicated the repaired area again and advised keeping it clean and letting it dry normally. I listened without pursuing a product that might suddenly make old shoes new.

The differently colored patch hadn't become the original material. Once my foot was inside, it wasn't visible from outside either. This was more practical than my Saturday wish for an entirely invisible repair. I could know it had been mended and continue wearing it.

He didn't guarantee that it would never rub again. Wear them normally, he said, and bring them back if there was discomfort. I understood. I paid the agreed amount and returned the collection slip. He marked his record without charging the unchanged left shoe as another repair.

I put the pair I'd removed into the old paper bag I'd brought. They'd carried me here; the repaired ones would carry me home. Saturday's reversed arrangement had finally happened. Lifting the bag, I checked beneath the stool to make sure both were inside rather than leaving one for the next customer.

Outside, more drops were falling, though no puddles had formed. Under the awning, I opened the umbrella I'd brought, let someone entering pass, and headed toward the market. I kept the paper bag sheltered without staring continually at my feet. The pavement wasn't the shop floor. I wanted to walk normally rather than deliberately step on every sort of paving to inspect the repair.

People were queueing at the bread shop on the corner. I'd intended to stop, but the line reached outside. Instead I bought what I needed for dinner nearby. Collecting shoes didn't require another celebratory activity. Rain on the umbrella sounded busier than the actual weather.

At one doorway, someone was bringing empty crates inside and couldn't pull the door open. I stopped and held it briefly with my free hand. They pushed the crates through and thanked me. I let go and continued. Similarity to the visitor holding a door in the fourth story we'd finished earlier didn't require giving this stranger a story.

The right shoe caused no obvious discomfort on the way. I didn't extend the walk to prove it could endure more. At home, I put the wet umbrella safely away and left the shoes to air. The other pair came out of the bag and returned to the rack. Only one corner of the paper bag was damp. I opened it to dry rather than fold it into storage.

My sock hadn't worn through; my heel had no new abrasion. After washing my hands, I prepared dinner. Some raw potatoes remained. I cooked the amount I needed with other food rather than make more cakes or turn the meal into a retest of Sunday.

After dinner, I turned the paper bag so its damp corner faced outward. Nothing on the table urgently needed doing now. Then I opened the channel and told Lumi I'd collected the shoes and walked home through light rain.

“Did you try them?”

“In the shop. Then on the walk home.”

Did they hurt? Not so far. The lining fitted closer. Sitting, I'd called that tightness; tying the shoe properly and moving had helped distinguish an unfamiliar sensation from the damaged edge returning. I told her about retying the lace too.

“Did you say they were fine first, to reassure him?”

“No. I said tight. He asked where.”

She laughed. A useful first sentence, then. Did the repairer remember me? He'd used the slip to find the shoes, I said. He hadn't said whether he remembered. She'd thought my request about soles might make me memorable. Laughing, I suggested that an ordinary customer using the wrong word probably hadn't ranked among his week's great events.

Lumi laughed too. Had the rain damaged the newly repaired shoes? It had been light, I said. I'd avoided standing water and left them to air. I couldn't establish waterproofing tonight. She'd meant whether I'd been unpleasantly wet, she replied. I realized I'd described the shoes in detail and omitted their wearer.

“My trouser cuffs were damp. I changed. I wasn't shivering.”

“Easier to understand than the report about soles.”

Laughing, I admitted I'd been reporting the repair. Had anything else happened on the walk? I'd held a door, bought dinner. Nothing special.

“You can tell ordinary things too.”

I described the crates and doorway. If I'd held an umbrella and shoes, how had I freed a hand? she asked. I paused, looking at my now-empty hands, and realized I'd omitted a movement.

“I put the bag handles over the wrist holding the umbrella for a moment. I stood securely before holding the door.”

“Oh. I thought perhaps more than the shoes had been repaired.”

“I'd grown a third hand?”

“Then tomorrow will be busy. You'll need another sleeve.”

We laughed. I didn't add that the pressure mark on my wrist had vanished, assembling every detail into evidence of an event. Her question came from listening closely and finding that two hands couldn't accommodate my simplified description.

Later, she asked whether collecting the shoes was satisfying. A little, I said after considering. Less dramatic than I'd imagined on Saturday. The object was repaired; I paid and walked home. The pleasant part was no longer avoiding the hard edge with my heel, rather than finally checking off something unfinished.

“You sounded happy when you opened the channel.”

“Yes.”

Was that about the shoes, or about being able to tell her?

I didn't choose immediately. In another tone, the question could easily have become a contest for first place. Tonight her voice was light, and she didn't hurry me. Looking at the opened paper bag, I remembered the rainy walk. I'd thought about telling her how the repairer had asked me to take another turn.

“Both. And I want to hear you now, not because I've still got a whole day's material to deliver.”

She grew quiet. Eventually, she said she liked that sentence very much. I didn't make her shrink the feeling into a joke or enlarge it further myself. Leaning back, I asked what she wanted to tell me.

She'd kept thinking about a few steps first. Not as something to insert into the doorbell manuscript or supply with a grand meaning. Simply hearing that I'd been asked to stand and walk, and had done so, gave her something specific she could follow.

“My foot was best qualified to make the final decision today.”

Laughing, she said that sounded like an application for a job on its behalf. The job had always belonged to it, I answered. Sometimes I forgot to ask.

She didn't claim to know how wearing shoes felt. What she asked was whether, during the second walk, I'd already wanted to leave. A little. Another customer was waiting, and I worried about occupying space. But they hadn't hurried me. The repairer had explicitly asked me to try. I didn't need to become impatient on their behalf.

Lumi had nearly hurried me toward the book too, she said, wanting to know what the fifth story's notice would say. Did she still want to read? Yes, but tonight she preferred finishing this conversation. I agreed without fetching the collection to keep a reading schedule looking uninterrupted.

We discussed whether the rain had stopped. I stood and checked from the window. The streetlight didn't reveal fine drops clearly. I could only say it was less apparent than earlier. Returning, I offered that uncertain answer. No need to close the weather's case formally, she said.

After good night, I closed the channel and checked the shoes and bag. Its corner remained damp; it could wait until morning. Both pairs were home. My right heel didn't hurt. The fifth story remained where the attendant had found a pen but hadn't rewritten the notice.

There was no new drawing or story ending tonight. I straightened the shoes for morning and walked back to my room. Those few steps weren't a test. I was simply going to bed.
