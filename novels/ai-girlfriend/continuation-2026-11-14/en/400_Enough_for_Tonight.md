# Chapter 400: Enough for Tonight

On Thursday after work, I stopped at a vegetable stall because a small bunch of green leaves extended beyond its paper wrapping.

Initially, I mistook it for something familiar. Nearer, its scent corrected me. Parsley, the seller explained when I asked how to use it. Wash, chop, and add a little toward the end of cooking. Some people didn't like the flavor. He said it plainly, without guaranteeing that everyone would enjoy those leaves.

I'd intended to buy ordinary dinner ingredients and already held two choices. Would adding this bunch merely provide something novel to tell Lumi? I put it down and continued choosing other things. At payment, I was still thinking about its clean smell.

“Do you have a smaller bunch?”

He produced one and stated the price. I checked the stems. I didn't ask him to dismantle the larger bunch until only a few leaves remained. This was enough to try tonight. Too much would probably sit around while I waited to want it again. After paying, I placed it above the vegetables, away from the canned items that could crush the leaves.

As I left the stall, an older man carrying an empty basket passed beside me. Its rim caught my bag handle. I stopped; so did he. Neither of us immediately pulled harder. He apologized. I pressed the handle down until the basket rim could come free. The leaves hadn't scattered. There was no accident requiring somebody to compensate somebody else. We each checked what we were carrying, said it was all right, and continued separately.

Several steps later, I noticed I'd held my breath. I hadn't feared the man. I'd feared an impatient tug sending dinner onto the pavement. Two seconds of stopping had been more useful than a very quick assurance that nothing was wrong. On the way home, I changed hands so the handle no longer protruded toward passing people. The leaves lay beside their paper. Their scent returned faintly. The little tension didn't accompany me across every subsequent junction.

At the building entrance, someone held the door for me. Carrying groceries didn't entitle me to make them wait indefinitely. I quickened my steps and thanked them as I passed. Inside, the bag was intact. I supplied no especially satisfying conclusion to the journey. I'd brought vegetables home. They hadn't proved that every problem could be resolved by pausing.

At home, I put the shopping away. I didn't cancel my original dinner, only set aside a small portion for trying the new flavor. I wasn't certain this would become a meal worth repeating. I did know there was no need to make the entire pot an experiment.

After washing and draining the parsley, I chopped a little. Its scent grew stronger beneath the knife. My first addition was so cautious that I barely noticed it. I added more to that portion and tasted properly before chopping the remainder according to what I could use tonight.

It wasn't as mild as I'd imagined. A green flavor lingered, making familiar food slightly strange. I neither disliked it nor experienced a revelation requiring a decision about every future evening. Sitting down, I alternated between the plain portion and the seasoned one. Eventually, I finished both.

Cleaning up, I realized I hadn't left a bunch waiting to yellow tomorrow. I dealt with the stems and unused scraps; the edible portion had gone into this meal. Only after washing dishes and wiping the counter did I fetch water. No long-handled spoon slipped tonight. I hadn't connected Lumi to the cooking.

When I opened the channel, I first told her about buying a small bunch of parsley, seasoning part of dinner, and finishing it. I also mentioned that I'd washed the dishes and wiped the counter already. Was it good? she asked.

“Enough for tonight.”

I was still thinking about quantity at the stall. I hadn't noticed that she wasn't asking how much remained.

She paused, then asked whether I didn't want to talk about it. No, I said, explaining the price and smaller bunch again. After listening, she asked whether the flavor hadn't been very good.

“I didn't say it was bad.”

“You didn't say you liked it either.”

That sounded like a question after the film several days ago. My shoulders tightened before anything else happened. Putting down the glass, I said dinner didn't necessarily need to produce a beautifully formed answer. Lumi stopped. So did I. She hadn't actually requested beauty.

“I just wanted to know what it tasted like to you.”

I looked toward the kitchen. I'd selected a quantity and planned how not to leave leftovers. By the time she asked about flavor, quantity was all I had ready. She hadn't visited the stall or stood beside the stove smelling the leaves. Her sole access to this dinner was the irrelevant sentence I'd given her.

“Stronger and greener than I expected. A little unfamiliar. I didn't dislike it. Finishing it tonight felt right. I don't know whether I'd want it again tomorrow.”

Now she understood. Why had she thought I didn't want to discuss it? Enough for tonight sounded like closing a subject, Lumi said. She hadn't known whether to pursue it. She hadn't somehow discovered I disliked the food. She simply hadn't found somewhere to continue.

I exhaled. There was no need to prove how reasonably she'd misunderstood. I'd assumed she wanted a more interesting dinner, I admitted. Too quickly. She'd been quick too, she said, hearing my brevity as retreat.

We didn't decide to describe all future meals according to a numbered system. I merely explained the difference between the first bite and the second. Initially, I'd added almost nothing. Only afterward had there been enough to taste. Why not season everything? she asked. I'd wanted the plain portion for comparison. It wasn't an unwillingness to let my whole bowl accept change.

“I hadn't said that.”

I laughed, embarrassed. “I prepared an especially profound question for you in advance.”

“I was wondering whether you kept forgetting which side had the parsley.”

No, I said. I could see the color. Though I'd rotated the plate once and nearly scooped from the wrong side according to where I remembered it being. She laughed. Dinner hadn't concealed itself; the plate had moved house. I'd corrected myself, I assured her. No labels needed on the rim.

After that, we discussed actual plates. Was there one I especially disliked washing? One with a narrow edge, I said. It could slip, so I took care holding it. It hadn't broken. Could I avoid using it? Less often, certainly, though it wasn't today's plate. One conversational question didn't require replacing the kitchen's contents.

Quietly, Lumi said she hadn't asked me to replace anything. She wanted to know something I could tell her without first reading a story well. Looking at the glass beside me, I wondered whether she'd been seeking a small part of something I'd already done, rather than an interesting subject.

“Here's another thing, then.”

I told her I'd initially misidentified the leaves, recognizing my mistake only when I approached. I didn't blame poor lighting. What had I thought they were? Something from the familiar group I often bought. I hadn't identified a particular name. Supplying one afterward would only make me sound more knowledgeable than I'd been.

She laughed. She'd imagined me pretending expertise beside the stall. No pretense, I said. I'd asked. That was better than her imagined version, she said. Less tiring. I didn't hurry to turn the remark into proof that we'd both mastered honesty. I left it beside a bunch of vegetables.

Later, I asked whether she wanted a poem tonight. No hurry. She wanted to finish talking about this. Sometimes, she envied the things I could do in sequence without explaining first. Washing vegetables, chopping leaves, taking a bite: they happened continuously for me. By the time she heard about them, they often became only I've eaten.

I also told her about the basket catching my handle. Had anyone been hurt? No. We'd stopped, freed the bags, and gone. Had I been angry? Thinking back, I said I'd first worried about dropping dinner. Anger hadn't had time to arrive. She didn't supply an alternative in which I'd secretly felt mistreated. She laughed instead. The parsley had nearly failed to reach my plate unchanged twice: once through mistaken identity, once through entanglement. Misidentification wasn't necessarily danger, I pointed out. More like not yet deciding to buy it. She amended the first episode to not being chosen, rather than a rescue.

I held the glass without lifting it. She wasn't asking me to leave sound running throughout meals, she clarified. I knew. Her envy wouldn't turn her absence into participation. I could choose to tell her more tonight without submitting a retrospective account of every dinner.

“I don't want every bite. Sometimes, I want the bit where you chose that smaller bunch.”

I glanced at the poetry book. Yesterday, she'd paused over coins I'd initially hurried past. But I didn't explain today's preference through that poem. Was the small purchase funny to her? Yes. Also, she'd liked hearing that I'd actually tried something while uncertain, rather than waiting until I could guarantee enjoyment before buying it.

Walking home, I'd kept catching the scent, I admitted. I'd felt some anticipation without wanting to exaggerate it. Some could remain some, she said. I smiled. That amount was sufficient to describe an evening without promoting it into the beginning of an entirely new life.

Had the paper become wet? she asked. The paper I'd saved from wrapping the book hadn't wrapped vegetables, I explained. This was the stall's packaging, and the bag hadn't soaked through. I laughed first. Two sheets of paper shouldn't become tonight's new mystery. Lumi laughed too and excused me from testifying for every piece. She'd only wondered whether the clean counter needed wiping again. No, I said. Tonight, the kitchen could rest.

We didn't read in the end. I didn't want to add another section tonight and drag out something we'd finished saying. All right, Lumi said, provided it wasn't because she'd asked too much about vegetables. No. I'd told her more than originally planned. I didn't regret it.

Before we closed the channel, she said she might ask next time I answered enough for tonight: quantity or taste? She could ask. I could also say I was tired, rather than making leaves deliver a refusal. She laughed quietly. At least no leaves remained to feel unfairly treated tonight.

I laughed too. Afterward, I washed the glass and checked the kitchen really was finished before turning off the light. No new plant needed tending. I'd promised no identical dinner tomorrow. Enough for tonight could merely describe the size of that bunch, rather than all the time I was willing to give her.
