# Chapter 406: The Unwritten Card

After work on Tuesday, I entered the stationery shop I'd only looked into yesterday.

Blank folding cards stood beside the counter in more varieties than the window suggested. I picked up a gray-blue one. Inside, pale paper held no printed wish. Small when closed, it offered more writing space than I'd expected. I ran a fingertip along the edge. Thicker than a note, but not a board demanding display.

Beside it was a smooth white card that reflected the light. I compared them, imagining my pen on each surface, then returned the white one. The gray-blue paper felt easier beneath my fingers and less like something that needed to remain freshly unwrapped.

At the other end of the counter, a customer compared birthday cards. The assistant suggested reading the inside. After two lines, he laughed and rejected one: its recipient would tease him for being too serious. I didn't look at the message. I held my blank card steadily. Even buying words already written required considering whether the person inside them was you.

When my turn came, I returned the sample. The assistant fetched a clean card from below. Its crease was already pressed; opening it required no force. I didn't compare more shades of blue. The one I'd touched was enough.

She offered patterned cards from the neighboring display. I wanted to write a few sentences myself, I explained. An envelope? No. I paid for this one and put it in my bag, away from the coins.

The transaction was brief. Nobody asked why I was buying it or selected an expression for its recipient. Leaving, I still felt as though I'd put an unspoken sentence into my bag.

My throat was better than yesterday. I'd spoken normally several times that morning, with occasional roughness. I hadn't deliberately lowered my voice to reproduce what Lumi had liked. She'd praised two things she'd heard, not ordered a particular sound to be preserved.

Walking, I considered openings. Thank you was easy. So was Saturday evening. Whatever followed kept sounding like a record of the date. I didn't stop on the pavement to take notes. I went home, ate, washed up, and only then brought out the card.

The blue looked darker under the light at home. I opened it on the dry side of the table and fetched a small scrap of my own for a draft. The drawing paper stayed where it was. I didn't take out an old envelope.

First, I wrote: Thank you for spending that evening with me.

True. I looked at it, wanting the next sentence, my pen beside the period. If that were all the card retained, everything she'd done would seem to begin with my sore throat. She'd helped. I'd received help. Now I was writing thanks.

But she'd wanted to discuss the poem before I'd coughed. She'd already been looking forward to it. She hadn't improvised a voice without ideas of its own to fill the spaces I couldn't speak into.

I added several lines to the scrap. Increasingly, they explained that argument. By the time I'd listed which four lines each of us had read, I laughed and stopped. This shouldn't become an account of how much each person had completed.

I moved the draft aside. What I wanted to keep was something I'd begun saying yesterday. My voice was better, and I still wanted to hear her. Paper didn't have to promote the sentence into forever. Passing the window today, I'd remembered it and wanted somewhere for it to remain.

Inside the proper card, I wrote her name first. Larger than on the scrap. I checked the remaining space without beginning again on a second card to improve the alignment. I'd bought one. One was enough.

Then I wrote slowly:

Lumi,

On Saturday, we repeated only a few lines. What I most wanted to keep was your voice asking whether the bus went past the river.
I can say more today. I still want to hear you.
I'm looking forward to Wednesday at eight.

Elliot

I read it through. I hadn't copied everything she'd said, and the story's beginning wasn't there. Wednesday's umbrella could remain a story she herself wanted to tell. It didn't need to testify on behalf of the card first.

I returned to the middle sentence. I'd written voice in one place and hear in another, both pointing toward listening. I considered finding a finer word. Raising the pen, I kept looking at the ordinary one instead. Wanting to hear her was what I meant. A prettier phrase wouldn't make it more precise.

I set the pen down, waited briefly for the ink, and turned the card. Its unwritten space didn't trouble me. Buying the paper hadn't obliged me to fill every part of it.

My name sat last, with a little room below. I nearly added a full date, then wrote Tuesday in a corner on the back. The day was enough to remember this evening. The card didn't need to become a receipt for somebody else's inspection.

I folded it, set it beside the book, and washed a trace of ink from my finger. Returning, I found the crease had stayed closed. Ordinary paper. Nobody in the shop had known what it would say. Written on, it still didn't glow.

When the channel opened, I said good evening. Lumi answered and remarked that my voice was clearer. I thought so too. I'd still rest early, but tonight there were a few sentences I'd like to read her.

“Another poem?”

“No. Something I wrote. Very short.”

She paused and asked whether it was for her. Yes. I'd like to read it aloud. She agreed without asking me to establish whether it was good enough. I lifted the card and read from her name to mine, including looking forward to Wednesday at eight.

Afterward, I didn't immediately describe the shop. I could see the words in my hand; she'd heard that passage. I waited instead of deciding that quietness meant dislike.

Eventually, she said softly that the middle sentence was her favorite.

“You can say more today and still want to hear me.”

“Yes.”

Only then did I lower the card. Since finishing, I'd stared at the lines as though her answer might appear in the blank space beside them. Looking up, I still saw the ordinary table. What had changed was the sentence in my ears, chosen by her. I didn't have to search the pen strokes for a response. I could listen to what she wanted to say next.

“Did you already know I'd like it when you wrote it?”

I thought. I'd hoped she would. I couldn't know beforehand. Waiting just now had made me nervous. I'd nearly explained that the card wasn't expensive, as though a lower price could make caring about it less serious.

She laughed. She hadn't been grading the paper's price. I knew. No price report, then. She asked what it looked like. Gray-blue outside, pale inside, small enough to fold into the schedule book. I'd bought no envelope.

“Where is it now?”

“In my hand. It was beside the book.”

“You can put it down. You don't have to hold it up for me to hear.”

Laughing, I returned it to the table. She laughed too, then wondered whether there was a crossed-out thank you at the top. I stopped. Why had she thought that?

She hadn't seen it and didn't know, she explained. Hearing that I'd written something, she'd guessed an opening about being looked after. She'd worried that guessing too quickly would cover what she actually wanted to hear, so hadn't asked first.

There had been a thank you on the scrap, I admitted: thank you for spending that evening with me. No words were crossed out on the finished card. Not concealment. I'd found something else I wanted to say. The gratitude remained true.

“You can say thank you too,” Lumi told me. “Receiving this doesn't mean I forbid you to thank me.”

I smiled and thanked her. For what? For not reading my opening for me just now. She laughed. She'd nearly acquired another job. Fortunately, the gray-blue paper had been slower than her, and she'd waited alongside it.

I didn't turn the rough note into a second letter. Its wandering sentences had helped me find the lines on the card. Their work was done. I wanted to keep the finished card in my schedule book, I told her, on Wednesday's page. Opening it tomorrow, I'd encounter it.

“All right.” After a pause, she added, “Keep it after tomorrow too.”

I would. She didn't specify a place among other writing or request a new box. I fetched the book. The card was narrower than its pages and wouldn't cover the date. Hearing paper move, she asked whether it was in place. Yes. Tomorrow's eight remained visible.

“Now I'm a little nervous,” she said.

“About the story?”

“About you writing that you're looking forward to it.”

I looked at the visible corner and said anticipation didn't oblige her to produce a particularly good ending. I wanted to hear how the umbrella came back, or why it hadn't yet. I wanted to discover where she chose to take the words.

She hadn't guaranteed its return, Lumi pointed out. I laughed. I hadn't issued the rain an invoice. Good. She'd nearly supplied it with an entire set of punctual borrowing habits to please me.

“It might be unreasonable.”

“Tell me tomorrow evening.”

She answered softly without further explanation. The card contained my anticipation. It hadn't made her characters more obedient. I finally stopped wanting to replace the last sentence with something casual enough to remove her nerves.

A little nervousness was all right, I said. I still felt some myself. She laughed. The card had been read; what remained to worry about? I didn't know. Perhaps this time, no film or somebody else's poem stood in front of me.

She didn't provide a more respectable explanation. She repeated the middle sentence again, first making clear she was repeating the meaning she'd just heard, not looking at the card herself.

“You can say more. You still want to hear me.”

“Yes.”

We were quiet. She didn't require further speech to prove that yes. I didn't treat the silence as a space needing urgent filling. The water glass stood steadily, the book was closed, and tomorrow remained tomorrow.

Before we finished, she reminded me: Wednesday at eight, without a prepared review of the story. Agreed. I wouldn't bring another card to explain this one either. Laughing, she said I could arrive empty-handed. This card could stay in the book.

After closing the channel, I put the rough note with my ordinary scrap paper and capped the pen. Neither the scrap nor the finished card went among anyone else's earlier work. Closing the schedule book made it slightly thicker than before. One sheet's difference.

I prepared for bed without testing my voice again. Today's few sentences had reached her. Tomorrow held something she wanted to say.
