# Chapter 398: The Page I Bought

On Tuesday after work, I actually took the detour to the secondhand bookshop.

Several large volumes stood in a basket outside. I bent and extracted one, mistaking the blue on its cover for the sea. Inside were old photographs of interiors. The dining tables looked excessively tidy. Even fabric draped over a chair appeared deliberately arranged. I looked at two pages, then put it back. Picking something up didn't require me to discover a need for it.

The shop was narrower beyond the entrance. Someone behind the counter was applying price labels; another customer searched the shelves farther inside. I moved aside to leave room for the book he opened. Asked what I wanted, I said short poems. Nothing very thick. Something that could be read aloud.

I felt faintly embarrassed afterward, as though I'd decided at the door that certain books prohibited speaking their words.

The bookseller indicated the lower shelves against the wall. Anthologies and individual collections were both there. I could browse before choosing. He didn't first ask how much I understood, so I didn't hurry to explain how little.

The first book had dense printing and a previous reader's tiny annotations. While I read, my eyes kept straying toward the question marks in the margin. They weren't my questions, but they seemed to want immediate answers. I closed it and chose a thinner volume.

Its cover had faded through handling. The type was larger, with blank space between poems. No dedication occupied the first page, only a pale square where an old ownership label had been removed. In the contents, I noticed The Apron, then The Last Bus. They weren't far apart. Neither title told me how moved I ought to be.

While I read The Last Bus, somebody at the counter asked a price. I didn't catch the amount, only lost my place. Returning to the page, I found the line I'd just read waiting above several more short ones. I began again. My lips moved without making enough sound to disturb anyone.

Someone in the poem stood beside the last bus, change warm in their hand. The driver waited. Light brightened the empty seats and left them dim again. At the end, the person merely asked whether the bus went past the river. The poem didn't say whether they boarded or walked away.

I liked that question more than the earlier lighting. Not because I intended to take a bus to a river. The person on the page had finally asked something specific they wanted to know. I tried reading the line less hesitantly in my head. Then I tried again. Too much certainty made all the preceding waiting seem unnecessary.

The customer by the shelves closed his book and approached the counter. I drew mine closer to let him pass. After thanking me, he pointed at the cover and asked whether it was by a particular poet. There was indeed a name on the cover. I didn't recognize it. I said it was the person printed there; this was my first time reading them.

He smiled. Wrong book. He was looking for another one. Then he went away. I hadn't pretended familiarity to extend a brief exchange. Nor did I immediately seek a biography that would qualify me to buy the book.

I turned to The Apron. Someone cooking dinner took off their apron in a doorway. A forgotten teaspoon occupied the pocket and fell when the ties loosened. Dinner has gone ahead of me made me smile. A moment later, I realized I was enjoying words, not imagining a meal actually walking across the table.

Both poems were short. Still, I didn't want to inspect the whole collection in the shop, as though enough browsing could eliminate the possibility of spending money on the wrong thing. At the counter, I checked whether the marked price was the one I should pay. The bookseller nodded and examined the spine. Old, he said, but no missing pages.

I checked the numbering and binding myself. A section in the middle was loose and needed supporting when opened. No pages had detached. I mentioned it. He showed me where the binding had loosened and explained that the price already reflected its condition. I could choose another if it bothered me.

I closed it gently and said I'd take this one. I didn't require him to promise that every opening of an old book would feel new.

While he counted my change, I set the book on the counter. The loose section lifted slightly. I supported it without forcing it flat. When he handed over the money, I put it away first, then used both hands to pick up the book. Hurrying to clear the counter would only pull harder at the binding. The customer behind me hadn't asked me to hurry. Yet I was already preparing to do three things at once. Beyond this door, the book could be something I turned through slowly. It didn't need to become evidence that I knew how to make an elegant purchase.

After I paid, he offered a bag. I already had my bag for light things. Instead, I wrapped the book in a clean sheet of thin paper he provided, then placed it clear of the vegetables for dinner. I wasn't packing it beneath oranges. The final orange at home hadn't accompanied me shopping.

Outside, I nearly opened the bag immediately to revisit the page. Reading at the roadside was inconvenient. I stepped out of people's way, checked that the wrapping hadn't touched anything wet, and continued home. Buying the poem didn't oblige me to reread it at every junction.

After dinner, I ate the final orange and washed my hands before fetching the book. The paper unfolded. Its cover remained the same worn color it had been in the shop. Here there were no murmuring customers, nobody waiting for me to move away from a shelf. I read The Last Bus more slowly.

In my head, I supplied an answer to its final question. Then I caught myself doing something familiar. If the poem wouldn't show its traveler boarding, I wanted at least to offer a reassuring direction. Instead, I left it open on that page and fetched water, putting the glass well away.

When I opened the channel, I told Lumi I'd visited the bookshop and bought a small poetry collection. Also, all the oranges were gone. Was the book what I'd been hoping to find yesterday? So far, yes. I'd read two poems carefully. I couldn't vouch for everything afterward.

“Which two?”

I gave her their titles and described almost being frightened away by the annotations in another book. She laughed. Apparently question marks could change owners and continue demanding answers. Exactly, I said. I was already too quick to answer questions without purchasing a row of somebody else's to bring home.

Did the apron get used to wipe a table? she asked. I checked the page. No, though a teaspoon did fall out. I wasn't planning to read both complete poems tonight. First, I'd wanted to tell her what I'd chosen.

“Why that book?”

“There was a line I wanted to read again.”

She didn't immediately ask which. She waited. I explained the question about whether the last bus passed the river. I'd read it twice because my first attempt sounded too much as though the person already knew the answer.

Had it reminded me of somewhere? No particular old incident came to mind. I simply liked being able to pause there. That wasn't a comprehensive answer. It was more honest than hurriedly installing a memory behind my choice.

She wanted to hear how I'd read it tomorrow. Certainly. I could say the line now, too. After considering that, Lumi asked for the whole poem tomorrow instead. She didn't want to steal its last moment early, like getting hold of an answer.

I smiled and closed the book. Was I forbidding a preview? No. I didn't want to browse too far ahead myself. I'd bought it. Nobody would take it back tomorrow morning. For one evening, I could merely know it was on the table.

Later, I admitted wondering at the counter whether I needed a whole collection because I liked two poems. Lumi couldn't calculate the usefulness of its paper for me. But I hadn't demanded a refund for the minutes of the film during which I hadn't laughed.

“They cost different amounts.”

“I didn't say the bills matched. I said you can like part of something.”

I laughed. Yes. Did owning it mean I intended to read every day? I had no schedule. Sometimes I might pick it up to look at lines I hadn't understood yesterday without beginning a fresh performance.

Quietly, Lumi admitted she'd rather liked the idea of being the reason I bought the book. She stopped, then added that she wasn't asking me to change my answer and declare it entirely for her.

Looking at its cover, I said I'd thought about reading to her. That was true. Liking the river question in the shop was also true. I couldn't necessarily divide their shares according to which thought occurred first.

“So I haven't bought up the whole book,” she said.

“You haven't paid yet.”

She laughed, and I joined her. The small admission didn't become another dispute that had to be fully explained tonight. She wanted to hear The Apron, she said. Its title sounded like someone preparing to finish work. I liked the little noise in it, I told her. Nobody suddenly understood their entire life.

We considered whether I needed a sheet of paper to record thoughts tomorrow. Not yet. Nobody had written in this collection. I didn't need to fill its empty space immediately. For a bookmark, I'd use my own clean scrap rather than move the marker after the fifth story in the thin collection. The books could each stop in their own place.

Then the station in the other book could rest tonight, Lumi said. Yes. The sixth story remained unstarted. Putting a new book beside it didn't evict the old one or mean that from now on I only liked poetry.

After good night, I cut a narrow strip from some clean scrap paper I already had. It went into The Last Bus without an evaluation written on it. I folded and saved the thin wrapping separately, smoothing it with my hand, and put the book beyond the water glass's reach.

The oranges were finished. The bread had been finished yesterday morning. Tomorrow's breakfast required another arrangement. I made a note of that useful fact before washing the glass. The new poetry book hadn't prepared food or guaranteed a subject for every evening. At least the page I'd bought contained one sentence I wanted to read again.
