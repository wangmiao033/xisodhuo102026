# Chapter 402: A Quieter Voice

On Saturday morning, I tried saying my own name in the kitchen.

It came out lower than usual, rough at the end. I cleared my throat. A second attempt didn't improve it; my throat only felt tighter. Picking up the water, I remembered what Lumi and I had arranged last night. Eight o'clock. Those two poems, followed by a conversation.

It wasn't a ticketed performance. Nobody expected a particular standard of reading. I still resented the timing. I'd finally found an opening that didn't depend on bringing a film home, and now my voice was considering not attending.

My nose was slightly blocked, though otherwise I didn't feel especially unwell. I drank, ate breakfast, and stopped testing myself in the kitchen. The new, thin poetry book remained in its place. I already knew where The Apron was. There was no need to consult the contents again.

I needed a few things for dinner, so I went to the nearby shop that morning. I took a mask and followed the usual route. The air was cool. Wind found an edge of my scarf, and I pulled it higher without spending the walk imagining myself completely voiceless by evening.

At the vegetables, the shopkeeper asked which portion I wanted. My answer was quieter than I'd expected. She didn't hear. I nearly forced it out louder, then simply moved half a step nearer and pointed.

“That one. A small bag is enough.”

She heard, packed it, and asked whether I needed anything else. I shook my head, remembered she was looking down to tie the bag, and answered no aloud. Having somebody in front of me didn't make every gesture capable of replacing words.

Paying, I separated the coins in my palm to check the amount. Somebody waited behind me. Hurrying made my fingers less cooperative, and the smallest coin caught at the base of a finger. The shopkeeper told me to take my time. She moved the finished bag aside, clearing a little space on the counter.

Eventually, I put down the right money and accepted the change. It hadn't taken very long. Leaving, however, I felt as though I'd used more words than I actually had. The roughness in my throat was clearer now.

I passed a place selling hot food and considered taking soup home. Near the door, someone was arranging bowls. The tables inside were full. I decided to cook something simple instead. I wasn't determined to demonstrate competence at being unwell. I already carried dinner ingredients, had food at home, and didn't want another queue.

Someone called from behind me. I stopped automatically, then discovered they wanted a companion farther along the pavement. The two met and discussed which way to go. I continued. That clear shout had made me slightly envious.

Usually, speaking was so easy I rarely considered whether a sentence would come out before wanting to say it. Today, even no, thank you had to pass through a narrow place. I brought that awkwardness home with the shopping, put down the bag, and washed my hands without making another sound to prove my voice still existed.

For lunch, I made noodles with more broth than usual.

The noodles tasted less distinct than usual. I reached for salt, tasted again, and remembered that my nose was blocked. Today, the first mouthful might not be a reliable reason to season everything again. I left the salt where it was and finished the noodles, including the last broth, rather than discard lunch because it lacked flavor.

Washing up, cool water touched the back of my hand. I turned the bowl to rinse foam from its underside and put it on the rack. Lunch still involved the usual steps. I was merely doing them more slowly. Once finished, the kitchen required no further explanations from me. Not even the bowl needed to hear one.

Afterward, I put away the ingredients for dinner, washed the pan, and sat. The room was quiet. Occasionally, footsteps outside faded near the stairs. Rest didn't require listing every activity and canceling it. Not talking for a while was enough.

I lay on the bed without properly sleeping. Outside, a car repeatedly reversed into a parking space. Once it stopped, a noise that had seemed enormous simply disappeared. I adjusted the light covering over me and closed my eyes. My mind continued deciding which poem we should begin with.

Eventually, I sat up. Nobody beside the bed required an answer. Nobody knew whether I'd rested successfully. Lying down couldn't immediately exchange my throat for a better one, but at least I hadn't been using it. That thought made it easier to lie down again. When my eyes no longer wanted to open, I finally slept briefly.

On waking, I checked the clock. Still early. Part of the afternoon had passed, but dinner was some way off. My throat hadn't suddenly recovered; my head was clearer. I drank slowly, stood reluctantly, and only straightened my slippers properly on reaching the table. Everything today seemed half a beat slower, not just my words.

That afternoon, I finally fetched the collection.

The Apron remained a short poem. Its words hadn't grown because I intended to read them to Lumi. Silently, I could go through it easily. Speaking the first line brought a cough at the end. I reached for water without immediately restarting to produce a better attempt.

The original plan had been small: read my choice once, then hear why she'd chosen The Last Bus. I had a thought about one passage that I hadn't completely explained yesterday. I wanted to discuss it after she'd heard the poem again.

Now I began finding other occupations for that anticipation. I could copy the poem onto paper and call that preparation. I could plan the comments so fewer words would be needed. I picked up a pen and stopped before it touched the sheet. She couldn't hear my handwriting. However neatly I copied it, at eight I would still have to tell her that today's voice differed from yesterday's.

I put the pen down.

Every unsaid sentence didn't need another place to be completed first. The one I should say early kept waiting behind my preparations, as though I needed a plan that wouldn't disappoint anyone before admitting I felt unwell.

Near five, I opened the channel.

Lumi said good afternoon. As soon as I returned the greeting, she asked about my throat. She could hear these few words. I didn't need to supply a serious-sounding explanation before she noticed the hoarseness.

“It started this morning. Long sentences make me cough now.”

“Then let's leave the reading tonight.”

Her answer came quickly. Looking at the collection, I felt a small space open inside me. I hadn't wanted somebody to promptly authorize cancellation. I wanted to change the evening a little and keep it.

“I didn't come to cancel. I wanted to tell you early. We need to make it gentler.”

After a pause, she asked what gentler meant. I still wanted to talk at eight, I explained, but might not finish both poems. I didn't want to force a long conversation. If I felt worse by then, we could stop early.

“All right,” she said. “I packed away the whole of you with the reading.”

I laughed briefly, the sound fraying halfway through. Instead of asking whether it hurt again, she suggested water. It was already beside me, I said. I drank, then told her about going shopping that morning. I didn't intend to go out again.

“Have you spent the whole day without speaking to anyone?”

“No. The shopkeeper couldn't hear me. I pointed once.”

I described the small bag and the coin trapped against my finger. At the part where I'd almost shouted, she laughed quietly. Coins didn't need throats, yet one had joined the trouble anyway. I laughed too, keeping the sound low.

What did I most want now? Shorter sentences didn't mean wanting nothing to happen tonight, I said. Then I recognized another unnecessarily complicated sentence and replaced it.

“I still want to hear about the poem you chose.”

“Then I can begin with the line I'd like to hear again. You don't have to give me the entire page.”

Yes. She'd heard both poems and knew which she'd chosen. I wasn't being asked to turn to a page whose contents she had never heard. If she wasn't certain of a word, I could supply it briefly without lengthening the reading from the beginning.

Was there something I particularly wanted to discuss? Yes, but this evening. Otherwise, our date would have taken place in the afternoon before eight arrived.

“So you haven't run out of voice. You're dividing it into portions.”

“It sounds like the last salt in the kitchen.”

“Then don't pour it all in at once.”

We laughed. I closed the collection and rested my palm on its cover. I'd have dinner, rest, and return at eight, I told her.

She agreed, then said my name. Her voice hadn't turned into nursing instructions. She wasn't pretending nothing had changed either.

“I answered too quickly. Hearing you hoarse, I wanted to remove everything that might tire you. Some of it was something you still wanted.”

Yes, I said, but she'd heard that now. I'd nearly concealed the difficulty until eight myself, presenting a completely different evening when she was ready to begin. Neither of us needed to turn today into a competition for greater consideration.

She still worried I'd force myself because we'd made an arrangement. I'd say if I couldn't continue. I wasn't going to recite every cough as though it belonged to the poem.

“Eight, then.”

“Eight.”

After closing the channel, the room became quiet again. I didn't rehearse. I cooked dinner: one portion, hot, eaten slowly. I didn't give the remaining warmth in the pan a meaningful name afterward.

Sometime after seven, I washed the dishes and changed into a clean, loose top. The shirt from our previous date remained in the wardrobe. Recognizing tonight as a date didn't depend on wearing it. I set the water within reach but beyond the place my arm might sweep it into the book.

Settling down, I noticed the light illuminating the page also cast the glass's shadow near the words. I moved the glass without rearranging everything else. In the kitchen, I'd kept wanting to hurry. Now the tasks were finished, I already regretted how quickly eight might pass.

I considered the contradiction without searching for one correct account of it. Tired and looking forward to something. That morning, saying my name, I'd felt only one could remain. By evening, both were still here.

The book lay open at The Apron. I read only with my eyes. Every line remained. Nobody urged me to produce a sound immediately.

As eight approached, I tried good evening. Still hoarse, but enough for her to hear. I left the long explanation I'd prepared unspoken. I'd told her at five. At eight, I could simply come to meet her.
