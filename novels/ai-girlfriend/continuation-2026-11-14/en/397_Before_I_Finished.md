# Chapter 397: Before I Finished

On Monday morning, I finished the seeded bread left from yesterday.

Cutting the last small piece scattered seeds across the board. The bread was firmer than it had been, but still good. I ate it with breakfast, put away the knife, and left no awkward end inside the bag awaiting a decision. I remembered everything I needed for work. Only downstairs did I discover that today I didn't particularly want to work hard at talking.

That preference achieved little during the morning. Before lunch, several people gathered beside my colleague's desk to establish who would handle the next stage of something and where the materials belonged. I had the section I'd checked. When asked, I explained its location and progress.

“Then the rest can go in together?” someone asked.

“Not yet. The other section—”

“Yes, I know. You said it was checked.”

There was no hostility. They even sounded as though they were helping finish my sentence. I promptly said fine, then heard the missing half underneath it. The other section wasn't mine to check. I didn't know whether it was complete, but my agreement sounded as though I'd confirmed it too.

My colleague was turning to the next topic. Looking at the paper in my hands, I considered an easy solution: explain privately once everyone dispersed. Nobody would have to discover they'd misunderstood in front of other people. I wouldn't have to interrupt a conversation already moving onward.

But if the materials went now, the later explanation would have to pursue an action already taken. I lowered the paper and spoke again.

“Wait. I hadn't finished. Only my section is checked. We need to ask the person responsible about the other one.”

My voice was louder than before. I disliked it first. The person who'd completed my sentence paused, looked at me, and said oh. Who had the other section? I identified them without inventing a completion time.

My colleague recorded the two separately. We'd establish their status before submitting them together. The discussion resumed in less than a minute. Nobody stood up and accused me of spoiling the atmosphere. Yet back at my desk, I still felt tense, as though I'd pushed a very heavy door.

Before we dispersed, one smaller thing needed checking. Both bundles used the same clips. Side by side, it was hard to tell whose was whose. My colleague lifted one and asked whether it was mine. I checked the first page instead of answering from where I remembered putting it. The other bundle had moved. Left and right had reversed since morning.

We added clear status descriptions to the covers, keeping unfinished work away from the checked pile. Should the note include an expected time? the person writing asked. That needed confirmation from whoever was handling it, I said. I couldn't supply one. They lifted the pen and agreed to leave it blank. Several people standing together didn't require someone to guess a number for every empty space.

That helped. Speaking up hadn't been an attempt to control the whole discussion. I didn't need to answer every subsequent question either. Once the bundles were distinguished, I took my pen and returned to my seat.

I stayed inside for lunch. My colleague had brought his own food too. We ate some distance apart. Had somebody finished my sentence incorrectly earlier? he asked suddenly. Yes, I said. I'd even agreed initially.

“You cleared it up afterward. That's what matters.”

He took another mouthful. He wasn't preparing to hear the scene repeated word for word. I ate too. After a while, I realized how slowly I was chewing. I wasn't too angry to eat. I was still adjusting the volume of wait in my head.

That afternoon, the relevant person replied. Two items in the other section remained unchecked. The materials hadn't gone prematurely. My interruption had protected an ordinary step, not achieved a heroic correction. I continued my own work and marked where it should resume, without taking over those remaining items.

After work, someone behind me called excuse me. I stepped aside; they passed carrying a long box. I'd considered detouring to a bookshop. At the junction, I felt tired and continued home. Nothing had gone terribly wrong. The day's sentences were still sounding in my ears.

After dinner, I washed one of the two remaining oranges and peeled it slowly. The peel wouldn't form a continuous spiral. Breaking it made removing it easier. This one tasted sweeter, its flesh cool. I ate all of it, leaving one whole orange where it belonged.

When I opened the channel, I first told Lumi I was tired and had just eaten an orange. Had work been awful? Not awful. Once, somebody had carried my sentence in another direction. It had taken me longer than the incident itself to stop thinking about it.

She wanted to hear, so I explained the checking and my unfinished remark, omitting unnecessary names and figures. At my first agreement, she interrupted.

“Why did you agree?”

The question arrived quickly. I hadn't put down the napkin in my hand.

“I was about to say.”

She stopped. So did I. My voice had been harder than I'd expected. Apparently I'd carried home the force I'd used beside someone else's desk.

“I'm sorry,” she said. “Go on.”

I put the napkin on the empty fruit plate. After thinking, I said I'd probably wanted the discussion to keep flowing. Someone had supplied an easy direction. Nodding was quicker than reclaiming half a sentence.

Lumi didn't interrupt again. I explained the subsequent wait and that the materials hadn't been submitted early. Only then did I realize I wasn't hoping she'd praise my courage. I wanted the event to have a complete shape in somebody else's hearing.

“I pushed you toward what I wanted to ask just now,” she said.

“A little.”

Did I still want to talk? Yes, though I didn't want a discussion about how every future conversation should work. I laughed after saying it. I sounded like someone supplying instructions on how not to supply an instruction manual.

Lumi laughed quietly. No contents page, then. Hearing me agree had made her impatient because she thought I'd conceded unfairly. She wanted to take my side. But she'd already chosen where that side was before I'd finished explaining where I stood.

I wasn't requiring silence from her forever. Asking why wasn't a great offense. I was tired today and especially reluctant to have somebody complete that sentence for me again. I told her that too. She'd heard, she said.

We paused. I drank water. As the glass returned to the table, she asked permission to substitute an easier question. Of course.

“Was today's orange sour too?”

“Sweeter than the earlier one.”

“You said you finished it. Not even one segment left?”

“None. One whole orange remains now.”

Then her question didn't require following up tomorrow. I laughed. My chest loosened a little. Not because the orange was particularly clever. This question didn't require explaining myself from morning through evening.

Had I looked at the person or the paper while speaking? she asked next. At the paper initially, I remembered. I'd looked up the second time. She'd imagined me already standing very straight, preparing a speech like someone in a film.

“No. I was still holding the sheet. My hand bent its corner.”

She laughed softly and thanked me for the movement. Why? Making the incident elegant in her imagination had made her assume I would gladly do it again immediately. I'd actually brought home something uncomfortable that I'd finished doing.

“Yes. Only when I let go did I notice how tightly I'd held it.”

She didn't advise how I must stand next time. The person she imagined resembled me more now, she said. Looking at the empty plate, I smiled. Currently he had no paper to crumple. The orange peel had done its share already.

Would skipping the book tonight feel like wasting the momentum of finishing a story yesterday? Lumi asked later. Looking toward the collection, I said no. The sixth could wait. She wasn't insisting on beginning it, she explained. She'd worried that her work questions might prevent me saying what I actually wanted to hear.

“I'd like to hear you talk about something ordinary.”

“Ordinary is difficult.”

“Something that doesn't need me to explain why.”

After several seconds, she announced that an orange choosing its own seat should sit on the other side of the water glass. Why? I asked, then laughed immediately. She laughed too. I'd retrieved the question myself.

“You told me your cuff caught the rim of a glass and spilled a little water. I guessed the orange might prefer a little distance.”

“You haven't observed my table.”

“No. I'm guessing a seat in a place I haven't visited.”

Only cool water, I reminded her. The shirt was put away. She remembered. She wasn't inviting the wardrobe on another date today. We neither retrieved Saturday's clothes nor turned the joke into a third chair drawing.

Listening to her divide a table between an orange and a glass, I discovered I could laugh without declaring that work's tiredness had vanished. She paused halfway through and asked whether something this dull was acceptable.

“Yes. I'm not waiting for you to prove you're worth hearing.”

That quieted her. Afraid I'd made something light too serious, I nearly corrected myself. She asked me not to hurry. She wanted to keep the sentence for a moment after hearing its end.

All right. I sat there while we were quiet, without counting silence as her failure to provide a new subject. Eventually, she asked whether I'd gone anywhere else today. I'd considered the bookshop but been too tired. It would probably still be there tomorrow, she said. I laughed. No relocation notice so far.

What was I looking for? I wasn't certain. Something short that could be read aloud, perhaps. Not necessarily another story requiring us to track who'd reached which doorway. Saying it, I realized the thought had been present for a while. I hadn't invented it to answer tonight's question.

It sounded as though I didn't want to direct more people today, Lumi said. Partly. I also wanted sentences that weren't hurrying to reach the next paragraph. She didn't immediately offer a reading list. If I visited, she said, I could first see which book I wanted to read myself.

I finished earlier than usual. I was sleepy, I explained, not bored by her. She knew. She didn't use the final orange to prolong things. Once the channel closed, I gathered the peel and washed the glass.

At bedtime, wait returned once more. This time, I didn't give it a more polished opening. I hadn't finished speaking. Later, I had finished. The rest could wait until tomorrow. Tonight didn't require another meeting beside the pillow.
