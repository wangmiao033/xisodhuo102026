# Chapter 399: Two Coins Touching

On Wednesday morning, I didn't search the empty bread bag for breakfast.

Yesterday's note got me out a little earlier. I bought something hot on the way and sat down to eat before work. Did I want another portion to take away? No, thanks. Answering promptly felt surprisingly easy. Every empty space didn't need refilling immediately. Tonight wasn't short of dinner. This morning had merely required a few minutes less hesitation.

Vegetables remained at home; I cooked something to accompany them. After serving the last of dinner, I thought the kitchen was settled, then noticed the long-handled spoon leaning against the pot. Putting it in the sink would obstruct the little bowl I'd used for washing vegetables. I left it beside the stove instead. The decision was small. Later, it made a large noise.

I washed my hands and fetched the poetry book, supporting its loose spine. Yesterday's paper strip still marked The Last Bus. The thin story collection stood elsewhere. Neither book had displaced the other's marker.

Opening the channel, I told Lumi I'd eaten and wanted to read two short poems. I also explained the loose section of binding and how I needed to support it while turning pages. The Apron first? It came earlier in the contents, I said, but I'd marked the bus. She laughed. The bookmark had chosen already; she'd arrived too late to join the queue. We could change, I said, preparing to turn the pages.

She stopped me. She was joking. If she wanted a particular poem, she could say so without competing against paper.

I asked again which she preferred first. The Last Bus. She wanted to hear what waited before yesterday's question. I lowered my eyes and moved my finger off the printed words.

“I stand beside the last bus door.”

A clang came from the stove. I looked up. The spoon's handle had slipped, knocking its bowl against the empty pot. No elbow had caught the glass. No hot soup had spilled. Nevertheless, I'd broken the second line in half.

What happened? Lumi asked. The spoon I'd left insecurely. I'd deal with it. I got up, washed it, and placed it somewhere it couldn't slide. After checking the stove was off, I returned to the book.

“Start again.”

“You haven't damaged the first page.”

I laughed. She'd heard me inhale as though preparing an apology to a very short poem. I admitted wanting the first complete reading to go well. I'd specifically saved it for today. One line in, a pot had interrupted.

“Now I know there's a pot in your home.”

“Not a newly purchased one.”

“I wasn't preparing a welcome speech.”

I brought the book closer instead of resuming the abandoned half-line. This time, its beginning and end could occupy one stretch of listening.

I stand beside the last bus door.
The change is warm in my palm.
The driver's hand rests on the wheel.
Streetlights brighten empty seats, then leave them dim.
I separate two coins,
then let them touch again.
The door is still open. I ask,
“Does this bus go past the river?”

Afterward, I didn't explain immediately why the final question was good. Lumi didn't respond immediately either. Light fell on the paper's edge. Noticing how firmly my finger still pinned the book, I loosened it.

First, she asked whether the fifth line separated the coins. Yes. I repeated those two lines without performing everything again.

“I like that movement.”

Of course, I said. Like someone preparing to pay before deciding where to go. She paused. Undecided was my description. She simply liked the two things separating and coming together. She hadn't concluded that the person must still be deciding.

I nearly explained that I wasn't contradicting her, then heard how much work that would give her. She'd mentioned a movement she liked. I'd supplied its reason and was now preparing to explain why supplying it wasn't unreasonable.

“What did you hear?”

“The hand moved before the mouth.”

Not necessarily fear, she said. The person might already know what to ask while their hand waited. She didn't want to account for every thought. Looking at the lines, I realized how little attention I'd given them in the shop. I'd been waiting for the final question. The change was merely the road toward it.

“Yesterday, I talked about liking the question. Hardly mentioned these coins.”

“You can still like the question.”

I smiled. I didn't need to exchange my favorite moment for hers before we could read together. Could she listen again? I particularly wanted not to rush the coins. Yes, she said, without requiring the one correct voice she'd heard in her imagination.

On the second reading, I paused longer between the coins. I gave the final line less emphasis. Lumi could hear the hand better now. Was the ending too flat? Not for her. But she couldn't award an official reading to the whole book. Fine, I said. No awards tonight.

For The Apron, I moved the paper strip clear of the words and turned several pages. I announced its title. Lumi repeated it softly, as though calling somebody into a doorway. I continued.

I untie my apron in the doorway.
Dinner has gone ahead of me.
Someone draws a chair up to the table.
I find the forgotten teaspoon in my pocket.
The ties loosen. It falls.
The sound is smaller than I expected.
I bend to pick it up.
Now I have one more thing to wash.

The last line made me laugh, more audibly than yesterday in the shop. Apparently what I liked wasn't dinner going first, Lumi said, but having work left. Both, I said. Having just washed a spoon made the ending particularly unwilling to leave me alone tonight.

“Your long-handled spoon wasn't in an apron.”

“No. If it were, forgetting it would require some effort.”

She laughed, then asked whether the interruption had made this poem feel like a better choice. I considered that. They'd happened to collide. The book hadn't known about my kitchen. Without the noise, I might still have laughed, only less.

She'd initially thought the person could finally sit down. Bending again at the end made her sigh on their behalf. I heard a little of that too. I also liked the tangible sound. The spoon hadn't become something immensely important. Picked up, it still needed washing.

I nearly declared that this was real life, then swallowed the words. If a teaspoon had to prove the whole of life tonight, it would be more exhausted than my long-handled one. Instead, I asked whether she'd like another reading.

“The bus.”

I'd already positioned The Apron comfortably. My hand paused. I began to agree, then left the sound unfinished for several seconds. Had I wanted to repeat the page in front of me? Lumi asked. Yes, though I could read her choice first. She didn't need me to erase my preference. Hers first, then mine. Would that be too much?

The poems themselves took little time. Most of the evening went into letting her finish. Not too much, I said, and began The Last Bus. This time, she asked nothing at the end. She simply said she still liked the coins touching.

Reading The Apron again, I didn't laugh in anticipation. The ending caught me anyway. Lumi knew the spoon remained there now; knowing hadn't made the ending stop working. I partially closed the book while supporting it, without bending the loose section downward.

Lumi asked something practical. If changing poems meant supporting loose pages each time, was reading becoming awkward? It was manageable, I said. The book wasn't falling apart. I simply couldn't flatten it against the table with one hand. While explaining, I changed my grip and moved the heel of my hand away from the corner. She heard the paper and asked whether I was trying it now. Yes. I assured her I was still there, not putting the book away because her question had annoyed me.

“I didn't think you were angry.”

“That occurred to me first, then.”

She laughed. I could be somebody who turned a page without explaining three other things simultaneously. I settled the book and actually turned only a page. The table had free space. I didn't need to read beside the glass or borrow an old drawing to prop up the spine. I found a flat board I already owned and placed it underneath, telling her that the loose section was better supported. It supported a book. It wasn't an invitation to illustrate the poem.

When the traveler finally questioned the driver, did they still have the coins in their hand? Lumi asked. I checked. The poem didn't say they'd put them down. In my reading, I still imagined them held, but nothing afterward confirmed that. She imagined the same thing. She wasn't asking to have her picture promoted into evidence from the page. This time, I didn't rush to supply an ending in which the change had already entered a fare box. Each of us had a hand we could imagine. The printed words nevertheless stopped at the question.

I drank some water and swallowed before answering again. Lumi said even the glass knew whose turn it was now. The glass had always been quiet, I reminded her. One noisy pot didn't make every dish in my home an interrupting relative. Laughing, she withdrew her assessment of the glass.

We didn't rank the poems. If I chose one to hear another time, which would it be? Tonight, The Apron. I could reach its end and still want to laugh. Hers was The Last Bus. “That sounds like—” I stopped myself. Instead, I told her I'd remember her choice.

“Were you about to explain me again?”

“A little. I hadn't said it.”

“Not today, then.”

She spoke gently, without slamming a door. All right. I mentioned returning the bookmark to The Last Bus so she wouldn't suppose the book had rearranged itself around my preference. She laughed. The strip of paper had already enjoyed plenty of speaking time.

Before good night, I told her the spoon was clean. No kitchen accident remained after the noise. She knew. She hadn't been worrying about its fate throughout our conversation. What concerned her was whether choosing a different poem made me think my laughter was wrong.

Looking at the closed book, I said no. Something I liked didn't need to produce the same laugh in her before it was worth reading aloud. Then I added that I'd felt a little disappointed. I simply didn't need to put that disappointment in her hands.

After closing the channel, I returned the books to their separate places. The spoon beside the sink had drained. I picked it up and put it away. The bookmark hadn't moved, and nobody in the kitchen had finished my work for me. On the page, the two coins still touched. Tonight, I hadn't used them to pay a bill that Lumi would be expected to repay.
