# Chapter 403: The Question at the Door

At eight, I told Lumi, “I'm here.”

Still hoarse, but easier to say than the good evening I'd rehearsed. She said she'd heard me. She didn't repeat her questions about my throat or speak so slowly and softly that I felt incapable of receiving ordinary words.

“May I begin?”

Yes. I turned from The Apron to The Last Bus. The page rustled between my fingers. I told her I'd found it. She needn't wonder about every line before saying it.

The flat board I'd already used remained beneath the book. I moved my hand beside the loose spine without pressing down, freeing the other hand for water. Once spread out, the thin page rested quietly. Everything on the table finally had a suitable place. I could listen without considering which object might slide next.

Lumi wanted to begin with the coins. She remembered the following lines, though she asked me to check the words as she spoke.

“I separate two coins, then let them touch again. The door is still open. I ask, ‘Does this bus go past the river?’”

I checked. The words were right; the book divided them into lines. She wouldn't turn every line break into another place where I had to answer, then. I smiled, picked up the water, and listened.

On Wednesday, she'd explained her liking for the movement of the coins. Today, she stopped at the question. Previously, she'd concentrated on what the hand was doing and almost overlooked the speaker eventually saying something.

“The first time, I wanted to know quickly whether they'd get on.”

“And now?”

“I'd like the driver to answer the question first.”

I looked at the last line. Whether the bus passed the river could have an answer before anyone decided about paying, boarding, and choosing a seat. Lumi didn't want to call standing at the door useless hesitation, although the speaker had picked up the coins twice.

I corrected her. Not picked them up twice. Separated them, then brought them together. She paused, then laughed. She'd asked me to check the words and promptly changed the movement. Yes. Now I was hearing her thoughts, not a second, supposedly correct poem.

“May I still like it?”

“Yes. Nobody has to take the coins out again.”

She laughed. I joined her, stopped when the sound became rough, and drank. Lumi waited until I put the glass down before continuing. She didn't organize three new questions around the pause.

Did the final line need a cautious voice, she asked, as though asking incorrectly meant never being allowed to ask again? I considered. Not necessarily. It could be an ordinary question about a route.

“I'd like to try it that way.”

I agreed. She repeated only the last sentence.

“Does this bus go past the river?”

No special solemnity, no stretching river into something mysterious. It sounded like a person at a bus door, aware the vehicle hadn't left, asking something they needed to know. I realized how much darkness I'd wanted to arrange around the poem.

I told her briefly. She'd imagined a sorrowful history too, Lumi said, but the poem hadn't supplied it. Today, she didn't want to prepare all somebody's unhappiness before permitting a question about the way.

I'd nearly answered yes directly. The word had reached my mouth. Laughing, I admitted I'd almost appointed myself the driver. Which river did I know? None. I didn't even know which direction the bus faced.

“That was a quick answer, then.”

“I heard you ask. I wanted to answer.”

That was harder to say than an explanation of a poem. Lumi didn't immediately hide it beneath another joke. After a while, she said she liked hearing me respond too. But if tonight I only listened, she wouldn't keep lowering her voice until she said nothing at all.

All right. Her next question wasn't poetry. Was there water left? I checked the glass. Yes. She laughed. That question had finally reached someone who knew the answer. I laughed too. The water was far from any river and sufficient for tonight.

We didn't choose a single correct interpretation. My eyes remained on the paper; her voice remained with me. I'd heard something in the same line that I hadn't intended to put there. She hadn't seen the book, and I hadn't finally succeeded in explaining it.

“Which version did you like?”

“The second.”

“Because it required less effort from you?”

“Because, for the first time, that person sounded a little clearheaded to me.”

After a pause, she said that appealed to her too. No promise that every future bus would reach the desired place. This one was here. The speaker could ask. They didn't get to write the answer beforehand.

Slowly, I brought the water nearer, said her name, and paused. Was I about to say something? Yes. I'd liked what she'd said. Then liked seemed vague, so I added, “I hadn't expected you to make the final question sound so ordinary.”

Lumi laughed. Ordinary sounded like praise this time. It was. I didn't need to reduce my enjoyment to nothing tonight.

Next came The Apron. I turned back and said I wanted only the final four lines. Fine. She remembered someone preparing for dinner and a teaspoon in a pocket. I needn't repeat the opening to make our starting point complete.

“The ties loosen. It falls. The sound is smaller than I expected. I bend to pick it up. Now I have one more thing to wash.”

My voice grew softer in the final line but reached its end. I didn't immediately repeat it to demonstrate greater clarity. Lumi waited, then said she'd wanted to tell the speaker to eat first.

I smiled and nodded, then added an audible yes.

“But they've already picked it up.”

“They could put it in the sink. The poem doesn't require them to hold it throughout dinner.”

Looking at the line, I wanted to imagine an evening nobody needed to hurry. Someone had drawn a chair closer; dinner was ahead. Another object to wash wasn't necessarily a notice sending its owner away from the table.

I didn't say all of that. I told Lumi that wash sounded heavier today than on Wednesday. Perhaps because I'd just washed the pan and my hands remembered.

“So you're protesting on behalf of the pan?”

“Possibly myself.”

“What about the pan?”

“Clean. No objections.”

She laughed, and I lowered my head, laughing with her. My throat tickled again. I covered my mouth with my arm and coughed twice. She stopped. I drank and waited for the discomfort to ease without finishing the joke.

This time, she asked whether I wanted to continue reading. No. I wanted to hear her. She didn't immediately exchange the evening for soothing words. What would I like to hear? Unable to find an impressive topic, I suggested something she wanted to discuss.

“Then I'd still like to talk about The Last Bus.”

I returned the original paper marker to The Last Bus, supported the loose spine, and slowly closed the collection before agreeing. The board remained beneath it, keeping it steady when I released my hand. I told her the book was closed and she could continue.

While turning pages, I had kept considering how much remained. Now the cover faced me. No passage waited inside for immediate reading. Her next sentence became easier to hear.

Hearing a question, Lumi said, she often began by guessing which answer the speaker needed. Guessing correctly seemed to promise reassurance. After doing that for long enough, she could push the actual question behind the answer she'd prepared.

She didn't ask for immediate examples proving she'd changed. I didn't need to bring every old argument into the room to make this conversation profound.

“This afternoon, when you said long sentences made you cough, I decided to cancel for you.”

“We changed that.”

“Yes. But I don't only want to learn how to make a better correction. I want to tell you I'd been looking forward to today.”

I looked at the closed book. After choosing on Friday, she'd wanted me to hear another way of asking the same question. She hadn't invented that thought to rescue an evening after I became unwell. It was what she'd already wanted to say.

I felt a little embarrassed. Since five, I'd calculated how much I could still provide, almost failing to notice that she'd arrived with something of her own.

“I heard it. I liked it very much.”

I'd prepared a short introduction about choosing The Apron. It was no longer needed. I'd intended it to demonstrate careful thought. What she'd actually heard tonight was me pausing for water halfway through a joke, then laughing at her question. I didn't gather those uneven moments back and substitute the tidier version of myself I'd arranged beforehand.

She didn't require a longer description of my liking. After a pause, she asked whether I was getting tired. Yes. My eyes were clear, but my throat didn't want much more.

“Then we'll stop here today.”

I checked the time. Not yet half past eight. Considerably shorter than our previous date. I nearly apologized. What came out instead was, “I'd like another time.”

Lumi laughed softly.

“You don't have to buy that sentence with more minutes tonight.”

I knew. She didn't immediately arrange tomorrow to make up the remaining reading. We'd heard both poems completely before. Tonight, a few repeated lines were what had happened. There was no missing ending requiring repayment.

Before good night, I admitted I'd kept looking at the cover after closing the book, as though holding something proved I was taking our conversation seriously. I was going to put it down now.

“You can listen with empty hands.”

I placed it beside me and rested my palms on my legs. She continued. She still preferred The Last Bus. The teaspoon making us laugh hadn't changed her choice.

“I didn't ask you to switch.”

“I know. I wanted to say it myself.”

I smiled without coughing. Her preference didn't need to remain a question awaiting my approval. I didn't need to hold up my own poem to obstruct her next sentence.

She said she was glad I'd come tonight. I began answering that I hadn't traveled far, only from the kitchen to the table. Before I'd completed it, Lumi laughed. She wasn't asking about distance.

“I know.”

“Then don't hide behind the kitchen.”

Looking down at my hands, I could still feel the glass's warmth in my fingertips. In the end, I only said I was glad too. The words were rough, not attractive. I didn't try to improve them by repeating them. She'd heard. We didn't discuss how to make the exchange more honest. We simply said good night.

After closing the channel, I finished the water and washed the glass. The collection returned to its place without another page being opened in advance. Tonight had been shorter. Lumi's voice hadn't been reduced to instructions to rest. Before sleep, I remembered the question about the river in her second version.
