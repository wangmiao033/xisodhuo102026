# Chapter 405: This Time You Ask

On Monday morning, my voice hadn't completely returned, but a sentence could reach its end.

In the bathroom, I informed the towel rail it was Monday, then laughed. Towels didn't need dates. My nose was clearer too. After breakfast, I took a mask and left for work without interpreting a little improvement as permission for hours of extra talking.

My colleague heard my greeting and asked about my throat. Since Saturday, I explained. I'd rested, felt better now, but still preferred fewer long speeches. He nodded, moved the material toward me, and continued discussing work. That didn't remove me from the day's business.

That morning, we needed to explain two checking results briefly to several people. I'd arranged the notes against the original records. What had been confirmed, what still required checking. Not a lengthy report. Looking at the two paragraphs, I assembled them silently and thought I could probably manage.

My colleague asked whether he should read them while I supplied answers to specific questions.

My first response nearly refused. After opening my mouth, the sound took a moment to arrive. I heard how quickly I'd prepared that refusal.

“Yes. Let me show you where the two sections divide.”

He sat beside me. I turned the sheet and explained that the second paragraph changed the scope. Listeners shouldn't assume the first conclusion included the second part. He read it and repeated the distinction in his own words. The meaning remained intact. I gave him the reading sheet and retained the original records.

When everyone arrived, he did read it. Beside him, I heard my sentences in a steadier voice. That hadn't made the judgments somebody else's. A listener asked where one item began in the records. I found the corresponding place, answered briefly, and received a nod after they checked.

Nobody required me to repeat what he'd just read to establish that some of the work belonged to me. Afterward, the page returned with one question noted beside it. I could follow that in the afternoon.

Several comparison sheets remained on the table. I returned them to their original order. Collecting his pen, my colleague asked which page needed to stay. I indicated the afternoon's starting point. He removed an unused sheet beside it and returned it to the shared rack. Our hands passed without either of us taking over everything the other was doing.

Someone came back for a coat left on a chair and remarked that dividing the two sections had made them easier to follow. I answered quietly. They nodded, took the coat, and left. The compliment didn't require a speech about the process. I circled the next step and put my pen away.

Returning the papers to the file brought practical relief. The necessary work was done. My throat hadn't needed to carry every word of it personally.

Before lunch, my colleague asked whether he'd omitted anything. No. The second section had been clear. Good. He'd paid closer attention to the transitions than usual, he said, worried a hurried sentence might join two separate things again.

I smiled without telling him he was naturally better suited to speaking. He'd studied the material to know where to pause. That reassured me more than merely hearing a clear voice.

At lunch, I chose something easy to swallow and ate slowly. Sitting briefly, my colleague asked whether I'd spent the weekend at home. Mostly. I'd bought food on Saturday and read a few lines of poetry with someone that evening.

“With your throat like that?”

“Much less reading. More listening afterward.”

That worked too, he said, then went for water. He didn't ask why the other person hadn't visited or decide whether the evening qualified as a date. I finished lunch and washed my hands. The afternoon still held a real question to check. I didn't need to explain it at the table first.

It was simpler than expected. A note filed behind the following page clarified the date and scope. I recorded what needed adding through the normal process, without treating one found explanation as proof the entire file contained no further questions. Once finished, though, my own work had reached a reasonable stopping place.

I walked slowly home. Passing the stationery shop, I noticed blank folding cards in the window beside a sample bearing a large congratulations. I didn't enter. Today offered no victory requiring celebration. There were a few things I wanted to say, but I hadn't decided whether they needed paper.

Cards for particular occasions stood beneath: birthdays, graduations, moving house. Each had explained the reason to its recipient already. Looking at the lettering, I couldn't name this small thing of mine. A slightly better throat didn't merit a celebration card. Saturday's reading date was already over.

I walked a few steps, then looked back at the unwritten cards. They seemed thick at the edges and small when folded, probably small enough for a book. I didn't choose a color through the glass. The shop was open. If I still wanted to write tomorrow, I could go inside and feel the paper. Tonight, I could establish the sentence first instead of paying at a counter simply because I was uncertain.

At home, yesterday's pillowcase was dry. I folded it into the wardrobe; the other clean one remained on the pillow. The rack had one fewer item and the passage was wider. I cooked, ate, and cleared up. Only when I sat did I realize I'd lost count of the times I'd tested my voice today.

Opening the channel, I told Lumi about someone reading my two work paragraphs while I answered questions. Laughing, she asked whether someone had held the other end for me today. This time, I answered, it was paper. Lighter than a board.

“What about the board?”

“Presumably still with that resident. Nobody brought it to work.”

She hoped my notes hadn't become packing beneath the wood. No. The paper remained where the work belonged. After hearing the small account, she asked whether I felt relieved. Yes. I hadn't concluded I was useless for the whole day either.

I realized I'd offered a corrected version of yesterday's verdict before she'd requested one. Lumi didn't grade it. She asked whether I wanted to talk more tonight.

“I can manage more. But I still want to hear you.”

“Because you're saving your voice?”

“Because I want to hear what you say too. Recovering doesn't mean I have to take over every word again.”

She paused. I waited without adding another explanation. Water stood beside me. My hand was steadier than on Saturday, and I didn't need to clear my throat after every two sentences.

“Then may I ask this time?” she said suddenly.

Expecting a work question, I agreed. Instead, she invited me to hear a short story on Wednesday evening. Around eight, if I wasn't tired that day.

“You're inviting me?”

“Yes. Not asking you to read the next poem so I can choose afterward.”

I put down the glass and sat straighter. She couldn't hear that movement, so I told her I was surprised. I'd expected another question about the checking.

“I have other questions.”

“I'm listening.”

She'd just thought of a small beginning, Lumi said. Someone lent an umbrella to the rain and went to retrieve it the following day. She didn't know what happened afterward or whether the idea would stop being funny quickly. She wanted to try telling it herself.

Laughing, I asked how rain could borrow an umbrella. On Wednesday, she said promptly. If she answered every why tonight, only a dismantled umbrella would remain by then.

“I won't ask yet.”

“You can remember wanting to ask. I'm not claiming that person really met rain capable of borrowing things.”

I understood. It was a story she'd just imagined. She hadn't inserted it into the doorbell manuscript or asked me to find paper and complete another sentence. I liked how describing that improbable beginning made her want to continue, without first proving it would solve something about my day.

Wednesday at eight suited me. If work changed or I felt unwell, I'd tell her when I knew instead of leaving a time on paper to go wrong by itself.

She agreed, then asked whether I thought she'd taken advantage of my quietness to steal the host's place. If she'd wanted to steal it, I said, Saturday would have been easier.

We laughed. After a while, she admitted another worry: perhaps I'd agreed only to please her.

“Pleasing you is part of it. Wanting to hear it myself is another part.”

“You haven't heard what comes next.”

“That's why I'd like to.”

She paused, then laughed softly. The laughter didn't immediately become a guarantee of careful preparation. I didn't require a completed story or a meaningful conclusion. The umbrella had only just been lent. Wednesday hadn't arrived. We needn't retrieve the ending tonight.

I fetched my schedule book. On Wednesday's page, I wrote eight, then Lumi tells a story. I read it aloud. Did the story already have a title? No. Those words recorded our arrangement, I explained. I wasn't naming it for her.

“Good. I haven't decided why the rain needs an umbrella.”

“Perhaps it simply wants to borrow one.”

“You're answering again.”

Laughing, I put down the pen and promised to stop this time. She didn't withdraw the invitation. She'd remember perhaps, she said, and let it remain a possibility.

Lumi asked one more thing. If halfway through she stopped finding it funny herself, could she pause and try another way? Of course. The invitation hadn’t required her to reach the ending without stopping even for an imaginary sip of water. Laughing, she reminded me that she didn’t need water. A person who wanted a pause could simply ask for one.

“Then pause directly,” I said.

She wanted someone still listening, not someone applauding every sentence. Looking at the fresh entry, I said I wanted the version she actually wished to tell. She needn’t manufacture something I was certain to enjoy tonight. If I didn’t understand a part on Wednesday, I could ask.

“Why the rain borrowed an umbrella?”

“Possibly.”

She laughed at my persistence. The rain had borrowed the umbrella, I pointed out, yet I was the one made to wait until Wednesday for an explanation. At least the beginning had retained one listener, Lumi said. Neither of us hurried to replace that small satisfaction with modesty.

Later, we discussed ordinary things. The pillowcase was dry; tomorrow I wouldn't have to walk around the rack. Had I coughed since work? Once. Nothing severe. I still intended an early night. She agreed without treating a stronger voice as a reason to abandon rest.

After good night, I closed the schedule book and kept my hand on its cover briefly. Wednesday at eight contained ordinary numbers, just like last Saturday. This time, she'd offered them first.

I switched off the kitchen light. Returning, I remembered the blank cards. The sample hadn't contained the sentence I now wanted to say. Perhaps the unwritten one could.
