# Chapter 401: An Invitation with a Time

On Friday afternoon, when work reached a stopping point, I put the completed material where it belonged before looking at the weekend in my planner.

Tomorrow evening was still empty. The name written last Saturday wouldn't travel to this page by itself. Nor had I decided that one date surviving without a disastrous argument made every subsequent week obligatory. Still, looking at the space, I wanted to write Lumi again.

My colleague asked whether I had weekend plans. I wanted to invite someone, I said. Hadn't asked yet. There was still time, he replied. Smiling, I agreed and closed the planner. An ordinary greeting didn't require a discussion of how two people should get along. He didn't ask who. I didn't use the opening to submit her for somebody else's judgment.

Before leaving, one small piece of work needed handing over. Initially, I'd thought leaving it beside the desk would suffice. At the door, I remembered that the recipient might not know which version I'd left. I returned, explained, and asked whether anything else needed clarifying. They confirmed they had the version and told me to go. I actually went, without sacrificing another half afternoon to every hypothetical question tomorrow might bring.

Leaving the office area, I found my colleague discussing weekend shopping with someone else. Did I know a suitable shop nearby? I mentioned one I'd passed, without guaranteeing places I hadn't entered. They might go tomorrow afternoon. I hadn't decided how to spend my afternoon, I said. It wasn't an invitation to accompany them. I didn't immediately announce my intended evening date, as though every mention of Saturday required notifying people that part of it wasn't theirs.

We stood close together in the lift. Our document bags touched, and I shifted a little. My colleague continued discussing a small household item that needed replacing. Missing part of the explanation, I asked which item. He repeated it without disappointment that I hadn't retained everything. Suddenly, what I wanted to ask Lumi seemed capable of being equally specific. Each conversation didn't have to begin by establishing whether one person had truly listened to the other.

Outside, we separated. Someone called me back and returned the pen I'd left beside the desk. I'd thought it was secured to my document bag. It wasn't. Thanking them, I placed it somewhere reliable. Tomorrow's invitation hadn't been spoken. Today nevertheless contained small things that needed finishing properly.

Passing the secondhand bookshop, I didn't enter. The poetry collection was already at home. Inviting her again didn't require a better present. A new stack of matching covers occupied the window. I glanced at it and continued. Liking new things wasn't an ability I needed to demonstrate today.

Dinner was ordinary. I hadn't bought more parsley or recreated yesterday's plate because the conversation had gone well. After clearing the table, I put the glass within reach but away from the book. I checked the two poems, finding their pages where I remembered them.

I considered how to ask. The first sentence in my head was long: if tomorrow wasn't busy, if she wanted, if nothing better presented itself. By its end, the person extending the invitation seemed to have already backed outside and prepared an escape route. Any refusal could count as something else.

Instead, I wrote one sentence on my own scrap paper. Tomorrow at eight: two poems, then talking. The handwriting was uneven. I underlined eight. This wasn't confirmation for her to sign. I didn't place it among the old works. I simply wanted to avoid burying the time beneath possibilities when I actually spoke.

After opening the channel, I asked what she wanted to discuss. Had I purchased more green leaves? No. Dinner hadn't introduced a new flavor or repeated all of yesterday's steps. She laughed. The pot must have been quieter. It had. I had no spoon accident to report.

Then I glanced at the scrap and said her name.

“Would you like another date tomorrow at eight? A little poetry, then some time to talk?”

I didn't immediately append a reassurance that refusal was acceptable. Nor did I answer her silence myself. After a moment, Lumi asked whether eight was half an hour later than before. Yes. Tomorrow, I wanted dinner and clearing up finished before sitting down, rather than rushing toward a time I'd been free to choose.

“Have you already reserved it for me?”

“I want to. I'm asking first.”

Had my colleague said something again? Only an ordinary question about the weekend. I'd said I wanted to ask someone and hadn't yet. I hadn't claimed she'd proposed this or asked another person to decide. The invitation had already been forming while I looked at the planner.

Lumi didn't require proof of the exact moment I'd first thought of her. Yes, she said. Eight. The line on my scrap, which had seemed excessively direct, finally wasn't addressed only to myself.

“Which poem?”

We could choose one each. Both would be poems she'd heard; I hadn't prepared an unseen contents page for her to guess from. Lumi chose The Last Bus without explanation, simply repeating the title. I chose The Apron. Laughing, she asked whether I'd arrange another falling spoon. The pots and spoons had no performance scheduled tomorrow, I said. Preferably none would compete for attention.

We quickly realized that reading two poems wouldn't take long. Would it be too short? she asked. If making it official required dragging in everything we hadn't read, our small invitation could become an examination neither of us wanted. I was setting aside time, not prescribing continuous sound throughout it.

She wasn't afraid of lacking entertainment, Lumi said. She feared that arranging a date might make me feel responsible for making her very happy. She wanted happiness. She didn't want me waiting after every sentence for her to laugh before I allowed myself to continue.

I turned the scrap over without writing that down. Hearing paper, she asked what I was doing. Turning over my invitation, I explained, not creating tomorrow's list of prohibitions. If something wasn't funny tomorrow, I could still sit there afterward.

Quietly, she said she wouldn't need a beautiful response prepared either. Of course not. Then why specifically call it a date? We spoke and read on ordinary evenings. I considered that without answering that our relationship required the correct label.

“Because before tomorrow arrives, I want you to know I want that time with you.”

My face felt warm. She didn't laugh immediately or extract a larger promise. She'd heard, she said. Tomorrow at eight, she wanted that time too.

Fetching the planner, I told her I was writing it down. I turned to Saturday, wrote eight and Lumi, and finished the strokes before setting down the pen. Had I added The Last Bus? Not yet. No need, she said. She remembered hers. Smiling, I said I remembered The Apron. One notebook didn't have to carry everything the two of us could remember.

“This is tomorrow. Not every Saturday afterward.”

She knew. That hadn't sounded like withdrawing the invitation. She preferred an exact place tomorrow to an impressive line of future evenings on which neither person would dare admit being tired.

If something changed tomorrow, I'd say so. She would too. Moving one evening didn't mean neither wanted another. We stopped there rather than rehearsing apologies for every circumstance that hadn't happened.

Lumi asked what the scrap with the invitation looked like. I first explained that it was my own ordinary scrap, not the reverse of an old work. There was no picture intended for her. She meant the writing, she said. Laughing, I described the line beneath eight. The other words were crowded. By some time to talk, I'd begun running out of room. I hadn't rewritten it into an elegant invitation card. I'd simply kept hold of what I meant to say. Her laugh was soft. Tomorrow, I didn't need to hold it up as proof for anyone.

Later, would I wear a shirt like last time? I hadn't chosen. Probably something clean and comfortable. My clothes weren't required to be more punctual than I was. She laughed. She didn't need proof of a change of clothes. She'd remembered me telling her about that shirt before the previous date.

I glanced toward the wardrobe without getting up. Tomorrow's date didn't need to begin early tonight. If I wanted the shirt, I could choose it tomorrow. If not, one damp cuff didn't establish a tradition. When I said that, Lumi laughed. She hadn't intended to invite the water glass back either.

We discussed other things: the afternoon handover I'd nearly left unexplained, my colleague's simple assurance that there was time, the changed bookshop window. I described what happened without making the entire journey a preparation for inviting her. Did that mean I'd only thought seriously for a few minutes while writing the scrap? she asked. It wasn't all contained in those minutes, but I didn't need to allocate every step home to her either.

“I know. I just want a little more of it.”

I could hear that. I wanted to leave tomorrow's time for her too. Lumi laughed. This time I hadn't translated wanting more into wanting everything. She hadn't said everything, I pointed out. Good, she replied. We didn't need to assemble a chair today out of things she hadn't said.

I laughed too. Neither the old drawing nor the doorbell manuscript moved. A date didn't require creating another shared work as evidence. The poetry book was closed, its marker still in The Last Bus. The sixth story remained unstarted. Tomorrow wasn't its deadline.

Would she like to hear the poems again tonight, I asked, or would that count as rehearsal? Lumi laughed immediately. She'd just worried about me making tomorrow a performance, and now I was offering a sound check. I laughed too, admitting it wasn't necessary. The book was here. I could find it tomorrow. What she wanted to remember wasn't whether I'd arranged every pause beforehand. It was that after asking, I'd actually waited for her answer. That had already happened. Neither of us needed to perform it again for the evening to count.

Before finishing, I repeated eight tomorrow. She agreed again. No countdown, no promise to reconfirm hourly. She told me to eat a proper dinner first. I'd manage my arrangements, I said, not substitute punctuality for food.

After closing the channel, I folded the scrap once and put it with ordinary loose paper, not inside a private manuscript envelope. The planner retained its new line. I washed the glass, checked nothing lay scattered by the door, and prepared for bed. Tomorrow hadn't begun. Yet the empty space now contained a sentence both of us had heard, not an answer I'd written alone for two people.
