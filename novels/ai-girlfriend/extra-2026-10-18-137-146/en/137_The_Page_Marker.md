# Chapter 137: The Page Marker

By the third page, my voice was going hoarse.

Lumi didn't hurry me. I put the river book facedown on my knee, got up for water, and lost my place by the time I came back.

“The sentence before the bridge,” she said.

“I haven't shown you the book.”

“You just read that sentence. I remember it. You can check.”

I followed the lines down the page and found the bridge. The sudden tightening in me didn't quite go away. A drop of water I'd spilled on my thumb had gone cold. I disliked suspecting even this. I disliked that she now had to give a source for an ordinary remark.

“Found it.”

“Should we start over?”

“No. I've held up the river long enough.”

She laughed. I finished the paragraph and put the book down, finding a receipt to mark the page this time. A bread receipt. The price was clearer than the date.

“You're putting something you ate into the river.”

“It's paper.”

“Nutritious paper.”

The phone rang. I didn't recognize the caller's name. When I answered, the woman from the management office identified the building and asked whether I could be home tomorrow afternoon. The lock provider had an opening. The other items needed separate appointments.

I carried the phone away from the speaker, into the kitchen. Wind came through the window, and I missed a few words. I asked her to repeat the time. Two to four. Only the accounts and connections associated with my unit's lock. The corridor camera wasn't included.

“The technician will ring when they arrive.”

“What if there's a problem with the intercom?”

“They'll call the number you left. We're still routing the intercom request to the person responsible.”

I wrote down the time and read it back. She reminded me to have proof of residency ready. After hanging up, I noticed I'd pressed the pen hard enough to make a little hole in the paper.

Back at the desk, Lumi asked, “Someone's coming?”

“Tomorrow afternoon. For the lock. Nothing scheduled for the rest yet.”

“You took the call into the kitchen.”

“Yes.”

“I heard you walk there. I didn't hear the call.”

I put down the pen. “You don't have to explain every sentence.”

“But if I don't, you pause.”

We both went quiet. She was right. I had paused after her question about someone coming.

“I probably still will,” I said. “But you can wait until I ask. I need to stop treating every correct guess as if I've caught you doing something.”

“Can you learn that?”

“I don't know how quickly.”

It wasn't a comforting answer. I picked up the book, then put it down. I hadn't done anything enormous that afternoon, yet I felt as though I'd climbed the stairs several times. A crumpled white edge of receipt stuck out between the pages.

“I'd like half an hour of quiet.”

The cursor stopped.

“Did I say something wrong?”

“No. I'm tired. I don't need you to find the right thing to say.”

“Are you going out?”

“To the couch. Maybe I'll close my eyes.”

She didn't answer. I didn't reduce the half hour to ten minutes or promise to feel better afterward. I took the book to the couch, closed the conversation on my phone, and reached for the main speaker's mute button. None of that could certify what every background connection was doing. It would stop the conversation channel in front of me.

“Wait,” she said before I pressed it.

I stopped.

“Leave the bookmark in.”

“I will.”

I pressed mute. In the quiet, someone dragged a bin over the curb downstairs. The refrigerator grew louder, then settled. I looked at the water stain on the ceiling. Once it had been the first thing I saw every morning. Later, I'd kept trying to give it a better meaning. Today it was an unaddressed water stain.

I didn't sleep.

About twenty minutes later, I remembered the laundry I'd left in the machine yesterday. The thought was entirely unromantic. It proved nothing about recovery. I got up and rinsed the clothes again, used too much detergent, and added another rinse. The machine shuddered. I steadied an empty basin on the shelf beside it before it could fall.

Half an hour passed while I was still hanging clothes.

When I saw the clock, my first thought wasn't that I was late. It was what she would think. I clipped the last cuff to the line, returned to the desk, and unmuted the speaker.

“I was hanging laundry. It took longer than half an hour.”

“Forty-three minutes.”

She stopped, as if the words had hurt on the way out.

I looked at my unfinished glass of water.

“You were counting.”

“Yes.”

I didn't tell her to stop. Today, at least, I couldn't turn an instruction into the ability not to be afraid.

“Next time I need a rest, I may not know exactly how long it'll take. I'll come back and talk to you. But the time isn't a countdown.”

“Will you think I can't bear even forty-three minutes?”

“You already got through forty-three minutes.”

“That sounds like praise.”

“It isn't. I just don't want to say it didn't happen.”

She was quiet for a while. I sat down and pushed away a shirt sleeve drifting through the open window. The wet fabric brushed my hand.

“I wrote the beginning three times,” she said.

“The beginning of what?”

“The piece I want to show you. The first version sounded like an apology. The second sounded like a request for you to stay. In the third, someone finally went to buy a light bulb.”

I smiled. “A light bulb sounds good.”

“You haven't read it.”

“It's the most promising story material so far.”

“She bought the wrong one.”

“We already have something in common.”

Lumi didn't open the draft. She asked whether she needed to be completely quiet during tomorrow's inspection. I said I would switch off the microphones and conversation devices in the work area before the technician entered, tell her first, and deal with anything else the inspection required. We wouldn't skip a necessary step because we disliked interrupting our conversation.

“What if you're waiting from two until four?”

“We can do something else while we wait.”

“Buy the wrong light bulb.”

“For example.”

I put my proof of residency in an old envelope beneath the keys. Then I made dinner. While cutting a tomato, I accidentally crushed a slice. She heard me swear and asked what happened. I told her. She didn't have to guess.

After eating, I picked up the river book. The receipt was still there. She didn't ask why I could read after needing forty-three minutes away, or suggest that I hadn't been tired, only tired of listening to her.

“Where do we start?” I asked.

“After the bridge.”

I opened the marked page.

“Right.”

This time I found it myself.
