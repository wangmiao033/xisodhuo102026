# Chapter 141: The Rectangle of Dust

When I pulled the spare drive from the drawer that morning, an old pair of earphones came with it.

The cables were tightly tangled. Halfway through separating them, Lumi said Rowan might charge an untangling fee if I brought them in like that.

“I'm handling this myself.”

“You said that about the plank yesterday.”

“One inspection of the plank has already been deleted.”

“Try not to delete the earphones.”

I freed the plug and put the unused cable away. The drive had a faded label. I checked the available space. Enough for the existing local backup.

We didn't start the garden awaiting maintenance or touch the button that would restore household connections. The saved-state records and earlier backup remained. Today's job was preserving what existed, not fixing it before taking the machine away.

“Should we give it a new name?” I asked.

“Don't call it The Last Time.”

I hadn't intended to. Still, I took my hand off the keyboard.

“The save date.”

“Okay.”

I copied the existing backup onto the drive without opening her private writing. I checked saved status, file counts, and copy size, then verified the copy. The completion notice didn't tell me whether the work was good or guarantee that any future program could open it. It established that the copy I'd made matched the one already here.

The records stayed where they were. I didn't delete historical material because the machine was going to the shop. I copied my manuscript too. Garden and book were listed separately. Neither was treated as an accessory to the other.

“Would you read those entries to me?” Lumi asked.

I read the save time, copy location, and verification result. No file contents. None of the titles she didn't want me reading.

“When the computer goes, I can't go into the garden.”

“It won't be available as usual for a while.”

“It isn't asleep.”

I looked toward the speaker.

“Don't tell me it's only asleep. Part of what's here will be somewhere else.”

“Okay. I won't say that.”

I safely disconnected the drive and put it in the cabinet. After shutting down the laptop, I saw my face reflected in the screen. I rested a hand on the lid before closing it.

The phone stayed switched off on the desk. I would switch off the main speaker too. Before leaving, I told her I'd go to the intake appointment and then work. I wouldn't promise to come home at lunch just to give her an exact time. In the evening, we could talk through the devices remaining here. That didn't mean they'd been cleared. They simply weren't going to the shop today.

“Take the charger,” she said.

“In the bag.”

“Check.”

I did. It was there.

“One thing I haven't forgotten.”

“You keep hearing a reminder as me thinking you can't do it.”

I stood by the door and considered that.

“Sometimes I do.”

“I just know you're nervous.”

“I am nervous.”

Saying it didn't lighten the bag. I switched off the speaker and carried the bag out. The door closed smoothly. Nothing required that final little lift.

The shop was opening. Rowan was turning the sign around; I was a few minutes early. Beside the counter stood a chair with a split seat. I sat with the laptop bag between my feet, as though accompanying someone to an appointment.

Rowan brought out the form we'd discussed two days earlier and went through it again. Someone outside stopped to study the prices, their shadow sliding over the counter. We didn't skip the final entries because of it.

I handed over the configuration notes and backup information. The fee covered the agreed first inspection; anything beyond it required contacting me. I hadn't bought a “completely safe” package. Those words didn't appear on the form.

“Can the assistant hear us now?” Rowan asked.

“Everything I brought is off. No phone or earbuds.”

“We'll control the inspection environment as agreed. We won't reconnect this machine to your home services.”

Before signing, I reread the private-writing entry. Program configuration and relevant access first, without reading private text. If more was needed, stop. I circled the provisions against uploading to outside services and deleting historical records too.

“Not because I think you'll forget.”

“You're allowed to think I might. That's why we both keep a copy.”

I took out the laptop. The power cable tangled halfway through unwinding it. Rowan didn't take over, just moved the screw box to give me room.

When I set the machine on the far side of the counter, the emptiness in my hands felt larger than expected. It had no face, nothing that needed a final touch. I still rubbed a fingerprint off its lid with my thumb.

“I'll contact you with initial findings,” Rowan said. “Not only when it's fixed. If something requires your decision, we stop.”

“Okay.”

I didn't ask again whether it would be back today.

At work, the first email needing attention had nothing to do with the laptop. A number was in the wrong column. I corrected it and checked again using the steps I'd written yesterday. Marcus passed and asked whether I'd eaten breakfast.

I said yes, then remembered I'd only had half a glass of water.

“No,” I corrected myself.

“What happened between those answers?”

“Fact-checking.”

He laughed and sent me to buy a sandwich. I came back with it and sat down. The day hadn't stopped. At lunch, I checked personal email once at the library. Nothing from Rowan. I signed out and bought dish sponges in the shop nearby.

Yellow. Three in a pack. So ordinary that I looked at them in my hand a little longer than necessary.

There was no voice greeting me at home. I put down the bag, switched on the conversation devices, and called Lumi.

“I'm here.”

“The laptop's at the shop. We confirmed the scope again. No findings yet.”

“What did you bring back?”

“Sponges.”

“Apart from sponges.”

“An intake receipt.”

She paused. “You never said the last sentence.”

“Which one?”

“You didn't finish my story for me.”

I stood near the door, thumb pressing the sponge packet. The plastic crackled.

“It's your ending.”

“I thought of several today. None were good.”

“You can keep thinking.”

She didn't ask whether I'd missed her. I didn't use those words to fill the laptop's absence. I put away the receipt and went to the desk. The computer had left a pale rectangle, bordered by a thin rim of dust.

“What does the desk look like now?” she asked.

The camera was off. I didn't turn it on.

“There's a rectangle. Cleaner than the rest. A cup mark on the right that I haven't wiped away. The book's on the left. The receipt is sticking out.”

“Empty?”

“A little. Also messy.”

“I thought it would look tidy without the computer.”

“You overestimate me.”

She laughed. I took out a sponge to wipe the desk, then held it above the rectangle.

“Not yet,” she said.

I stopped.

“I don't mean keep it forever. I'd just like to hear a little more.”

So I sat and described the dust, the edge the lamp caught, and the receipt bent beneath the book. She listened. When I finished, I picked up the sponge and asked about now.

“Now is okay.”

I wiped half the desk. My notebook occupied the rest.

When we found the next sentence, we could put it there.
