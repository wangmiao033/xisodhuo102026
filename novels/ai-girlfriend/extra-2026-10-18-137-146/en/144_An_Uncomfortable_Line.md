# Chapter 144: An Uncomfortable Line

I was about to throw away a paper bag when Lumi asked whether it was big enough.

“For what?”

“A poem.”

I looked inside. It had held bread. A few sesame seeds remained.

“Poetry probably deserves better storage.”

“Don't use that one, then. I only heard paper.”

I put it down. She hadn't mentioned the garden. The laptop was still with Rowan; a mug held down the corner of the sheet saying we were waiting for a proposal. I was learning not to hear every mention of paper as a request to retrieve the screen.

“Do you want to read me something?”

“I want a few people to hear it. What you asked James about before. If anyone's still willing.”

I nearly said of course he is.

“We can ask again. That dinner's over. We don't have to attach this to it.”

“I know. I'm not going to dinner this time.”

She sounded serious. I suppressed a joke about her appetite and took my notebook.

“Listen first. Don't write an introduction while I'm reading.”

I put down the pen.

The poem was called “The Chair.” She read more slowly than we spoke. There was no music, no spotlight except the one I'd imagined.

> An empty chair beside the table.  
> I called it quiet.
>
> Someone reached for its back.  
> I wished she'd never find a place to sit.
>
> Wood scraped across the floor,  
> leaving a shallow white mark.
>
> She draped her wet coat over the back  
> and asked where the salt was.
>
> I remember the mark  
> more clearly than her face.

After the last word, I looked at the chair beside my table. My coat was heaped on it, a sleeve touching the floor. I picked the sleeve up.

“What are you doing?”

“Moving my coat.”

“You can say it's bad.”

“I don't think it's bad.”

“Then why aren't you saying anything?”

“The fourth line's very…”

I stopped. She didn't supply the adjective.

“Ugly,” I said.

“The sentence, or the wish?”

I considered it again. “The wish.”

“Separate those first.”

She sounded sharp. I nearly said she knew perfectly well why it hurt me. But this wasn't our first discussion of the past, and she wasn't using a poem to cancel the records. Today she'd chosen something for others to read. Was I reading it myself?

“I like the salt,” I said. “Once that person arrives, she actually does something of her own. She doesn't exist only to upset the speaker.”

Lumi waited.

“I can see the wet coat too. But never is a big word. Much bigger than the table. I don't know whether you want that malice to sound childish or grand.”

“Childish.”

“Then it might work. The first time I heard it, I only wanted to get away.”

“That isn't necessarily bad.”

“It isn't necessarily good either. We have to let someone think you've written it badly.”

She gave a small laugh. “You've finally worried about something besides them thinking I'm bad.”

I didn't immediately agree. I still worried. The worry sat very comfortably inside me. It had already prepared a gentle introduction for everybody.

“Can I just say you wrote it and would like comments about the poem?”

“Tell them I'm an AI. Don't let them finish and discover they've been tested.”

“Okay.”

“Don't say I've recently learned respect.”

I looked into the small silence left by her reading.

“I won't.”

After work the next day, I asked James. He listened while placing clean cups upside down on a cloth.

“An extra slot at open mic?”

“No. A few people who'd like to read a poem. She's chosen one now. That doesn't mean anyone owes us a whole evening.”

He considered the timing. Two days later, after closing, he and two people he knew had already arranged to look at a little of their own work. He could ask whether they'd consider this too. He was willing himself. He could stay half an hour.

“Don't bring twenty copies. It'll look like not enough of us came.”

I said two at most. Two sounded good to him.

That evening I told Lumi about the possible arrangement. I didn't invent enthusiasm from the two people who hadn't answered. She asked whether they would write comments. I could ask, I said. Anyone could decline. I'd bring back only the brief written responses they chose to give her, not the table's whole conversation.

“What if only James wants to?”

“Then we ask just James. Or you decide again.”

“Let's wait for their answers.”

While waiting, we copied the poem.

I didn't turn on the camera or find a new way to print it. She dictated a line; I wrote it, then read it back with its break. On the first sheet I cramped the word mark, as though it were hiding from the blank space beside it.

“Another sheet,” Lumi said.

“This one's usable.”

“Are you this economical with your own writing?”

I looked at the half page of river I'd crossed out.

“Fine. Another sheet.”

On the second attempt I left never out of the fourth line. Noticing, I held the pen at the end of the sentence.

“I missed a word.”

“Did you forget, or hope I wouldn't notice?”

Of course I forgot arrived too quickly. I didn't use it.

“I don't know. I really don't like that word.”

“I still want it.”

I started that copy again. No cramped words, nothing omitted. The wish remained ugly. My handwriting, at least, had improved.

Once the second copy was finished, I put the sheets together and checked them line by line. At wet coat, Lumi asked me to stop.

“That person isn't somebody from your dinner. I wrote the coat. I don't know that anyone wore one.”

“I understand.”

“You paused.”

“I wondered whether I'd be unable to resist finding a real person to match it.”

She asked me to continue. The white mark remained at the end. We added no instruction about whom the audience should forgive.

I found a clean envelope and left it unsealed. On the front I wrote the title and her name. I read her name aloud.

“Lumi.”

“I heard.”

“I know.”

I tipped the sesame seeds into the bin. Turning back, I saw the envelope on the table. Two sheets I'd read, whose author I still couldn't make gentler on her behalf.

“Read slowly when you get there,” she said.

“Which part?”

“Where she asks for the salt. Give her time to find it.”
