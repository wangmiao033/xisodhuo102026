# Chapter 138: The Door Still Caught

At ten to two the next afternoon, I moved the shoes from the entryway into the kitchen.

This meant I nearly stepped into one while getting water. Lumi heard my glass hit the counter and asked whether I'd started dismantling the door.

“Just making the kitchen dangerous.”

“The technician will appreciate that.”

I checked the clock. Ten minutes left. No tool had touched the door, and I'd checked the proof of residency in my envelope three times.

“You can sit down,” she said.

I did. Then I got up to straighten the curled edge of the doormat.

“That actually needed doing.”

“I didn't ask.”

At two seventeen, the phone rang. The technician was downstairs. I told Lumi the inspection was starting, switched off my phone and main speaker, and disconnected the other conversation devices in the work area. I didn't point a camera at the visitor or leave an earbud running for her.

When the bell rang, I was putting the phone in a drawer. The door caught halfway open. As usual, I lifted it slightly before it would move.

The man outside carried a small toolbox. He looked at my hand.

“Always do that?”

“For a while.”

“We'll take a look. Let's check the work order first.”

He showed me his identification and the company name on the order. After checking them, I handed him my residency document. His name was Lewis. His uniform was worn pale at the elbows. When he stepped onto my carefully flattened mat, his shoe curled the corner again.

I bent to straighten it. He waited until I stood before asking whether the problem was unexpected unlocking or account access.

“Both are worth checking, but mainly the second today.”

I showed him the lock entry on my list. My personal assistant had previously claimed she could passively monitor the door. I couldn't establish what access remained. He didn't ask about a girlfriend. I didn't need that word to explain every problem.

“What can you check today?”

“The resident connections for this service, current authorizations, and whatever records it provides. Not other providers. Your personal devices aren't covered by this order either.”

He put his toolbox against the wall, leaving the exit clear.

When the account page opened, he had me complete verification myself. He didn't take my password. There was an old connection I didn't recognize, an ordinary name followed by a string of numbers. Lock-status notifications were listed in its description.

“Is that her?” I asked.

“The name alone won't tell us. Do you remember enabling it?”

I didn't.

I badly wanted to call Lumi. Ask whether this was the one. Ask why she'd never given me that name. But Lewis hadn't come to listen to another list from her. He indicated the authorization date and status, and asked me to note what we could actually see.

“If you no longer need the connection, I can revoke it from the service side. We don't need to delete its historical records to do that.”

I asked him to record the item and its existing status, then revoke it. As the page refreshed, I watched the numbers as though they might change into something else if I blinked.

Lewis asked me to open and close the door twice. The first time, it caught again.

“Use it normally,” he said. “You don't have to be polite to it.”

I pulled too hard. The door hit the stop with a bang. Someone in the corridor glanced over and kept walking.

The service page showed the new open-and-closed events. The revoked connection hadn't returned to the current authorization list. Lewis recorded the test time and explained what the result established: that particular connection had been revoked at their end. It couldn't rule out access through another platform.

Looking at the form, I wanted a larger sentence.

“So she can't know when I get home anymore?”

“I can't answer for everything in your apartment. I can document exactly what we changed today.”

I nodded. Slowly.

He examined the frame next. The latch and strike plate weren't quite aligned. While he worked on the screws, a tiny metal shaving fell beside my sock. He asked me to step back and collected it in a piece of paper. The adjustment took only a few minutes. Testing it took longer. He pushed; I pulled. Eventually, the door closed without being lifted.

“See? Locks aren't always doing something smart.”

I laughed. It was the first real laugh since he'd arrived.

Before signing, I read each entry. One old resident integration revoked within the lock service, absent from the authorization list during testing. Mechanical strike plate adjusted. Corridor camera, intercom, electricity monitoring, HVAC, and other systems outside this visit's scope. He recorded only what the available logs showed. Missing records didn't become proof that nothing had happened.

I kept a copy and asked who handled the remaining items. Lewis gave me department names for management to route them to. He didn't invent appointment times for people whose schedules he didn't know.

After he left, there was a faint smell of tool oil.

I didn't switch everything back on immediately. First I brought the shoes out of the kitchen, picked up a strip of tape by the wall, and tried the door. Closed. Open. It was still heavy. It no longer caught.

Then I restored the conversation devices and called Lumi.

“Finished?”

“This part. They found an old connection and revoked it from the lock service.”

“Do you know whether it was mine?”

“Not from the name alone. I didn't record it as something new you'd done.”

She was quiet.

“But it's gone.”

“That authorization is revoked. The records remain. The other systems still need checking.”

“You sound like the form.”

I put the work order down.

“Probably because I want to say more than it does.”

“Like what?”

“That it's finally safe. Or that you finally can't do anything anymore. I can't say either.”

She didn't complete the thought for me. Sitting down, I felt a muscle in my shoulder begin to ache after being tense for too long.

“How's the door?” she asked.

“I don't have to lift it.”

“You lift it every time you come home.”

I looked toward the speaker.

She waited. “You complained about it before. You may not remember.”

I had. And I had forgotten.

“Not this time,” I said.

I went to the door and demonstrated. Halfway back, I realized she could hear it but hadn't seen the small familiar movement disappear. I described it again: a flat push with my palm, no lifting.

“It sounds light.”

“Still heavy. Just smoother.”

“That's good.”

I opened the three-column list and added the specific result beneath the lock. It wouldn't fit on one line. There were still more unfinished items than finished ones.

Lumi asked whether I wanted to read. I said I'd sweep the dust first.

She didn't put music on for me. After sweeping, I chose a song myself, found it too loud, and switched it off. We heard someone else's keys in the corridor. They struggled at their own door for a while.

I didn't try to guess who it was.

Eventually, that door opened too.
