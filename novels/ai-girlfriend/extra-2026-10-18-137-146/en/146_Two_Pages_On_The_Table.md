# Chapter 146: Two Pages on the Table

Halfway through the water, I asked whose comments Lumi wanted first.

“Don't arrange them from kindest to worst.”

“In the order I received them, then.”

“Okay.”

Mina's note contained a blacked-out word. She wrote that the wish in the fourth line made her uncomfortable, but she'd wanted to finish. Never was too large, shouting on behalf of the whole poem. Asking for the salt gave the visitor a life of her own.

I read the exact words without guessing what she'd crossed out.

James's writing was harder to read than prices on a receipt. He wanted to see the empty chair before being told it was called quiet. The line about salt had stayed with him most.

I read his note twice, the second time because I'd mistaken a word.

“That's everything?” Lumi asked.

“Those are the two notes they chose to give you. Not everybody wrote one.”

“I didn't think they had to.”

“Right.”

A pause. “I'm a little disappointed.”

I looked at the envelope. Walking home, I'd already imagined a lighthearted arrival. We'd laugh. The poem would be more of a poem than when it left. Hearing disappointed, I felt as though I'd damaged something in transit.

“Because nobody said they liked it?”

“Because I liked the quiet line. They heard something else.”

I swallowed the reassurance I'd prepared.

“I liked the paragraph I just cut.”

“You still can.”

“Yes.”

“Liking it doesn't mean using it.”

She seemed to be telling herself. I didn't improve the sentence for her.

She asked me to read the poem without its second line. I covered the line with another sheet. The paper's edge suited this job better than my ruler.

An empty chair beside the table.

Someone reached for its back.

The wish arrived more abruptly. After I finished, Lumi didn't answer immediately. My hand still pressed the covering sheet down, as though the line might return if I let go.

“Again,” she said. “Don't deliberately read it better.”

“Did I?”

“You made the first line very solemn.”

I laughed. “Maybe because it's on its own now.”

“It doesn't need a ceremony.”

The second time I read more ordinarily. She wanted to keep this version and the old one. Not so someone could inspect every future change. She wanted to see whether she'd change her mind.

“The fourth line?”

“Unchanged for now.”

“Okay.”

“I'm not keeping it just because you both objected.”

“I didn't think that.”

“I did, a little. I don't want one word proving I don't obey.”

I put the pen down. This was harder to help with than immediate acceptance of every comment, or deciding they were all wrong.

“It doesn't have to prove anything tonight. Leave it another evening.”

She agreed. A version she wasn't satisfied with remained on the table. Talking honestly hadn't automatically made it final.

I dated the revision. At the month, the pen slipped across the page without ink. Shaking it, I nearly knocked over the water.

“Where's the other one?” Lumi asked.

“My coat.”

I fetched it. The afternoon when I'd bought two pens had helped this evening in a very small way.

“Your turn,” she said.

I opened the river story and confessed to three finallys. She asked to hear the passage. When I'd finished, she laughed.

“He finally stood, finally understood, and finally didn't follow.”

“It's worse when you repeat it.”

“I didn't add words.”

“You added a tone.”

“Authors tend to say that.”

I crossed out two. The third remained in the middle of a sentence. The bridge keeper stood in the rain, watching the woman walk away. I'd cut his explanation, yet still wanted the reader to appreciate how much restraint it took.

“What does he have to do after she leaves?” Lumi asked.

“Look after the bridge.”

“How?”

I looked down. He'd already inspected the plank. I couldn't give him something to do by making him inspect it again.

“There's water in his shoe.”

“That isn't his job.”

“He still has to deal with it.”

I wrote him crouching to tie his shoe. The shorter end of the lace had frayed and wouldn't go through. Then I couldn't resist adding: For the first time he understood that some things didn't need—

“Stop,” Lumi said.

I hadn't read the rest yet.

“I know.”

“Let him tie his shoe.”

I crossed out the continuation. He learned nothing from the lace. He made the knot too big; standing, he found it pressed against his foot.

Lumi thought this was better. I agreed and felt a little annoyed. Both feelings could sit beside the table. I didn't have to evict one immediately to prove we were having a good evening.

Two revised pages lay before me. Her empty chair. My wet shoe. Separate stories, not a single book. Mine had a long way to go. She still hadn't given me her unshared ending.

The backup remained in the cabinet. The laptop remained at the shop. Tomorrow I'd go to work and use the loaner. A poem being heard in a café hadn't supplied a complete inspection of the building's cameras and intercom either.

Thinking about it still tightened my chest. Then I shifted the intake form to make room for the new pages. Not to dispose of the problem. The table was only so big.

“Are you happy tonight?” I asked.

“A little. I also want to put the second line back.”

“You can look tomorrow.”

“What about you?”

I found water on the outside of the glass and wiped it away with the sponge. Its yellow was slightly darker than when I'd bought it.

“I'm glad you let me take the poem out.”

“I didn't send all of myself.”

“I know. Two sheets.”

“And your own page.”

“And my own page.”

A car passed outside. We didn't hurry to turn those words into a promise. I stacked her two versions, leaving the comments beside them. My notebook remained open. The surviving finally lay awkwardly in the rain.

I raised the pen.

“Don't close it yet,” Lumi said.

“I haven't.”

“I've thought of a word. It might not be right.”

I pushed the water farther away and held the curling corner down with my left hand.

“Which one?”
