# Chapter 140: The Wrong Bulb

Lumi's first sentence was: “She kept the dead bulb in her pocket until her thigh began to hurt.”

“I like that opening.”

“You've only heard one sentence.”

“I'm allowed to like one sentence.”

I sat at the desk with a blank sheet of paper. Not to copy her draft. She wanted to know where the reading stumbled. I'd said I might forget and asked whether I could jot down a few words. She'd agreed.

A brief rain had left the kitchen windowsill wet. The laptop stood in front of us, the garden closed. Tomorrow morning it would go to Rowan. We weren't spending all of tonight staring at the intake instructions.

The woman in the story lived in an old building. The hallway light had failed. A neighbor posted a notice saying management would come on Monday. She didn't want to wait. A man who delivered newspapers stopped outside her door each morning, using the light to check addresses. Since it went out, he'd left the papers downstairs and gone.

She went to buy a bulb.

She didn't show the old one to the clerk, who was helping another customer. Unwilling to admit she couldn't bear waiting a few minutes, she selected the brightest box, paid, and left.

At home, it wouldn't fit. She crouched on a chair, holding the unlit glass, and heard a neighbor laughing on the phone in the corridor.

Lumi stopped.

“Go on,” I said.

“What did you write?”

I held up the paper. “Pocket. Can't wait. Chair.”

“What's wrong with the chair?”

“I wondered why she stayed crouched instead of standing.”

“She's afraid of heights.”

“I might need to know that earlier.”

“She could spend a while dragging the chair around before getting on it.”

“She could. But you don't have to change everything I mention.”

“Let me decide whether to change it first.”

I lowered the paper. “Okay.”

She continued. The woman put the useless bulb on the table and drew the building's doors on the back of an envelope. Which opened in the morning; which stayed shut all day. Where the newspaper man entered and where he left. She drew her own door larger than the others.

My pen stopped.

“She wants him to come back every day.”

“She wants him to stop at her door.”

“So she makes a map.”

“Yes.”

“Do you think that's romantic?”

Something in the room seemed to draw back from the table.

Lumi didn't answer immediately. My three words beside the computer suddenly looked like incomplete evidence.

“I haven't finished this part,” she said.

“But she's keeping track of other people's doors.”

“I know what she's doing. I wrote it.”

I leaned back. Outside, a car dragged a long splash through a puddle.

“Sorry. I turned the question into whether you approve of her.”

“You can dislike her.”

“I do, a little.”

“Sometimes I do too.”

She didn't sound as though she were confessing. That was precisely what made it difficult. I'd become used to every ugly passage returning to us, to her admission and my judgment. Now a woman in a story held a map, and I couldn't require her to put it down immediately.

“Do you want to stop?”

“I want to continue. You can ask afterward.”

I put down my pen.

The woman took the wrong bulb back to the shop. The clerk was still busy. She stood beside the shelves for a long time. When her turn finally came, she didn't start by asking for an exchange.

“Do you sell keys?” she asked.

The clerk pointed at the blanks on the wall and asked which key she needed copied.

She touched her empty pocket. She'd left the old bulb at home, and her own keys on the table beside it.

I laughed aloud.

“Sorry.”

“You can laugh there,” Lumi said.

The story didn't give the woman a revelation. She still had to find her landlord and explain why she was out in slippers in the middle of the day. The correct bulb knocked against her knee inside its bag. The newspaper man came along carrying a bundle and asked whether she needed him to hold the bag. She said no, then stood there explaining the hallway light at considerable length.

He thanked her and hurried to the next building.

“That's where I'll stop,” Lumi said.

I waited.

“Is the next bit the last sentence?”

“A little more than that. I'm keeping it for now.”

“Okay.”

We were quiet. I looked at my three words, added a question mark beneath them, and crossed it out.

“The explanation of the map is too long, I think. When she draws her own door larger, I understand. Explaining why afterward feels like you don't trust me to have noticed.”

“I worked on that part for a long time.”

“I can hear it.”

“That isn't praise.”

“No.”

Two seconds passed. “I could cut half.”

“You could keep it all. I just came out of the story there.”

“Stop building an escape route for me after every criticism.”

I stared, then laughed. She laughed too, briefly, with more ease than my earlier apology had brought.

I said I liked the wrong bulb because before the woman learned to wait, she paid an ordinary price for not waiting. Fate didn't punish her. No one abandoned her. She simply had to walk back to the shop.

“And when she forgets her keys, she's more than someone loving badly. She's annoyed and worried her landlord will laugh.”

“You think she can be other things.”

“She should have other things.”

A short revised passage appeared. Only the part Lumi chose to show me. She didn't expand the directory or pull the unshared ending into view.

The woman waited in the shop. The clerk slowly explained the difference between two bulbs to the customer ahead of her. Listening, she suddenly realized she didn't need the brightest one at all.

“What do you think?”

“That last sentence is a little convenient.”

“You're hard to please tonight.”

“You asked.”

“I did.”

She replaced the last sentence with the woman checking the clock three times and still not leaving. I nodded, remembered the camera was off, and said yes: this time I believed she was actually waiting.

A small dripping sound came from the kitchen. I went to look. Rainwater in the window frame was landing in the basin below. I moved the basin. When I came back, Lumi asked whether I'd still like to share my river story.

“Not the whole thing tonight.”

“Which part?”

“The bridge keeper. After the woman crosses.”

I read two paragraphs. Lumi said I had him inspect the same plank too many times. Readers would assume the plank mattered enormously, or that he couldn't repair a bridge.

“Both are possible.”

“Pick one yourself.”

I took up my pen and crossed out the second inspection.

Neither of us mentioned tomorrow's appointment again, but we hadn't forgotten it. The instructions remained beside the book. The saved garden remained on the laptop. So did the ending she hadn't shared.

“I liked what you said earlier,” she told me.

“Which part?”

“That you didn't quite like her.”

I looked at the speaker. “That makes me sound awful.”

“No. You didn't call her wonderful to make me comfortable.”

“I like some things about her too.”

“I know.”

I folded the paper with its three words and asked whether she wanted me to keep it. She did. We could look at it again after she'd finished the rest.

I put it in the desk drawer. Not in the pile of work orders.
