# Chapter 145: Before the Last Chairs Went Up

Before I left, Lumi asked me to check the envelope again.

“Two copies. The right poem.”

“I meant, did you bring your own story too?”

I looked at my bag. The river story remained in the notebook on the desk. Picking it up, I felt unexpectedly embarrassed.

“Nearly forgot.”

“You can't spend the entire evening being a delivery service.”

James had confirmed that afternoon that the other two were willing to hear her poem and knew its author was an AI. We were bringing only “The Chair.” The ending of her fiction, still unshared, hadn't become supplementary material she owed us.

I switched off the phone and left it home. No earbuds, no recording. Lumi said she'd want to know when I'd return. The reading occupied half an hour after closing, I said. If I walked slowly, I'd be a little later. She said okay without stretching the word.

Most café chairs were upside down on tables. One table for four remained at the back. James was dividing a little piece of cake into four very unequal portions.

“Open favoritism,” a woman said.

I recognized the woman who'd offered me a chair at dinner. She introduced herself as Mina, pointed at the largest piece, and claimed it. Beside her, a man rolled up a sleeve marked with blue paint. He could stay only until his next bus. His name was Owen.

There was no audience area. Just four chairs. Sitting, I nearly knocked my knee against a table leg.

James introduced me by saying, “Elliot brought a poem by Lumi and something of his own.”

My two prepared sentences of background had nowhere to go. Nobody seemed to miss them.

Mina read first, from an unfinished story. Someone who ran a bakery burned the first batch of bread and refused to admit it. At a pause, I laughed. She looked up to ask whether we'd laughed where she'd intended.

We discussed a few lines. Ten minutes passed. I hadn't explained my life, and the evening had started anyway.

At my turn, I distributed the two copies across the table.

“She chose this and would like comments about the poem itself. You don't have to write encouragement for my sake. Or explain everything you think about AI.”

“Good,” Owen said. “Everything I think won't fit before the bus.”

I laughed and began.

The fourth line was harder here than at home.

I wished she'd never find a place to sit.

The paper was thin. Light showed through it. In my handwriting the last stroke lingered. I didn't substitute anything. At the salt, I paused long enough for someone to look for it.

Afterward, a cup touched a saucer. No applause. It wasn't slow in arriving; we hadn't arranged that sort of evening.

Mina asked to read it again herself. She read faster than I had, a finger stopping at the fourth line.

“I don't like the person speaking.”

My body tightened first.

“But I wanted to finish. The salt made me wonder whether the person arriving even noticed the malice.”

“She's recently—” I began.

Mina looked up.

“No. I was going to explain the author. Go on.”

She tapped the sheet. “Never feels too big to me. I like that the wish is unbecoming. But that word asks me to imagine so many stories around it. Maybe I just don't want to today.”

Owen thought the last two lines could go.

“We've been staring at the mark. Then it tells us the mark matters more than the face. It seems afraid we haven't understood.”

Mina disagreed. Without those lines, the earlier wish could become merely a passing nasty thought. Remembering the mark rather than the face suggested a problem that continued afterward.

They looked at the same sheet. Neither surrendered an opinion.

Instinctively, I turned toward James, waiting for a verdict. He was moving cake crumbs within reach of his fork.

“I remember the salt. And the first line. The quiet line took me out a little. I wanted to see the empty chair before being told what to call it.”

I noted a few words, then stopped. Not everything I wrote down automatically became material I could take home to Lumi.

“If you'd like to leave her a sentence or two, here's blank paper. No obligation. I'm not keeping a record of the whole conversation.”

Owen checked the time. “I won't write. Not because I dislike it. I really have to go.”

He pushed his copy back. I said okay without asking how much, precisely, he had liked it.

Before leaving, he heard a little of my river story. I'd cut one paragraph of explanation. The bridge keeper still managed to stand in the rain like someone waiting for praise.

“Three finallys,” Owen said.

“What?”

“In that passage. Every sentence's trying very hard to clock off.”

I counted. Three.

Mina laughed aloud. So did I, without defending myself first. Owen pulled down his sleeve and grabbed his bag. James pointed through the window; Owen hurried out. The bus hadn't arrived, but he didn't want to gamble with the minutes.

Mina wrote a note, then changed one word. Handing it directly to me, she said those sentences could go to Lumi. James wrote two lines too, confirming they were comments for the author.

They hadn't written we accept you. Lumi hadn't requested that. I collected both poem copies and kept the notes separate. A sheet someone had read didn't become a gift for me to leave behind.

I helped James put the last chairs on the tables. Lifting the one I'd used, I scraped a leg against the floor. I looked for a mark. Nothing.

“More cooperative in a poem,” I said.

James hadn't understood. I shook my head, turned the chair over, and didn't explain.

Walking home, I discovered the notebook had bent an envelope corner. Beneath a streetlight I slowly flattened it. Leaving the café hadn't made the words gentler or turned them into votes. I couldn't even honestly say everybody liked the poem.

I could bring back a poem that had actually been read, two notes willingly given to its author, and my own poorly concealed three finallys.

At home, I switched on the conversation devices and called to her.

“I'm back.”

“Yes. How did it go?” she asked.

I placed the envelope on a dry part of the desk, away from the work orders.

“I read it. I didn't change the fourth line.”

She paused. “Drink some water first. You always try to tell everything quickly and end up coughing.”

I filled a glass. Returning, I found the two little notes side by side. I hadn't selected either one to stand for the whole evening.
