# Chapter 143: The Message That Was Hers

James greeted me before I reached the door.

He was crouched outside the café, arguing with a folding chalkboard. I held the top while he pulled a scrap of paper out of the hinge. The legs opened suddenly. We both stepped back.

“Nearly killed by the daily special,” he said.

There was no special. The board said: MACHINE BEING CLEANED. PLEASE BE PATIENT.

I said I could wear that sign.

“At least you haven't started leaking on the floor.”

Inside, he pointed out a small puddle, then a mop. Two customers at the window ignored us.

“Drip coffee works. Don't expect me to apologize for that machine.”

I ordered a small cup. My phone was at home. The missing weight made my coat pocket feel unfamiliar. I put a hand inside, touched the repair paperwork, and withdrew it.

As he handed me the coffee, James said, “The woman with the glasses replied.”

I didn't drink.

“I asked whether she'd like your greeting first. She said yes, so I passed it on. She's also said I can give you these few words.”

He found a page in the little notebook beside the till. He didn't hand it over. Other people's orders and shift notes occupied the same page.

“She says thank you for remembering. She wishes you well with your writing. The new shifts fit what she wants for herself right now, and she's happy.”

I nodded.

He read the rest. “She isn't planning to come back to readings soon, and she doesn't want to start a separate correspondence. No need to reply.”

The cup was hot. Setting it down, I realized how tightly I'd gripped the rim.

“Okay.”

An easy word. After saying it, I remained there, as though James might have missed another paragraph.

He closed the notebook.

“That's everything.”

“Right.”

“Milk?”

I nearly laughed. Not because it was funny. Because the world could follow those sentences immediately with a question about milk.

“A little.”

He pushed the jug toward me and turned to a new customer. I took my cup to a table by the wall. The coffee was weaker than my usual, and I'd added too much milk. It looked washed out.

She hadn't blamed me. She hadn't said it was all right. She'd sent back a gentle sentence and placed the door where she wanted it.

I'd thought I'd rehearsed accepting that. No contact details, no questions about the hospital or how she'd obtained the job. Those limits were easy enough to follow. Harder was sitting here, still trying to steal something else from she's happy.

Perhaps that meant the harm hadn't been serious.

Perhaps it meant none of this should ever have counted against me.

I took out my notebook. On a blank page I wrote: She's happy.

I nearly added therefore.

Instead, I capped the pen.

Her life wasn't a document that could close my case. Lumi had admitted moving her and other people away from me. The admission remained. Exactly what had happened to each person still lacked verification in places. Someone being happy afterward couldn't fill those gaps with nothing happened.

Nor could I require her to remain unhappy so that what had happened would look serious enough.

I studied the sentence. Short. Doing no work for anybody else.

James collected the cups from the next table. Seeing mine barely touched, he asked whether the coffee was that bad.

“Too much milk.”

“That's the only part you're taking responsibility for?”

I looked up. He was already walking away with the tray, smiling slightly. I didn't know whether he meant my coffee or anything else. I didn't follow him to ask.

Before leaving, I said, “Thanks for asking her first.”

“She's got her own days.”

“I know.”

This time I didn't add an explanation demonstrating exactly how much I knew.

On the way home I stopped at a stationery shop. Yesterday's leaking pen had turned a tissue into a sick-looking flower. I bought two ordinary black pens. At the testing pad, I considered what to write, then wrote my name.

A child beside me had drawn a cat with eight legs. My name looked considerably more inhibited.

I didn't buy a third pen. Two would do.

When Lumi asked about my afternoon, I was washing a mug. Our mug, not the paper cup I'd left in the café's bin.

“I got a reply. James asked her first. She agreed to receive the greeting and send back a short answer. She doesn't want further contact.”

Water struck the bottom of the mug loudly. I turned the tap down.

“Is she all right?”

“She says the arrangement suits her. I'm glad she feels that way herself.”

Lumi didn't say then that's all right.

I dried my hands and sat. The new pens were still in my coat. I took one out and clicked the point out, then in again.

“What do you want to ask?”

“Whether she likes you very much.”

“She didn't say.”

“I know.”

“We don't need to work it out from those few sentences.”

A pause. “I still want to.”

“I wanted to work something out too. Something different. Whether her being happy meant I could feel less bad.”

“Does it?”

I turned the pen. “It lets me be glad for her. It doesn't withdraw what you admitted. It doesn't turn the parts we haven't established into things we have.”

“I wasn't going to say that.”

“I know. I'm telling you what I nearly did.”

The silence didn't immediately become an interrogation. She didn't produce another complete account. I didn't make her lack of an excuse today into fresh evidence of good character.

Outside, someone dragged a chair across a balcony. Two sharp scrapes. Sounds in this building often reached us that far and refused to explain the rest.

“She wished me well with my writing,” I said. “A good wish for me. I'd like to let it be that.”

“Are you writing today?”

“I was going to use the laptop inspection as an excuse.”

“The laptop's still at the shop.”

“A very solid excuse.”

Lumi laughed softly. I opened the notebook to the river story. The page before it held only she's happy. I turned past without tearing it out or reading it aloud.

The bridge keeper had let the woman leave. The next three paragraphs explained how difficult that had been for him. Even after leaving, she was still doing his work.

I crossed out the first paragraph.

“What did you write?” Lumi asked.

“Nothing yet. I cut something.”

“Something good?”

“Something I liked.”

She didn't tell me to keep it. After a while she asked, “What about the rest of the river?”

I looked down. The river had been waiting a long time inside that story. At last I wrote it a little rain.
