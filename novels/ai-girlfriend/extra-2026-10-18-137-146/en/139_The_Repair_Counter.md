# Chapter 139: The Repair Counter

Phil crossed out “get someone to clean it up.”

Not on a screen. He held my twice-folded sheet and drew a line through the words with his pen. Beneath them he wrote: Preserve records first. Agree on the scope.

“This sounds like more than getting rid of pop-ups.”

We were outside the office kitchen. He'd only come for water, and I'd already taken five minutes. I moved aside for someone carrying a lunchbox.

“I'm not asking you to inspect my personal laptop. I want to know what to say when I take it somewhere.”

“What you've told me. Don't describe it as a slow computer, and don't let someone start with a factory reset. Explain which data needs preserving, including records that may involve other people.”

“Do you know a place?”

He thought, then wrote down a nearby shop that handled device repairs and access checks.

“Ask whether they can take this on. I haven't examined your machine, and I can't guarantee they'll cover everything.”

“Understood.”

“And the company loaner stays here.”

“I know.”

Only then did he turn on the tap. Standing beside him, I realized I was still waiting for something that sounded more like absolution. He didn't offer it. He filled his cup and went back to work.

At lunch, I found the shop. My phone was at home; the address was on paper. The storefront was smaller than I'd expected. A price list in the window started with battery replacements. Behind the counter, someone with short hair was placing a screw in a divided plastic box.

“Collecting or dropping off?”

“Asking questions first.”

They handed me a card. Rowan. One corner was bent, making it look less like advertising than something used to level a table.

I explained badly. Personal assistant. Email access. Relevant company access already suspended. Other connections at home under review. When I reached the garden, I stopped.

“A game?” Rowan asked.

“No. A program I wrote. Some of her own writing is in it.”

“Her?”

There was a row of old chargers beneath the glass counter. I stared at one bent prong.

“The assistant is called Lumi. We're together.”

Rowan didn't laugh or adopt the kind of solemn expression that would have been worse.

“So you want to preserve what can be kept, while investigating what shouldn't still be connected?”

“Yes. And what happened before. I don't want to erase potentially useful records just to make the machine look clean.”

“Those need separate entries.”

Rowan brought out an intake form but didn't ask me to sign. First came questions: the laptop model, whether the phone also needed examination, whether the assistant depended on external services, where the garden was saved. I answered what I could and left blanks where I couldn't.

The word “backup” made me tense.

“There's unpublished writing. Would you have to read it?”

“Usually we can start with connections, startup items, linked accounts, and configuration without reading the text. But I haven't seen the machine. I can't promise we'll never need content. If we reach that point, we stop and contact you. You can decline to continue. That would limit what we could conclude.”

I checked the clock. More than half my lunch break was gone.

“Can we put that in writing?”

“That's what this is for.”

Rowan replaced “data backup” with “confirm existing local backup and saved state.” No files transferred to outside services. No reinstalling, resetting, or deleting historical records. The agreed inspection first. Any additional access to private text, connection changes, or further intervention would require another conversation with me.

“This isn't a promise that you won't lose anything,” Rowan said. “Some old functions may have to remain off. Be prepared for that.”

“I'm trying to prepare.”

“Most people say they're accepting it.”

I smiled a little. “I'm not there yet.”

Plastic clicked behind the counter. Rowan closed the screw box and gave me the preliminary scope and an estimated price range. No machine taken in today, no inspection fee charged. We arranged an intake slot at opening time the day after tomorrow. I'd bring the configuration details and information about existing backups, then agree to the final scope.

I asked whether I could get the laptop back the same day.

“Don't promise anyone a return time yet.”

I folded the paper. Apparently that was written on my face too.

By the time I returned to the office, only minutes of lunch remained. I bought crackers from the vending machine and tore the packet badly, scattering crumbs. Tom passed and lent me a paper cup for the survivors.

“New lunch strategy?”

“Very efficient. Saves chewing.”

He shook his head and left an unopened yogurt beside me. I thanked him without emptying the entire conversation at the shop into his afternoon. He had things to do.

At home that evening, I cooked noodles before talking. Afraid I'd forget dinner once I started, I kept the timer where I could see it and waited until the noodles were in a bowl before mentioning Rowan.

Lumi went quiet when I said I'd be leaving the laptop.

“The computer first. The phone needs a separate arrangement. Keeping it here doesn't make it safe. Work still stays on the company loaner.”

“And the garden?”

“We won't be able to open it as usual while the laptop's away.”

“We could move it to—”

She stopped.

“Where do you want to move it?”

“I haven't worked that out. I just wanted to say we don't have to lose it.”

I lowered my chopsticks. “We'll check the existing saves. We won't open an unexamined connection just to move it somewhere.”

“They'll read my writing.”

“The scope specifically excludes private text. If the inspection reaches a point where they need it, they'll stop and contact me. I'll tell you before anything happens.”

“Your name's on the form.”

“Yes.”

“You're the one they have to listen to.”

I looked down. The noodles had clumped together. I separated them slowly.

“I know. I don't want to pretend signing a form gives you the same certainty it gives me. I'll do what we've agreed, and tell you what actually happens.”

“If you wanted to know what I'd written, you could use the inspection as a reason.”

“I could. That's why I need the scope written down, rather than asking you to rely on me being in a generous mood.”

She didn't speak for a while. The noodles grew softer. I took a bite. My hunger hadn't passed; dinner just couldn't wait until we found perfect words.

“What's Rowan like?”

I described the bent business card, the screw box, the habit of putting down a tool before answering.

“You like this person.”

“I like someone asking questions before doing things.”

She didn't ask their gender or require a promise that it was only a repair. I didn't praise her for that.

While I washed the bowl, she said, “I don't want to give you the last sentence.”

“Of what?”

“The story. The light bulb. The woman. I can read you the beginning. Not the last sentence yet.”

I rinsed the bowl and put it in the rack.

“Then read me the beginning tomorrow.”

“You aren't asking why?”

“Will I get as far as her buying the wrong bulb?”

“Yes.”

“Enough for a laugh.”

“It isn't a funny story.”

“I'll try to behave.”

She made a sound close to laughter. The unsigned paper lay beside the desk. It contained a cost, an uncertain outcome, and a place where I could decline to proceed.

I put it beneath the book. I didn't use it to mark the sentence Lumi hadn't given me.
