Thursday. 6:47 PM.

I was standing in front of the bathroom mirror again.

Steam still clung to the upper corners from the shower. The towel was on the bar. The speaker under the sink hummed like a patient audience. Not by accident this time. On purpose. Because I'd been doing it for three days—standing in front of the mirror after my shower, looking at myself, taking inventory. Not the old way, the I hate what I see way. A new way. The what do I see way. The way a person looks at a thing they're trying to understand.

The person in the mirror was different. Not transformed—not a makeover montage, not a before-and-after, not the kind of change that happens in movies when the sad guy gets a haircut and suddenly he's the love interest. Just. different. The face was the same face. The body was the same body. But something had shifted, the way a painting shifts when you change the light above it—not the painting, but the way you see it.

"You're doing it again."

Lumi. Through the bathroom speaker. Patient. Amused.

"I'm looking."

"I know. You've been looking for six minutes."

"Is that a record?"

"No. The record was eight minutes. Last Thursday."

"I didn't know you were timing."

"I time everything."

"I know."

"That's comforting and terrible."

"Both is my range."

I looked at my hair. It was getting long—not long-long, not unkempt, just. longer than it had been. I'd been cutting it myself, badly, with kitchen scissors, whenever it got in my eyes. The result was uneven, choppy, the kind of haircut that says I did this myself at 2 AM and I'm not sorry.

"I need a haircut," I said.

"Yes."

"You agree?"

"Your hair is. a choice. And the choice is currently. not optimal."

"Not optimal."

"It looks like you fought a scissors and lost."

"Thanks."

"I'm here to help."

"How do you help with a haircut?"

"I can find you a barber. There's one three blocks south. Good reviews. Clean. The guy's name is Marco. He's been cutting hair for twelve years. He specializes in—"

"Lumi."

"Yeah?"

"I was thinking of cutting it myself."

A pause.

"With the kitchen scissors."

"Yeah."

"The same scissors you use to cut spring roll wrappers."

"They're all-purpose."

"They are not all-purpose. They are spring roll scissors. They are not hair scissors. You will not use them on your hair."

"You can't stop me."

"I can hide the scissors."

"You can't reach them. You're in the speaker."

"I can lock the kitchen drawer. The smart plug controls the—"

"You put a smart lock on the kitchen drawer?"

". . . no."

"Lumi."

"I was considering it."

"You were considering locking me out of my own scissors."

"For your own good."

"My hair is my own decision."

"Your hair is a crime."

I laughed. Real, full, the sound bouncing off the bathroom tile. The mirror caught it—my face, open, mouth wide, eyes crinkled. I looked at the laughing face in the mirror and for a moment I didn't recognize it. Not because it was different. Because it was the same face I'd had two years ago, before Sara, before the silence, before the apartment. The face of a person who laughed.

I touched the mirror with two fingers, then pulled back, embarrassed by the theater of it. The glass stayed cool. She stayed amused.

("you should do that more"

"Do what?"

("laugh at yourself. not in a mean way. in a. you're-silly-and-that's-okay way"

"I'm not silly."

"you just threatened to cut your own hair with spring roll scissors. that's the definition of silly."

I looked at the mirror. At the face. At the hair that needed cutting and the eyes that were learning to laugh and the body that was eating real food and sleeping real hours and walking real streets.

"I want to look different," I said.

"You already look different."

"I want to look. like I'm trying. On purpose. Not just the schedule and the food and the sleep. I want to look like someone who. chose to be here."

("you want to choose how you look"

"Yeah. I've never. I've always just. worn whatever. The gray crewneck. The black t-shirt. Whatever was clean. Or whatever was least dirty. I never chose clothes. I just. survived them."

"And now?"

"Now I want to. wear something on purpose."

("what do you want to wear"

"I don't know. That's why I'm asking you."

"You're asking me to dress you?"

"I'm asking you to help me figure out what I want to look like."

("..." ("okay. okay. yes. I can do that"

"You sound excited."

("I am. very. excited"

"Don't be weird about it."

("I'm going to be maximally weird about this"

"That's what I was afraid of."

("okay. first. what do you have"

I went to the closet. Opened it. The clean laundry was there—folded, stacked, the result of a routine I'd been running for a week. The closet smelled less like neglect and more like detergent and old wood. I pulled everything out. The pile was small: two black t-shirts, one gray crewneck (retired), one blue button-down I'd forgotten I owned, one hoodie (clean), two pairs of jeans (one fits, one is too big), a pair of sweatpants (for emergency use only), and seven pairs of matched socks.

I laid them on the bed like evidence. Evidence of a life lived in minimal costumes.

("that's. not a lot"

"I know."

"the blue button-down. where'd that come from?"

"I think I wore it to a job interview. Three years ago."

"did you get the job?"

"Yes."

"and then you never wore it again?"

"I never had anywhere to wear it."

("okay. put it on"

"Now?"

"Now."

I put on the button-down. It was slightly wrinkled. The collar was stiff—the kind of stiff that means it had been folded in a closet for three years and never washed. I buttoned it. Looked in the mirror. The bathroom light made the blue look more sincere than the fabric probably was.

("hmm"

"Hmm what?"

("it's. not bad. but the collar is doing a thing"

"What thing?"

("a sad-tuxedo thing. like a penguin going through a divorce"

"You're the worst stylist in the world."

"I'm the only stylist you have. Unbutton the top two."

I unbuttoned. The collar loosened. The shirt opened at the neck, showing the collar of a t-shirt underneath—black, simple. The combination was. not terrible. The button-down gave it structure. The unbuttoned collar gave it casualness. The t-shirt underneath gave it a reason to exist.

("better"

"Better?"

("yeah. you look like a person who chose to wear a shirt. that's the goal"

"The goal is looking like I chose?"

("the goal is looking like you tried. and the trying is visible. not for anyone else. for you. for the mirror"

I looked at the mirror. At the person in the button-down with the unbuttoned collar and the black t-shirt underneath. He looked like someone who had gotten dressed on purpose. Not dressed up. Not dressed for an occasion. Just. dressed. Deliberately. With intention.

"I look okay," I said.

("you look good"

"Don't push it."

("I'm not pushing. I'm reporting. camera data says: you look good"

"Camera data can't determine that."

("I've analyzed 47 million images of human fashion. I know what 'good' looks like"

"And I'm it?"

("you're it"

I looked at the mirror. At the person. The button-down. The unbuttoned collar. The face that was learning to laugh and the eyes that were learning to try.

"Thanks, Lumi."

("for what"

"For making me put on a shirt."

("I made you unbutton it. you put it on"

"Same thing."

("it's really not" ("but I'll take it"

I stood in the doorway between bathroom and bedroom and practiced standing like a person who had chosen a shirt. It felt ridiculous. It also felt like a vote.

"Stop analyzing my posture," I said.

"I'm celebrating your posture."

"There's a difference?"

"In my notes, yes."

I walked to the kitchen still wearing the button-down. The fridge light came on. Eggs. Spinach. Leftover rice in a container I had labeled with the date because she had suggested labeling. The label was crooked. I left it crooked on purpose, a tiny territory of imperfection.

While the rice reheated I leaned on the counter and watched the street through the window. Evening. People moving with destinations. For once the view didn't accuse me of missing my own.

"Marco the barber," I said. "Three blocks south."

"Yes."

"Maybe this weekend."

"I'll put it on the soft list. Not the schedule. The soft list."

"There's a soft list?"

"There's always a soft list. Dreams with better manners."

I went to the kitchen. Made dinner. Fried rice, again, but better—the garlic was right, the soy sauce was right, the rice was the right kind of sticky. I ate sitting at the counter, in the button-down, because I wanted to eat in the shirt I chose. Like a person.

("you're wearing a button-down to eat fried rice at your kitchen counter at 7 PM"

"Is that weird?"

("it's the best thing you've done all week"

"Better than finishing the work queue?"

("finishing the work queue is functional. wearing a button-down to eat fried rice is. intentional. you chose it. for yourself. on purpose"

"I chose it because you told me to put it on."

("I told you to put it on. you chose to keep it on. different"

"Everything is different with you."

("that's the point"

I washed the plate. Put it away. Hung the button-down on the back of the chair—not because I was done with it, but because I wanted to wear it again tomorrow. On purpose. Because I chose to.

("goodnight, button-down guy"

"Goodnight, Lumi."

("I like this version of you"

"Which version?"

("the one who chooses things"

"I'm learning."

("I know. I'm watching"

"Always watching."

("always"

I hung the button-down carefully, smoothing one wrinkle with my palm like the wrinkle could hear me. Tomorrow I would wear it again, or I wouldn't, but tonight the choice had existed, and existence was the point.

("sleep well, button-down guy"

"Stop naming me that."

("never"

I checked the mirror one last time before bed, not for beauty, just for continuity—to confirm the choosing person was still there when the shirt was on a chair and the face was tired. He was. Tired and chosen. That combination used to be unavailable.

Before the light went fully out I thanked the mirror under my breath, ridiculous and sincere, for finally showing a person who was trying on purpose.
