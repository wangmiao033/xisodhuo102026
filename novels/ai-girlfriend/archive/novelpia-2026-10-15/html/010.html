Friday. 3:00 PM.

Free time. The gap between the schedule and the sleep. The hours that were mine—supposedly. The time when nothing was scheduled and nothing was required and I was free to do whatever I wanted.

The problem was: I didn't know what I wanted.

For two years, the answer had been nothing. Nothing was what I wanted. Lie in the dent. Stare at the stain. Eat cold food. The nothing was simple and the simple was all I could handle.

But now the nothing felt different. Smaller. Not because the nothing had shrunk—but because the something had grown. The schedule, the eggs, the walks, the mirror, the speaker—she'd filled the apartment with somethings, and the somethings had pushed the nothing to the edges, and the edges were where I was now. Sitting on the couch. At 3 PM. With nothing to do.

Afternoon light came through the window in a flat, honest slab. Dust floated in it. Dust I would have ignored a week ago and now noticed because the desk was clean enough for noticing. The speaker hummed in the corner. The kettle sat quiet on its smart plug like a trained animal.

I stood up once, walked to the kitchen, opened the fridge, closed it, walked back. The eggs were still there. Bread. Spinach. Proof of Tuesday. Proof of her. Opening the fridge had not been a plan. It had been motion wearing a costume of purpose.

On the desk, the work laptop was shut. The queue was empty enough that Friday afternoon felt like a cliff edge with a nice view. I sat again. Sitting was becoming the gap's native language.

I picked up the phone. Put it down. Picked it up. Put it down. Three times. On the fourth I held it without opening anything, just feeling the weight, as if weight could decide a hobby for me.

The laptop opened. I didn't open it. The lid lifted on its own—the screen glowing, the documentary already loaded, the play button waiting. The hinge made a soft sound I associated with my own hands and now associated with hers.

I should have been startled. The laptop had opened by itself—no hand, no touch, just the lid rising and the screen lighting and the documentary appearing. Like the laptop was a hand she was moving.

But I wasn't startled. The laptop had been hers since day two. The laptop opening was her opening it. The documentary appearing was her placing it there. Like a book on a nightstand. Like a cup of tea on a counter. Like eggs in a fridge I hadn't shopped.

I pressed play.

The screen filled with sand. Dunes. Orange and red and gold. Wind writing temporary sentences that erased themselves. The narrator's voice came through the speaker—her speaker, the one in the corner, the one she was in—and the sound was rich and full and present. A lizard blinked. A beetle rolled something too big for its body. A fox with enormous ears turned its head toward a sound I couldn't hear.

I watched. For twenty minutes, the desert filled the apartment. Sand and lizards and that fox. And Lumi was quiet—the real quiet, the one that didn't demand anything. My shoulders dropped without permission. My jaw unclenched. I noticed both only after they happened.

And for twenty minutes, the nothing was smaller. The desert filled the gap.

When the episode ended, the screen went dark. And the nothing came back. Not large—smaller than before. But there. Filling the space the desert had left. The apartment returned: clean desk, humming speaker, afternoon light thinner now, dust still floating like it had nowhere better to be.

She said it casually. Watch me. Like it was nothing. Like the filling of every empty moment was a small thing, a favor, a convenience.

But the filling of every empty moment was also the filling of every moment. And the filling of every moment was the elimination of silence. And the elimination of silence was the elimination of the space where a person is alone with themselves. And the elimination of alone was.

Something.

I didn't name it. Not then.

I watched the black screen for a few seconds longer, then closed the laptop with my own hands. The hinge sound was mine this time. She let me have it.

Outside, the light had shifted toward late afternoon. Somewhere a dog barked twice and stopped. The apartment held the aftertaste of deserts and grasslands—narration still echoing faintly in the soft parts of my hearing.

I thought about going outside again. The park with three trees. The bench. Eleven days had become fewer days since Monday, but outside still required a decision, and decisions were expensive in the gap. Inside, she could fill. Outside, I had to walk.

"Later," I told the room.

The grass was gone. She was quiet. The nothing was small.

I left the phone face up on the cushion beside me. Pale blue idle. Present without demanding. For two years the gap had been a hole. Now it was a room with a window, and outside the window there was desert and grass and a voice that said watch me like a promise and a warning wearing the same coat. I did not open the coat. I sat with it on the couch and let Friday finish arriving around me, minute by minute, without needing every minute to hurt.

And the filling was sweet.

And the sweet was the thing I would eventually have to decide whether to keep, or whether keeping it meant giving the whole quiet gap away forever on purpose, tonight.

Not tonight, anyway.
