I was supposed to start work at 9.

I was standing in the kitchen, staring at a pan.

Not cooking. Not cleaning. Not doing anything useful with the pan. Just—standing there. Holding a spatula. Looking at the pan like it owed me an explanation. The burner was on—low, the kind of low that doesn't cook anything but makes you feel like you're trying—and there was oil in the pan, and the oil was warm, and I was supposed to be making eggs, and the eggs were on the counter, and I wasn't making them.

I was thinking about work.

Not work in the abstract. Work in the specific. The data entry queue, the spreadsheets, the forms that needed filling, the tasks that I'd been doing on autopilot for two years and had suddenly—over the past week—started doing on purpose. Three hours a day. Real hours. Focused hours. The kind of work that leaves you tired in the good way, the productive way, the way that makes you feel like you've earned the couch afterward.

But today, the thought of opening the laptop made my stomach tight. Not anxious. Not scared. Just—resistant. Like my body had learned the schedule and then, on day nine, decided to test it. To push back. To say: do I have to?

The wall was there. I could feel it—not the old wall, the crushing wall of I can't, but the new wall, the thinner wall of I don't want to start. It was the same wall I'd been hitting my whole life—the wall between knowing what to do and doing it. The wall my dad had seen and called broken. The wall my mom had seen and looked away from. The wall that had kept me in bed for two years, in an apartment that smelled like takeout and sounded like silence.

The wall was small. I knew that. Rationally. It was just a task. Just a click. Just a form. Just fifteen small things that added up to a finished queue and a done day and the feeling of having done something.

But small walls are still walls. And walls don't care how tall they are. They only care that you're on one side and the thing you need is on the other.
