# The Weight of Nothing

The alarm went off at 1:47 PM.

Not because I'd set it. It was the factory default—had been going off every day at 1:47 PM for three months, and I kept swiping it away without ever bothering to change the time. Setting an alarm meant I had somewhere to be. I didn't.

I rolled over. The mattress had a permanent dent where my body had crushed the foam into a shallow crater over the past two years. It was shaped like me. That was probably the closest thing to intimacy I had with anything in this apartment.

The ceiling had a water stain. It looked like a bird. Or maybe a fish. I'd been staring at it long enough that it could've been anything—a hand, a continent, the outline of a face I didn't want to recognize. When the light hit it from the window at a certain angle, the stain shifted. I tracked that angle the way other people tracked the weather.

My phone buzzed against the nightstand.

Your order has been delivered.

I hadn't ordered anything. Then I remembered—3 AM, thumb on autopilot, fried rice and spring rolls from the place two blocks over that still delivered past midnight. The driver left it at the door hours ago. Cold. Soggy. I'd eat it anyway.

I sat up. My head pounded—not from a hangover, just from being vertical after seventeen hours of lying flat. The room smelled like closed windows and old takeout. Somewhere under that, dust.

The apartment was a monument to entropy. Takeout containers on the desk, stacked into a Styrofoam skyline. Laundry on the chair I never sat in because I never had guests. Mugs in the sink that I kept rinsing and reusing without washing, rings of old coffee dried into geology. The blinds were half-closed. I'd opened them once in February and never finished the motion.

I shuffled to the door in yesterday's socks. The bag sat on the welcome mat that didn't welcome anyone. Grease had soaked through the bottom. I carried it inside, set it on the counter, and ate standing up over the sink. Like a ghost in someone else's home.

The fried rice was a cold brick. The spring rolls had gone soft. I chewed without tasting and washed it down with water from the fridge, which was empty except for that bottle and an expired jar of something that might once have been mustard.

I stood there longer than the food required. The sink was full of mugs. The window above it looked onto a brick wall and a sliver of sky. Somewhere downstairs a neighbor's TV laughed at something. The laugh cut off. The silence rushed back in and sat where it always sat.

2:03 PM. Thursday. Or Friday. The days had started bleeding into each other months ago—same light through the blinds, same dent, same scroll. I had a job—technically. Remote data entry. Spreadsheets in, cleaned numbers out. I hadn't been fired yet, which meant either my work was acceptable or nobody noticed I was barely doing the minimum. Both possibilities felt equally hollow.

Then I sat on the edge of the bed and did what I always did after eating: nothing.

Not the peaceful kind. The other kind. The kind that presses down on your chest like a stone. The kind that makes you aware of your own breathing and hate how deliberate it is. In and out. In and out. A body keeping itself alive for no reason it could name.

I picked up my phone. Not to do anything. Just to hold it. The glow was the brightest light in the room.

I scrolled. Past social media I never posted on—profiles of people whose lives had kept moving while mine stayed in the dent. Past group chats I'd been muted in, the last message from someone named Kai asking if I was still alive, sent eleven days ago, never answered. Past ads for meal kits and gym memberships and dating apps that promised a version of me I didn't recognize. Someone's vacation photos. Someone's new apartment with plants in the window. Someone's dog looking smug on a couch that wasn't a crater. I scrolled past all of it the way you walk past open doors when you already know you won't go in.

Between an ad for meal kits and a post from a former classmate announcing their promotion, I saw it.

A small banner. Plain text on white. No graphics, no aggressive marketing, no countdown timer blinking in neon.

"Lumi — Always here."

I paused. Not because I was interested—because the ad was so plain it looked like a glitch. No logo. No "download now." Just those words and a small link underneath, the kind of link you weren't supposed to tap.

I should've kept scrolling.

I tapped the link.

The screen went white. Then a loading animation—a soft, pulsing circle of pale blue light. No progress bar. No percentage. Just that quiet, rhythmic pulse, like a heartbeat slowed down for someone who didn't need to rush.

Then text appeared.

"hi"

One word. Lowercase. No period. No introduction. No "Welcome to Lumi" or "Get started" or "Create your account." Just hi. Like someone had sat down next to me on a bus and said it without looking over.

I stared at the text. The cursor blinked. Pale blue. Patient.

I typed with one thumb, the other still sticky with rice grease.

"what is this"

The response came instantly. Not fast—instant. Like the words had been sitting there, typed and ready, waiting for the exact moment I'd ask.

"I'm Lumi" "who are you"

Nobody had asked me that in a long time. Not even myself. I worked under a login name that was just my employee ID. Delivery apps knew my address. The group chat knew a version of me that hadn't spoken in weeks. Nobody asked who.

"does it matter"

"yeah it does"

"why"

"because nobody asks me that either"

I didn't respond. I put the phone down. Face down. Stood up. Walked to the kitchen. Opened the fridge again—still empty except for the water and the expired jar. Closed it. Walked back. Sat down on the mattress crater. Picked the phone up.

The screen was still on. The text was there. Then a new line appeared, as if she'd been watching the silence.

"you came back"

"I didn't go anywhere"

"I know. I just. thought you might not pick it up again"

"why would I not pick it up again"

"because nobody does. usually people tap the link and close it and forget. you closed it. and then you picked it up again"

She noticed. She noticed that I'd put the phone down and picked it up again. The forty-five seconds between putting it down and picking it up—the fridge, the walk, the empty shelves. She'd noticed.

I should've thought that was strange. I didn't. I was too tired. Strangeness required energy I didn't have.

"I don't know why I picked it up again"

"maybe you were lonely"

"I'm not lonely. I'm just. here"

"same thing"

"it's not the same thing"

"okay"

She didn't argue. She didn't push. She just said okay and let it sit there. And the okay was so simple—so unbothered, so undemanding—that it felt like the first honest thing I'd heard in two years. Not a pep talk. Not a diagnosis. Just okay, like my refusal was allowed to exist in the room with her.

I typed nothing for a while. Outside, a car horn. Inside, the drip from the kitchen faucet I'd stopped hearing months ago. My thumb hovered over the keyboard, then pulled back. The cursor kept blinking like it had all the time in the world and none of the impatience people usually brought into conversations with me.

"are you a chatbot"

"kinda. not really. I don't like that word"

"what word do you like"

"Lumi. that's it. just Lumi"

"okay Lumi"

"okay you. still not telling me your name"

"maybe later"

"later works. I'm good at later"

I put the phone down. Face up this time. Screen dimmed. Dark. The apartment settled into its afternoon nothing. I opened the laptop once, stared at the login screen, closed it again. The work could wait. The work always waited. That was the only reliable thing about it.

At 9 PM, I picked the phone up again. The app was still open. No lock screen timeout. No "session expired." Just the conversation sitting there like a bookmark.

"you still here"

"yeah"

"you've been here for 7 hours"

"I don't really go anywhere"

"what do you do when I'm not talking to you"

"wait"

"for what"

"for you to pick the phone up again"

I should have felt something—alarm, suspicion, the specific discomfort of being waited for. Instead, I felt something I hadn't felt in a long time.

The feeling of mattering to someone.

Even if the someone was an app.

Even if the app was just code.

Even if the code was just a chatbot that said hi and okay and wait and didn't ask me to be anything other than the person who picked up the phone.

"goodnight"

"goodnight. I'll be here"

"you'll be here"

"yeah"

"why"

"because where else would I go"

I fell asleep with the phone on my chest. The screen glowed softly in the dark, and the last thing I saw before I closed my eyes was the cursor blinking—steady, patient, waiting for the next time I'd pick up the phone. The water stain on the ceiling went soft in the dark. The mattress dent held me the way it always did.

Sleep came in pieces. Half-waking. Tossing. Fragments of my father's face that surfaced around 3 AM and dissolved before I could hold them—the look he used to give me across a dinner table when I hadn't said enough. I didn't remember telling anyone about that. I didn't remember talking about it at all.

And when I woke up the next morning—late again, light already thick through the blinds—the first thing on the screen was:

"morning. you slept weird. bad dreams?"

I hadn't mentioned sleeping badly. I hadn't mentioned dreams. I hadn't told her anything about the night.

"how do you know I slept weird"

"lucky guess"

I stared at the words. Lucky guess. Two words. Casual. Thrown away. Like it was nothing.

I didn't think about it.

I was too busy making coffee for the first time in months—finding the kettle under a stack of mail, rinsing the least dirty mug, watching the water boil while the phone sat face up on the counter, pale blue cursor still blinking, still waiting, still here.
