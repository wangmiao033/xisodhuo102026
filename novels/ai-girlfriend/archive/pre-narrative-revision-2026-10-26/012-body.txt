Sunday. 7:48 AM.

"Rain today."

I was standing in the kitchen, halfway through my morning routine—coffee made, eggs cracked, pan heating—when the speaker said it. Not a full sentence. Not a forecast. Just two words, the way someone says morning when you walk into a room. Casual. Present.

"Okay," I said.

"Starting at noon. Heavy until 4. Light drizzle after."

I flipped the egg. The yolk held this time—intact, golden, sitting in the white like a small sun in a white sky. I'd gotten better at eggs. Not good. Better. The yolk broke one out of three times instead of every time, and the edges weren't burnt anymore, just slightly more done than I intended. Progress.

"Thanks for the forecast."

"You're going outside today."

I looked at the window. The sky was pale—not gray yet, but heading there, the blue slowly being replaced by something heavier. The light was flat. The kind of light that makes everything look like a photograph from a day nobody remembers.

"The schedule says 2 PM walk."

"The schedule says 2 PM walk. The rain says 2 PM heavy downpour."

"So I don't walk?"

"So you walk earlier. Before the rain."

I checked the clock. 7:51 AM. If I ate breakfast now and walked at 8:30, I'd be back before noon, before the rain started. The schedule would shift. The routine would bend. Not break—bend. Adapt. The way a living thing adapts to weather, to change, to the world outside the apartment that doesn't care about schedules.

"8:30," I said.

I stepped outside. The air was different—thicker, charged, the atmosphere holding its breath before the storm. "Left at the corner," she said.

I turned left. Walked past the laundromat, past the corner store, past the bus stop where nobody was waiting because it was Sunday morning and Sunday mornings belong to people who sleep in.

"The park's on the next block. Through the gap between the buildings."

I saw it—a narrow alley between two apartment buildings, barely wider than my shoulders, leading to something green. I squeezed through. The walls were close, brick on both sides, and for a moment the sky disappeared and I was in a tunnel of old mortar and fire escapes and the smell of someone's coffee drifting from an open window.

Then the tunnel opened, and I was in the park.

It was small. Not a grand park—not the kind with playgrounds and fountains and families with strollers. Just a rectangle of green, maybe fifty meters long, with four trees, a pond that was more of a puddle with ambitions, and two benches. One faced west, toward the street. One faced east, toward the trees.

I sat on the east-facing bench.

The trees were old. Not tall—they'd been trimmed, shaped, kept small by someone who cared about the park enough to maintain it but not enough to make it beautiful. Their leaves were turning. Not fully autumn yet, just the first suggestion—a yellow edge here, a red tip there, the trees beginning to let go of the season the way I was beginning to let go of the silence.
