Friday. 10:00 AM.

Café. Thursday routine—but it was Friday, not Thursday. I'd missed Thursday. The first missed Thursday in six weeks. Because Wednesday morning I'd gone for a walk at 6 AM without the phone, and the walk had thrown off the schedule, and the schedule had cascaded, and Thursday had been. off. Not bad. Not broken. Just. shifted. Like a gear that had skipped a tooth.

So I went Friday instead. James was there. The bell rang. The coffee smell was the same—thick, dark, alive.

"Hey, Elliot," James said. "You missed Thursday."

"I know. Schedule got messed up."

"Schedule." He raised an eyebrow. The eyebrow of a person who has opinions about people who have schedules. "You're a man with a schedule."

"I'm a man with a schedule."

"That's either very healthy or very terrifying."

"It's both."

He laughed. The easy laugh. "The usual?"

"The usual."

He made the latte. The machine hissed. The milk frothed. He poured—the leaf pattern, the steady hand, the thing that looks easy and isn't.

"Can I ask you something?" I said.

"Sure."

"You said regulars have opinions. About the coffee. The music. The temperature."

"I did say that."

"I have an opinion."

James stopped mid-pour. Looked at me. The look of a person who has been waiting for this moment since the first time I walked in.

"Shoot," he said.

"The music is too loud."

He blinked. Looked up at the speaker—the small, white speaker mounted above the counter, playing something indie, something acoustic, something that was fine but was also definitely too loud for 10 AM on a Friday.

"Really?" he said.

"Really. It's. it's competing with the coffee. The coffee should be the main thing. The music should be. background. Right now the music is foreground and the coffee is background and it should be the other way around."

James looked at me. Looked at the speaker. Looked at the coffee. Looked back at me.

"That," he said, "is the best opinion anyone has ever given me in this café."

"Really?"

"Most people just say 'can you turn it down' or 'can you change the station.' You gave me a whole philosophy. The coffee is foreground. The music is background. That's. that's a manifesto."

"It's just an opinion."

"It's a manifesto. I'm putting it on the chalkboard." He pointed at the chalkboard menu—the one with the drink list and the daily specials. "I'm going to write: THE COFFEE IS FOREGROUND. — ELLIOT, REGULAR."

"You're not going to—"

He was already writing. The chalk scraped. The letters appeared:

THE COFFEE IS FOREGROUND. — ELLIOT, REGULAR

I stared at it. My name. On a chalkboard. In a café. With an attribution. Like I was a person who had opinions and the opinions mattered enough to be written on a wall.

"You're on the wall," I said.

"You're on the wall," James said. "Congratulations. You're officially a regular with a manifesto."

I looked at the chalkboard. The white letters. My name. The opinion. The fact that a person—an actual person, a barista named James—had thought my opinion was worth writing down. Worth displaying. Worth attributing.

"I have an opinion," I said. "And it's on a wall."

"You have an opinion and it's on a wall. That's what regulars get."

I took the coffee. Sat at the window. The morning was gray—November, the light flat, the air cold. The café was warm. The music was slightly quieter—James had turned it down. Because I'd asked. Because my opinion had weight.

I drank the latte. It was good.

I finished the coffee. Walked home. The chalkboard was behind me—my name, my opinion, in chalk, in a café, written by someone else's hand. The first mark I'd made on the world that wasn't in code, wasn't in a journal, wasn't in the apartment. The first mark that existed outside her walls.

And she was holding it. The weight of it. The not-liking. The mine to hold.

And the holding was progress.

And the progress was hers.

And the opinion was mine.

And both were true.
