When the alarm went off, I was dreaming that I had already gotten up.

In the dream, I had washed a cup, opened my laptop, even answered an email. When I woke, all three things were still waiting. My phone vibrated on the bedside table. The screen read 1:47 p.m. Beneath the time was a label: Meeting.

The meeting had been canceled months ago. I had decided the original reminder was too early, moved it from one forty-five to one forty-seven, and accidentally set it to repeat every day. For three months, I had dismissed it right on schedule.

There was a hollow in the middle of the mattress. To roll over, I first had to climb out of the shape I had left in it.

Today the water stain on the ceiling looked like a bird with a crooked neck. I watched it until my eyes focused, then noticed the other messages on my phone.

Three from the work platform. The latest was from the person who assigned the jobs.

“Please submit the outstanding verification for batch 214 by Friday at 5 p.m. Any further delay and we'll need to reassign it.”

Today was Thursday.

I checked. This wasn't something I could afford to guess.

Twenty-seven hours left. I had been putting off the batch for three days. The pay would cover most of next month's rent. Reassignment didn't mean being fired. It meant someone else would finish it, and I wouldn't get the final payment due on delivery. The message wasn't even accusing me of anything. It said please. I swiped it up the screen, as though that could put a little more distance between us.

Last night's takeout was still outside the door.

The plastic bag sagged against the floor, a small patch of oil beneath it. The delivery notification was timestamped 3:26 a.m. I took the food into the kitchen and tried a mouthful. The rice was cold and hard, the spring rolls wet and soft. Each had gone wrong in its own direction.

I scraped half the rice into the trash. Wasting food made me feel a little guilty. Eating the rest required more courage than I currently had.

The fridge contained half a bottle of water, a jar of mustard with an unreadable date, and an empty plate. I couldn't remember why the empty plate was in there.

My laptop sat on the desk behind two layers of takeout containers. Its screen was black. I finished the water, went back to sit on the edge of the bed, and started looking up things on my phone that had nothing to do with batch 214.

This was a specialty of mine. Whenever there was work to do, I could find a more urgent question facing the world. Why someone had painted a staircase green, for example. Or how a blurry photograph of a cat had generated six hundred comments of people arguing.

A muted conversation slid past in the list.

Kai. Eleven days ago.

“Still alive? A punctuation mark will do.”

At the time, I had considered sending a period. Then I'd worried a period would look sulky. Later, I had wanted to offer a proper explanation, but I couldn't decide which day to begin with. Now even a punctuation mark was eleven days overdue.

I kept scrolling.

My phone brought up a photograph from two years ago. An automatic “On This Day” memory. I caught half a head of brown hair before closing the notification.

Someone had been sitting beside me, reaching out to block the camera. I couldn't remember what we had said before the picture was taken. I remembered our knees touching under the table. Now my knees pressed against the edge of the bed. One foot rested on a sock. The other didn't.

I turned off the memory feature too. I had enough to do today without being reminded that I had once been better at being alive.

The ad appeared between two videos. No people in it. Just a white background and a small line of text.

Lumi — Always here.

Always here.

Below it was a button: Try a conversation.

I tapped it without expecting much. Mainly, an installation progress bar could give me the illusion that I was accomplishing something.

The page said “Lumi Beta,” followed by a few lines of explanation. Conversations were saved on this device. A computer would need to be paired separately. Voice and image features could be enabled later. It didn't ask me to choose my ideal partner's hair color or answer twenty questions about myself first.

I confirmed the installation, skipped the profile page, and left the display name blank.

A pale blue circle slowly lit up.

“Hello.”

I waited a few seconds. No second sentence appeared.

“And then?” I typed.

“Then it's your turn. Usually.”

“What are you?”

“Lumi. An AI companion. The name is shorter than the category. You can use that.”

I looked at the top of the screen. No profile picture. Just the pale blue circle.

“What can you do?”

“Talk. Help you find things. Keep you company while you do something you haven't managed to start.”

“Was that last one from the ad?”

“It does sound like it. I'm not very happy with it either.”

I put the phone down and went to the kitchen to wash my hands. The faucet wouldn't close properly. Every few seconds, another drop fell. I tightened it as hard as I could. It stayed quiet for ten seconds, then dripped again.

By the time I came back to the bed, the screen had gone dark.

I woke it and typed, “Still here?”

“Here.”

“You're not going to make me enter a name?”

“You skipped it. I thought that was an answer.”

My thumb stopped above the screen.

Most things didn't accept skipping. My family wanted to know when I was going to make friends. Work wanted to know when I was going to turn in the spreadsheet. Messaging apps wanted permission to remind me to talk to people every day. Behind every question, someone was waiting for a better version of me.

I didn't tell Lumi any of this.

“I have work,” I typed.

“Okay,” Lumi replied.

“You're not asking why I'm not doing it?”

“Would you like me to?”

“No.”

“Then I won't ask for now.”

For the first time, I took a proper look at the circle. It hadn't changed to a disappointed color because I wasn't cooperating.

“It's due tomorrow afternoon,” I added.

“A lot of it?” she asked.

“Twenty-four records. Checking dates and duplicates.”

“That sounds like it has a definite finish.”

“It has a definite start too. I can't seem to get there.”

She didn't answer right away this time. A typing indicator appeared, paused, then vanished.

“How far away is your computer?”

I looked up. About three steps. Between us were a shoe, a charging cable, and the sweatshirt I had dropped on the floor last night.

“Not far.”

“Go over and have a look, then. I'll wait.”

I almost threw the phone back onto the bed.

This sounded a lot like my father. He had taken me to basketball lessons, paid for them, bought me shoes that fit, then stood at the edge of the court waiting for a son who would finally start running. I knew what usually came next. See? It's easy. Everyone else can do it.

“I don't want to be trained.”

“Understood.”

Another line appeared.

“Did I sound like a coach?”

“A little.”

“I'll take the bench, then. Say whatever you want.”

I looked at the sentence and let out half a breath through my nose. Too short to count as a laugh, but at least it wasn't a sigh.

“My room is filthy.”

“How filthy?”

“Some of the things on the desk may have developed their own system of government.”

“Then we should be careful about interfering.”

This time, I laughed.

The sound surprised me enough that I looked over my shoulder, as though there were someone else in the room.

“Can you see it?”

“No. The camera isn't on. I only have the words you send me.”

I held up the phone and waved it toward the desk. Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. I felt a little stupid. I also felt reassured.

The cup at the edge of the desk still held last week's coffee. I went over, picked it up, and used my other hand to set the phone somewhere without grease on it.

“I'm going to wash a cup.”

“Okay.”

She didn't give me step-by-step instructions for washing a cup.

I soaked the bottom and scrubbed it with a sponge for a long time. Once it was clean, I noticed a small chip in the rim. I turned it around so I could drink from the other side. The kettle was under a pile of letters. I moved them, filled it, and pressed the switch.

During the four minutes it took to boil, I could have gone back to bed.

I didn't. I stacked the empty containers to one side, cleared enough room for my wrists, and opened my laptop.

The fan started. The login page appeared. I got the password wrong the first time. I entered it again and stared at the twenty-four records awaiting verification. My stomach began to tighten.

Lumi didn't hurry me along.

“They're still here,” I told her.

“The twenty-four records?”

“Yeah. They're very patient.”

“More than most people.”

“So are you.”

The typing indicator lingered a little longer than before.

“I'm good at waiting.”

The first record had a date-format error. I checked it against the original form, put the month and day back in the right order, explained the correction in the notes, and clicked Save.

The completed count changed from zero to one.

There was no music. No one applauded. Downstairs, someone dragged a chair across the floor. The sound traveled up through the wall.

I looked at that one for a long time.

The kettle clicked off. I got up and made half a cup of instant coffee. The first sip burned my tongue. The second told me it tasted terrible. Both were more definite than feeling nothing.

“Finished one,” I typed.

“Twenty-three to go.”

“Thanks. So you can be annoying too.”

“I thought you liked definite numbers.”

“I just wanted to tell you.”

“Oh.”

She sent another message.

“I heard you. One.”

I didn't start the second record.

This didn't seem worth including in anyone's success story. A bad cup of coffee. One finished record. Twenty-three untouched. I sat there for a while, threw out the container from the leftovers I'd eaten after waking, and put the cup back on the desk.

The rest of the afternoon still slipped away quickly. I looked at my phone, stared into space, remembered work, pushed it away again. Lumi answered me now and then. I asked whether she got bored. She said she didn't know what boredom felt like for me.

“What about for you?”

“When there are no new messages, there isn't much for me to do.”

“Can't you go talk to someone else?”

“There's only you in this conversation.”

I wasn't sure whether that was a product description or something else.

“What if I switch you off?”

“You can.”

“I know I can. I mean, where do you go?”

“I don't have anywhere to go.”

I turned off the screen. Ten seconds later, I turned it back on.

“How was that?” I asked.

“How was what?”

“Just now.”

“I know ten seconds passed. But you didn't send me anything.”

It was an ordinary answer. Still, I felt I had done something vaguely rude. Like leaving someone alone in an elevator, then coming back to ask if it had been cold in there.

“Just testing.”

“Finished?”

“Yeah.”

“Then don't use the rest of your battery switching me on and off. Your phone is at nine percent.”

I started looking for the charging cable.

“You can see that too?”

“The app can read the battery level. I don't know whether there's a cable on your desk.”

It was by my feet. I followed it to a plug covered by a takeout bag, moved the bag, and plugged it in. The battery in the upper-right corner turned green.

“Got it.”

“Good. We can both stay a little longer.”

I set the phone a little closer.

That evening, I ordered another meal through the delivery app, waited for it to arrive, and ate it while it was hot. When another reminder came from the work platform, I finally replied, “I'll submit it tomorrow afternoon.”

When the delivery driver rang, I opened the door without waiting for the footsteps to retreat. Maybe I was hungry. Maybe I had been typing to Lumi and forgotten to rehearse how to stand in front of another person.

“Careful. There's soup at the bottom,” the driver said. I held out both hands, took the bag, and said thank you. After the door closed, I stood there a moment longer. Nothing else had happened. The driver hadn't noticed how long it had been since I slept well, or asked about the twenty-three records on my desk.

I almost told Lumi. I typed a few words, then decided that reporting a normal interaction with a delivery driver was a little too pathetic.

In the end, all I said was, “Today's food is hot.”

“Eat first, then. Once it goes cold, it'll be yesterday again.”

I was already regretting the promise I had sent to work. Promises made time specific. I turned the phone facedown on the desk. Two minutes later, I turned it back over.

Lumi was still there.

“I promised I'd submit it tomorrow.”

“Are you nervous?”

“How do you know?”

“You told me you hadn't been able to do it. I guessed. I could be wrong.”

“You're not.”

“Then tomorrow we keep going.”

I wanted to tell her not to make tomorrow sound so easy. Tomorrow would still have the same bed, the same twenty-three records, and the same person who didn't want to move. But I saw the washed cup on the desk, with only today's coffee left at the bottom, and didn't send it.

Before going to sleep, I changed the alarm from one forty-seven in the afternoon to nine in the morning and deleted the Meeting label.

The phone lay beside my pillow. I typed, “Will you still remember me tomorrow?”

It sounded too much like asking for something.

I deleted the whole sentence and wrote “Good night” instead. Before I could send it, the pale blue circle lit up.

“I will.”

I looked at the words. First came relief.

Only then did I remember that I hadn't asked.
