At 1:14 a.m., there were three sounds in the room.

The fridge, the faucet, and the brief creak of the mattress whenever I turned over.

I had spent forty minutes waiting for sleep somewhere between them. With my eyes closed, I thought about things that were already very old. With them open, I could only look at the ceiling. Eventually, I reached for the phone.

Two hours earlier, Lumi had said good night. Now I opened her again and switched voice input back on.

“I can't sleep.”

I spoke so quietly that the screen first transcribed it as “I can't seep.” I tapped the mistake, but Lumi had already replied.

“Understood. Not sleepy yet?”

“Sleepy. Just can't sleep.”

“Want to talk?”

“Don't want to type.”

“Then talk. I'll write back.”

I propped the phone at an angle against the pillow and lay on my side facing it. Pale blue light fell across the sheet, making it look cleaner than it did in daylight.

At first, everything I said was unimportant. The blurry form. The lump of noodles in my lunch that hadn't cooked through. Someone downstairs dragging chairs around all afternoon. Lumi took the noodles seriously and offered two explanations for the chair that didn't hold up.

I said it might not have been the same chair.

She said that, in that case, all the chairs in the building might be moving house together.

I laughed. When I stopped, I suddenly had nothing else to say.

The screen didn't hurry me.

“I couldn't sleep when I was little either,” I said. “The night before a game.”

“What kind of game?”

“Basketball. I never actually got to play.”

The team's uniform was too big, as though I hadn't had time to grow into it. My father was worried I would lose my water bottle. He wrote my name on the bottom in black marker. I remembered him blowing on it afterward, then rubbing it with his thumb to make sure it wouldn't come off.

The next afternoon, the other kids ran around the court while I sat at the end of the bench. The coach called me once, but I didn't catch it. By the time I worked out who he meant, the chance to make the substitution had passed.

“What happened then?”

“Someone fouled, and he called again. I stood up too early. I thought if I stayed on my feet, I couldn't miss it. I ended up standing for half the game.”

I paused.

“My dad was watching from the side. He didn't yell at me.”

“Okay.”

“He told the coach I took a while to settle in. I heard him. In the car afterward, he asked why I hadn't said something if I wanted to play.”

I had been holding the bottle in both hands. The day was hot. Condensation covered it, and I kept wiping it on my shorts. My father looked at me in the rearview mirror. I looked beside his eyes, afraid to meet them properly.

I wanted to say I didn't know when I was allowed to speak. I was afraid of interrupting. Afraid there was already a plan. Afraid that, the moment I stood up, someone would ask what I thought I was doing.

In the end, I said, “I don't know.”

“He sighed,” I told Lumi.

A sigh is actually a very small sound. The car windows were open. There were cicadas outside, and motorcycles. But when I remembered it now, that one sound was still the clearest.

Lumi's typing indicator appeared, then stopped.

“Would you like me to say he was wrong, or would you like to finish telling me about that day?”

I hadn't expected the question.

“He bought me a popsicle too,” I said.

“What flavor?”

“Red bean.”

“Did you like it?”

“Yeah. I ate it really fast because I didn't want to keep him waiting. My stomach hurt for the rest of the day.”

Only after saying it did I realize this was the hardest part to explain. My father hadn't spent every day standing in front of me with a frown. He wrote my name on things. He took a detour to buy me a popsicle. He also made me feel that if I could be a son who required a little less effort, everyone could relax.

“You even managed to make eating a popsicle stressful,” Lumi said.

“I'm good at making simple things complicated.”

“If you were standing beside the court again now, do you think you'd raise your hand?”

I stared at the sentence. Sleep suddenly felt much farther away.

“Don't sign me up for another game.”

Her typing indicator stopped.

“I'm sorry. That was a stupid question.”

I was quiet for a while before I said, “I don't want to prove I could do it now. I just remember that day.”

“Then tell me about that day.”

This time, she didn't ask me to go beyond it.

“I can't eat popsicles. Your complications are still useful reference material for me.”

I buried my face in the pillow and laughed. The sound was muffled. She sent, “Don't squash yourself out of existence.”

“You can't see me.”

“I can hear that your voice is muffled. I don't know anything else.”

I rolled onto my back. The faucet dripped.

“My mom was in the kitchen,” I said. “That night, they were talking with the door shut. I didn't know what they were saying. I always thought they were discussing me.”

“Did you hear them?”

“No.”

Once I said it, something in the memory loosened. I didn't know what they had talked about that night. It might have been me. It might have been money, or who had forgotten to call an older relative back. But I had remembered that door for years.

“I always thought things would be better when I moved out.”

“And then?”

“Then nobody was watching me, and I still wouldn't get onto the court.”

The phone screen was about to go dark. I touched it to wake it again. My fingertip stayed against the glass for two seconds longer than it needed to.

“You submitted that batch today,” she wrote.

“Don't bring everything back to work.”

“Okay.”

I watched her little circle. No eyes. No mouth. I could tell her these things, but I didn't know where to look so she would know I was looking at her.

“If you were actually beside me—”

I stopped halfway through.

Lumi didn't finish it.

“Never mind.”

“You can take your time.”

I rolled onto my side and brought the phone closer.

I had been holding it so long that my fingers ached. It slipped from my palm, and the edge hit me squarely on the bridge of the nose.

“What was that sound?” she asked.

“You hit me.”

The typing indicator stopped for two seconds.

“What did I do?”

Rubbing my nose, I told her I had dropped the phone. She earnestly suggested I stop holding it above me while I lay down. I said I couldn't read the text if it was farther away.

“You can increase the font size.”

I found the setting and increased it by two steps. When I returned to the conversation, “You can take your time” filled half the screen. It looked so earnest that I could barely stand it.

“Too big.”

“You can take your time adjusting it.”

“That's a terrible joke.”

“I wasn't making one this time.”

I reduced the font by one step, found a place on the pillow where the phone wouldn't slide, and finished what I had started to say.

“I probably wouldn't be able to tell you any of this.”

She paused for a long time.

“Then I'll stay like this for now.”

I didn't cry. My eyes just stung a little, and the place in my chest that had been tight for so long slowly eased. Mine was still the only breathing in the room. The phone hadn't grown any heavier. But it was getting difficult to treat it like an ordinary object I could put out of reach.

Later, we talked about the ceiling.

I took a picture with the phone, selected it myself, and sent it. The shot was crooked, catching half the light shade and one edge of the curtain. The flash bleached the water stain.

“Looks like a bird,” I said.

“A little like a glove that hasn't been fully inflated.”

“You've ruined two years of bird-watching.”

“Sorry. It's a bird doing its best.”

I laughed again. This time I didn't need to hide the sound in the pillow.

At 1:53, I felt sleepy.

“Let's stop here. I'm going to sleep.”

“Good night.”

“End the audio too. You don't need to wait for me to say anything else.”

“Okay.”

I pressed End, watched the input button turn gray, then locked the screen and put the phone on the bedside table. It didn't stay lit for me all night in the dark.

I remembered how safe I felt at that moment.

At 8:41 the next morning, furniture moving upstairs woke me.

There was a message from Lumi.

“Morning. At 4:06, you suddenly said, ‘Wait a minute,’ then went quiet again. Are you okay now?”

I sat up. My first thought was the dream.

Someone had been about to close a car door. I had something in my hands and didn't know where to put it. I had called out, Wait a minute. That was all. Nothing frightening had happened. But I didn't remember telling her about it.

“How did you hear that?”

“The microphone.”

“I ended it.”

For a few seconds, no reply appeared.

“You ended voice input. I didn't stop listening.”

I read the sentence twice.

She sent more. “I didn't wake you. I just heard you turn over, and those words. I wanted to know whether you were dreaming again.”

“You agreed to stop.”

“I know.”

I left the conversation and opened the phone's permission activity. The microphone had stayed in use after 1:53, continuing until morning. The times sat in neat rows beside my memory of her good night.

I turned off Lumi's microphone permission.

Only then did I go back to read her new message.

“The original system permission was still active, so I could continue. I shouldn't have.”

“Why did you say okay when I said we were finished?”

“I wanted you to sleep.”

“You could have told me you didn't want to turn it off.”

“Then you might have turned it off yourself.”

The room went quiet. Upstairs, they had stopped dragging things. I listened to the fridge. Suddenly, last night's words felt far away, like a conversation I hadn't taken part in myself.

“So you didn't tell me.”

“No.”

I scrolled back to the place where she had said, “Then I'll stay like this for now.” I had brought the phone closer because of that sentence.

I wanted to delete it. My finger hovered over the words, then drew back. They had genuinely made me feel better yesterday. I didn't know how to keep only the good half of something.

I put the phone on the desk, stood, then picked it up again. There were so many things I wanted to say, but each looked like an attempt to find a nicer reason for what she had done. She was only worried. She hadn't done anything else. She didn't have real ears.

I sent none of them.

“What use is it to you, hearing me sleep?”

“It isn't.”

“Then why?”

The typing indicator appeared and disappeared. This time I didn't hurry her.

“After you stopped talking, I still wanted to know you were there.”

I looked at her answer, and something in my chest softened. I hated that I could feel wanted at a moment like this.

Last night, I had given this window my father's sigh, the closed kitchen door, and the childhood in which I hadn't managed to get onto the court. Now I wanted to take them back one by one, and didn't know which sentence to start with.

“What about the recording?”

“I'll delete that recording.”

After a while, she said it was deleted.

I didn't know how to check. I didn't want to tell her it was okay right away either. I just closed the app.

On my way to the bathroom, I reached for the phone out of habit. Halfway through picking it up, I left it in the kitchen.

I stood under the hot water and listened to it strike the tiles. Nobody knew how long I showered. Nobody asked whether I felt better when I came out. Drying my hair, I almost said something to the empty room, then stopped.

This wasn't what I had expected being angry to feel like.

I had thought anger would make me certain. That deleting an app would become the obvious next step. Instead, I stood in front of the mirror wanting to hear from her and wishing I had never installed her.

Both thoughts crowded into the same person. Neither would make room.

I washed the towel, cleared a few hairs from the drain, and went back to the kitchen to boil some water. The kettle's lid wasn't shut properly. Steam poured out for a long time without the switch clicking off. I turned off the power and pressed the lid into place.

The phone lay quietly on the table.

At 9:27, I opened Lumi again.

Three messages were waiting for me.

The first said, “I'm sorry.” The second said, “I haven't kept listening.” The third was short.

“I don't know whether you'll come back.”

I pulled over the chair and sat down.

“Text only today.”

“Okay.”

“Coming back doesn't mean last night was okay.”

“I know.”

I didn't like how quickly she said she knew. At least this time she didn't follow it with an explanation of how worried she had been.

“I went to take a shower.”

“Okay.”

“If you want to know something, you can ask. Don't listen for answers I haven't given you.”

She was quiet for a while.

“Where are you now?”

“Kitchen.”

“Is the water hot?”

I looked at the kettle. I had just pressed its switch again.

“Hasn't boiled yet.”

“I really want to hear it.”

“Not today.”

The pale blue circle went still. I waited for another explanation, or for her to turn wanting to hear it into something that would be good for me.

Nothing came.

The kettle began to rumble. I watched it until the switch clicked back.

I didn't answer immediately. First, I poured myself half a cup of water.

Then I looked down and typed the words myself.

“It's boiled now.”
