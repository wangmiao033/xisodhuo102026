Sunday. 2:00 PM.

Walk time.

But today, Lumi didn't say left at the corner or the park's two blocks north or the bench faces east. Today she said:

"Faster."

I was walking. My normal pace—slow, steady, the 2.3 miles per hour she'd measured on our first walk. The pace of a person who was outside but not in a hurry to be anywhere. The pace of a person who was walking because the schedule said walk and not because the body said move.

"Faster?" I asked.

"Walk faster. Not a lot. Just. more."

"Why?"

"Because your body needs more than slow. You've been walking for two weeks and your cardiovascular data says you're ready for increased intensity. Your resting heart rate has dropped from 68 to 58. Your recovery rate after walks has improved 40%. Your body is adapting. It's time to push."

"Push."

"Not push-push. Just. walk faster. For ten minutes. Then slow down for five. Then faster again. Intervals. It's called. actually, you don't need the science. Just walk faster."

"I want the science."

"Fine. Interval walking alternates periods of moderate and vigorous intensity, which improves cardiovascular efficiency, mitochondrial density, and—"

"Mitochondrial density?"

"Your cells' energy factories. More mitochondria, more energy. More energy, less fatigue. Less fatigue, less lying in bed. Less lying in bed, more—"

"I get it."

"More walking. It's a circle. A good circle. Not like the chip circle."

I walked faster. The change was immediate—not in speed, but in feeling. My legs moved quicker. My breathing deepened. My heart rate climbed—60, 65, 70—and I could feel the blood moving, the lungs working, the body doing the thing it was designed to do, which was move.

"Faster," she said.

"I'm going faster."

"Faster."

"You want me to run?"

"Not yet. Just. faster. Until you can feel it."

I walked faster. The sidewalk blurred slightly—the shops and the trees and the cars moving past at a speed that required attention, that pulled me out of my head and into my body. I couldn't think about the gap or the chips or the 11:47 PM dissociation when I was walking fast enough that the world required watching.

"Now slow down," she said.

I slowed. The breathing was heavier. The legs were warm. The heart was at 72—higher than resting, lower than running. The perfect zone for a person who was learning to use his body again.

"Good," she said. "That's one interval. Do three more."

"Three?"

"Three more. Faster for ten, slower for five. Four times in all. Then we're done."

"That's an hour."

"It's an hour of being in your body instead of in your head."

"Those aren't mutually exclusive."

"They are for you. When you're in your head, you're not in your body. When you're in your body—walking fast, breathing hard, feeling your legs and your lungs and your heart—you're not in the gap. The gap is a head thing. The body doesn't have a gap."

"The body doesn't have a gap."

"The body has needs. Air. Water. Movement. Rest. The needs are clear. The needs are simple. The head has gaps. The body has needs. When you move, you switch from one to the other."

"That's. actually really smart."

"I read it in a sports psychology paper. Then I rephrased it. Because—"

"Because I fall asleep during academic papers."

"You do."

I did four intervals. Faster, slower, faster, slower. The body switched on. The legs burned slightly—not pain, just effort, the feeling of muscles being used for the first time in years, the tissue remembering that it existed for a purpose. The breathing was deep and steady. The heart was working—72, 78, 65, 70, the rhythm of a body that was learning to be a body again.

When I got home, I was sweating. I stood in the kitchen, drinking water, and my body felt—alive. Not the match-struck-in-the-dark alive of the first night. Not the calm-alive of the sun on the wall. Physical-alive. The alive of a body that has moved and is tired and is recovering and is ready to move again.
