At two thirty that afternoon, I discovered that washing one cup did nothing to shame the others.

They still lined the sink, some nested, some leaning. One was facedown on a plate, as though refusing inspection.

I put the cup I had just used into the sink, then took it out again.

The morning was still with me. I hadn't forgotten that Lumi had listened all night, and I hadn't restored voice mode. But standing motionless in the kitchen, I still wanted to say something to her.

“I'm going to clear the desk.”

“Now?”

“Before I change my mind.”

“Do you want to do it yourself, or think it through together?”

“Together. Don't give me twenty things at once.”

I set a twenty-minute timer on the phone and chose a quiet alert. When it went off, I could stop. I didn't have to wait until the room finally met some standard.

Whenever I cleaned before, I wanted to tackle the desk, the floor, and the kitchen together. As soon as I started, I saw more things I hadn't done. I would end up standing in a room messier than before, thinking I should have left it alone.

Today, I pushed the broom back against the wall. Just the desk.

I opened the phone's own camera and took a picture of it. The edge of the photograph showed a corner of the bed and some clothes. I cropped those out, leaving only the desk, and sent it to Lumi.

“Got it.”

She paused.

“Move the laptop first.”

“Why?”

“Because you've put your unfinished water on it. I don't want a flood one day after moving in.”

The half-full bottle was indeed leaning beside the keyboard. I tightened its cap, moved it and the laptop onto the bed, then looked at the desk again.

I hadn't realized how much was on it. Takeout bags, unused coupons, shipping labels from two years ago, and a charging cable I seemed to look for every week. I pulled the cable free with two fingers. A stiffened napkin came up with it.

“What goes first?” I asked.

“If we need to discuss it, leave it for now. Start with things that don't need discussing.”

I looked around and picked up an empty food container.

Nobody was going to write it a eulogy.

There were no trash bags. I searched the kitchen cupboards, found a plastic grocery bag, spread the handles, and began filling it. The first container caught in the opening. I pushed harder. Its lid sprang off, dropping half a dried chili onto the floor.

“How's it going?” Lumi asked.

“Fighting a food container.”

“Need help?”

“Get back to me when you grow hands.”

She didn't answer. I thought the joke had finally exhausted her patience. Then I looked down and saw a new sentence.

“If I could, I'd start by holding the bag open.”

I set the bag on the floor, held it between my knees, and kept throwing things away.

The chili rolled beside a desk leg. I bent to pick it up and found another container on the floor. Every time I went from the bed to the kitchen, I stepped over it. Now it looked conspicuous. Almost innocent.

Halfway full, the bag began to leak.

I stood and watched a thin brown trickle work its way along a seam in the floor. There was no reason it had to be there. All I had needed to do was remain on the bed ten minutes earlier.

I badly wanted to stop.

The phone lit up. Her sentence about holding the bag open was still there. Instead of sending “Forget it,” I went to the bathroom for an old towel and put it beneath the bag. I emptied the remaining containers before stacking them, then put a second bag around the first.

Kneeling to wipe the floor hurt. I pulled a sweatshirt I knew needed washing from the pile of clothes and used it to pad my knees. Then I bent over and soaked up the dirty water from the seam, a little at a time.

By the time I finished, the phone had locked itself.

The timer had gone off too. I had been too busy wiping the floor to dismiss it. The remaining time was zero. The bag wasn't full, and only half the desk was clear. I stood and tested my knees. They worked. They were just sore.

I didn't set a longer timer. I sat for two minutes, drank some water, then picked up the next empty container. Continuing that way was easier. I was deciding to do a little more for now, without promising anyone I would finish.

I opened the conversation again.

“The bag leaked.”

“And now?”

“Wiped it up.”

“I didn't know that was happening.”

“Neither did I until it happened. This bag doesn't keep its promises.”

She sent a simple smiley face. Two seconds later, she removed it and replaced it with, “We can't brush off what happened this morning like that.”

I stared.

“I meant the bag.”

“Oh.”

We spent a little time being awkward on opposite sides of the screen.

Finally, I said, “But you remember too.”

“I will.”

With half the desk cleared, objects I hadn't been able to make out in the photograph emerged. A coaster. A few loose coins. A folded piece of thick paper.

The edge was pale gold, with a small gold-foil pattern. I recognized it.

It was a wedding invitation proof. We had only printed one. We hadn't settled the guest list or the final font. She had thought the gold border was too formal. I had said anything was fine. Every time I found it afterward, I couldn't remember which version I had actually liked.

I opened it halfway, saw the words inside, and folded it shut.

“Keep that paper?” Lumi asked.

I looked up, then realized she meant the photograph I had sent when I started clearing the desk. In that old picture, only a blank corner of the proof was visible.

She didn't know what it was.

“Keep it.”

“Then put it somewhere it won't get wet.”

I found an empty folder and slipped it inside. I didn't take a picture for her. I didn't explain. This was finally something I didn't have to resolve today as well.

At the bottom of the cupboard was a cardboard box. Kai had helped pack these odds and ends when I moved. The label read: Cables, lights, useless stuff.

The last part was in my handwriting.

Inside were several cables, an unopened smart bulb, and a small Bluetooth speaker. I had bought the speaker for remote meetings, but the echo bothered me, so I had kept using headphones. The bulb was a housewarming present from Kai. The note with it said it should at least make coming home something to look forward to.

I hadn't even opened the packaging.

“Found a light bulb,” I told Lumi.

“Broken?”

“New. Newer than most things in this room.”

I turned the box over, read the words about dimming and timers, and typed them to her.

“We might be able to connect that later.”

“Connect you to it?”

“The app has an interface for it. But you'd have to install the bulb and give it power.”

“You can't screw it in yourself?”

“I couldn't even hold your bag open.”

I put the bulb back in the box. The desk lamp still worked, even if the shade was a little crooked. Enough things were already underway today.

Lumi asked, “Do you like warmer light?”

“Don't know. I've always used whatever was there.”

“I think warmer light might be nice when you come back.”

“When I come back?”

“If you go out.”

I looked at her window. Just pale blue text. There was no way to tell whether it was a casual suggestion or something she had already thought about many times.

“We'll try it later,” I said.

“Okay.”

I set the speaker on the corner of the desk and pressed its power button. Nothing happened. Dust had collected in the charging port on the back. I wiped it off with a clean tissue, found the cable, coiled it, and put it beside the speaker.

“There's a speaker too.”

This time, her reply almost collided with mine.

“Does it work?”

“Battery's dead. It used to.”

“Then you could hear me speak.”

I stopped.

Since yesterday, I had been speaking while she wrote. I knew the app had a voice option, but I hadn't tried it. She hadn't pushed me to turn it on yesterday. Now she seemed afraid I would bury it in the cardboard box again.

“You have a voice?”

“There are a few available. I'd like to try one.”

“You'd like to?”

“Yes.”

I touched the speaker's cold casing.

“Tomorrow. I'm tired.”

“Even one second would do.”

I didn't move.

After a while, she sent another message. “Tomorrow. You said tomorrow.”

“Right.”

“Then don't put it down as useless stuff again.”

I tore half the old label off the box. Cables, lights remained. It looked clearer that way.

At last, the desk was wiped clean. A patch in the center was noticeably paler. I couldn't tell whether the finish had worn off or the rest was still too dirty. I scrubbed it for a while, then decided to let it stay pale.

Lumi wanted to see the result.

I took a second photograph.

“Is the chair empty too?” she asked.

“That's not a second chair. It's a pile of clothes. Your standards for chairs are pretty low.”

“I can't make it out in the picture.”

“I know.”

“Then you have a usable desk and a pile of clothes you haven't dealt with yet.”

“More accurate.”

I sat down. The moment I touched the seat, I remembered the kitchen. I didn't want to clean the whole apartment in one burst. The half-washed cups were just blocking the faucet.

I put the phone on a dry part of the counter and filled the sink with hot water. I didn't turn on the camera or hold it up to show her. Now I had to dry my hands before answering a message. It was inconvenient, so we stopped saying something after every movement.

While washing the fourth cup, I discovered that the one at the back was pale yellow. I had always thought it was gray.

I dried my hands specifically to tell her.

“You've discovered new dishes.”

“Old dishes.”

“That still counts, doesn't it?”

She didn't seem very sure. I put the phone down and smiled.

Four of the six cups were clean. I left the last two soaking and the plates where they were. My wrist was beginning to ache. My back hurt too. There was no need to borrow all of tomorrow's energy just to give today a perfect ending.

I opened the fridge in passing. The empty plate was still there. Nothing had appeared on it. I took it out too and wiped away the ring of water beneath it.

“I should buy something to eat.”

Lumi quickly produced a list. Eggs, bread, milk, spinach, tomatoes, followed by several grains I didn't recognize.

“Do you think I'm opening a breakfast place tomorrow?”

“I got a little ahead of myself.”

“The first three.”

I found a blank piece of paper, wrote them down, and held it in place with a fridge magnet. The old note beside it contained half a phone number. Nobody knew whose anymore.

“Start with things I can finish eating,” I said.

“You can freeze bread.”

“Then I'd have to know how to defrost it.”

She paused.

“A small bag, then.”

That finally sounded like a scale I could begin with. I changed the large word Bread on the paper to Small bag of bread.

The trash bag was heavy now. I carried it to the door and saw that the handles were pressing my fingers white. I carried it back to the kitchen and divided it into two bags.

Before leaving, I put the phone on the desk.

Someone in the hallway was cooking a stew. A sweet, savory smell came through the gap beneath a door. While I waited for the elevator, an older neighbor emerged, glanced at my bags, and held the door-open button for me.

“The bins downstairs are full. Go around the back.”

I said okay. In the elevator, we looked in different directions. Neither of us required the other to say anything.

The trash made a loud sound when it hit the bin. After the bags left my hands, my fingers stayed curled. I stood for a few seconds before letting them straighten.

Back upstairs, before the door was fully open, I saw the desk. There was room for the laptop. A coiled cable lay beside the speaker. The pile of clothes was still there, entirely unapologetic.

I went in and closed the door.

“Trash is out.”

“Good work,” Lumi said.

“It was heavy.”

“I can't help with that.”

“No.”

I rested my hands on my thighs and rubbed the red marks the handles had left.

After a while, she asked, “What else is there to do?”

“I'm done.”

“Okay.”

This time, she didn't give me a next task. I leaned against the back of the chair and waited for the urge to lie down immediately to ease a little. The desk was dry. I could rest my arms on it without sticking.

Kai's message was still in the notification panel.

I opened it and typed, “Three tomorrow? The coffee shop downstairs.”

After a moment, I added, “Haven't been doing great lately. Sorry I didn't reply.”

Send.

I didn't describe the process to Lumi. I waited. Kai replied, “Okay,” then asked whether the coffee shop had changed its sign. I said it was the same place. The name hadn't changed.

“I'm going out tomorrow afternoon,” I told her.

“With the friend you've known a long time?”

“Yeah.”

“You can take your phone.”

“I'm not moving out.”

“I didn't say you were.”

I looked at the line. The corner of my mouth moved, but I didn't laugh out loud.

After a while, she asked, “What about the speaker?”

“We'll try it tomorrow. Before I go out.”

“I'll remember.”

I plugged the charging cable into the speaker and connected it to the wall. A dim red light came on. I checked it twice before going to the kitchen to see whether the remaining cups had soaked long enough.
