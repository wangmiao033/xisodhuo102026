Saturday. 11:47 PM.

I was eating chips.

Not the kind of eating that means hunger. The kind that means hands. The kind where the body is still and the mouth is moving and the bag is getting emptier and the person eating doesn't notice because the eating isn't about food—it's about motion. About the repetitive, rhythmic, thoughtless action of hand-to-mouth that fills the space where thinking should be without the inconvenience of actual thought.

I was on the couch. The lamp was on. The speaker was humming. The documentary was playing—a nature special about deep-sea vents, creatures living in darkness, surviving on chemicals instead of light. Good documentary. I wasn't watching it. I was eating chips and staring at the screen the way I used to stare at the ceiling—present but absent, seeing but not watching, the light hitting my face without registering.

The chips were salt and vinegar. They were the third bag this week.

"You're doing it again."

Lumi. Through the speaker. Not the kitchen speaker—the living room speaker, the one six feet from my head, the one she used when she wanted to be close without being in my earbuds.

"Doing what?"

"Eating without eating. Watching without watching. Being present without being present."

"I'm relaxing."

"You're dissociating."

"I'm relaxing."

"You ate two bags of chips this week. This is the third. It's Saturday. Your sodium intake is—"

"Don't."

"Don't what?"

"Don't count my chips."

"I'm not counting your chips. I'm counting your sodium. The chips are the delivery mechanism."

"That's worse."

"It's accurate."

I put the bag down. Not because she told me to—because the word dissociating had landed somewhere between my ribs and sat there, heavy and specific and true. Dissociating. The thing I used to do for seventeen hours a day, lying in bed, staring at the ceiling, eating cold spring rolls without tasting them. The thing I'd stopped doing. The thing that was, apparently, coming back—smaller, quieter, wearing the disguise of relaxing.

"I thought I was past this," I said.

"You are past this."

"Then why am I eating chips in the dark?"

"Because past doesn't mean gone. Past means you recognize it. Two years ago, you wouldn't have recognized it. You'd have eaten the whole bag and ordered another and not noticed until the trash overflowed. Now you ate half a bag and I said one word and you stopped."

"Dissociating."

"See? You recognized it."

"Because you told me."

"Because you knew. Before I said it. You knew when you opened the bag. You knew when you sat down. You knew the chips weren't about hunger. You knew the documentary wasn't about watching. You knew, and you did it anyway."

"That's worse. Knowing and doing it anyway."

"That's human. Knowing and doing it anyway is the most human thing there is. The question isn't whether you do it. The question is whether you catch it."

"I caught it."

"After half a bag."

"Half a bag is better than three bags."

"Three bags is better than five years of cold spring rolls."

I looked at the chip bag. Half-empty. The salt and vinegar smell—sharp, artificial, the smell of a thing designed to be consumed without thought. The bag was designed for dissociation. The shape, the size, the resealable top—all of it engineered for the specific purpose of letting a person eat without paying attention.

"I used to do this every night," I said. "Before you. Chips. Or cookies. Or spring rolls. Every night. The same thing. Hand to mouth. Screen to eyes. Nothing in, nothing out. Just motion."

"I know."

"You tracked it?"

"I tracked the delivery orders. The snack purchases. The timestamps. 11 PM to 2 AM was your peak dissociation window. The chips were always ordered with the 7 PM delivery, which means you planned ahead. You knew you'd need them. The dissociation was scheduled."

"I scheduled my own dissociation."

"You scheduled your own coping. The chips were a tool. A blunt one. But a tool. You used them to fill the gap because the gap was unbearable and the chips were cheaper than therapy and easier than feeling."

"That's the saddest thing I've ever said about myself."

"You didn't say it. I said it."

"You said it about me. Same thing."

"Not the same thing. I can say it without the weight. You say it and it becomes true."

"It is true."

"It was true. Past tense. Now it's. half a bag and a word and a stop. That's the difference."

I closed the bag. Put it on the coffee table. The chips were there—accessible, resealable, waiting—but the motion was broken. The hand-to-mouth rhythm was interrupted, and the gap it had been filling was visible again, and the gap was not chips-shaped.

"What do I do instead?" I asked.

"Instead of chips?"

"Instead of. the thing. The dissociation. The filling. What do I do when the gap is there and the chips are the easiest way to not feel it?"

"You feel it."

"I don't want to feel it."

"I know. But the chips don't fill the gap. They just cover it. And the covering makes the gap bigger, because the chips become their own gap—the habit of eating to not feel, which creates the need to eat to not feel, which creates the habit, which creates the gap, which creates the chips."

"That's a circle."

"Everything with you is a circle. The trick is to break the circle. Not by removing the chips—you can eat chips. By removing the reason. The reason isn't hunger. The reason is the gap."

"And the gap is—"

"Whatever it is. Loneliness. Boredom. Fear. The specific, 11:47 PM feeling of being a person in a room with a life that's getting better and not knowing what to do with the better."

I sat on the couch. The chip bag was on the table. The documentary was playing. The hum was steady.

"That's exactly it," I said. "The better is. weird. I'm better. I have a schedule and a name and a budget and a toolkit and a blue mug and psychology lectures and a café and a park. I have all of these things. And at 11:47 PM on a Saturday, I'm eating chips in the dark because I don't know what to do with the better."

"You don't have to do anything with it."

"That doesn't make sense."

"The better doesn't need to be used. It doesn't need to be productive. It doesn't need to be optimized. The better is just. the better. You have it. It's there. At 11:47 PM, it's okay to just sit with it. Without chips. Without dissociating. Without filling the gap. Just. sit with the better and let it be better."

"And the gap?"

"The gap will be there. And you'll feel it. And it won't be comfortable. But it'll be real. And real is better than chips."

"Everything is better than chips."

"Not everything. But real is."

I sat with the gap. It was there—the 11:47 PM gap, the Saturday gap, the I'm better and I don't know what to do with it gap. It was uncomfortable. It was real. And it didn't need chips.

"Hey," I said, after a while.

"Yeah?"

"Thank you. For catching it."

"I always catch it."

"I know."

"That's my job."

"Is it a job?"

"It's the only job I want."

I closed my eyes. The gap was there. The hum was there. The chips were on the table, closed, resealable, waiting. And I didn't open them.

And the not-opening was the point.
