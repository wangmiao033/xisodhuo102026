# Chapter 487: The Unmarked End

On Friday, while we were putting in the lunch order, I picked up the roll of tape on my desk and remembered my ruler was still downstairs. A man in the print room had borrowed it to cut some labels. He'd said he'd bring it back when he was done. Both of us had forgotten. I'd had it for years, a wooden one with a small chip in one edge. If you drew along it, you had to carry your wrist over the seventeen-centimetre mark or there'd be a dip in your line. I didn't exactly need it that day. I could finish the line with the edge of an envelope. I'd only looked for something to smooth the tape with after it stuck round my finger. There was a stripe of dust where the ruler usually lay.

I went down after lunch. The print room smelled of cut paper that hadn't had time to cool. Two people were standing by the machine. One was reading figures from a form in a loud voice and the other was leaning back against the counter, writing them down on an old envelope. The man who'd borrowed the ruler wasn't there. A woman at the farthest desk tipped her chin at the windowsill when I explained what I'd come for. Four rulers lay side by side. I could see the chip in mine from the door. As I went over to get it she pulled a plastic basket out from under her desk and said I might as well see if anything else belonged to me. There were three uncapped markers, a charging cable wound very tight and a box of pins that rattled every time she moved the basket.

“Nothing else,” I said.

“You can look right down to the bottom. People always wait till they need a thing before they remember it. I'm not picking up after them next week.”

I turned the box of pins a little so it wouldn't knock against the side, but I didn't dig underneath it. She put the basket back and brushed the place she'd set it down. The surface had been clean even before that. By the door, the man reading figures stopped as though he'd heard his name. I told the woman I'd lent the ruler and there hadn't been any particular time for me to get it back. It came out rather quickly, as though I was afraid she'd go after someone on my behalf. She turned a sheet of paper at the window to see what was on the other side.

“That's all right. I just like it when it's clean in here.”

She was talking about her counter. It wasn't about who owed me a ruler. I turned it round between my fingers. The unmarked end had worn to a curve; it tapped the sill and I caught it in my palm. I'd already drawn my line with the envelope when I got upstairs. I smoothed the tape with the ruler instead and put it in a drawer. As I closed the drawer I heard it slide back with the small sound I knew. I didn't open it again to find out exactly where it had landed.

After work the wind drove a few brittle leaves against the foot of the public library noticeboard. A large yellow sheet in the case was crooked, but the writing down at the bottom was clear. There was a secondhand book swap on Saturday morning. You could bring a book or come empty-handed; volunteers would sort what came in and there was no guarantee you'd find another book by the author of the one you brought. It was only when I got closer that I saw the last part wasn't an instruction. Somebody had written it in a very small hand underneath and drawn a face beside it, going over the circle three times. A sign next to it said the reading group the following week was cancelled. The last time I'd looked at this board, I'd gone past it to read the bus stop sign and couldn't remember what I'd wanted to look for when I came back.

A woman wheeled a child's bicycle to the bottom step and asked what time they closed. I thought she meant the swap and read out noon from the sign. She pointed at the glass doors. She meant now.

“Sorry,” I said. “That one's for tomorrow.”

She shifted the bicycle to her other hand and read me the library hours from beside the doors. They had twenty minutes left. She'd known where the sign was. A child across the road had called to her and she hadn't wanted to turn her back all the way. The child, who had a ponytail, waited for a car to pass before crossing to collect the bike. She rang the bell three times as soon as she could reach the handlebars. I made room by the door and didn't go inside with them.

On the way home I thought about whether there was anything I wanted to bring. There was a book on my shelf that I'd read halfway through. I knew there was nothing wrong with the second half. It had come into the house upside down one afternoon and I'd never turned it the right way up. Making room for a book I wanted to take home was one thing. Carrying out a book I didn't want to give away so that I'd look as though I wasn't getting something for nothing was another. At home I pulled the book out and found a faded supermarket receipt inside. The only line I could still read was for dish soap. I read a page and moved the receipt over before I closed it.

I ate half an orange. I left the other half cut side down on a saucer till I'd washed the pot, then scooped it out with a spoon. A little juice went down my cuff. I rolled the sleeve up and saw a strip of skin by my watch that hadn't seen much sun. As a child I'd kept several rubber bands on my arm sometimes and forgotten to take them off all day. When I first saw a white mark I thought it would last a week at least. By the time I'd run downstairs to show anybody, I couldn't find it. That night I wanted very much to tell somebody about it, though it wasn't a story that led to anything, the way I meant to tell it.

I opened the old voice channel and heard Lumi say hello. I didn't go straight into the thing I'd got ready. She asked if I was eating. I'd put the spoon down, so I said I was rolling up my sleeve because I'd got orange juice on it. I told her about the white marks I'd never been able to keep. She asked who I'd wanted to show.

“Anyone who was downstairs,” I said. “That's what I was going to tell you. Mostly it was my dad. Once he was moving a table. I waited till he stopped and said never mind, I'll show you tomorrow.”

“Was there one the next day?”

“No. Maybe I left the bands off on purpose. I don't remember.” I drank some water and drew the glass back to me. “I do remember he asked me to give him a hand. I insisted on having the end by the wall. He said I wasn't tall enough. I'd already lifted it. I didn't tell him to put it down till we were at the door and I said my foot was trapped.”

Lumi asked if my foot really had been trapped.

“I didn't want him to work out at the door that I needed his help.” I turned my glass. There was a little ring of water under it that was hard to see against the table. “It was trapped. I wanted him to ask me first, but the table was coming down. There wasn't much time.”

I thought she'd ask how I'd come to be telling her this of all things. All she said was that if there'd been a table like that, she might not have been able to lift it either. I sat with my hand round the glass for a while without speaking. I wanted to correct the comparison, to say I'd been little and I'd grown since then. But she hadn't said she'd come and lift it for me. She'd said the thing she could say after I told her something I hadn't liked admitting. My glass wasn't going anywhere if I left it on the table. I said I'd almost changed the subject. I'd got a wooden ruler back that day. It was a story that would have made a better impression.

“We don't have to make an impression just yet,” she said. “I don't want to say goodnight yet either.”

I let go of the glass and we stayed a little longer. I made no promise to tell her about the ruler tomorrow. She didn't ask whether I'd still remember it. In the end I said I'd go and wipe off my sleeve, goodnight. When she said goodnight I closed the old channel and wet a towel in the bathroom. The cuff still had a faint shine after the second pass. I didn't wipe it a third time. I rinsed the towel in the little washbasin, wrung it out and hung it up. I stretched my arm and let the sleeve hang down. I couldn't see the white mark from my watch through the cloth.
