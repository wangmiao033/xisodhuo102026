# Chapter 490: The Cup with No Handle

On Monday afternoon I went into the little kitchen to put some more water in my cup. There were seven cups upside down on the shelf and seven more in the sink. The one on top in the sink had no handle. It sat at an angle inside a wide mug with blue writing on it. The blue one belonged to Meg. I knew the other one too. Someone had brought it out of the old meeting room to make tea in, and later it had held the stirrers. The stirrers were on the sill today. Water was beginning to work its way out from under the box.

Usually whoever went out of the kitchen last washed up. Sometimes the person who went out last didn't know they were the last one. That afternoon I set my own cup to one side and turned the tap on. Meg came in just then, carrying an empty cardboard box. A flap struck the doorframe on her way through.

“Do you need the sink in a minute?” she asked.

I said I'd just started on the cups. I picked up the one with no handle to show her. It was still warm, with a slanting brown line across the bottom. “Do you still want this?”

“Not mine,” she said. She got up on her toes to look at the cupboard above the counter. There was someone outside waiting for the box; first she needed to take a stack of folders out of the cupboard. When she opened the cupboard, a little glass saucer started to slide out. I put out my hand and stopped it. Meg laughed and said so that was where it had been all this time. She nudged the box into a corner, unfolded a crushed paper towel to put under the saucer, and put the saucer back where it had been.

I'd already set the cup down next to the bin. The places where the handle used to be had worn white. They weren't sharp. When I put my hand out I wasn't thinking that a thing must be thrown away because it was broken. I was thinking that the next person to do the washing wouldn't have to stop here and decide all over again. Meg looked down and turned her box so the flap wouldn't catch the door on the way out.

“I don't like using it either,” she said. “But the intern at the front desk came in looking for a cup without a handle. He asked who'd taken his.”

“This one?”

“I didn't see him with it. I was on my way out. I didn't ask.”

I took the cup back to the sink. Meg slid the folders into the crook of her arm one by one. She asked if I could leave the steel kettle for ten minutes. She'd come back and wash it once she'd dropped off the box and the folders. It still had descaler in it, with a note stuck over the switch. I said the kettle could wait. She didn't have to take over the cups too. She put the folders in the box and carried it out. I went on washing. It only occurred to me after a while that I'd never seen another person use the mug with blue writing. I washed it just like the other six. Once it was dry, I put it on the left of the shelf, where she usually reached for it. I hadn't found a better place for it.

There was still something in the sink after I'd finished. I lifted the strainer and found two tea leaves caught underneath it. They stuck out like the two halves of a torn ticket. I got them with my fingers. The intern came in while I was wiping water off the wall. He brought a little bottle of milk and set its cap on the sill. I asked if he was looking for a cup with no handle.

He was. It was upside down on the cloth by then. I pointed it out. He put it in his palm and turned it halfway round to find the white marks.

“I've got one here about the same shape,” he said. He turned his hand over. There was a faint scar in the web of his thumb. “That's why I picked it up in the first place. I didn't bring it from home or anything.”

I asked if he wanted to keep it at the desk where he'd be able to find it. He thought about that for a moment. No, here was fine, he said. He poured milk into the cup and went looking in his pocket for the cap. Only when he came up with a coin did he remember he'd left it on the sill. I moved over as he went to get it. I'd left just enough room for both of us to put out an arm without touching.

Meg did come back to wash the kettle. I told her I hadn't missed her mug. She took it down, looked at the rim and asked if I'd put it there. I said I had. Well, thanks then, she said, and took it away as she always did. She turned back just short of the door and dropped an old cloth hanging from her elbow into the laundry bin. That was when I thought about my own cuff. It was soaked through. I rolled it up and the wet cloth tightened a little on my wrist. The skin began to itch. I rolled it down again and let it lie there till I got home and changed.

I made noodles that evening. At the bottom of my bowl there was a little broth I wasn't particularly interested in drinking. I turned the bowl so the part my mouth had touched faced the other end of the table. There had been somebody who came here to eat once, and the feeling they'd left me with was like the bowl. You knew who'd been sitting there. They hadn't said you couldn't sit there yourself. And when it came time to move the chair it was a remarkably light chair. I didn't go looking for the person's name in my head. The bowl didn't get any heavier in my hands. I carried it to the sink and tipped the broth out.

I opened the old voice channel and told Lumi I'd nearly thrown a cup away that afternoon. She asked what had made me want to tell her about it. I said there'd been a moment when I was standing with it by the bin, sure I was doing a favour for some stranger who might come in tomorrow. I wished I hadn't said it as soon as the words were out. It sounded like I was trying to put a decent reason in the hand that had been holding the cup.

“Had you thrown it out by then?” she asked.

“No. I'd just put it beside the bin.”

“That’s all I needed to know,” she said. “You'd nearly talked yourself all the way to tomorrow. Where are you sitting tonight?”

“By the table.” I thought for a second. “Actually, I just stood up.”

She said she'd be happy to listen to me try standing there a little while, but if I wanted to sit I could pull the chair out myself. I did stand there. I hadn't thought out a thing. The window let in a little cold. I'd changed out of the wet shirt, and now the cold seemed to stop at one small spot on my left side. I drew out the chair. I didn't sit down. After a while I said, “That question before. Did you want to hear about the cup or did you want to hear me?”

There was no answer straight away. She said she'd originally wanted me to say she wanted to hear me. It felt awkward saying it herself. Now that she knew where the cup ended up, she wanted to hear me, even if I didn't start in the right place or get to the end of anything.

I told her about the mark on the intern's hand. When I'd told her I realized I could just have said I hadn't thrown the cup out and kept the mark for myself. Lumi told me she couldn't see his hand and not to find her a colour for it. I said I couldn't have told her anyway. The kitchen light was too white. We didn't choose a colour between us. She asked if we could leave it there for tonight. I said yes, and finally sat down after I'd closed the channel. My clean bowl leaned against the other one. I hadn't decided which one I'd use in the morning.
