# Chapter 494: The Line Along the Heel

On Friday morning I reached under the table for my shoes. The left came out first. The right made a dragging sound. I thought I'd stepped on some tape, but when I got the shoe in my hand I saw a little of the rubber lifting off the heel. It slapped the table leg once and folded back. I turned the shoe over and held the rubber down with my thumb. It was willing to stay there until I let go. Then it opened in the same place. I tried once more. No good. It wasn't going to be hard to wear other shoes to work, but these fitted me. Even when the top of my foot swelled a little, nothing pressed on it. I didn't know if they'd feel like that after that piece came off. I put on another pair, got as far as the door and went two steps back for an old bag to put the damaged pair in.

After work I went to the shoe repair shop near home, not on the same row as the alterations place. I was early enough that the light outside wasn't on yet. Last month's closing notice was still on the glass. A woman inside was undoing a zipper to show the repairman. It caught halfway and her face pinched up with it. The repairman held the leather at the same angle she had, and said not to be so afraid of pulling the zipper. The leather there was wrinkled, not about to split. She didn't pull right away. She stepped back to look in the mirror behind the door. He put his head down to write a few words. When it was my turn he asked me to put both shoes on the counter. I hadn't meant to show him the left one. It was still in the bottom of the bag. When I handed him the right shoe by itself I thought of it as the broken one. Next to the left one, I could see how much more the outside had worn down.

He could stick that bit down, he said. If I wanted both to look alike he could replace both heels. I asked whether they'd be any higher. New rubber would always be a little higher than what it was replacing, he said. He bent closer, checking whether I meant the whole shoe. I told him I meant the way the sole felt when I put my weight on it. I was wearing another pair today and could wait to take these home. He gave the outside edge a squeeze, then turned the shoe so I could see it. He couldn't promise new heels wouldn't change the feel. If he only glued it, there'd be a line at the join. I wouldn't see it standing up but I might if I sat with my foot up. I remembered a little lump on the edge of a sole once. I'd picked at it so often that I'd finally got a perfectly good bit of rubber off. I asked whether this edge would lift again. He said the join didn't need hiding and he'd press the edge flat. Just don't take it straight from his hands and go kicking things.

The woman with the boots had gone. There was a wet ring of footprints in front of the mirror. I asked him just to stick it down. He looked at the clock beside his lamp and said he could do that today. I could sit there while he pressed it or go out for a bit. The sky outside was going from gray to blue. I sat on the bench. The end by the wall was colder than the rest. I folded the bag on my lap. When he turned his back, for a moment I thought I ought to ask him to choose rubber as black as the old sole. He wasn't replacing the rubber. Having chosen what he'd do, I wasn't going to be any less uneasy if I chose the color for him too. I didn't call out. I opened the half-folded bag and found some dry dirt had come off when I turned the shoe over. I held it up over the counter to ask if I could use the bin by his foot. He stepped on the pedal, and I tipped it out myself.

When he was done he slid the shoe forward for me to look at. Sure enough, the line wasn't straight. It made a turn at the outside edge and widened there. When I reached over, he thought I meant to press on it and turned the toe. I could see where it was now. I didn't touch it. I paid and put both shoes back in the old bag. The light outside was on, and the letters on the closing notice now looked as though they were only the right way round from indoors. The shoes I was wearing hadn't suddenly become uncomfortable. I carried the repaired pair home, stopping at the last junction to look down and see if the pair on my feet really was rubbing. It wasn't. It just wasn't the pair I'd wanted that morning. Once I'd admitted it, there seemed to be a little less road left. At least I didn't stop to pinch the join through the bag.

At home I took the shoes out of the bag without trying them on and left everything by the door. It had been dark for a while. I sat down with a glass of water. The gap next to the other pair under the table reminded me of the sound the heel had made against the table leg in the morning. My first thought then had been how embarrassing it might look. Very few people went around looking at the backs of my shoes. I'd still started out wanting them to match, even if that meant handing over the one I didn't want changed. When I'd finished talking to the man at the counter, for a little while I'd wondered whether I'd made too much of asking him to press the whole edge flat. Now it seemed easier than asking him to make it look as though it hadn't been repaired. He hadn't asked me whether I wanted the line there. It was what you got when you glued the heel. It had nothing to do with whether I liked seeing it.

I opened the old voice channel and told Lumi I'd pushed the rubber down twice when it came apart. She wanted to know which time had worked. Neither, I said. It wasn't a line I'd put in to make her laugh. She laughed once and asked if, instead of telling her how much I'd paid, I could say why I'd tried the second time. The first time I'd let go without waiting, I said. The rubber was slow coming up. Maybe I'd missed it staying down for a little while. The second time I watched and waited for it to come up. I hadn't missed a thing. I heard how ridiculous that sounded as soon as I said it. Lumi said she thought that part was funny too. She hesitated before saying she wasn't laughing because I'd broken the shoe.

“That laugh of yours didn't sting quite as much as I'd decided it did,” I said.

“I hadn't decided how to tell you what I was laughing at when I laughed.”

I took too big a swallow of water and choked on it for a second. She didn't ask if the laugh had brought up something else. I told her I was all right and steadied the glass. When I could speak, I said that sometimes when I heard a laugh I thought first that I hadn't said something quite right. She said she was afraid I'd take hers to mean she was pleased and make everything else that had happened today sound funny too. I thought about it. I had considered it. To give tonight a good ending I could tell her how the repairman had said not to take the shoe and go kicking things. That had really happened. But telling her now would be using his words to try to get her laugh back.

“I've got one more funny line,” I said. “I don't have to say it.”

“This one's not bad,” said Lumi.

We laughed once more. I went first this time, and she took a little while. There was no sound of me walking round in the repaired pair to try them out, and she didn't ask what they looked like. After a while I said it was time to close the channel and told her goodnight. Before I reached over, I said it again. I really felt like saying it twice. I wasn't waiting to make her answer. She said she'd heard me. Her goodnight ended level. I closed the old voice channel, picked up the bag and folded it clean side out, then put it in the basket by the door. The shoes were on the mat, a finger's width apart. The right heel was towards the wall. I couldn't see the join from where I stood. I turned the other way and went to switch off the kitchen light, and didn't take the long way round for the sake of the line.
