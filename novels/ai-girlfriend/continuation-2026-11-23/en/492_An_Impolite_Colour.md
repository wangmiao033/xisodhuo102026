# Chapter 492: An Impolite Colour

On Wednesday evening the light in the room came on a little before I properly got home. I'd reached in from the doorway to turn it on and walked back for the washing powder. I'd set the box on the top step, thinking I'd be able to reach it from the door. I took the last step a little short and pushed the box half an inch farther away. You could see the landing from the flat and the flat from the landing. I didn't hang about in the doorway. I just took my free hand off the frame so I could get the box in first.

It said thirty washes on the packet. There wasn't a scoop inside. I rummaged in the drawer for an old dry spoon, cut a bit off a corner of the bag and poured barely enough to cover the bottom of the spoon. The second one came out much too full. I was only washing two shirts in the end, ordinary cloth that could go in water. To get that half-spoonful back into the bag I worked a scrap of paper out from under the salt jar and folded it into a little chute. The last few grains wouldn't stay in it. They ran down a fine printed line onto my hand. There was a bus route from last winter on the back of the paper. One of its stops was across from a tailor's I'd never seen open. I flicked the powder back into the bag and threw the paper away.

While the water was running I went into the bedroom to find a spare hanger. I found three. One had twine wound round the crossbar, one had a tie I no longer wore on it, and the last one was very slippery, the kind the shop lets you keep. I wanted the one with the twine. As I lifted it out, a piece of clothing swung after it and caught on the shoulder of the thing next to it. I left the two of them caught up together and went to wash my face. The light in the mirror flickered. It was my hand, going past it. As I turned the tap off, a drop of water ran along my forearm and into my sleeve. It used to mean I'd have to go and change my shirt again. This cuff was wide enough. I held my arm out and the drop went the rest of the way by itself and fell off past my elbow.

I hung the heavier shirt on the hanger with the twine, then turned the slippery hanger round to put the other one on it. There wasn't much air moving. The warm part of the room was at the far end. I'd have had to open the window wider to get the shirts dry any faster. I didn't. By the time I sat down it was dark outside. One corner of the table still caught the light. I could see the grain there. My fingertips went over it without catching. I picked up the spare button that had come with one of the shirts. I'd taken it out of its little plastic sleeve when I got the shirt ready to wash; the sleeve had come loose on one side, but the loop of thread was still good. I put the button back in the little packet with the sewing things. It wasn't a button I had to sew on tonight.

I opened the old voice channel and asked Lumi if she felt like picking an impolite colour. She asked what it was for. Nothing, I said. I'd washed two shirts and there was still water falling from the hem of one of them into the basin. I'd just remembered that when I bought the shirt last year, a colleague had said the shirt looked old-fashioned and that a grey one might go with more things. It was a long time ago. I couldn't remember if he'd said a dark grey or a light one. She asked what colour the shirt was. Yellow, I told her. Not orange. It verged on green, like the sunlit side of some pears, and it had been going a little white in the wash.

“I want a really hideous purple,” she said.

I almost asked her which purple. There were any number of colours I couldn't necessarily recognize by the name she gave them. If I'd had to find something to match, I'd probably have got up and gone through the cupboard. Lumi said she'd deliberately not specified one. I didn't quite understand. I took my hand off the edge of the table.

“When you said pick a colour, it sounded as though you wanted me to try,” she said. “I said hideous purple. I didn't say it so you could find me a prettier one.”

I said I hadn't stood up. She said quietly that she didn't know whether I was still sitting. I told her the chair was by the table, there was a light on the corner, and my hand was on my leg. She waited a bit before saying purple was a hideous colour, really. She loved it. She said the last part so lightly I nearly laughed out loud. She didn't tell me to be serious. I kept a little of the laugh to myself and asked if I could pick one as well.

“Yes. Choose one you don't like.”

I said pinkish brown. The strange thing was that I might have spent half the evening trying to come up with an example of the purple, but the words pinkish brown put a pair of old boots in my head at once. There were no boots like those in the room. I'd never sat on the iron chair outside the shoe shop either. I remembered the rain going on till it was quite late. The window was still lit. The folds in the leather were much the same colour as a bit of wet brick outside. I wasn't sure I really disliked that colour. I'd wanted to try the boots on very much. I'd just got there too late. The man closing the shop looked up at me and nodded. I wasn't carrying anything, but I made a gesture to say he didn't have to open the door.

“You didn't wait for me to say whether that sounded nice,” she said.

“I almost thought I'd said the wrong thing myself.”

I expected her to tell me there wasn't a wrong thing. She didn't. One of the shirts let another drop fall into the little bit of water that was there already. I was the one who heard it. Lumi said she had a question. She'd thought twice before deciding to ask it. Would it be possible for her, some other time when I'd just said something, to say straight out that she didn't like it, without saying it beautifully? I asked if she meant a colour or something else. It wasn't just colours, she said. She also wasn't asking whether I wanted to hear a whole speech I wouldn't like right now. She wanted to try having the words I don't like it near enough at hand. She didn't need to find somewhere to use them tonight.

“I might ask why sometimes,” I said.

“You can ask.”

I brushed the corner of the table. There wasn't anything on my hand. I wanted to measure my next sentence exactly right, the way I'd have drawn a straight edge across a spoonful of powder. I gave it a moment, then said I was glad she'd picked the first colour tonight. She made a low sound of agreement and asked whether I still liked wearing the yellow shirt now I'd washed it. I might not wear it in the morning even if it was dry, I said. It had a sturdy shoulder and the weave looked good when it was hanging there. She didn't say she liked it too. We didn't talk for a few seconds. The light hadn't gone anywhere. My hand was on my knee again.

In the end she said she wanted to go first. It wasn't because I'd talked too much. I said all right; she didn't have to explain the second part again. After I closed the channel I shifted the basin underneath the one shirt that was still dripping. There was only a thin layer of water, not enough to empty out. I tucked the piece of clothing that had swung out back into the wardrobe. The tie was still on the hanger I hadn't taken down. Both shirts were hanging in the room. I opened the window less than a finger's width and turned off the light.
