# Chapter 488: From the Far End

On Saturday morning I took a cloth bag when I left the house. Halfway to the library I found there was still a glove in it. I hadn't put it there yesterday. It was too thick for the weather. It lay in a corner and through the cloth I could feel which way its fingers curled. I turned the bag round on my shoulder so the glove lay on the side against me. There were people outside the library but no queue. The notice yesterday had said the volunteers would sort the books that came in. I thought we had to wait till somebody had a hand free and stood there a little while before I worked out that the people outside were only there to get the sun.

The swap table was set across the space between the reading room and the passage to the toilets. At the far end of that passage there was a chair with a brown bag hung over the back. I went round by the reading room, where a volunteer was pushing an unsteady pile of books into place with a piece of cardboard. She didn't ask me if I'd brought one. She said fiction was by the window, to put the rest back where I'd found it, and children could take two. A man by the table had three books open under one hand, like flat roof tiles. He asked her what she'd meant by the last bit. She pointed underneath to two cartons of children's books. He loosened his grip a little to show her one and all three closed. He made himself a space to open them again. In the meantime I looked at two travel journals and a cookbook that was sixty years old, without picking any of them up.

There was a way round the table that I'd thought went right through until I came to the end and found somebody had stacked empty boxes in a corner. I'd have to step over one to get out that way. I doubt anybody would have said anything, but the box was close enough to knock over, so I went back the way I'd come. A woman in a grey coat had taken the place I'd left. Her hands were behind her, one around the wrist of the other. She asked if I was waiting for a book. I said I wanted to see the table again from the far end. Some of the titles and covers were crowded together and I might be able to read them from the other side.

“I want to know if it's been here the whole time,” she said.

The book she was looking at was a paperback with an umbrella on the cover and soft corners that curled up. I looked at it over the top of a thicker book and couldn't remember for certain. She said sometimes she picked up a book and thought everybody who'd come before her must have decided against it. As far as you knew there hadn't been anybody before you. You might be the first person who'd touched it. She picked up the umbrella book and read the back, then looked at me again. She didn't ask whether I thought she should take it. There was one thing she still wanted to find out. She turned a corner up and recognised the edition that had faded in her grandmother's window when she was young. A flowerpot had left a faint circle on a page of the one at her grandmother's. It wasn't there in this copy. She turned five or six pages, then closed the book and tucked it under her arm. It wasn't a lost book she'd come to get back. It was one she still wanted to read.

When I got to the far end, a collection of stories about fishing off a bridge was gone. Under a book about vegetable gardens there was a little book of essays about swimming. The cover was dark blue. There were no people on it, just a lane rope across the upper part. It was more ordinary than I'd expected. In the first essay the author learnt to let people go past halfway up the slow lane of a public swimming pool. If you didn't, there'd be a crowd of people at the end, all too embarrassed to switch lanes. Six sentences in, I couldn't see what was on any other cover. Somebody pushed a folding cart behind me and a wheel bounced. I remembered where I was and stepped out of the way to read on.

The woman in grey came to take the brown bag off the back of the chair. She put her umbrella book inside and asked if mine taught you how to turn. I'd looked under both flaps. I said it might, but the part I was reading was about how the author had hated wearing a swimming cap as a child.

“You can't do both the same day.” She put the bag over her shoulder and turned for the door. She meant getting the turn right and keeping the cap in place. I laughed, too late to tell her I wasn't good at either of them. The volunteer came up the passage and turned the empty cartons stacked by the wall. She reached under the table, took out a thin book with stickers and showed it to the man. He still had his other three open and put up a hand as soon as he saw the cover. Not his sort of thing. Books for kids count separately, she said. So he took it as well and left with all four. I didn't go back over the table to see what I'd missed.

Before I put the swimming book in my bag I wondered if I ought to ask the volunteer to mark down that I hadn't brought anything. She was moving a cup of tea that was too hot away from the sign that said to mind the step. A little girl was asking her if books from the swap had to be brought back. No, said the volunteer. Take it home and read it. I waited a moment without saying I'd bring a book to make up for it. When I got home I found the glove had pressed against the cover just where the lane rope was and left a short, shallow mark. I tried with my thumb from the other direction and could still see it. It wasn't visible in dimmer light and the paper wasn't torn. I took out the glove, put it somewhere else, and left the book on the table.

After lunch I thought about the slow lane and why I'd remembered the notice about the swap so clearly. It had been some time since I'd gone to a public pool. If I went again, I'd probably still look along the slow lane to see who was swimming more slowly than me. Sometimes all you think about when you get to the side of a pool is which part of the water you can get into without looking so uncomfortable. I went back to where I'd been reading. The first time the author let people past, he kicked off the wall, reached for the lane rope and cut a finger on the rough plastic. He didn't see the little line till he picked up a spoon to eat that evening. He hadn't always known where to let people past him. I wanted to read another page.

Later somebody upstairs shut a window hard. I could tell it was the narrow one in the passage because of the little knock that came last, not every time. I squared the swimming book on the table and sat listening to the cars outside for two minutes. I didn't open the voice channel. I didn't put those two minutes aside as time I'd owe somebody later, either. I took an orange from the bowl and turned it till I found the end where the skin would come off easiest. I ate it first and washed my hands before I touched the book again. I finished the first essay that day. The next one began at an outdoor pool, not the same place. The sun moved off the windowsill a little at a time. I left the book I hadn't given away where it was and found a different scrap of paper that wouldn't leave ink on the page. I slipped it in where the second essay began.
