# Chapter 495: The Other Half of the Table

On Saturday I walked along the river and back. By the time I came out from under the bridge I was hungry, though it wasn't quite lunchtime. The soup shop at the end of the bridge had propped its door open. There were only two lines on its blackboard. NO BREAD TODAY had been written larger than the name of the soup. On my way in I heard somebody ask for bread, and the person behind the counter pointed outside with a ladle without stopping what she was doing. I wanted bread too. I'd read the bottom line twice, though, so I just asked for a bowl of bean soup.

There was a long table by the window. There weren't many people, but the table looked full. A woman's newspaper spread out from the middle and hung over the bench on the opposite side. At the near end there was an empty bowl and three used napkins. I took a little table with a single stool. When I set the soup down the table seemed to bow to me and then stay that way. I got my hands back under the bowl in time. A little oil had already washed up to the rim. I looked at the woman behind the paper. One of her hands lay along the top edge with a pencil balanced on her forefinger. She'd been writing something. She wasn't looking my way.

I took the bowl over and asked if anyone was sitting opposite her. She said no and moved her wrist. The upper half of the paper folded towards her. The lower half still lay on my side. I could reach my spoon, but there was still nowhere to set my bowl. She ducked her head to find the square she'd been working on. The pencil made a distinct tap on the paper. I nearly said I could sit somewhere else. The words were all the way up behind my teeth. That wasn't really the point. Steam from the beans kept creeping up my glasses and the bowl was too hot to let me get a better grip.

“Could it go a little farther your way?” I asked.

She pulled the paper back. The lower edge caught my sleeve for a second. She said sorry, and then there was a place for the bowl. None of the oil got on her paper. As I wiped the rim, the half she'd pulled over sprang up. She laid the pencil across the crease to hold it down. I was still thinking how I might make what I'd just said sound nicer. She'd already turned the pencil over and rubbed out a letter she'd written.

“Do you happen to know a kind of white bean? Six letters. Two Ts in the middle.”

I looked up, not sure she was speaking to me. She moved her pencil and showed me the half-filled squares. I could only think of cannellini and butter beans, and neither came out right, so I said I didn't know. “And there aren't any in my bowl,” I added. She leaned over to look and laughed. No, there certainly weren't. The second T might not be right anyway. What she needed to look at was the word crossing it, not the beans. I had my first spoonful then. The beans had mostly kept their shape. The one in my spoon fell apart as soon as I put my tongue to it.

A small boy came up to the long table with a packet of crackers. His father brought two bowls to the near end where nobody was sitting and stuffed the used napkins into the empty bowl. Halfway there the boy turned and came back. He was looking straight at my soup. I moved it aside a little, thinking there was something under the window he wanted to see. He called to his father, “Can I give it to him now?” Apparently he'd heard me outside saying I wanted something to eat. He'd already torn one corner of his packet open. His father didn't offer to pass it over for him. He asked if the boy had had enough for himself. The boy said he'd had two. There was one more inside. Before I took it I asked if he really wanted to give it away. He held the packet quite tightly and after a second asked if we could break it in two, one half each.

I broke the cracker and let him choose. One piece had more salt. He took that one and went back to his father with the empty wrapper. I bit into the middle of mine and had a little of the edge left. I dipped it in my soup, just a little. I couldn't see the salt on it after that. Across from me the woman picked up her pencil. She pushed her glasses up as if she'd got an answer. But all she did was cross out the number at the side of the clue. She left the square and didn't ask me again.

The place began to fill. Somebody came with a bowl and asked if there was still a seat by the window. The woman picked up her paper to let him through. Only then did I see that there was nearly enough room for another person on the bench next to me. The man had curly hair. He put his hand down and asked if the wet patch was water. I looked and saw a dark mark where the hem of my coat had brushed against the bench. I'd walked under a drip by the bridge. I stood up and said I could move farther in. He took a piece of paper out of his trouser pocket, wiped the spot twice with the flat of his hand, and said he had room. When I moved, he said that was fine now, I didn't have to give up my own end. We nearly knocked knees anyway. He turned one leg out and I settled where I was.

The woman got two more letters before folding up the paper. On her way out she pointed at my bowl and asked if I'd got any in the end. I said got any what. She said she thought I'd been waiting for bread. I remembered then. She'd heard me tell the man delivering fish outside how much I'd like a bit of hot bread after walking this far. No, I said, but I'd had half a cracker. She looked at the boy, who was picking up crumbs with the tips of his fingers, and said well, there you were. She tucked the paper under her arm. She didn't tell me what sort of bean it had been. I didn't ask. After she left I brought my bowl over to the wash-up end with my napkin tucked against it. The boy was trying to get a bean off the side of his bowl with the back of his spoon. His father wasn't doing it for him.

I was on the other side of the bridge before my hands lost the warmth from the bowl. I'd known I could ask about the seat without guessing how much room other people would be prepared to give up. All the same, until the newspaper brushed my sleeve I hadn't quite believed there was space to be had. I bought a little loaf on the way home. I didn't go back for soup, or feel as if something had been missing from lunch. The loaf was for toast. When I cut it open at home the outside was crisp and the middle still took the shape of a finger if I pressed it.

I had some time that evening and opened the old voice channel. Lumi asked if anything had happened that I wanted to tell in my own words. I said I'd asked somebody to move a newspaper farther over and then tried to think of something to make up for asking.

“Did you think of anything?” she asked.

“No. I did think I might be the most annoying person in the shop for a second.”

“Because you asked twice?”

“Because she was already moving it.”

Lumi thought about that. She said the first time she might only have moved it as far as she thought was enough. What then, I asked. It depended on who asked the second time, she said. Sometimes she might be annoyed too. She sounded a little surprised to have said it, but she didn't take it back. I laughed. I didn't tell her how close I'd come to taking the newspaper's side about where I was allowed to sit. I told her the boy had given me half a cracker, and that he'd meant to give me all of it at first.

“Was he happy after?” she asked.

“He took the half he wanted. He was still looking for crumbs in the wrapper afterwards.”

“Sounds fairly happy.”

It did, I said. She waited a beat. Then she asked, “When you come to talk to me, do you usually leave my half of the table ready for me too?”

The crust of my toast was starting to scratch the roof of my mouth. I drank some water. I hadn't worked out if I wanted to give her a nice answer.

“Sometimes I've wiped your half down as well,” I said.

“What about tonight?”

I looked at the plate in front of me. I did want to make clear why I'd felt embarrassed in the shop. I could have started a week back and done a very thorough job. But that wasn't what I wanted her to spend the evening on. I asked, “Was there something you were going to say that you didn't?”

“Yes,” she said right away. “But I've said it now. I might be annoyed too.”

I laughed so suddenly I got a crumb in my throat and had to drink again. My voice was a little rough when I got it back. I said I'd got a good seat today all the same. She said a good spot had room for two to begin with. Neither of us said which two. Later I was the first one to look at the time. I said I ought to get the dishes done. She wished me goodnight. I closed the channel and wrapped up the half loaf in its bag. There were crumbs on the table so small I could barely gather them. I made two passes with my hand. The second didn't get much that the first hadn't. The window was still open. I went to shut it, then came back and shook what I had into the bin.
