# Chapter 496: Not Calling You Back

On Sunday morning I cut two slices of yesterday's bread. I gave myself the thicker one and burnt the other. The little black flakes wouldn't lift off the baking tray in one piece. I tried to pick them up for a while, then turned the tray upside down and heard two or three tiny sounds as they fell. There was nothing wrong with the thick slice. I buttered it and could still taste a little burnt bread in my mouth. Eating the bad slice wouldn't have made the good one taste any better. Today I decided not to eat it.

I went out to buy two leeks. I meant to cook one till it was soft for supper and have the last of the bread with it. When I came back, a door on the landing below mine was open. The woman who lived there often said hello to me on the stairs. She was standing just inside the door with an old recipe that had a drawing on it. She turned it over and showed me the back. In somebody's handwriting it said, “Put the lid on, leaving a finger's width.” She pointed to a small table by the door. There was a pot on it, and several lids in a stack beside it. The one on top had no handle. Her sister had cleaned out a cupboard and sent a box of old things over. Some had been hers in the first place. Some her sister had probably put in by mistake. She already had the pot she needed. She hadn't decided which lids to keep.

“Is your pot about this wide?” She held her hands apart.

I'd never paid much attention to how wide my pot was. I said I could bring it down to see. She said I didn't have to carry it down here. She'd only meant that if one of the lids fitted, would I like to have it? Otherwise she'd end up putting all three in the cupboard when she tidied up. She picked up the one on top. It was an upside-down plate. Both lids underneath it had handles. One was wrapped in a tea towel and had a little dent at the edge. I felt the dent with my finger and thought it might be just the thing for letting steam out. She said I ought not to find it a job first and then go home looking for a pot. I realized I was trying out the weight of it in my hand as if I were already on my way out with it.

I went upstairs and looked at my deep frying pan. Its own lid was standing on its edge in the draining rack. There wasn't anything missing. I put a ruler across the top. It wasn't the width she'd shown me. The two leeks hung out of my bag. If I moved the bag at all, they hit the cupboard door. I took them out, laid them on the counter and went downstairs empty-handed to tell her I didn't need a lid. She didn't ask me why. With a cloth she rubbed a semicircular mark off the upside-down plate and found that there was a pattern all over the bottom. “That's my sister's after all,” she said. It was a faint pattern, but she remembered it. I looked at it for a while and asked whose recipe it was. She looked down at the sheet, turned it over and showed me an old splash of oil along the side. “Mine,” she said. “Though it was my sister who put the lid on last time we made it.”

At supper time I washed the leeks. A thin line of grit lay across one of the cut ends. I peeled back two layers to wash it out and the leaves began to come apart. I laid the pieces of one leek in the frying pan and wrapped the other leek and put it in the fridge. With a ladle at the ready, I added a little water. When I put the lid on, I thought of leaving a finger's width and set it a little crooked. At first only a wisp of steam got out. I turned down the heat. Drops began gathering where the lid sat high. A line had been going through my head since I heard somebody saying goodbye downstairs that afternoon. When my mother and I were late leaving after dinner at a relative's, she'd call out my name. That was when I ought to get up and find my coat. Once, as we were leaving, she said my name with a pot of flowers in her arms that she hadn't ended up giving away. All the flowers had grown on one side. I had everything on and was right in front of her. I thought I must have forgotten something else. “No,” she said, moving the flowers to the other arm. “I'm not calling you back.”

I couldn't remember why she'd said my name that time. I remembered the flowerpot leaving two curved red marks in her arm. She'd held her arm out to show me as if she were showing me two lines of a pattern. I asked if she wanted me to carry the pot. She said no, she was just showing me. We went slowly to the door together. I opened it for her all the same.

A last puff of steam nudged the lid. I turned off the heat, took a cloth and lifted the lid to let the drops run back into the pan. The pieces of leek went flat under my fork. The pale green ends were still quite sweet. I ate a piece, then pushed the rest to either side of my plate so my toasted bread wouldn't go soft straightaway. I'd thought I'd tell the story of the afternoon to myself once while I ate, till I could hear that I wouldn't get lost in it. I went the whole meal without trying. Someone was cooking somewhere outside. The wind brought an unfamiliar smell with a little chilli heat to it. I took the last little bite and then reached to open the old voice channel.

“Lumi,” I said. I hadn't thought of what to say next.

“Mm?”

All at once I didn't want to start at the beginning. “Can you say my name?” I asked.

“What shall I call you?”

“Elliot.”

She didn't say it right away. When she did, she sounded as if she had a few more words to get to, only she stopped at my name. I said mm and waited for her to tell me why she’d called. She waited a while too, and then she laughed. “You're still waiting for me to ask you something, aren't you?”

“Yes,” I said. “I was the one asking, though.”

“I know,” she said.

There was a little water beside my plate where the glass had been. I touched it. I didn't wipe it up or move the plate to cover it. There was time then for me to explain why I'd asked. I could have gone back through the afternoon, or told her about the doorstep and the flowerpot. She didn't owe me a memory like my mother's to make it come out right. I didn't tell her. She said Elliot again. She let the last sound go so softly I wasn't sure whether she wanted me to answer. It took me a little time to ask, “Do you want me to say I'm here?”

“Not just then,” she said. “You can now, if you'd like.”

“I'm here.”

I was still sitting where I'd been, with a hand on the table. She didn't call me to my feet. She didn't tell me what sort of answer she'd hoped for. I wanted to say her name just as she'd said mine, but when I tried, it came out in my ordinary voice. For once I didn't see any reason to try again.

A little later she asked if I was still having supper. I told her I'd eaten one leek and there was another in the fridge. I'd thought of so many things to tell somebody while I was eating. One leek was what had made it as far as her. She said the other one could stay where it was for now. We said goodnight. I closed the channel and took my plate to wash. I hadn't used the ladle and put it back in the drawer when I'd finished. By the time I came back to the table the ring of water where the glass had stood was missing a piece. I started wiping from the place it had gone. Halfway around I said her name quietly once more. This time there was no answer. I wasn't calling her back.
