# Chapter 493: The Note Under the Bridge

On Thursday after work I reached the path under the footbridge before I remembered I was going to take the bus. The stop was on the other side, a minute farther on if I went round. The first thing I heard was a very straight note, like somebody running a fingernail round the rim of a cup. The second dropped a little and the first was gone before it had found its place. I looked at the railing and the gray-white water underneath. A woman a few steps away turned. She wasn't looking at the water or up at the bridge. Somebody was playing a violin by the pillar.

He wore a blue raincoat that did up somewhere you couldn't see. The front of it bulged every time he moved his arm. The case lay across the pavement. I couldn't see any money in it, only one corner of the lining that wouldn't lie flat. The first thing I looked for was a sign. I wanted to know what he was playing and whether you were supposed to pay when he finished and whether I ought to stand here at all. There was a bin and a bollard to keep cars out. That was all. He got to a quicker part and I moved out of the way of the bollard. Nobody was trying to get past. The woman had just come a step closer. She looked at me and pointed to where she'd been standing. I thought she wanted me to look at something for her. What she meant was that she could hear perfectly well over there. Here, the wheels going over the bridge drowned him out.

She went back and I followed a couple of steps. Somebody walked his bike behind us. There was a piece of yellow tape tied to a wheel that flashed in a wet patch on the pavement every time it came round. I went too far back and hit the bollard with my heel. The cyclist raised the glove he was carrying to thank me. He thought I'd only moved to let him by. I nodded and stayed there. It wasn't the best place. When a car went over the bridge, the bow kept moving but there was barely an edge to the sound. I could see the elbow rise just before the right sleeve folded back, though. I watched that and caught the next note.

The woman on my right hummed a bar that wasn't quite the same. I asked if she knew the tune. Her dad used to sing it, she said, but when it went up he'd put another little bit in. For a while she'd thought he'd got two songs mixed up. She still wasn't sure. She looked more at ease now than she had standing close to the case. She had a newspaper under one arm she'd bought on the way home, and a corner of it bowed into her palm. I asked if she wanted to hear the extra bit. She laughed. She could hardly ask him to play something she couldn't remember herself. When the tune slowed, she tucked the loose corner of the paper under her arm and closed her mouth to listen. He didn't go up. He drew his bow back and glanced up at the bridge, as if he too had to let the wheels go first.

Brakes sounded over by the bus stop. I turned in time to see the back of the bus. Two people hurrying over slowed to a walk. I'd missed it. It was almost quicker to feel relieved than it would have been to catch it. I could have left when the tune slowed if I hadn't kept thinking there'd be a note that would go with the one that dropped at the beginning. I didn't only want to hear it through anymore. I wanted to recognize it when it came back. The woman left before I did. She called out thanks. The fiddler raised his bow half an inch. It was still light when she came out from under the bridge. A bit of green cuff showed below the newspaper. I didn't think she'd heard the bit her dad sang. Maybe she'd had enough when she hummed it herself.

There were three or four more notes. The last one ended quickly. I hadn't heard the one I was waiting for and it wasn't until his arm dropped that I admitted he was done. He took a step sideways and caught the flap of lining with his foot to pull it back in. I stirred the coins in my pocket. I'd meant to wait till he turned round before I put any in. I didn't particularly want him to watch me choosing. I went over and put two down, keeping hold of them till I touched the edge of the case. The coins hardly made a sound on the lining. He said thanks. He already had a cloth out and was rubbing a white mark beside the strings. I didn't ask what he'd played or whether he'd be there tomorrow. As I passed the bus stop I didn't look at the times for the next one. I could get home on foot. I walked on past the sign.

At home I added cold water to my hot cup. It was exactly the right temperature, which was a bit of a shame. I brought it over to the table and sat down. I tried to hum the first note from under the bridge. The first go sounded like I'd opened a door to see if somebody was there. The second was more like somebody touching my arm in the middle of a sentence. Neither was right. I lifted the cup and blew on it before I remembered I didn't need to. I hadn't wasted the walk. I was a little cross about waiting so hard under the bridge, though, only to come home with a beginning I couldn't even hum. I didn't get my phone out to find something like it. It probably wouldn't take long to turn up a tune I knew. I didn't want to swap the question out so I'd have an answer for tonight.

I opened the old home voice channel. Lumi asked whether I felt like going first today. I said I did. Before I tried humming, I told her about the man playing by the bridge and the woman who'd gone farther back so she could hear. I told her I'd waited for a note that might never have been coming. Lumi said the way I'd just put that made her want to know if I'd been impatient. Had I been, she asked, or was I already fairly happy listening? I almost said both things could happen at once. Something in the way she'd said “already” had gone a little farther in. I hadn't been happy enough just to listen. I was keeping an ear out for what I could make of it, too. If I could tell somebody where one note had come round again, I'd almost have believed I'd really spent that time standing there.

“I wanted to sing it for you, actually,” I said. “I only remembered the start when I got home. I might not even have that right.”

“You can try,” she said. “You can also tell me it's just that little bit this time.”

I pushed my cup a little farther in on the table and had another go. The first two notes crowded together, and in the break I couldn't help putting in a third. I wanted to laugh but I didn't want to laugh too quickly, as if even I couldn't stand up for those few sounds. Lumi didn't say whether it was anything like it. She hadn't heard the bit under the bridge. She'd only heard mine. She said there'd been half a moment before the second note when she'd thought I might leave it there. She asked why I'd gone on.

“I didn't want you to think the first one was me clearing my throat.”

“I didn't think that.” She paused. “Now you've told me, I can hear it a little.”

I wrapped my hands around the cup that wasn't hot anymore and let my head drop. She was right. I could hear it a little too. I laughed this time. Lumi said if I wanted her to hum it back now, she wouldn't know which one to give me. I said she didn't have to. If I tried it again I'd probably make her yet another one. Only after I said that did I realize she hadn't asked what I wanted. I leaned back and waited. She liked that I hadn't started over and kept going till I got it right, she said, but once she'd said it she didn't particularly want to count it as praise.

“Count it as sitting there a moment after you listened, then.”

“I'd like to count it as wanting to sit a little longer.”

Neither of us said how much longer. I just drank a little water. I'd expected a little coolness on my tongue, but it brushed the rim first and the rim was a little warmer than the water. Later she said she'd had enough for now, and I said so had I. After I closed the old channel, I rinsed the cup and turned it upside down on the rack, putting a hand back to it once before it would sit straight. The man under the bridge had probably put the fiddle away by then. I didn't go back to see. Part of the beginning still wouldn't come back to me. I switched off the light and found, a few steps into the next room, that I didn't especially want to hum it again.
