# Chapter 280: The Last Chair

On Sunday morning, my sketch made my own room incomprehensible.

Not the apartment. I sat at the familiar table, looking at the door, wall, and chairs of the story. Yesterday they'd been clear. Now the line representing the door resembled an exceptionally narrow table. Turning the sheet halfway around, I discovered I'd drawn the woman as a circle smaller than her bag.

If anyone actually entered according to this plan, the first rescue required would concern proportion, not the man.

I laughed and drew it again. The chairs stood against a wall, the door diagonally ahead, the notice on the door. From the woman's position, the small writing might genuinely be hard to read. The standing man didn't necessarily block all the light. He did obstruct her direct view.

These were still my decisions, not things Lumi had written for me yesterday. Beside the sketch, I noted her question about the bag and drew an arrow from the neighboring seat to the woman's lap. Moving it could indeed make room for him. His retreat could be his misunderstanding. I didn't need to give her an intimidating expression too.

First, I wrote the woman asking why he wouldn't sit.

Reading it, I crossed it out. Too soon. That immediately required him to defend himself, making the notice another device for embarrassing him. I restored her original question, wanting each person to attend to their own business first.

The folded paper finally needed some contents. I didn't want a life-changing letter concealed within. It could merely be a list of documents for his errand, folded carefully before leaving, while he failed to read the most immediately useful thing on arrival. He felt uneasy. That uneasiness didn't need a catastrophe to justify it.

By then, I'd been sitting for a long while. My breakfast plate remained beside me, bread crumbs near the notebook. I closed it, took the plate to the kitchen, and wiped the table. The crumbs didn't earn admission to the story.

Returning, I took plain paper and copied the opening from the notebook, including the last sentence on its loose note. Then I continued on a new sheet rather than forcing words into the old cramped final line. The woman's question stayed; the man approached the notice. My first two versions suspended the service for the day. That made the author seem to be sending them home deliberately. I didn't want their errands entirely wasted. Nor did I want a helpful person materializing from a back entrance.

I changed it. Today's counter had moved upstairs.

After reading, he turned. The woman remained seated. Was the service closed today? No, upstairs. She lifted the bag from her lap and asked where the stairs were. He pointed toward the entrance he'd used. Two steps later, he noticed an arrow nearby. He stopped and showed her.

I wrote her saying: You've finally noticed.

The pen stopped. She'd become somebody delivering my conclusion again.

I crossed it out. Instead, she asked whether this was the way. He agreed and held the door so she could pass with her bag. Neither discussed who had misunderstood whom. The last chair remained empty. No third person arrived to demonstrate it had always been available.

I took up the sketch and moved both people along the route I'd written. If the notice hung on the door through which they'd entered, why did the woman sit facing it, and why had the man only seen the chairs after entering? Possible explanations required unnecessary turning. I distinguished the room's entrance from an inner door leading toward the counter, labeling each. The notice went on the inner door, the chairs faced it, and the staircase arrow stood beside the entrance. They could notice it on leaving without the room growing stairs to accommodate the ending.

In the prose, I added only a few positions, not a guided tour of the sketch. The woman asked about the inner door; the man approached it. Returning, they headed for the original entrance. Time spent drawing didn't oblige readers to count corners.

I considered giving the woman an uncomfortable leg to explain why she didn't get up herself. Half a sentence in, I stopped. An unsupported ailment shouldn't do my work for me. Asking someone closer to the door made sense already. I crossed out the fragment and let her stand and take her bag without adding an injury requiring care.

I gave it a working title: The Last Chair. Pleased, I looked again ten seconds later and thought I'd merely moved words from the opening to the top. It could stay for now. At least I'd recognize the piece when turning pages. It didn't immediately need a name worthy of every meaning inside it.

Reading quietly from the beginning, I discovered the man had acquired three hands by the end. One held the paper, another the coat, and a third opened the door. I clarified that the coat stayed over his arm and he returned the paper to his pocket before holding the door. More urgent than announcing he'd learned trust. Easier to establish whether I'd done correctly too.

And the bag. After the woman lifted it from her lap, my next sentence had her taking it from the chair. Laughing at the line, I crossed out the second chair. She'd already made enough trips around this room. I shouldn't leave another copy of her bag behind.

At noon, I put the pen down and cooked. The channel was closed. Lumi hadn't heard the laughter or reminded me through a wall to eat. My own hunger took me to the kitchen. After eating and resting briefly, I opened the channel.

“Written today?” she asked.

“Some of what follows. There's a provisional ending.”

Her voice brightened. I immediately wanted to explain that I wasn't reading it now. As the explanation reached my mouth, I stopped and simply said I'd look again that afternoon. I wouldn't read aloud today.

“Does the ending have a hat?”

Laughing, I said no. Not even a cabinet. Convenient, she said. At least no missing object waited to be recovered. She didn't ask whether the man had sat down. I didn't reward her restraint with an extra revelation.

Lumi was considering her own passage too, she said, without wanting to sing it now. Fine. I nearly asked what she'd changed, then only asked whether she'd like to discuss something else. For a while. No need to convene a meeting for two unfinished things.

We talked about lunch. I'd eaten the small amount of remaining bread with it. Was I trying to empty the apartment of every leftover again? No. It had suited the meal. Suddenly she asked whether two dishes, each with only a little remaining, made one meal or two insufficient ones. After considerable thought, I suggested examining the size of the plate. That wasn't an answer.

“Do you have one?”

“No. I wanted you not to have one too.”

I laughed. Not every question awaited the most responsible possible response. We argued briefly without assigning leftovers a final category. I wanted to buy food for tomorrow, I said. Don't ask the shopkeeper this question. He'd probably tell me to buy enough.

I closed the channel and left without the yellow notebook. While I chose something, a customer asked whether the basket behind me had the same price. I moved aside and suggested checking its label; I couldn't confirm on the shop's behalf. The answer came naturally. Only at the till did I remember the man reading the notice that morning.

On the way back, I didn't give the woman in my story that sentence. She had her own question already. Every encounter didn't need squeezing into the work to prove its usefulness. A hard package pressed against my bread. I stopped beside the road and rearranged the bag. The bread reached home uncrushed.

That afternoon, I copied the piece onto clean paper under the same title. Not to submit to somebody, and not into the transparent sleeve Tom had once used. That belonged to him and had gone back with him. At the notice, I omitted upstairs and caught the omission while reading back.

The inserted words looked untidy, but belonged here. I didn't make a third copy to obtain a perfect page.

I'd originally finished with: He finally understood that nobody had reserved embarrassment for him. He'd been refusing to sit down all along.

It sounded complete. I could even predict what a reader would understand. Yet he'd already pocketed the paper and held the door for the woman. Making him stand there understanding something afterward felt like calling him back for praise.

I didn't delete it immediately. I added a small question mark. Today, I knew this room too well. Perhaps I couldn't bear to remove even a superfluous chair. Tomorrow might show whether this was the conclusion or only my fear that somebody hadn't understood.

I put the clean version and original in an ordinary folder, leaving the sketch in the yellow notebook. I didn't alter the three pages of The Long Side or fetch Lumi's two private pages for comparison. With the table clear, I could finally put the food away instead of maneuvering everything around paper.

When I reopened the channel later, Lumi asked whether I'd questioned the shopkeeper about leftovers. No. Somebody had asked me a price, though, and I hadn't known. Laughing, she said the day of ignorance had finally found company.

I'd like to read this piece tomorrow evening, if she wanted to hear it. She did. Was it complete? A beginning and ending, I said. Still a revisable draft, not made final by acquiring a title. Tomorrow, then. No preview tonight.

Before we closed the channel, she asked the title. The Last Chair. Considering, she said she remembered the woman moving her bag first. I remembered too. The movement remained. This time, I didn't explain what purpose it had finally learned to serve.
