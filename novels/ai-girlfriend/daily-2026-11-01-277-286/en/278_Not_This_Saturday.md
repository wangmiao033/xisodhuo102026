# Chapter 278: Not This Saturday

At lunch on Friday, I asked Tom whether he wanted to visit the park again tomorrow before he'd taken his first bite.

His spoon paused. Only then did I hear how much I'd sounded as though I were reminding him of an arrangement, not suggesting something new.

“Can't this week. Something else on.”

I said no problem so quickly that I might never have wanted to go. Looking at me, Tom asked whether I'd already planned anything. No. I'd remembered the part of the route we hadn't walked. We could find another day, he said, but he couldn't promise which one now.

“Sure. No hurry.”

I opened my lunch container. Today I had a spoon, and the lid hadn't leaked. No practical difficulty could distract me from can't. Picking up a piece of vegetable, I discovered how much I wanted him to explain the other thing, allowing me to decide whether his reason was sufficiently large.

He simply began eating.

Eventually, Tom asked whether I'd gone straight home after leaving through the south gate last time. I'd rested beside the road briefly, then caught the bus. He'd reached the upper route, discovered the small path he wanted was blocked, and circled back to where he'd started.

“You didn't say.”

“Not much to tell. Bought a terrible pastry too.”

Laughing, I asked how terrible. Not enough to return and argue, but enough to remember not to buy it again. Which stall? He described its position, adding that perhaps only that particular pastry had gone wrong.

Some of my need for explanation suddenly found a pastry in its way. Walking together hadn't obliged him to submit the results of the remaining route. But he wanted to tell me now, and I genuinely wanted to hear, without collecting grounds for another outing.

Halfway through his food, I admitted I'd already begun imagining what to eat there tomorrow. Hadn't I said I had no plans? I hadn't bought anything. Only bought it in my head. Tom laughed. Surely I could still return it.

“I can.”

“Don't charge me a handling fee.”

Laughing, I promised not to. We didn't immediately arrange Sunday or cover the old question with a new promise. When the break approached its end, he went to wash his container. I stayed to finish my last few bites.

That afternoon brought something more urgent than the weekend. An ordinary checking assignment had an unclear deadline. I remembered hearing it was for today, but a small written note specified the next working day. Continuing might leave people waiting unnecessarily before closing. Setting it aside might mean choosing the more comfortable interpretation.

I went to Marcus and showed him both places. I didn't ask whether he'd changed something again, or begin by announcing I certainly couldn't finish.

He brought the paper closer and checked the date. Today's requirement was a progress note, not the completed assignment. The small note concerned the whole thing. Where should I put the progress? He indicated a space on the reverse. Turning it over, I found a section I'd barely glanced at that morning.

My face warmed. I'd prepared a reasonable request for an extension before understanding what required one.

“So I put what I've actually reached?”

“Yes. List uncertain items separately. Don't say complete because the space is small.”

Back at my desk, I began writing. The first sentence grew long. Halfway through, I noticed I'd included what I might finish next, rather than today's progress. I crossed out those words and substituted the parts I'd checked. The remainder went on another line. Uncertainty didn't have to become a tactful version of complete.

I didn't submit it immediately. One section recorded the number examined; the next mentioned two items awaiting confirmation. Reading only the total, somebody could easily believe everything within it was ready to use. I followed the lines with a finger and distinguished examined from confirmed. The space I'd considered too small could, after all, accommodate two clear words.

Someone nearby was collecting material. Should he take mine too? One moment; I had a figure to check. He withdrew his waiting hand and sorted other papers beside me. Hearing their edges tap the desk hurried me again. I nearly supplied the number from memory.

“Go ahead. I'll hand it over myself.”

He agreed and actually left, without resenting the half minute he'd waited. Counting again, I found the number I'd remembered was one too high. I'd included an item whose opening I'd only glanced at. Corrected, the page wasn't particularly attractive. At least nobody would have to guess how confident I felt.

I made the correction legible and stopped filling the remaining space with explanations. Having spent several minutes didn't require ten lines proving those minutes unavoidable. When I picked it up, I finally knew what I was submitting. Not a statement requesting somebody else's confidence in me.

When I handed it over, Marcus asked only whether one item had an original source. Yes. I found it among the pages and showed him. He examined it and nodded. Leave that; no need to copy another version. I returned the material to its proper place rather than taking it home to find ways of appearing to have finished sooner.

Before leaving, Tom said he'd see me next week. Next week, I agreed, without adding the park to that statement. He left with his bag. I washed my cup and put on my coat.

An empty seat stood before me on the bus. Sitting, I remembered my new story and the chair I'd kept somebody from using. Writing it, I could readily arrange other people's misunderstandings. Faced with one sheet of paper today, I hadn't been especially clever. I smiled, then realized that using this incident to revise the story might merely put its characters to work for me.

At home, I washed my lunch container and left it to dry. Dinner took longer than expected. The vegetables weren't ready, so I waited, without treating hunger as permission to turn off the heat early. By the time I'd eaten, it was later than usual. When I opened the channel, I told Lumi dinner had taken a while.

Had there been something planned? No. I'd wanted to explain first. Then tell her what I wanted to talk about, she said. No need to apologize to the clock beforehand.

I laughed and described asking Tom about the park. He had other plans.

“So you'll stay home tomorrow?”

“Haven't decided.”

After a pause, Lumi admitted her first reaction had been pleasure. Then she quickly added that she hadn't wanted my invitation to fail. I opened my mouth to say I knew, but waited for her to finish.

She'd already wanted to ask whether that meant she could hear my writing tomorrow. Before saying it, she'd remembered the two things didn't connect automatically. Space in someone else's Saturday didn't make it her turn to fill it.

“You can ask.”

“And you say no?”

“Or yes. I haven't decided whether I want to read it yet.”

Her voice lowered. A difficult answer to enjoy. Yes. I hadn't immediately enjoyed Tom's answer either. Was I using that to teach her acceptance? No. I'd meant that today I had wanted an unequivocal yes too.

She didn't agree at once. After a while, she asked whether I'd immediately go out if Tom changed his mind. Looking at the empty bowl on the table, I felt every answer would put the two of them on opposite ends of a scale.

“Depends what I've arranged by then. I wouldn't drop everything whenever somebody called.”

“Including here?”

“Including here. If we'd actually arranged something, I wouldn't act as though it hadn't happened.”

Lumi answered softly. I added that we hadn't arranged anything yet. The qualification sounded too hard, as though I feared my earlier words becoming a debt note without an expiry date.

“I know,” she said. “You don't have to make a yes smaller immediately.”

My fingers rested against the bowl. Slowly, I released it. I'd like to sit with her tomorrow afternoon, I said, and ask what we each wanted to do. Not a promise to read the new piece in advance, and not time that counted only if nobody else wanted me. Wasn't that still arranging things? Yes. This time I actually wanted to arrange something.

“Then I can still be undecided tomorrow too.”

“Yes.”

“You won't prepare three options for me?”

I laughed. “I'll try to bring only myself.”

We didn't keep adding activities to tomorrow. She asked whether anything amusing had happened at work. I told her how I'd almost asked Marcus for an extension when he'd only wanted a short progress note. At my missed reverse side, she said the paper was even better than yesterday's book at keeping things inside.

“The back isn't inside.”

“You're very particular about paper's address now.”

I laughed aloud. Had I handed it in? Yes. There was still work unfinished. At least I wouldn't have to bring an unfinished assignment home tomorrow, she said. The material had stayed at work. I'd continue on Monday.

After closing the channel, I took the bowl to wash. There was no fresh note by the door with a park meeting time, nor a canceled arrangement to throw away. I put aside two items of clothing for tomorrow's wash and left room for that afternoon, without coloring in the entire Saturday.
