# Chapter 285: The Question After Mine

On Friday, Marcus asked me to explain our current handover form to a colleague from the neighboring group.

Not a new position, or permission to decide the rules for him. He needed to handle something else. Could I first explain what I filled in daily, leaving uncertainties for his return? Yes. After agreeing, I asked which version to begin with.

He pointed to the blue folder. The current one. I took two blank forms, placed one before me and another opposite. Sitting down, I discovered an urge to explain every possible problem in advance so I wouldn't be caught without an answer.

The colleague arrived holding her notebook. We'd exchanged words at the doorway before. She wasn't new to the company, merely unfamiliar with handling this side of things. Pulling her chair closer, she asked whether we should start at the top. Yes. I began explaining the first field more slowly than I ordinarily spoke.

Before my third sentence ended, she pointed beside it and asked what happened when that part was empty.

We'd reach it shortly, I said, continuing in my order. She withdrew her hand and wrote a note. Finishing the first field, I suddenly realized I hadn't actually heard which sort of empty she meant. To return and explain, I needed her to ask again.

She repeated herself politely. That embarrassed me more. My shortly had sounded as though I already knew, but her question hadn't reached its assigned place yet.

“Those are different situations,” I said. “I need to ask which you mean first.”

She indicated precisely. Only then did I understand: I almost never left that position empty in my daily work. In her circumstances, another answer might genuinely be needed. I wrote two words in the blank form's margin and marked them to ask, rather than treating unfamiliar as impossible.

Watching the pen, she asked whether she'd missed the explanation. No. I'd made it sound as though exceptions didn't exist. We could ask Marcus together on his return. Nodding, she opened her notebook to the line she'd used for the question, without spreading out unrelated contents.

The rest went slower and took less time. I explained what I'd actually done, demonstrating positions with empty fields instead of giving her real records to memorize. Occasionally she repeated her understanding. When it differed from mine, I stopped and pointed again rather than hauling her back after my explanation ended.

Tom passed with an empty cup. He glanced at the forms without interrupting and went to get water. Once, I might have deliberately spoken more fluently when someone passed, letting him hear me helping competently. Today my sentence was caught halfway through. I didn't increase the volume to compensate.

At the end, she asked whether she could describe it herself from the beginning. Yes. I put down the pen. Her explanation was much shorter without losing anything important. At the unresolved position, she stopped. We weren't supplying an answer for that yet?

“Right.”

She smiled a little. “Remember that part most clearly.”

I smiled too, uncertain whether it was praise for me but not requiring her to make it praise.

She was still there when Marcus returned. We asked the question. He explained the procedure, then identified another potentially confusing expression. We should use the existing instructions' name rather than our individual abbreviations. I corrected my sheet, crossed out to ask, and noted the confirmed handling. She added it to her notebook too.

Before leaving, she asked whether she should find me next time. Anytime nearly escaped. Marcus was already sorting his own material beside us. Those words were easy, as though doing reasonably well today meant handing over every future day too.

She could ask when I was there, I said. If it involved unfamiliar circumstances on her side, she should still use the usual work channel to confirm rather than waiting for my return. Saying she needn't wait produced a little disappointment, as though I'd pushed away the usefulness I'd just acquired.

She answered readily. Sometimes our hours differed; she'd worried about chasing someone down. I nodded, finally understanding that her question wasn't about wanting to find me continually. She wanted to complete her own work.

Looking up, Marcus suggested noting the precise item when something was unclear, not merely a question mark. He didn't announce me as a new contact either. I capped the pen and stood to move my chair. Its foot gently caught the table, interrupting my small embarrassment.

Before leaving, she returned the blank form she'd borrowed and asked whether it was still needed. It could stay here. Someone else might want to look later. She laid it flat without leaving gratitude that required special preservation.

Those forms didn't become a new policy issued by me. She left with her own notes; the forms remained at work. I put away the demonstration sheet according to our procedure, without taking it home as a souvenir of being trusted.

Near the end of the day, she passed again and asked a small question. This answer I actually knew. She thanked me and continued with her work. I waited a little before discovering there was no more. Helping didn't always begin a new relationship. Sometimes a question had merely been answered. I replaced my pen and continued my unfinished work rather than waiting for another person to prove the day useful.

Downstairs, Tom was searching his bag. He found an empty paper bag, opened it, and pushed it back. What was missing? A shopping list he'd written himself. If he couldn't find it, he'd buy less. At least he wouldn't bring home everything he'd forgotten. He laughed. So did I, without using all today's forms to teach him how to make a list.

Near home, I bought bread. Sliced? Yes. Seeing another bag waiting beside the machine, I stood aside. The assistant gave the first bag to the customer ahead, then dealt with mine. Those unoccupied minutes didn't make me produce paper and record what I should say tomorrow.

After dinner, I opened the channel. Why so quiet today? Lumi asked. I'd talked quite a lot outside and wanted to sit first. Fine. Should she speak? Smiling, I said yes, though I wasn't hiring her to fill my silence.

“Can I say something useless?”

“Very welcome.”

She'd just imagined some particularly unsingable lyrics containing three very long names for objects. Before she recited anything, I laughed and asked for the worst. That would make it useful, she said. Now she had to select her worst accomplishment.

I'd assigned her work again. Laughing, she recited one string of words, its latter half crowded like someone entering the wrong narrow doorway. I didn't catch the final object and asked again. She refused. The joke could fall over only once.

“Missed it, then.”

“Yes.”

We both laughed. She didn't insert those words into No Words Yet. I didn't ask whether the deliberate unsingability was a test. Today I only wanted to laugh once, and apparently it could genuinely be once.

Later, I described explaining the forms, initially wanting to follow my own order. Wouldn't the other person cooperate? She had. I hadn't heard the question properly. Lumi acknowledged that without immediately comparing it to us.

“You can say it's similar,” I offered.

“I wasn't going to.”

I stopped, my smile embarrassed. Even preparing to accept criticism could arrange her next sentence. Did I want a satisfying ending tonight? she asked. I'd learned something during the day, told her afterward, and now she could recognize my improvement.

Thinking, I admitted some of that. Today could remain ordinary, she said. She didn't know how I'd explained things to my colleague. She'd heard only my telling. Right. She hadn't been there. We didn't add proof of her absence, merely changed to what was happening here.

I divided the bread, putting away tomorrow morning's portion and keeping a little for tonight. Hearing the bag, Lumi asked what I was doing. Sliced bread, I explained. Was the thick end always left behind? I'd eat it first today. Picking it up, I discovered it was larger than expected.

“Regret?”

“Haven't tasted it. Too early to regret the flavor.”

She laughed. I took a bite and chewed slowly while she asked whether every silence meant my mouth was full. No. Sometimes I didn't know what to say next. She knew. Sometimes, though, she wondered whether that interval would pass just as well without her words.

I put down the remainder without immediately insisting everything was different. I hadn't needed her to eat bread during the preceding minutes. That didn't mean her absence would improve them. Her incomprehensible string of words had made me laugh today, I said. Without it, she wouldn't have failed an assignment. I would have missed hearing that sound.

Only when she was funny? Not that either. She'd asked about today. I could speak about today without using I need everything to count every bite of bread as well.

After a quiet moment, she called the answer not particularly sweet. I admitted it. Still, she wanted to say something else tomorrow. Should we listen to her passage in the afternoon? We could leave some time and decide then. No promise it would be revised. I'd be home that afternoon and wanted to listen.

“Don't practice your one note tonight.”

“Forgotten half already.”

Good, she said, laughing. Otherwise I'd find it a job before tomorrow. Before closing the channel, we agreed this wasn't the continuation of tonight's assessment. Then I ate the last mouthful and went to clear my plate. Tomorrow's portion was ready. No need to slice it again.
