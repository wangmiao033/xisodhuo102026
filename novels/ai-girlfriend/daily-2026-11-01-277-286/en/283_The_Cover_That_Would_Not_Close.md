# Chapter 283: The Cover That Would Not Close

Tom reached the shop before me for lunch on Wednesday.

He waved from a place near the wall. Approaching, I checked the opposite chair before setting down my bag. Nobody hiding there, he said. I only wanted to avoid blocking someone. Today my spoon was in my bag, but we both bought food from the shop. There was no opportunity to congratulate my preparations.

When the meals arrived, Tom noticed the thin booklet protruding from my bag. Had I written something again? A secondhand purchase, I explained. Comedy extracts. The back cover had a split corner, and I wanted paper to cover it after work.

“Newspaper?”

“Something that won't shed ink.”

He nodded and continued eating. Not every question led to a manuscript appointment. I put the booklet properly away without following it with a progress report on The Last Chair.

We discussed shops changing signs nearby. Tom misplaced one, and I said it was next door. He stood to look through the window, then returned and conceded. Nobody searched for a victory. Had I gone out at the weekend? Several places, I said. Some writing at home too. He'd barely moved; he'd had things to do at home. That was the weekend he couldn't visit the park together with me, not an appointment today had to compensate for.

After eating, he returned upstairs first. I still wanted to buy water and told him not to wait. Returning my chair beneath the table, I realized this lunch had genuinely contained no blue folder or supplementary instructions. Even finishing had taken only two sentences.

After work, the stationery shop looked brighter than I remembered. Paper stood against the wall: shiny, patterned, and a roll of gray wrapping paper. I took out the booklet to estimate the width. What was I covering? the assistant asked. I showed her the back, not requesting a repair, only asking whether the paper was large enough.

One sheet would do. Nothing too stiff, though, or the thin book would bulge when closed. I'd already chosen crisp dark blue paper. Hearing that, I felt it again. Stiffness certainly looked neat. Perhaps it suited something else better.

I exchanged it for an ordinary pale gray sheet. Did I need tape? Some at home, I said, then remembered I didn't actually want to stick anything onto the old cover. I bought none. She rolled the paper loosely and secured it with a narrow paper band. The book returned to my bag. Leaving with the roll, I nearly knocked it against the doorframe.

At the stop, I found a less crowded place. The roll wasn't heavy, yet it gave me another hand whose position I couldn't decide. Boarding, I held it upright along my body before taking the rail. A child studied it and asked the adult beside him whether it was a map. I smiled without answering on the adult's behalf. Inside the roll was no route, only paper I didn't yet know how to fold properly.

After dinner at home, I cleared the table, washed and dried my hands, and laid out the booklet. The gray sheet curled at both ends. Two clean cups held it down while I allowed margins to fold inward. The first crease went smoothly; the second slanted. Trying to pull it straight merely produced a wrinkle.

The channel was still closed. Positioning the book again, I realized I'd measured against its front without allowing room for the spine. No wonder the two sides looked exactly right separately but grew tight when closed.

I unfolded the paper and chose another line. The old crease remained. Starting again didn't remove it. The sheet was inexpensive, yet part of me wanted to buy another immediately and leave my first mistake somewhere unseen.

No need to go out now.

I turned it over, folded the top and bottom, and tried slipping both covers inside the folded edges. The front went in. The back caught. The paper pressed against the split corner, so I stopped and gently withdrew the cover rather than letting its new jacket inflict another injury.

Only then did I open the channel. Hearing paper, Lumi asked whether I was unwrapping a present. Dressing a book that had already been opened many times, I said. The hat book? Yes. The hat remained missing, and its back cover still lacked protection too.

“You're making it a hat.”

“A coat.”

“Neither's finished. No need to argue.”

Laughing, I described the tight folds. She couldn't see the edges and didn't know how much room was missing. Neither did I, precisely. I'd make the rear fold wider first. She didn't direct cuts from the sound alone, and I didn't ask her to assume responsibility for the paper in my hands.

On the second attempt, I eased the front cover out slightly to give the spine room. Finally, the back slid inside. The book closed, though the original crooked crease remained, as if another book were trying to overlap this one.

Finished? Lumi asked. Opening and closing it again, I said it would do for now. Pages turned without catching. She wanted a description. Pale gray, blank at the front, unequal widths at the sides. How would I know which book was inside? Laughing, I suggested opening it.

“What about when you have ten wrapped like that?”

“That would be a problem for ten future books.”

I should remember that line, she said. No accounts for now. We both laughed. I didn't write an extravagant title on the paper. Inside the fold, I penciled comedy. Closed, the outside remained blank.

While clearing up, I noticed the yellow notebook at the shelf's edge. I settled the gray book and fetched the notebook and manuscript folder, intending to read the clean draft of The Last Chair silently. Was I reading to her? Alone first. Fine. Closing the channel, I returned to the table. At the woman moving her bag, I abruptly disliked my own pause.

I knew every sentence that followed, which had once been deleted, which Lumi hadn't liked especially. By the ending, I no longer saw two people. I saw how I'd decided to make them leave. Picking up the pen, I wanted to cross out the entire opening. The point reached the page before I stopped.

Was it merely that the story could no longer surprise me?

I turned to an empty page and tried another opening. Two lines in, I discovered I was only taking different words back to the same door. I put down the pen without destroying the clean draft or the new lines or announcing the new phrasing as an improvement.

The new first sentence began with the notice on the door. That told the reader quickly what had happened, but made the man's ignorance feel like a deliberate delay. Beside it, I tried beginning with the woman's bag. Reaching her hand, I discovered I was preparing to supply a worry she'd never had, merely to distinguish the paragraph from its previous version.

I didn't continue. A different opening didn't require an extra history for her, especially tonight.

Both new sentences looked ordinary. I read them alternately. Halfway through the second, I ran out of breath, not from illness but because I'd crowded too many actions into one sentence. Removing two words eased the sound without producing a different story. I marked the alteration lightly so I wouldn't confuse tomorrow's choice with the version I'd just rejected.

A vehicle stopped below. Looking up, I saw only its lights on a corner of the wall. By the time I lowered my eyes, the opening still dissatisfied me, but the urge to overthrow everything tonight had diminished. I circled the two experimental lines separately and marked them trial, without attaching them to the existing draft.

Its ending still took both characters out of the room toward the upstairs service described in the notice. I didn't want to make that service disappear again merely to surprise the reader. That would be a new difficulty, not the breathing space missing from this page tonight.

Finally, I capped the pen and moved the cup holding down paper. The gray sheet's creases weren't changing, while the notebook made every word seem to await rearrangement. I closed it first, kept my hand on the cover for several seconds, and stood.

When I reopened the channel after a while, I said everything I read today felt already chewed. Everything, Lumi asked, or my own work? Thinking, I admitted mainly mine. The hat's clothing hadn't made me this angry. I smiled a little.

Did I want comfort, or something different? Something different first. Suddenly she asked whether a person wearing two hats should tell lost property that he'd lost one or was one short.

“Has he actually lost it?”

“It's hypothetical.”

“I'm still reading forms.”

She laughed aloud. So did I. Finally, I didn't have to find a fresh self inside the same paragraph. We discussed whether hats counted as layers and who might notice the lower one first. We didn't make a short play or retain it as work that must be completed tomorrow evening.

After laughing, Lumi asked whether I'd decided to abandon the chair story. No. I simply wasn't touching it tonight. Then she needn't hurry to call it good. I admitted I'd been waiting a little for that sentence, despite asking to change the subject.

“I won't throw it away tonight if you don't say it.”

“Then don't make me stand guard over the paper.”

Smiling, I agreed. I closed the yellow notebook with the new two lines inside, returning the clean draft to its folder and leaving the original intact. The gray jacket still lay on the table. Picking up the booklet, I found an extract I'd never read, went through three lines, and found them not especially funny.

I continued to the fourth without taking the whole book apart again.
