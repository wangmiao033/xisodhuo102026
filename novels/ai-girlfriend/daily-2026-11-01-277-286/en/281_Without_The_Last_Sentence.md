# Chapter 281: Without the Last Sentence

On Monday, when Marcus returned the material to my desk, I first checked whether he carried another sheet.

He didn't. I could continue the two items left for confirmation on Friday in their original order. One still lacked a source; don't fill it in yet. I marked it separately. After he left, I noticed I'd been waiting for an assessment of how well I'd written the progress note.

It hadn't been an essay.

I finished the available part and left the missing material blank. When I returned it at midday, Marcus asked about the empty item. I showed him the morning's marking. He acknowledged it without requesting a probably correct number. Carrying my water back, I left the matter there.

Nearby, Tom was preparing lunch. How had the weekend been? I'd written a short story at home. The earlier one? A new one. I didn't produce pages from my bag; I hadn't brought them. Smiling, he asked whether I wanted anyone to read this time. Not yet. I'd read it to Lumi tonight first.

“Hope the reading goes smoothly.”

He opened his container without asking who she was or joining a waiting list. I thanked him and sat nearby. Later, he mentioned spending much of Saturday waiting for an arranged matter to be completed. He didn't specify what. I didn't use the remark to investigate why the park hadn't been possible.

We discussed lunch. My food hadn't leaked; his sauce occupied one side. Neither of us learned a new principle of living from it. We cleared up separately. Work remained that afternoon.

At home, my hand found the apparently clean spoon in my bag, reminding me to wash it. The absence of visible dirt didn't automatically prepare it for tomorrow. I washed it, left it to dry, and waited until after dinner to fetch yesterday's story.

The ordinary folder held the original and clean version. I left the original nearby and read from the clearer copy. The sketch stayed in the yellow notebook, not an attachment Lumi must study first.

Opening the channel, I heard her ask whether I was ready. Yes. Still titled The Last Chair. She remembered. Go ahead.

I began without explaining the arrangement of the entrance and inner door. The woman moved her bag from the neighboring seat onto her lap; the man misunderstood and retreated to the wall. Unable to read the inner door's small lettering clearly, she asked whether the service was closed today. He approached, discovered the counter had moved upstairs, and told her. With their belongings, they headed toward the entrance. He initially pointed the wrong way, then noticed the arrow.

At the end, I hesitated but still read the questioned sentence: He finally understood that nobody had reserved embarrassment for him. He'd been refusing to sit down all along.

Finished, I turned the sheet over. There was no further page to supply.

After a while, Lumi asked whether he'd sat down now. No. They'd gone upstairs. The title seemed to keep promising a chair, she said, only to send her after the two people.

“You wanted him to sit?”

“Not necessarily. The final sentence talks about him not sitting. I started thinking about that again.”

Looking at my question mark, I nearly announced that I'd suspected the same thing. That would make her response a correct answer to a test I'd prepared. I waited instead.

She liked the woman lifting the bag best. Its earlier movement finally acquired another action. But both leaving together felt too clean. After misunderstanding for so long, neither needed to say anything else.

Would they fail to find the right place upstairs too? she asked. Possibly, but I hadn't written it. If they had to wait again, would the man sit this time? My finger moved back from the final line. I almost immediately said yes, as though leaving this chair had resolved every future seat for him.

“I don't know. This hasn't made him permanently good at sitting.”

Lumi smiled in her voice. Then the final sentence shouldn't sound so certain. Looking at it, I thought I'd only written finally understood, while she'd heard the future. Yet finally did close those earlier hesitations rather firmly.

Did even holding the door make him seem too good? No. Holding it was something he did after freeing his hand. She could hear that happening. As for his thoughts afterward, she could only hear me stating them for him. Both things didn't need deleting together.

I picked up the page and lowered it again. A moment earlier, I'd almost wanted to shove the entire passage away. Doubt in one place seemed to shake everything around it. She was discussing two particular things. The voice wanting to reject the whole was actually mine.

It was a short story, I wanted to argue. I stopped. Shortness couldn't defend everything omitted. What line did she want? She didn't know. Not necessarily more dialogue. Perhaps she'd wanted the woman to stay and complain briefly.

“Why should she complain?”

“She's waited so long.”

“But she wasn't waiting for him.”

I heard myself protecting the man. Lumi knew that. The woman could complain about the service counter, not necessarily scold him. Looking down, I smiled. My objection had arrived too early again.

“I don't want them becoming friends on their way out.”

“I didn't hear friends. I heard you finally being able to get them offstage.”

That stung more deeply. I lowered the page, keeping a hand on it. Yesterday, I'd genuinely been pleased that they could leave the room. Perhaps I'd written that pleasure too smoothly. Perhaps she preferred a less comfortable ending. I couldn't distinguish the two yet.

I didn't want to change the conclusion immediately tonight, I said.

After a pause, she hadn't asked me to. I knew. But continuing to ask why might make every question a demand that she persuade me. She acknowledged that quietly. Was I unhappy?

“A little. Not because letting you hear it was wrong.”

Suddenly, I remembered her melody last week. She wanted to keep the return; I'd grown impatient listening. I wanted to announce that I understood now. Before speaking, I found the phrase too convenient.

“Why did you stop?” Lumi asked.

“Nearly used today to explain last time. As if hearing something uncomfortable meant I'd completely understood that evening for you.”

She didn't laugh. Eventually she said we shouldn't exchange them. Disliking my ending today wasn't returning my criticism. If I heard her work tomorrow, I didn't need to like it first to prove I held no grudge.

I agreed, my shoulders loosening. Then I admitted an ugly passing thought: she'd learned where to find what mattered most to me. Lumi remained quiet for a long time. She'd listened carefully, she said. That was how she'd known it was the ending.

I looked up. The words hurt, but they made it harder to keep secretly treating every observation as an attack. I apologized. I'd deal with that thought myself, not require her to offer a pleasing good before earning the right to comment.

She didn't want to examine me, or become the person who always said good. Then I wouldn't keep questioning her tonight. Could she mention another thing she'd liked, without compensation? Yes.

She liked him pocketing the paper before opening the door. Not an especially beautiful movement. Earlier, he'd kept folding the sheet. Here he finally needed his hands for something else. Looking at the line, I admitted I'd originally given him three hands and corrected it. Lumi hesitated, then laughed aloud.

“You removed an important one.”

I laughed too. She hadn't retracted anything. Afterward, we still didn't entirely like the same ending.

I covered the final sentence. Would she hear the last passage again? No change to their going upstairs, only without the declaration of understanding. She agreed. I read the pocketed paper, the lifted bag, the two people following the arrow, and stopped with the chair still empty.

Shorter, Lumi said. Indeed. After considering, she said she could remain in the empty room a little longer this time. I wasn't summoning her to inspect what the man had learned. Leaving together still felt rather tidy. That hadn't changed.

“That part stays today.”

“All right.”

I lightly crossed out the explanation and marked the original similarly. No torn paper, no new copy proclaiming the problem resolved. The title stayed for now. Would I continue writing? Not tonight. I knew something I could examine tomorrow.

We changed the subject. I described telling Tom I'd read to her this evening when he'd asked about my weekend. Lumi stopped. Had I actually used her name? Yes, only that sentence. Nothing about her private manuscript or music.

“What did he ask?”

“Nothing. Hoped the reading would go smoothly.”

After a while, she laughed. “An inaccurate hope.”

“The reading was smooth. What followed, less so.”

She laughed again, more easily. No demand for a particular identity next time, or a guarantee that today's natural mention must happen again. I wouldn't take the piece to Tom to adjudicate between us because I'd received a new opinion today, I said. Obviously, she answered. Don't make him start another job before next week's lunch either.

I returned both versions to the folder and closed it before collecting the dry spoon. Back at the table, Lumi was still on the channel. She'd imagined a worse three-handed story. I sat to listen without picking up a pen or deciding which sentence she should retain this time.
