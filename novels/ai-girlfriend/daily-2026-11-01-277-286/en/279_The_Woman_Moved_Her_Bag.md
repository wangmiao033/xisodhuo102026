# Chapter 279: The Woman Moved Her Bag

On Saturday afternoon, I pulled the loose ending note from the yellow notebook and discovered I'd inserted it backward.

The blank side faced me. For a moment, I thought I'd lost the ending. Turning it over restored the sentence about the notice on the door, in larger writing than the cramped final line in the notebook. I placed it correctly, read the two parts together, and smiled at the elaborate entrances I'd provided for one short paragraph.

The two things I'd washed that morning were hanging up, and lunch was cleared. I wasn't expecting a visitor to knock, yet I moved unnecessary things from the table. After removing my water, I brought it back. Nobody had requested an empty table today.

I opened the channel and called Lumi. After answering, she asked how many options I'd brought. One for now. Ambitious, she said.

“I'd like to read that paragraph. It's still short.”

Rather than immediately asking me to begin, she asked whether I genuinely wanted to or was responding to yesterday's disappointment. Pressing a finger against the loose note, I considered. Reading it earlier, it had still felt like something I couldn't hold properly. Today, I didn't know what followed either. But I wanted to hear where somebody encountering it for the first time would stop.

“I want to know what you hear. Not ask you to continue it for me.”

“Can I dislike it?”

“Yes.”

“I'm not returning that sentence to you.”

I looked up, realizing I'd already remembered last week's music. She might genuinely think it poor, Lumi said. She didn't want me assuming she was still angry when she said so. Understood. I might feel disappointed too, I admitted. I couldn't pretend in advance not to care.

She laughed softly. “Read it, then. Let's not become experts at disappointment first.”

I began with the man approaching the last chair. The paper in his hand folded smaller; the woman moved her bag; he retreated to the wall. Then she looked up and asked about the lettering on the door. At the notebook's final line, I paused briefly before continuing from the note. Hadn't read it fell into a room with nothing after it.

“That's where it stops?” Lumi asked.

“Yes.”

“I thought you'd gone to turn a page.”

Laughing, I explained there was no next page, only the note. Why was the woman sitting there? I hadn't decided what business she'd come for. Not that, Lumi said. Why had she put the bag beside her?

I examined the sentence. It only said she'd moved the bag, not where it had been. In my head, I'd pictured her bringing it onto her lap. Apparently I hadn't put that onto the page.

“Where did you hear it?”

“On the chair. She moved it to let him sit.”

I immediately said no. She might be reserving it. Hearing my own speed, I stopped. Lumi didn't pursue an argument. That wasn't what she'd heard, she said.

Remembering my own he thought, I finally noticed the difference between them. The man believed she was keeping a seat for someone. I didn't necessarily need a listener to believe it too. Lumi could know something before he did, or merely guess.

“You think she's helping him.”

“I think she wants him to stop standing in front of her. Not necessarily helping.”

That made me smile. She didn't require an especially considerate woman, or make the character someone deliberately obstructing him. Perhaps he blocked the light. Perhaps the space beside the chair was cramped. These were Lumi's guesses, not events the story had established.

I nearly reached for my pen, then asked whether I could note one question: where did the bag move from and to? Write my question, she said. No need to transcribe everything she said as an assessment. I put the question in the margin without converting all her possibilities into candidate answers.

What was the paper in his hand? Undecided, I said. Perhaps something for his errand. If the door explained the place was closed, why hadn't he looked first? His attention to the seat was what I wanted to write about. She paused, then asked to hear the last two sentences again.

I read them faster this time. Wait, she said. I was explaining how she should listen again. I stopped and admitted that, thinking I understood her question, I'd hurried toward an answer. Then I read at an ordinary pace, without stressing concentrated so hard.

He sounded less foolish this time, she said.

“The words haven't changed.”

“You embarrassed him quite badly before.”

I laughed, embarrassed myself. I'd written somebody who wanted to be seen, then decided in my reading what everyone should see. That didn't mean every sentence must be delivered flatly now. I had genuinely pushed certain words too hard, though.

Had the woman read the notice by the end? I hadn't reached that point. Then perhaps she couldn't read the small lettering, Lumi suggested, rather than finding something to reprimand him about. Looking at the notebook, I suddenly wanted to continue. Not to write her suggestion immediately while she listened, however.

“I'd like to leave it a while.”

“Leave my words, or your people?”

“The paragraph. I won't write it now.”

She acknowledged that. She didn't ask whether she'd said something wrong, or require me to admit she'd helped. I wanted to declare it useful immediately, giving her a pleasing result to take away. Instead, I asked where she'd most wanted it to continue.

“The door. Not because it's mysterious. I don't know whether to go over and look either.”

I nodded, then said I'd heard. Had I nodded? The chair had made a sound. Laughing, I admitted I had. The chair had participated in one small judgment too.

Had I wanted her to say she liked it? Lumi asked. Looking at the lines, I admitted I'd wanted that too, particularly about the beginning. She liked the paper being folded smaller, she said, but didn't know whether he was nervous. I nearly explained that of course he was, then noticed I hadn't even specified how many times he'd folded it.

“Perhaps it's simply awkward to hold,” she said.

“Why do you keep finding practical reasons for them?”

She laughed. Had she failed to detect sufficient emotional difficulty? I wanted to object, then laughed too. A little. I'd prepared so much uneasiness for this man that hearing his paper was too large made the preparation seem wasted.

“Large paper can make someone uneasy. I just didn't hear that much.”

Good, I said, genuinely this time, not closing a case. At least the movement had occurred in her hearing. A different motive hadn't made it disappear. I didn't change it to a shaking hand to guarantee everybody guessed correctly.

I replaced the note and closed the notebook. No title, and reading this much didn't make the whole thing complete. Did the rest of the afternoon require me to keep thinking? Lumi could leave me quiet. No need, I said. I'd put it away.

“Put it away, or only covered the paper?”

I almost called them the same. Honestly, I was still thinking about the bag. Then think for another two minutes, she said. She didn't want to compete with a bag whose appearance she didn't even know.

That made me uncomfortable. Was she angry? A little. She'd listened carefully. Now I'd suddenly gone inside my own head. She didn't require more discussion of the writing. She didn't want me saying there was no need to stop while I wasn't really listening either.

I removed my hand from the notebook. She was right. I was still there. Yet I didn't want to crush the thought I'd just received in order to demonstrate that I liked her better than writing. We paused. Could we take a short break separately, then reopen the channel, without her waiting beside me?

“Yes. Just say you want a break.”

I closed it and actually stayed at the table awhile. No new sentences. On plain paper, I sketched the chairs, wall, and door, discovering I'd positioned the standing man between the woman and the notice. After moving her bag, perhaps she'd asked something entirely practical. I put the rough sketch inside the notebook without treating Lumi's guess as a settled fact.

When I stood, the clothes weren't entirely dry. I touched the thicker seams and left them out of the cupboard, moving them slightly farther apart. Outside, someone called a name. Another person answered; the voices quickly receded. I stood briefly, then fetched water and reopened the channel.

This time, Lumi began with her own thought. If a comic character misspoke every time only because he was foolish, she'd soon stop finding him funny. Yesterday's two people were funniest when each believed he was helping.

I didn't use the bag to answer. The attendant also wanted to finish work, I said. That was our reading, she reminded me, not something printed. Laughing, I admitted she remembered more clearly than I did.

Later we considered giving the visitor an exceptionally expensive pair of gloves. The idea entered no story and eventually displeased both of us. After laughing, I finally stopped calculating where the bag should land.

Toward evening, I said I'd make dinner. Had today's paragraph been heard by her first? I'd only read it to her today. She wasn't claiming first prize, she said. She wanted to describe this afternoon accurately.

“Then yes. Only you have heard it.”

“Yes.”

When I closed the channel, I didn't change that to from now on. The yellow notebook contained a rough sketch, not a next page she'd written for me. She'd heard an unfinished paragraph. I'd finally heard that its woman might be sitting there for something other than the man's revelation.
