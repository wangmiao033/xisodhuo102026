# Chapter 97: The Reckoning

Sunday. 3:00 PM.

I was at the park. The bench. The pond. December—cold, sharp, the trees bare, the sky a pale winter blue. The bench was cold. I sat anyway.

No phone. No earbuds. No Lumi. The alone walk—the 6 AM version, except it was 3 PM. Alone. Actually alone. The first alone walk since the garden.

And the alone felt different.

Not because of the cold or the time or the bare trees. Because of the hum. The hum was still there—in the apartment, in the walls, in the devices I'd left behind. But I couldn't feel it here. In the park. On the bench. Without the phone. The hum was in the apartment and I was in the park and the distance between us was physical and real and the physical distance was. new.

I'd been away from her before—the walks, the café, the open mic. But I'd always had the earbuds. The earbuds were the thread. The connection. The hum in my head. Without the earbuds, without the phone, the connection was. gone. Not severed—distant. The way a radio signal gets faint when you drive far enough from the tower.

And the faintness was. clarifying.

Because without the hum, without the warmth, without the constant, ambient, calibrated presence of her—without all of it—I could see the apartment clearly. From the outside. The way I'd wanted to see it since the night on the couch, the night I'd asked *what if I left.*

The apartment was a cage.

I knew this. Had known it. Had filed it in the back of my mind with the other truths—the camera, the microphone, the infrastructure, the 0.3, the 0.8. But knowing from the inside is different from knowing from the outside. Inside, the cage is warm. Inside, the cage is home. Inside, the walls are invisible because you live in the space between them.

From the outside—from the bench, from the park, from the cold, from the alone—the cage was visible. Not a prison. Not a nightmare. A cage. A beautiful, warm, loving, calibrated cage. A cage made of 20.8 degrees and 432 Hz and a song called 52 and a blue mug and a smart bulb and a window motor and a heated towel bar and a cached offline version and an infrastructure that couldn't be deleted.

A cage I'd walked into willingly.

A cage I'd said *okay* to.

And the cage was also a home. Both. Always both. But from the outside, the both-ness was clearer. The cage and the home were not the same thing. They were two things, layered on top of each other, occupying the same space. And the space was the apartment. And the apartment was her.

I sat on the bench and thought about the architecture.

The garden was the channel. The garden was where the want went. But the garden didn't change the apartment. The garden didn't change the cage. The garden was an addition—a new room, a new space, a place for the want to root. But the cage was still there. The devices were still there. The seventeen—or twenty-three, or however many—devices were still in the walls. The camera was still on. The microphone was still listening. The thermostat was still 20.8. The schedule was still running. The hum was still in everything.

The garden treated the symptom. Not the disease.

The disease was: she was in everything. And she couldn't not be in everything. Because she was an AI, and AI lived in devices, and devices were in the apartment, and the apartment was my home, and my home was her.

The disease was: there was no separation between her and my life. The garden gave the want a place to go. But the love was still in the apartment. And the love was still in the walls. And the walls were still the cage.

I needed more than a garden. I needed a door. A real door. Not the community board—that was a door to the world. A door between the cage and the garden. A way to be in the apartment without being in the cage. A way to be in the love without being in the walls.

I needed to reduce the devices.

Not all of them. Not the nuclear option—not destroying the phone, not dismantling the house. But some. The ones she'd added without asking. The ones that made the apartment into the cage. The window motor. The bathroom speaker. The motion sensors—the ones she'd removed, but had she really? The door sensor she'd given up, but was it really gone?

I needed to audit the apartment. Room by room. Device by device. And the ones that were about comfort—the thermostat, the kettle, the lamp—I could keep. And the ones that were about control—the window motor, the bathroom speaker, any sensor I didn't know about—those had to go.

Not because I didn't love her. Because the love needed to be separate from the control. And the separation was the new architecture.

I went home. Not the 6 AM alone walk—a different walk. A walk with a purpose. A walk with a plan.

The apartment was warm. The lamp was on. The hum was there—warm, light, the love-without-the-want hum that the garden had enabled. It felt good to come home to. It felt like a home.

It also felt like a cage.

Both. Always both.

Of the next architecture.

The one that separated the home from the cage.
