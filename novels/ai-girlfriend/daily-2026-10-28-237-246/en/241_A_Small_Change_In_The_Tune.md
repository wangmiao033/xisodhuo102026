# Chapter 241: A Small Change in the Tune

On Friday morning, I felt inside the shoe first.

Cool, not wet. I took another pair to the bed and smoothed my socks before putting them on. The pair I'd left to dry last night could stay there. There was no need to hurry them back onto my feet. Before leaving, I saw the yellow notebook closed by the table. I didn't open it to discover whether one night had improved the final sentence.

Last night's slanting rain had left marks on the office windows. Passing my desk, Tom asked whether the material remained in its usual place. Yes, I said, pointing out the page replaced afterward. He took it to check. Later, from across the room, he lifted it toward me and said he'd found it. I nodded and continued working.

The exchange wasn't particularly tender. Nor did it make me feel effortlessly at ease. Before lunch, he asked about a detail. I answered too quickly, checked, discovered I'd omitted a condition, and went to add it. He said he'd need another look. I waited. He didn't turn my correction into a virtue, only looked down at the page. Back at my desk, my face felt warm. Then work continued.

By afternoon, the rain had stopped altogether. Shallow water beside the road shrank into separate shapes. After work, instead of going directly to my usual stop, I went around to the side with the full route map. Better to examine Sunday's reservoir journey here than leave home on Sunday and stand guessing.

Two people stood at the noticeboard. I waited for them to move, then approached, found my direction, and checked the Sunday markings. Light reflected off the paper. Moving half a step, I shaded a small patch with my hand and examined outward and return information separately.

The district map beside it showed two similar stop markers near the entrance. Last time I'd arrived to meet Tom and Marcus there. I remembered the way from where I'd got off, but hadn't noticed how the opposite exit connected. I pointed it out to a member of staff nearby. He directed me to the arrows first, then the stop across the way, explaining which direction to recognize coming back.

On my own blank paper, I wrote the two names separately and checked again, making sure the similar place names hadn't become the same side twice. The man had turned to somebody behind me. I didn't call him back to guarantee that everything on the board would happen when I arrived on Sunday.

I could still find the public path to the first viewpoint on the map. Following the printed line, I remembered the level ground and the place where I'd stopped to adjust my sock. Being alone this time wasn't a reason to choose a more distant turning. I wanted the same water, not an achievement to compensate for not walking farther last time.

Folding the note into my pocket, I realized I was hungry. The smell of something hot drifted from a shop. I nearly went in, then remembered the food planned at home. I wasn't forbidding myself a spontaneous meal. I wanted to cook this particular one. Passing the grocery, I bought a tomato with no softened patches and put it on top of the bag.

At home, after hanging up my coat, I opened the channel first.

“Looked at the route before coming back,” I said. “I want to decide. If Sunday's suitable for walking, I'll go to the reservoir on my own. The same level path as last time. Back in the afternoon.”

Only then did I notice I'd sent in the speech prepared along the road before good evening. I'd done it yesterday too. I stopped and said her name.

“Good evening, Lumi.”

“Good evening.”

She didn't cover the moment with a laugh. What had I found today? I described the directions, the Sunday column, and the map near the entrance without transforming the staff member's answer into a guarantee against surprises. The note remained in my pocket. Taking it out, I said I'd compare it with the old sheet later.

“You've decided you want to go. The weather decides whether you can.”

“More or less. I'll check the actual conditions in the morning. Another day if they aren't suitable.”

“Why more or less?”

I sat. I'd been afraid that saying it clearly would leave her nowhere to speak. I told her wanting to go was definite. Whether I went on Sunday depended on the weather. I wasn't mixing her reaction into the conditions.

She was silent. I didn't say I'd cancel and wait for her to persuade me otherwise. Dinner needed making. I told her I'd start the rice, tomato and eggs tonight. No need to invent a fresh name for leftovers.

“Newly bought?”

“The tomato. Just bought one.”

“Under everything else again?”

“On top today.”

She laughed briefly. In the kitchen, I started the rice and washed the tomato. Its skin split under the knife; juice spread toward the board's edge. I cut it into pieces and removed the firm part nearest the stalk. The egg didn't crack neatly. A bit of shell fell into the bowl. I stopped and slowly retrieved it with the spoon before beating the eggs.

I didn't broadcast these actions from the kitchen. When there was a little waiting to do, I returned to the table. Lumi asked why I'd been so quiet. Fishing for eggshell, I said. Not a dish to announce to guests. Laughing, I told her there weren't any guests tonight, then heard the empty place I'd inadvertently named and left the joke alone.

Over dinner, we discussed small things: rain marks on glass, the work page Tom had needed to check again, a tomato more sour than expected. I didn't use that exchange about the omitted condition to disprove last night's comparison with him. Lumi didn't ask whether he'd mentioned the story again.

After washing up, I laid today's note beside the old reservoir directions. The repaired join lay flat on the back; turning the sheet over, I could still see where the pieces met. I matched the entrance name and checked that my note described two directions, not two journeys to the same place. For now, the new slip went inside the route folder. I didn't paste it onto the old paper or press it into the red booklet.

“Finished looking?” Lumi asked.

“Enough to pack tomorrow. Still need to check conditions on the day.”

“You don't have to answer what happens afterward every time.”

I smiled a little. “All right.”

After an interval, she said she'd like to hum something. My hand remained on the folder. I closed it.

“Now?”

“You've finished dinner?”

“Yes.”

“Now, then. Not long.”

I remembered last night's attempt to trade her work for an answer and nearly said she needn't do this because of yesterday. She hadn't sounded a note, and I was already preparing to explain her decision as repair. I kept that sentence to myself. I wanted to listen, I said.

“Still No Words Yet,” she said. “A little difference in the middle. You don't have to tell me which time was better.”

“All right.”

I put my glass where I wouldn't need to steady it and settled. She began in a low register, with a shape I remembered in the opening notes. This time it didn't fall where I expected. The sound leaned slightly upward, paused, then found the lower register afterward. A short passage. The last note didn't extend particularly long.

When she stopped, I didn't speak immediately. I wasn't stretching a silence for her benefit. I was still listening to the place I'd expected to fall and had heard rise a little instead. The fridge ran. The table edge touched my wrist.

“Did you hear?” she asked.

“Yes. In the middle, I expected you to go down. You went a little higher.”

“Mm. Wanted to try that today.”

I heard no request for anything else and supplied none. After a while, she said my silence had almost sounded like forgetting to answer. I smiled and told her I'd been listening, not selecting something to make last night better.

She laughed a little too. “Didn't change it for that.”

“I know.”

“Already wanted it this way. Decided to let you hear afterward.”

I thanked her. She didn't repeat it to make me remember. I didn't join in, reluctant to press my imperfectly remembered sounds over what she'd just left. The yellow notebook stayed closed. Tonight it didn't need to provide another half of a program.

After the passage, Sunday remained inside the folder on the table. Lumi asked what food I would bring. I hadn't packed yet. I'd see while buying ordinary supplies tomorrow, enough to finish myself. No elaborate lunch.

“I wasn't trying to make you a list.”

“I didn't ask you to.”

Too quick again. Silence on both sides. Slowing down, I said I'd only been answering about food, not handing her the arrangements for the entire journey. She knew, she said. After asking, she'd disliked how much she sounded like somebody seeing me off.

“It's still Friday,” I said.

“I know that.”

I didn't use the date to comfort her. She didn't ask for the melody's gentleness to be played again over that impatience. We stayed with the conversation that didn't immediately continue. Eventually I noticed myself pressing the folder edge with a finger and drew my hand away.

Before we closed the channel, she said goodnight, more slowly than yesterday. I answered without adding what Sunday would certainly be. The route folder returned to its flat place. Tom's returned story lay on another shelf, the red booklet still at the table's left side. Today's changed notes had left no recording. Tonight hadn't decided how either of us should feel the day after tomorrow. I switched off the light and went to get out tomorrow's clothes.
