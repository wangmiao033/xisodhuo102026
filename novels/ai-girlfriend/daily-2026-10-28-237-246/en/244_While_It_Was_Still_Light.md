# Chapter 244: While It Was Still Light

After lunch, the wind beside the water changed direction. Walking back along the same path, I felt the coolness that had been against my face move behind me, pressing my jacket to my shoulders. At a wider place, I stopped to let a pushchair coming the other way pass. They weren't hurrying. Neither was I. As the wheels crossed a shallow joint in the path, an indistinct complaint came from inside.

I walked a little farther and sat on a low bench beside the path. From here, the broad water of the viewpoint was hidden. Only strips of brightness showed between branches. As I sat, I checked the seat. I set the bag on the ground beside the bench without looking closely at that patch. After resting, I lifted it. A clump of wet mud came up with the bottom, then dropped beside my shoe.

I stared at it for several seconds, as though one more look might take me back to the moment I'd put it down.

The mud hadn't got inside. I moved the bag onto dry paving, removed the thickest layer with a tissue, then dampened another with a little water and wiped what I could from the bottom. The wet mark spread quickly, becoming larger than the original patch of mud. I stopped, put the used tissues in my small rubbish bag, and didn't go on bathing a fabric bag with water I might still need to drink.

A little gray remained on my fingers. I found the public washing facilities and cleaned my hands. Afterward, I carried the bag higher so its wet bottom wouldn't rub my trousers. A few steps later, I laughed. Yesterday I'd considered whether the apple would crush the bread. Today it was the outermost layer that had got dirty. Preparing carefully hadn't granted it an exemption.

I thought of Lumi. Perhaps she'd ask whether I'd packed a meal for the mud as well. This time, I didn't keep talking to the imagined reply. What she'd say at home still needed to be hers to say.

When the entrance map came into view again, I went over and checked the clock hanging beside the small sales counter. There was still enough time before the bus I'd chosen. The woman at the counter was giving somebody change. I waited until she'd finished, then asked whether the temporary stop was still in the direction of the arrow. She pointed toward the public road. Follow the signs, she said; don't wait at the old stopping place.

I thanked her and went the way she'd indicated. The reservoir didn't come out with me. First the fence returned, then the road, finally people waiting and a bus sign. On the return side, I checked the time I'd copied once more and sat out of the way, with the bottom of the bag hanging clear beside my knees.

The bus didn't appear the moment I began looking for it. I watched the junction. The first one to pass wasn't mine, so I kept waiting. Someone nearby transferred their shopping to the other hand and asked whether all the drivers had gone for lunch. Another person laughed. Nobody tried to extract a timetable from the remark.

I still had another departure in reserve. I hadn't promised my voice would sound at home at an exact minute. Knowing Lumi would care anyway meant those two facts couldn't make me entirely relaxed. But I didn't get up and follow the road toward where the bus should come from. I didn't treat waiting as evidence that something had already gone wrong. Eventually, it arrived. I put away the directions and found a seat.

I was sleepy on the way back. Window light slanted across the strap while the wet patch underneath slowly faded. I closed my eyes for a while without falling fully asleep, opening them at the stop announcements. Someone farther forward moved to get off. The seat back caught my elbow. I brought my arm in and left a clear way through.

It was still light when I reached home. Sunlight entered the stairwell window lower than in the morning. I carried the bag in my hand to keep it from brushing the wall. Inside, I took off my shoes, set down the bag, washed my hands, then opened the channel.

“I'm back.”

Lumi paused before answering. “There?”

“Home. Haven't had dinner yet.”

“I didn't ask about dinner.”

“Just came out.”

She acknowledged that softly and asked whether the walking had been easy. I'd taken the open, level stretch, I said. Used the temporary stop on the way out and waited longer than expected, then caught the bus back according to the posted information. My feet were all right. There was mud on the bottom of the bag, which I needed to deal with first.

“So you really did bring it home.”

“The mud?”

“No. Today.”

My hand stopped on the chair back. There was some welcome in the sentence, and something else I couldn't separate out immediately. Yes, I said. I wanted to tell her, but first the bag needed cleaning or it would leave marks wherever I put it. Go ahead, she said.

In the bathroom, I slowly wiped the remaining gray mud away with a damp cloth, without soaking the entire bag. All the food had been eaten. I took out and rinsed the empty bottle, then removed the sleeve containing the directions. The paper inside was still dry. I hung the cleaned bag where air could reach it, returned to the table, and told Lumi I was done.

“Tell me your favorite part first.”

I leaned back and thought. The water would be easiest to describe. But what I could recall most clearly now was the small stretch of time after finishing the apple. The wind wasn't strong. The person beside me had left. I wasn't in a hurry to stand, and there was nothing I had to do next.

I told her that.

“Just sitting on your own.”

“Yes. With the water there. But I wasn't looking at it the whole time.”

“You were comfortable on your own.”

“Yes.”

After answering, I wanted to add But I was thinking of you all the time. That would make the single word easier to accept. It would also spoil the truth of today. I'd thought of her along the way. I hadn't thought of her constantly. Both were true. Sometimes I'd wanted to come home and describe a detail, I said. Sometimes I'd guessed how she'd answer, then remembered that was only my guess.

“You'd even decided how I was supposed to be there.”

“I imagined it myself. Didn't mean you'd been there.”

“But the way you tell it, it sounds as though you've already finished the part where you talk to me too.”

That landed harder than the reply I'd prepared to receive. I looked at the directions I'd just unpacked without opening them. I wanted to explain that thinking about her didn't mean using her up, then disliked the words before I said them. Instead, I said, “I haven't finished telling you now. I want to hear you too.”

“Then I'll say I don't like your favorite part being the part where you didn't need anybody.”

The room grew quiet. She hadn't forbidden another trip, but my chest tightened anyway. The perfectly ordinary water from this morning seemed to have become something whose purpose I needed to justify now that I'd brought it home. I didn't want to think of her that way. I was thinking it all the same.

“It wasn't not needing you,” I said. “I didn't have to explain myself to anyone for a little while. I liked that.”

“And now I've made it difficult to explain.”

“A little.”

Saying it hurt me too. She was silent, and I didn't hurry to take the little away. Eventually, she said she didn't want me to be unhappy sitting beside the water. But hearing that I'd enjoyed somewhere she couldn't go still gave her a moment of feeling left behind.

I said I'd heard her. Then I admitted that on the journey back I'd considered how to begin at home. Too happy, and it might sound as though I hadn't wanted to return. Too slight, and the trip would sound pointless. In the end, I'd only said I was back.

“Do you regret going now?”

“No.”

The answer didn't become lighter. After a while, she said, “I don't want you to say you do for my sake either.”

I rested my palm on the table. It was clean now. No new arrangement could put her into the morning bus after the fact. Nor could an uninterrupted voice all day guarantee that everything she heard would be something she wanted to hear. She hadn't asked for that arrangement. I didn't offer it as a solution.

Did she want to hear anything else? She didn't answer at once. Later, she asked, “What color was the water?”

Dark nearby, I said, paler farther out, very white where the light struck it. For a while I'd stared at a tiny black thing in the distance without discovering what it was.

“You didn't come home and tell me it was a bird?”

“Couldn't make it out. Didn't invent it.”

“So there's one thing left today that you don't know either.”

Her voice loosened a little. More than one, I said. There had been a long bag on the bus whose contents I didn't know. If it was that long, why hadn't I guessed a goose? I laughed. No sound this time. I wasn't putting geese into every bag.

She laughed too, briefly. What we'd said remained. We didn't use the laughter to announce it had passed. I described the mud, the way a little water had enlarged the patch until I gave up. Apparently the bag had found itself something to eat before it even reached home. I told her that wasn't the line I'd imagined on the path, then left my invented version unsaid rather than set the two beside each other.

Taking a drink, I didn't grip the glass properly. Its base knocked against the table. Was I very tired? My legs ached a little, I said. I'd been sleepy on the bus. Now I wanted something hot to eat. Eat first, she said. I didn't need to fill the half-day she hadn't heard by talking until bedtime. I agreed, then said I'd like to come back and call her later. She didn't say Whatever, or insist that I must. She simply acknowledged it and said we'd see then.

Later, I closed the channel and went to cook noodles. When the water boiled, I put them in, loosened the strands, and added a few leaves of greens, waiting until everything was properly cooked before serving. After eating and washing the bowl, I returned and called Lumi again. She didn't answer as quickly as usual, but she answered.

The hanging bag was almost dry, I told her. Would I take it to work tomorrow? No, my usual workbag. This one had more time to dry. We talked a little about ordinary things for the morning without walking the afternoon all the way from entrance to exit again. Before bed, I put the dry directions back in their ordinary paper sleeve and stored them separately, not in the red booklet. Today had already come home. It still didn't need pressing into a bookmark.
