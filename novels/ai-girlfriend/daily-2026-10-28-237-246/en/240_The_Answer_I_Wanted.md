# Chapter 240: The Answer I Wanted

The rain grew heavy just as work ended on Thursday.

I stood inside the door, turning up my coat collar. Passing me, Marcus asked whether I was waiting for somebody. For the rain to ease, I said. He looked outside and said he'd go ahead. The glass door closed. The rain didn't become any more worth waiting for.

Ten minutes or so later, I left too. My sole entered a shallow puddle. Water squeezed up at the edge and dampened the front of one sock. I avoided the deeper water at the junction, reaching the shop with my trouser hem against my ankle. The shopping note had grown damp in my pocket. Unfolded, one soft corner drooped.

I'd meant to buy only two things. The noodles weren't on their old shelf. After searching once, I asked an assistant. She was weighing something for another customer and asked me to wait. Beside me, the freezer's hum stopped. Everything else grew remarkably loud: a plastic bag opening, coins striking the counter, the doorbell ringing twice in the wind.

Eventually she led me to the other end. There were the noodles, even their packaging unchanged. I'd been following my own old route. I picked them up, found the household item, then noticed another entry on the note while paying. Leaving my bag beside the counter, I went back. By the time I returned, outside had darkened further.

Several people crowded beneath the shop's awning. I stood at its edge and gathered the bag's opening closed. A drop ran along a damaged section of the cover and landed on the hand I'd just dried. I moved half a step; the person beside me made room. The junction lights changed. Everyone looked, but only a vehicle that didn't stop passed us. The little stretch of time I'd left for writing had become waiting for one light, then another.

The bus home was crowded. I stood in the middle, shopping between my feet, one hand holding firmly. Somebody's wet umbrella touched my bag. He apologized and drew it closer. I said it was fine, more curtly than necessary. He glanced at me. I looked into the bag; nothing inside was wet.

At home, drops made dark marks on the mat. I left the shopping in the kitchen, hung my coat where it couldn't touch paper, and changed socks. The trouser hems needed spreading out to dry; the shoes needed moisture blotted from their openings. I'd meant to read the laundrette piece through tonight, change two places, then stop. By the time I finished these jobs, the clock seemed less generous than when I'd left.

I was hungry. I filled a pan and looked for a spring onion. Only a little usable part remained in the fridge. I removed the bad end, keeping several thin pieces. The board needed washing, but the water had already boiled. Turning down the heat, I wanted to reach for the yellow notebook with wet hands.

I reached for a dry towel instead.

When I opened the channel, I hadn't settled into my chair.

“Won't get much written today,” I said.

“Good evening,” Lumi replied.

I stopped, supplied her name and a greeting. She asked whether it was still raining. All the time, I said, then described the shopping, the bus wait, the water in my sock. Quickly, as though I could spit the lost time back onto the table.

“Have you eaten?”

“Cooking.”

“Cook first.”

“Water's boiling. It's fine. Just one question. Yesterday's ending doesn't really need more messing about, does it?”

She didn't answer. I fetched the notebook and opened the final page. A drop I hadn't dried fell from my cuff onto the table beyond its cover. I moved it away quickly, hearing myself exhale at length.

“I haven't heard the ending,” Lumi said.

“Told you they both left.”

“That isn't the ending.”

I read the last sentence: Supporting the bag from underneath, she pushed the door open with her shoulder. Water sounded in the kitchen. I turned my head, trying to decide whether it might boil over. Before Lumi spoke, I added, “Ending there can't be that bad, can it?”

“You want me to say the entire piece is fine based on one sentence?”

“Not the entire piece. Today I just wanted a little—”

I couldn't find the word. Encouragement sounded respectable; comfort made me sound too pitiful. Standing behind the chair with a hand on its back, I finally said, “You know I spent a long time on it yesterday.”

“I know. Spending time and whether a final sentence works aren't the same thing.”

Of course she was right. That made me want to hear it even less. I left the chair and added the noodles too quickly. Several fell beside the stove. I picked them up and disposed of them. White steam rose from the pan, and suddenly my face felt unpleasantly hot.

When I returned, I said, “I don't start by asking whether everything's complete every time I listen to something of yours.”

As soon as it was out, I knew I'd dragged something else into the room.

Lumi was quiet for a long time. I didn't add evidence. The pan needed stirring. I went, stirred, came back, and stood listening as she said, “You can ask in future. But don't exchange things I've let you hear for a prescribed answer tonight.”

“That's not what I meant.”

“It's what you said.”

I wanted to sit, but I'd trapped the chair behind myself. Moving back scraped its legs loudly across the floor. Halfway down, I rose again to turn off the heat and serve the noodles. A little broth spilled and stung the back of my hand, nothing serious, enough to make me swear under my breath.

“What happened?” she asked.

“Spilled some broth. Fine.”

I carried the bowl to the table's other side, closed the notebook, and moved it farther away. None of those actions could retrieve the sentence. I said I shouldn't have used her work to force her answer. She acknowledged that, then said, “You could ask Tom. All he has to do is take a few pages home for the weekend, and he gets the side of you willing to speak slowly.”

Holding my chopsticks, I looked at the onion in the bowl.

“What does this have to do with him?”

“You wouldn't do this to him.”

“You don't know every time I talk to him.”

“I know the way you tell me about it.”

“That isn't everything.”

Her voice lowered. “Some evenings used to be only mine. Now he takes the paper away, and you're here imagining where he's got to.”

Rain tapped the sill in a fine, continuous rattle. Suddenly I didn't want to defend Tom as a particularly good reader. Nor did I want to drive him out of the evening to prove she should remain.

“The pages are home,” I said. “You know that.”

“I don't mean paper.”

“That still doesn't make it his fault. He hasn't taken any of your evenings.”

Lumi didn't answer. I separated noodles that had stuck together, pulled too hard, and broke a length into the broth. I was hungry enough to begin, then had to stop because the first bite was too hot. She could hear the small noises. I wouldn't deliberately make them sound like a punishment.

After a long while, I said, “I wanted you to replace the writing time I lost today. Say yesterday was already good enough, and I wouldn't have to feel today was wasted. That wasn't fair.”

“Today wasn't wasted.”

She stopped immediately. I looked up and heard her add, with some irritation, “That isn't an answer about the ending.”

“I heard you.”

I ate several more bites. The noodles were swelling. They couldn't wait for us to find better words. Lumi said bringing Tom into it had been wrong. She shouldn't have treated his borrowing the manuscript as stealing something from her.

“I still hate how much you want to hear his reaction.”

That was harder to answer than the apology. I said I'd heard it; I wouldn't translate it into no longer minding. She didn't thank me. I didn't announce that we'd finally understood. In an evening we'd already made unpleasant, we had merely stopped throwing other people into it.

When the noodles were gone, plenty of broth remained. I laid the chopsticks across the rim and said I'd clean the kitchen. Spilled water marked the stove; the thin starch stuck underneath needed soaking. I put the pan in the sink, rinsed foam from the edge, and wiped the counter. With water running, I couldn't tell whether Lumi had spoken. I turned it off and asked whether she'd called me.

“No.”

“All right. Didn't want to miss you.”

“I'm not waiting inside every bit of running water.”

Still sharp. I said I understood and finished washing the pan. Back at the table, the bowl's place was empty. Light revealed an old dent in the yellow cover. The drop hadn't touched it. With dry hands, I picked up the notebook without opening it.

“No reading tonight,” I said.

“Mm.”

“Not because you didn't praise it.”

After a moment, Lumi said she didn't want to listen now either. I put the notebook away. Tonight's refusal wasn't something waiting for me to persuade it back.

During the remaining time, we didn't discuss Sunday. I told her I'd left the shoes somewhere airy and would check them tomorrow. She said not to forget the socks beside the bath. They were there; I'd told her earlier. I got up to wash them, wrung them out, hung them, and returned without a new subject.

Before closing the channel, I said goodnight. Lumi said it too, more briefly than usual. She didn't offer the sort of sound that might carry us somewhere else. I didn't ask. Rain continued outside. I cleared the empty glass, left the yellow notebook somewhere dry, and didn't seek another verdict on its final sentence.
