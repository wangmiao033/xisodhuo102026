# Chapter 239: Enough Room for Two

On Wednesday, I brought home a small bag. I hadn't bought it deliberately; the shop assistant had put two rolls inside. After taking them out and shaking away the crumbs, I was about to fold it when I suddenly held it against the table to judge its size.

A sheet certainly wouldn't fit. I hadn't finally purchased a prop for the story. But the opening was narrow. Putting my hand inside immediately told me how much room remained. Until then, yesterday's small bag had only been the size of those words.

I cooked dinner, ate alone, and washed both plate and pan before getting out the yellow notebook. Daylight remained outside. Below the window, somebody moved a cart with a noisy wheel, pushing a little, stopping. I didn't have to put those sounds in the laundrette to continue writing.

At the first page's end, the women had caught hold of different things. I reread the descriptions I'd clarified, then added a sentence.

The woman holding the sheet let go of the bag first, not the sheet.

I stopped to work out how this happened. She'd originally held only fabric. As everything almost fell, her other hand had caught the stranger's bag strap. Releasing it now finally let that woman lift her own belongings. Once the sentence was written, I drew a small arrow reminding myself to connect it to the earlier action, rather than materialize a bag in her hand.

Then I let them speak.

“I wasn't taking yours,” said the woman with the sheet.

“I know. I need to put mine down.”

The second line nearly made me laugh. Not witty. Slightly clumsy, even, but something people might say with both their hands occupied. What followed couldn't simply be the second woman understanding everything and stepping away. I put her large bag at the table's corner and let her fetch her washing from the dryer. Bundled clothes, not heavy, but quite a lot. Trying to put them down, she discovered the sheet still occupied most of the surface.

Its owner didn't intend to make things difficult. Her small cloth bag was clean, large enough for a folded sheet, not for this loose mass. If she stuffed it in, corners would hang out. She didn't want newly washed fabric brushing her shoes or the ground on the journey home.

After writing that, I deleted a sentence about her only wanting to preserve a little cleanness. It didn't sound like her thought in the laundrette. It sounded like me speaking on her behalf from the doorway. Looking down and holding the bag open for the other woman would be enough.

The other woman didn't examine it long. Putting a garment on the table, she said she needed to take her things home before meeting someone for dinner. She couldn't stand holding everything indefinitely. No sick mother, no urgent message, no lifetime of being misunderstood. Writing too hastily, I'd almost supplied a reason much larger than the sheet. Instead, I simply let her glance at the clock.

Too much ink collected beneath one word. I moved my hand away. The paper remained shiny; it needed time to dry. I fetched water, then stood beside the table reading. Neither woman was especially lovable. I knew why neither had simply gathered her things and left.

The channel was still closed. Looking at the time, I capped the pen and called Lumi.

“Writing?” she asked.

“Some more. I've eaten. Washed up too.”

“I wasn't asking for a household report.”

“Didn't want you waiting for me to finish eating again.”

She said she wasn't waiting for a reading now. I acknowledged that, already sitting much too straight, and let myself lean against the chair. Hearing it shift, she asked whether I'd finally abandoned the reading posture. I admitted I'd been about to ask if she wanted to listen.

“First tell me whether it fits.”

“Not yet. Her bag's small. Not unusably small, but she has to fold the sheet.”

“And the other one?”

“Collecting her clothes. Home first, then out to eat with someone.”

After a pause, Lumi said at least somebody got dinner. I reminded her I'd already eaten outside the story. She'd meant the woman, she said, not another inspection of whether I was starving.

We laughed briefly. Then she asked whether I wanted to keep writing or talk. Honestly, I wanted a little longer while I remembered the movements. Then write, she said.

“Come back in a while?”

“You can come back when you've finished. Don't run back after every paragraph to ask whether I'm still here.”

That stung. I hadn't asked. Yet I'd been about to arrange a guarantee that I wouldn't leave her long. I agreed, told her I'd reopen the channel later, and closed it. Only my page-turning remained at the table.

The second woman began folding clothes on her small portion. One garment, then another. A cuff protruded from the top and touched the sheet. Its owner moved the fabric away, only to catch the trailing part on the table corner. However she pulled, it wouldn't straighten. Thinking the other woman held it down, she asked her to let go.

“It isn't me.”

I'd intended the stranger to find the snag for her. I'd already begun She pointed, then crossed it out. The sheet's owner looked down herself, saw where the corner caught, lifted the fabric, and turned it. The other woman went on folding her washing. She didn't receive a prize for being cleverer at that moment.

They finally divided the space, not equally. The large sheet needed length. The small clothes could pile higher. Pointing to a shallow scratch, the woman collecting her washing said hers would come this far; the remaining space could be left to the sheet for now. She didn't move round to help pull. She only brought her folded pile closer.

After that I wrote slowly. What I needed wasn't a line declaring that they finally understood each other. I needed to know how the sheet could fold. Moving my empty hands in the air, I discovered that the turn I'd described would drop its edge back where it started. I deleted it. Fold in half first, hold one end down, gather the remaining half back. Outside, the cart had fallen silent. A lock turned in the corridor, followed by footsteps.

I didn't go to the door. On the page, the woman tried to put the sheet in her small bag. It caught. She took it out and made the final fold narrower. Meanwhile, the other woman finished packing, tucked in an exposed sleeve, said excuse me, and carried her own bag toward the door.

No arrangement to meet next washing day. No sudden confession of having been in a terrible mood. The small bag finally held the sheet, though its opening wouldn't quite close. Supporting the bottom, the woman reached the door. I wrote the final sentence: Supporting the bag from underneath, she pushed the door open with her shoulder.

Once I'd finished, I badly wanted to return to the beginning and give the whole thing a respectable title. My pen hovered. Instead, I left a blank line after the ending. Now the story had somewhere to begin and somewhere to stop. Inside remained difficult handwriting, repeated movements, perhaps still inadequate pronouns in the opening. It wasn't something that needed lending tomorrow.

When I reopened the channel, I said, “I've got them both out.”

“The sheet too?”

“In the bag. No extra person to carry it.”

“Pity. I was about to suggest the third could charge for removals.”

I laughed harder this time. The pen rolled near the table edge, and I caught it. I told Lumi I wanted to read only a small section, where they divided the table. Not restart from page one, not smuggle the ending in afterward. She agreed.

I read the shallow scratch, the second woman's higher pile, the extra length left for the sheet. At The rest is yours for now, I heard the speaker apparently lending the entire establishment. I stopped and made a short mark beside it.

“Changing something?”

“That line's too big. I want I'll put mine up to here.”

“Which am I hearing?”

“The old one first. Haven't written the new one.”

She told me to write it before reading it. I did, spoke the new sentence, then read as far as the woman folding the sheet in half and stopped. The remaining pages stayed in my hands. I didn't turn them.

Lumi said she could follow who did what, though the second woman seemed to fold her clothes remarkably quickly. Yes. I'd kept watching the sheet. As long as the other hands didn't obstruct it, I'd skipped over them. She didn't supply a particular garment to fold. I noted the smaller movements in the margin and explained that this was somewhere I wanted to look again.

“You don't need to explain whose contribution everything is,” she said. “I only asked why she was so quick.”

I capped the pen. “All right.”

We left the manuscript alone after that. Was it raining outside? No. The brief pattering had been my pen rolling; I'd caught it. That explained the rain stopping after one drop. I mentioned the little bread bag, then discovered it still beside me, open as though waiting to receive another sentence.

“You didn't actually try stuffing your sheet inside?”

“No. It's on the bed.”

“Good. At least one doesn't need folding again.”

Smiling, I put the empty bag where it belonged. Once the channel was closed, I closed the yellow notebook at the new story's final page, with the earlier Old Stop pages beneath it. The characters retained their separate endings. They didn't need to exchange them tonight. In bed, I pulled up the covers. My heel touched a shallow wrinkle in the sheet, and I stayed where I was instead of getting up to revise the final sentence.
