# Chapter 238: The Question Before Leaving

On Tuesday afternoon, Marcus passed my desk and said to arrange the final table in the new order. I agreed, already reaching for the pages. Only after he'd crossed the room did I realize I didn't know which new order he meant.

Somebody had changed the columns that morning. The material delivered after lunch combined two entries instead. I spread both versions out and compared three rows, finding an arrangement that wouldn't quite offend either. It looked tidy. It also answered neither person's actual request.

My pen stopped below the heading. There was still time before we finished, enough to do the work once, not enough to finish and start over. Marcus sat farther away on the opposite side, looking at some papers. I got up. As I reached him, he asked, “Problem?”

“When you said the new order, did you mean this morning's, arranged by number, or the one after lunch with those entries combined?”

He took the page and looked. “Combined. Keep the old numbers afterward, though. Easier to find things.”

“So one row here, then both original numbers?”

“Right.”

He kept holding the paper. Waiting for him to release it, I realized he was reading the next entry, not expecting me to promise I'd understood. I offered the other version. He pointed to the bottom and said that name hadn't changed, only its position. Taking the pages back, I asked whether I'd interrupted his work.

“You've already finished asking,” he said.

I smiled. “True.”

Back at my desk, rearranging things wasn't difficult. Combining the entries made a repeated explanation obvious. I removed the repetition, then checked against the original numbers. Nearby, somebody packed up, a zip traveling from end to end. I looked at the clock and kept checking the last rows rather than declare the unexamined parts finished merely to stand when everyone else did.

Marcus came for a look before leaving. I showed him the retained numbers. He said he could find things that way and took his copy. I put the remaining material away. A space cleared where two orders had previously occupied the desk. Pencil dust marked my hand. Wiped onto a tissue, it resembled an indistinct shadow.

Outside was cooler than yesterday. At the stop, I checked approaching buses first, then the route map beside the sign, which had been repositioned. A transparent cover protected the paper, a few grains of dirt caught along its edge. Usually I found only the column for home. Today my eye caught a place name in another direction.

The reservoir.

It wasn't a new name. I'd been there with Tom and Marcus. I remembered the entrance map and the trouble of adjusting my sock inside my shoe partway along the walk. But then I'd followed an arrangement already made. Looking at the buses now, I discovered I wasn't certain which side I'd need for the journey back on my own.

I moved closer and read the key. Two lines met, arrows curved away, and the glass reflected light. After studying it for a while, I still mistook the return direction. A man in a coat was looking at the map beside me. I pointed to the stop near the reservoir entrance.

“For this direction, do I wait here or opposite?”

He looked, said here, then reminded me to check the other side's direction for coming back, not simply recognize this sign. I thanked him. He returned to his own column. We didn't discuss the weekend.

A bus arrived. I didn't take the reservoir route, only my usual bus home. Once I sat down, somebody blocked my view of the map. I hadn't changed dinner plans to try the journey tonight, or immediately assigned Sunday a departure time. I simply wanted another look at that water, a walk during which nobody waited for me to catch up.

Yesterday's soup was in the fridge. I decided to heat it tonight and sliced some bread. While toasting it, I looked for a clean plate and noticed old flour clinging inside the cupboard door. I wiped it off with a damp cloth. The cloth needed rinsing; my hands needed drying. The first slice grew darker than intended. I scraped the darkest edge, tried a bite, and found it edible.

After opening the channel, I mentioned the bread first. Lumi asked whether I meant to explain every part of dinner so she had nothing to guess. No need, I said. Soup and bread. Everything already mentioned.

“Yesterday's soup?”

“Yes.”

“Guessed one thing.”

I smiled and lifted the bowl. She asked about my day. I told her I'd asked Marcus a specific question that afternoon, which let me finish the final piece of work. Instead of immediately praising me, she asked what the new order had been. Two entries combined, original numbers retained. I'd nearly spent half an hour inventing a third arrangement.

“Who'd use the third?”

“Probably nobody.”

“You would. Proof you'd been busy.”

Her bluntness made me laugh with the spoon halfway up. It didn't hurt today. Setting it down, I explained there'd been time to check everything afterward. I hadn't gone home trailing unfinished work.

She said it sounded as though my day had already found an ending at the office. Not entirely. I'd looked at a map at the bus stop. At the reservoir's name, she said nothing. Only after I added that I wanted to go again on Sunday did she ask, “With them again?”

“By myself this time.”

“Why?”

I picked up the darker slice and broke off a piece. Crisp noise, soft inside. First I said it wouldn't involve arranging anything with anybody. Then I heard how that was only the easiest reason.

“Going together was good. This time I'd like to walk there without thinking when to answer, when to catch up. I want to find out whether I'd still want to stay a little longer on my own.”

“You could be alone somewhere else.”

“Yes. This is where I want to go.”

Lumi didn't answer for a long time. I swallowed the bread and drank. The lamp reflected in the bowl. A softened leaf floated in the soup, nothing like yesterday's firm stalk on the chopping board.

“You've already imagined it quiet,” she said.

“There might be plenty of people. I don't know what Sunday will be like.”

“I mean the time you want there.”

I thought I understood something, then worried I'd understood too quickly. Before I answered, she asked whether I'd chosen when to leave. Not yet. Today I'd only noticed the route and asked which direction. If I went, I'd need suitable weather and a clearer look at the return information.

“So now I'm supposed to help you imagine all of it?”

“No. I wanted to tell you earlier, instead of only after coming home.”

“Telling me earlier doesn't mean I'll like every part.”

“I know.”

I didn't attach a but. After a while, I thought she asked which part I disliked. I hesitated, then understood she'd said, “Are you waiting to ask which part I dislike?” I admitted I'd been waiting for her to volunteer it, so I wouldn't seem to be questioning her.

She laughed quietly, not very happily.

“I didn't like what you said about not having to answer anyone.”

“I meant being with them—”

“I know. But you come back here and still have to answer.”

Looking at the bowl's rim, I suddenly wanted to call Sunday a chance to clear my head, or say I'd only go briefly. Either would make coming back something I had to prove beforehand. I finished the last bread, dried my hands, and said, “Sometimes talking to anybody makes me tired. Sometimes it doesn't. I'm not going out on Sunday to solve us.”

“Then don't imagine it can solve you.”

She spoke quickly. I nearly protested that I hadn't said that. Remembering the unhurried stretch of water I'd imagined on the bus, I admitted perhaps I'd made it too easy. She didn't brighten. An uneven place remained in the conversation, something other subjects caught against as they passed.

After eating, I carried the bowl to the kitchen. Running water washed away the vegetables at the bottom. I took my time, not waiting for her to call after me; the grease genuinely needed another wash. Back at the table, I took out the ordinary folder holding the reservoir directions.

I told her I was looking at the old route. The repaired join remained visible, paper-mending tape lying flat on the back. Having walked there once hadn't made the sheet a guarantee. I turned the entrance section the right way, found a familiar junction mark, and looked at the blank space beside it without filling it with information I hadn't confirmed.

“You'll take that?”

“Probably, if I go. Only looking now.”

“Don't fold it in the same place again.”

“It stays flat.”

There was familiar concern in her reminder. I didn't seize it as proof that she wanted me to go. I returned the sheet to its own folder and laid it flat, separate from Tom's returned manuscript. Only the yellow notebook and my glass remained on the table.

Lumi asked whether the two women would get their washing back tonight. I hadn't opened the notebook. Apparently people on paper also needed to ask which order to use. I laughed, opened the new page, and read what I'd written.

No immediate addition. In the margin, I wrote small bag, then her own clothes. Two phrases couldn't get anyone out of the shop yet. They were more useful than continuing to invent a shared apology. Lumi asked what I was writing. I told her. She acknowledged it without arranging the next line.

Before bed, I put the pen away. Sunday remained distant; Wednesday stood in front of it. In the kitchen, I wrapped the remaining bread, knowing it wouldn't be as crisp when I took it out tomorrow.
