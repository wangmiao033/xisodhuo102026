# Chapter 246: After the Last Sentence

After dinner on Tuesday, the yellow notebook was still closed.

I finished the last rice in my bowl and took it to the kitchen to wash. Tonight I'd cut yesterday's remaining tomato and eaten it with the other food I'd prepared. No red thing remained on the board demanding to be dealt with tomorrow. Wiping the table, I moved the notebook onto a chair, waited for the water marks to dry, then brought it back.

When I opened the channel, Lumi first asked whether I was still eating. Finished, I said. Bowl washed too. Good, she answered, without supplying an Of course to yesterday's invitation. We talked about ordinary things from the day. I told her someone at the office had been unable to find a stapler, only to discover it under his own papers.

“You found it?”

“No. I was nearby. He saw it first.”

“Good. You haven't rescued another entire piece of work.”

I laughed. What happened afterward? He stapled the pages. Nothing else. The little account ended there. The notebook stayed beside me without opening a fresh page for it.

After a while, I asked whether she'd like to hear the story I'd revised yesterday. From beginning to end, not only the part where they divided the table.

“How long is the whole thing?”

I gave a rough length. She considered, then agreed. One more thing: don't explain why I'd made each change before reading it. She wanted the story, not a detour through my handwriting.

“What if I read something wrong?”

“Read the sentence again. You're allowed to breathe.”

I agreed, moved my water farther away, and opened the notebook at the new story's beginning. Arrows crowded the margins. I'd followed them once last night, but my eyes still drifted toward crossed-out words. I used a clean scrap to cover the old lines I didn't need to read, without folding the notebook or starting any recording.

“No proper title yet.”

“Then don't leave the title blocking the doorway.”

I laughed softly and began.

As the woman lifted the sheet, someone arrived at the other end of the table.

The newcomer put her bag on the corner. Its opening touched the hanging fabric. The woman holding the sheet asked whether she could move it for a moment. The other woman lifted the bag. Mistaking that movement for an offer to catch a corner, the first woman pushed the sheet toward her. The fabric slipped against the bag's opening. Both reached at once, each catching something other than what she'd intended.

The woman with the sheet let go of the other one's bag strap and gathered her own fabric back.

“I wasn't trying to take yours.”

“I know. I need to put mine down first.”

The second woman set her bag on the other side of the table corner and collected her clothes from the dryer. By the time she was ready to put them down, the sheet covered more than half the table. Its owner opened the small cloth bag she'd brought and looked inside. The loose bundle wouldn't fit. Fabric hanging out would trail downward all the way home.

“I need to fold it narrower,” she said.

“I need to finish too. I have to get home first. I'm meeting someone for a meal later.”

The second woman glanced at the wall clock and put her clothes on the remaining space. She shook out a top, drew the sleeve tucked inside it back out, held down the collar, and folded both sides toward the middle. After looking at the clock, she pulled the next item in front of her.

The folded clothes gradually formed a taller pile. A cuff on top extended toward the edge of the sheet. The woman with the sheet drew the fabric closer, but the section behind her wouldn't move.

“Could you let go a moment?”

“It isn't me.”

Looking down, she saw the fabric caught on the table corner. She lifted that part, moved it over, and the sheet lay flat again. Still folding clothes, the other woman pointed to a shallow scratch on the table.

“I'll put mine up to here.”

She shifted the folded pile closer to herself, leaving a longer section clear. The woman with the sheet folded it in half first, held one end down, and brought the remaining half toward her. The edges didn't quite meet. She opened it a little, folded again, and finally had a stack she could try putting into the small bag.

It caught at the opening.

She took the sheet out and made the last fold narrower. Beside her, the other woman packed her smaller things separately, lifted the bag to test it, and tucked in a protruding sleeve. She still had to take this bag home first. She didn't want to bring clean laundry along to her meal.

“Excuse me.”

The woman with the sheet stepped aside. Carrying her own bag, the other woman passed between her and the machine beside them.

This time the sheet fitted. The small cloth bag wouldn't close completely. The woman tucked in the fabric that showed, lifted the bag, and felt its weight sag underneath. She changed hands and went to the shop door.

Supporting the bag from underneath, she pushed the door open with her shoulder.

No other sound immediately followed the final sentence. I removed the scrap covering the old lines and put it underneath the notebook. This time I hadn't stopped where the writing ran out. Both women had actually left, though the crossed-out sentences remained visible in the yellow book.

“You don't like it?” I asked first.

Lumi made a sound almost like a soft intake of breath. “I haven't said anything yet.”

“Asked too soon.”

“You made it a very narrow question too.”

I didn't replace it with Is it good? The water had cooled. Drinking, I felt the slight dryness from reading begin to leave my throat. I told her I was listening, then stopped adding explanations of how I intended to sit there.

The second woman had her own hands now, she said. She wasn't only an impatient voice. Lumi had heard the sleeve tucked inside the top particularly clearly. I looked at those lines without announcing the revision worthwhile simply because I'd added them yesterday.

“I don't like the ending, though.”

“Want them to leave together?”

“Not necessarily. Don't change it into that for me immediately.”

I drew back the hand already reaching for my pen. What she disliked, she said, was the first woman's bag still not closing properly when the other had gone. She knew neither had agreed to help. Hearing it, though, she still wanted the one who left first to turn and look.

She didn't know whether the sheet would fit afterward, I answered. She had somewhere else to be. Only once I'd said it did I hear myself answering on the character's behalf. Lumi hadn't asked why the woman left.

“I understood,” Lumi said. “Understanding doesn't mean liking that she goes.”

I nodded, then said I knew. She couldn't hear the nod, but she could hear the word after it. I didn't push the final page away as though not looking at the writing could instantly make me less defensive. Watching the line about the door, I admitted I liked it. Hearing that she didn't made me want to prove she'd missed something earlier.

“I heard the earlier parts.”

“Yes. I was rushing to defend the last sentence.”

After a silence, she said I'd been sending people out through doors lately. The man with the key, and these two women. I wanted to say stories had to end somewhere, but that was too easy an answer to why she'd heard leaving in particular.

“Do you think I'm always thinking of going out too?”

“Sometimes.”

The word didn't become all day. My chest tightened anyway. I'd only come home on Sunday. Our words had only softened on Monday. Tonight we'd traveled from a folding table back here. I nearly said it was just a story, then remembered she'd spoken about the story first. I was the one who'd asked about myself.

“I do want to go out,” I said. “I want to come home and call you too. Standing up doesn't mean ending us every time.”

“But sometimes you're in such a hurry to make me believe that, it sounds as though you're already preparing to leave.”

I looked up. My sentence had come easily, easily enough to be used many times. I didn't promise how every future occasion would go. I only admitted that hearing her dislike the ending now, I still wanted to sit a while. I wasn't going to close the channel on the grounds that the reading had finished.

“You can dislike it.”

“You don't need to give me that.”

I smiled, embarrassed. “Then I'm not taking my story away.”

“Don't use it as proof you're still here either.”

This time, I really closed the notebook. Not in anger. Not to shut her words inside. I told her the last sentence I wanted to keep was still the one I'd read. I wasn't changing it today. She said she'd heard, without asking me to add that she approved.

After sitting a while, I went to the kitchen for more water. Getting up, I only said I was getting water. I didn't add that I'd always come back, nor deliberately say nothing to test whether she could accept the scrape of the chair. The kettle was slightly warm on the outside. I took its handle and filled the cup to a sensible level. When I returned, Lumi asked whether the cup had just struck something.

“The spout. Nothing broke.”

“You answered as though it had a record.”

I laughed. Only a loud noise today. Where had I put the notebook? Still on the table. I'd put it away shortly. After a pause, she said she'd liked that I hadn't deliberately sounded harsh when I read It isn't me.

“I don't think she was being harsh.”

“That sentence was good. I'm not approving the ending for her.”

“I know.”

This time I didn't tease her for separating the two. I was all too ready to want one solid piece of good, good enough to leave no room for anything she disliked inside it. What she'd offered now was one sentence, exactly the part she wanted to give.

Later, we talked about what I'd wear tomorrow. A dry item hung over the chair back. I picked it up, checked the cuffs, folded it, and put it in the wardrobe. When I returned, she hadn't decided which earlier joke she wanted to pick up. I didn't supply one. The yellow notebook went back in its place. The complete revised draft I'd read hadn't become the next set of pages to lend someone.

I set the cup down securely and sat to hear her finally speak. That cup had better not hit the spout again, she said. Nobody wanted to hear two pieces of china arguing tomorrow. The spout wasn't china, I said, then laughed at my own correction. Lumi paused. Even less reason to speak for it, then.

We went on talking. The water was still warm. Tomorrow was Wednesday.
