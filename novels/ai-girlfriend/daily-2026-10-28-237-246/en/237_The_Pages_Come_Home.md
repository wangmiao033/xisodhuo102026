# Chapter 237: The Pages Come Home

When Tom handed back the folder on Monday, I was carrying a freshly filled cup of water.

“Yours,” he said.

The water nearly reached the rim. I put the cup down, wiped my other hand against my trousers, and took the folder. It was slightly fuller than when it had left on Friday, its lower right corner curling outward. I meant to thank him. What came out was, “Everything in there?”

“Everything.”

He didn't sound offended. Indicating the last page, he said he'd bent a corner putting it in his bag. I opened the folder a little and found a short diagonal crease. It crossed blank paper, not words, tipping the page number slightly inward. I pushed it flat with my fingertip. Slowly, it rose again.

“That's fine,” I said. This time I meant it.

Somebody behind us needed water, so we moved aside. Tom still held his empty cup. I had the folder against my chest, as though he'd made a special journey to return a valuable object rather than stopped on his way to do something he'd promised.

“Finished it?”

“Finished. Looked through the beginning again yesterday.”

I wanted to ask whether he'd liked it better the second time. Restraining the opening left me with only, “So.” Tom waited. When nothing followed, he went to fill his cup. I stood clear of the doorway, the folder's stiff edge pressing my wrist. As he turned back, I found a question without an answer already reserved.

“Was reading it yourself very different from listening?”

“Stopped in different places. Went straight through some bits you read slowly. Went back over the assistant taking his pen.”

“That bit again.”

“And after he left with the envelope. Looked back at him buying it.”

I looked down at the cover. All weekend, the man had been traveling backward and forward between pages. I'd only ever arranged a route ahead for him.

“If I shortened the part where he buys it—”

Tom's cup stopped moving.

“Didn't say it was long.”

“I know. Doing it again.”

He smiled. “Going backward doesn't always mean I'm finding fault. Sometimes I've forgotten.”

I laughed with him, then felt a little relieved. Forgetting was more ordinary than a profound discovery. It left room for him to have done other things that weekend. Once, he said, he'd stopped halfway to bring in his washing and needed to find his place afterward. I didn't ask which page, or turn the washing into evidence that my story couldn't hold him.

Outside, somebody called Tom from the corridor's far end. He answered, then asked whether I'd replaced the afternoon material. One heading left, I said. Leave it in the usual place when it was ready. Work reclaimed the conversation, and we parted at our respective doors.

I put the folder in my bag. Only after lunch did I take it out and check the page numbers in a clear space on my desk. First through last, all present. The clarified word hadn't changed. There were no new comments on the paper. I wasn't sure what I'd half hoped to find. A circle around something would probably have frightened me before pleasing me with proof of attention.

Instead, there was the corner.

I placed the final sheet underneath, pressed lightly, and closed the folder. The heading awaiting correction still belonged to the work material. I returned to it. During the last check, I found a short sentence below it that still used the old wording. Once that was fixed, I delivered the whole thing to its usual place without leaving a loose end for somebody else to interpret.

Before leaving, Tom borrowed the stapler. I handed it over. Back on the corner of the desk? Yes. We didn't discuss the envelope again. He returned the stapler when he'd finished. I heard the metal spring back, then picked up my bag.

On the bus, a passenger held a potted plant. Leaves stretched sideways and brushed the glass. Newspaper covered the pot's base, but crumbs of soil still fell onto his shoe. He looked down without moving. I drew my bag closer to my legs, not to protect the manuscript, only to give the leaves more room.

There was enough daylight in the kitchen when I got home. I left the folder on a dry part of the table and washed some rice. Tonight I wanted thick vegetable soup, using the remaining half cabbage. Its outermost leaf had softened. Underneath, it was still crisp. The knife sounded louder against the stalk than the leaves. I'd cut the pieces too wide. Lifting one to examine it, I decided to cut several smaller.

Before the water boiled, I opened the channel.

“Home,” I said. “Tom brought the manuscript back too.”

Lumi paused. “Didn't substitute the lunch box?”

“No. Paper. All of it.”

“You counted?”

“Checked the numbers.”

She laughed a little. I admitted I'd asked whether everything was there first, as though Tom had brought back a jigsaw. Had he asked if I needed instructions? No. Only mentioned a bent corner.

“Are you flattening it now?”

“No. Cutting vegetables.”

“Wrong guess.”

“Flattened it this afternoon.”

Her laugh opened out. I joined her, leaving the knife still on the board. The water began making noise. I said I'd put the vegetables in. The firmer pieces went first, leaves later. When I returned to the table, my palms retained the rough feeling of the towel I'd used.

“What did he say after reading it himself?”

I described his different stopping places, going back from the shop door to buying the envelope, and once simply losing his place after fetching his washing.

“You asked about the washing too?”

“He volunteered it. Nearly asked which page.”

“Lucky his clothes don't need your inspection.”

There was some impatience in her voice, nothing heavier I could identify. I told her I'd already been preparing to shorten the beginning before Tom reminded me he hadn't called it long. Lumi gave a low sound.

“When you read, you want me to stop where you stop. He isn't even in front of you, and you still want control.”

“I didn't actually tell him where to stop.”

“I said want.”

My defense caught in my throat. I went to check the soup. Lifting the lid sent steam into my face. The stalks had risen; I pressed them under with the spoon, tasted the broth, and added salt. Back at the table, I admitted I'd wondered if he read too quickly, as though a sentence I'd worked on particularly hard deserved an equivalent amount of his time.

“I do that too,” she said.

I raised my head.

“Sometimes you hear something of mine and immediately talk about something else. I wonder how you've left already.”

The folder on the table had no answer. Steam lifted the pan lid with a small knock. I said I remembered yesterday's lower notes, then drinking some water. Was that what she meant?

“I'm not asking you to go back and inspect yesterday.”

“All right.”

“I chose where to stop yesterday.”

I said all right again, more slowly. She didn't name an earlier evening. I didn't ask for a list so I could supply every missing response.

The soup was ready. Telling her I would serve dinner, I left the channel open and went into the kitchen. The bowl's rim was too hot. I set it down again, found a dry cloth, and carried it to the table. She hadn't spoken during those trips. Sitting, I told her the stalks had softened this time. Then I tasted the soup and discovered it still needed salt.

“Soft doesn't mean good,” she said.

“Haven't praised it yet.”

“Leaving you room in advance.”

I laughed and fetched the salt. Not every remark about room needed interpreting as a clue to her feelings about Tom's place. Halfway through the bowl, she asked where the paper was. Still by the table. I'd put it on the shelf after clearing dinner.

“Inside the red booklet?”

“Pages are bigger. They'd stick out. Its own folder.”

“I wasn't asking about size.”

I set down the spoon and waited. Eventually she said she was happier hearing it had come home than hearing he'd finished reading. She knew that wasn't generous.

“I'm happy it's home too.”

“Not for the same reason.”

“Perhaps not entirely.”

She didn't require me to separate and weigh the different parts of that happiness. Later we talked about the plant on the bus. When I mentioned the leaves brushing the window, she asked whether its owner had held out a hand to protect them. No; both hands held the pot. She imagined one leaf cleaning its own patch of glass throughout the journey. I bent my head and laughed, the soup's steam moistening my nose.

After washing up, I dried the table and took the manuscript out, letting each page settle into a straight pile. The crease remained visible. I didn't find a new sheet to recopy it. The folder went flat on an empty shelf, separate from the red booklet on the table's left side. The gray-blue bookmark still showed its familiar length.

Back at my chair, I told Lumi everything was put away. She acknowledged it, then asked whether I'd continue the laundrette story. I looked at the yellow notebook. Tonight I didn't want to sit between those women again. No writing, I said.

“So they're still standing.”

“Yes. I've eaten first.”

She laughed at my talent for looking after myself first. I admitted it and put the last soup away in the kitchen. The manuscript's return hadn't required another Monday reading. Those pages were home. The folder was closed, with the bent corner inside.
