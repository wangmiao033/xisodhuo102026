# Chapter 243: The Seat Beside Me

When I drew the curtains on Sunday, the cloud outside was thinner than I'd imagined. The wall of the opposite building wasn't wet. Leaves moved below, but the wind didn't look strong. I stood at the window for a while and decided to go as planned.

Breakfast was ordinary. Crumbs remained on the empty plate. I filled the water bottle, tightened the cap, and turned it upside down to check before putting it in the side of my bag. Before leaving, I opened the channel and told Lumi the weather looked suitable. I was going today.

“Take care on the way,” she said.

“I will. Back this afternoon.”

I didn't repeat the stretch of time we'd already discussed. Had I remembered water? I patted the side of the bag, remembered she couldn't know what the gesture meant from its sound, and answered aloud. Yes, just filled it. She said the apple had better not travel with me all the way to evening.

“You've already chosen its mealtime.”

“I just don't want to hear tomorrow that it's been squashed again.”

I laughed, agreed, then said goodbye and closed the channel. When the door shut, I didn't keep hearing her final sound out in the corridor. Her voice at home had stopped. Only my footsteps remained on the stairs.

The bus stop was quieter than on a working day. A child crouched beside the bench, examining grass in a crack, and stood only when an adult called. I checked the route on the sign, then the Sunday column, and found that the gap between departures was longer than I'd imagined at home. It wasn't time for the next one yet. No bus had gone missing.

A fresh notice beside it told reservoir passengers to get off one stop before the old one for the time being, then follow signs for the public entrance. Its lower corner had curled up. I shaded the glare with my hand, checked which route it applied to and that the change was still in effect, then took out my copy.

Beside the old stop name, I drew a short line and wrote the temporary stop. Not having the repaired original with me made it easier to put pencil to paper. But a new sheet wasn't automatically a trustworthy one. I compared the name with the sign again.

A man carrying a shopping bag approached and asked whether I was waiting for that route too. Yes. He pointed to the timetable. There was still a wait until the next one. I'd seen that, I said. He sat down without offering a secret shortcut that would save me waiting. I considered whether changing buses might work, checked the board again, and found no more direct option. I sat at the other end of the bench.

With the bag on my knees, I finally stopped peering down the road every few moments. The child had left with the adult. The grass remained between the paving stones. Setting off a little later than intended hadn't made me miss the whole morning. I took out the bottle, drank, and screwed the cap tight again.

When the bus arrived, the man boarded first. I followed, paid, and found a window seat. The place beside me was empty. I kept my bag on my knees rather than appoint it a companion that needed a ticket. Two stops later, a woman carrying a long bag sat down. It wouldn't fit easily. She angled it between her feet and asked whether it had caught me.

“No,” I said, moving my toe a little farther aside.

She nodded and looked out. I didn't know what was in the bag. Soon afterward she got off, exposing a patch of seat fabric warmed by sunlight. I found myself wanting to tell Lumi about the arrival and departure, then realized there was nothing that had to be said immediately. Somebody had sat down, then left. She hadn't borrowed anything of mine or made today a place somebody else could lose a contest over.

The buildings beside the road grew lower. Part of my jacket was reflected in the glass, laying a faint cuff over the view whenever I looked out. I remembered making my own way to the entrance last time, then meeting Tom and Marcus to walk the path together. Some directions had stayed with me, along with the pace of trying to keep up with their conversation. Today nobody stood first to confirm the stop for me. I listened to the announcements and got off where the new notice had said.

An arrow at the junction did point toward the public entrance. First, I crossed to check the return-side bus information. The temporary stop served the journey home too. I copied down two suitable afternoon departures, neither the last service, allowing room for waiting and the walk back. My new words slanted a little. I supported the paper on its sleeve and finished without copying it again for neatness.

The approach to the entrance took a longer way round than the stretch on the old directions, but signs continued to mark it. Several patches of new pavement were paler than the rest. I followed the open footway rather than squeezing through a gap beside the barriers. Beyond a row of low trees, the entrance map finally appeared. I recognized it, but read the notices beside it again. The level public path to the first viewpoint was open today. A branch on the other side was temporarily closed, with a clear barrier across it.

I started along the line I'd walked before.

What I saw first wasn't a wide expanse of water. It was a small patch of tree shadow on the path. Wind moved the leaves, and their separate edges broke apart on the ground. I didn't stop to find a meaning in it, though my pace slowed on its own. An elderly couple walked side by side ahead, taking up most of the path. I waited for a wider place, said excuse me, and passed on the other side. They didn't have to stop their conversation.

Farther on, the pieces of water between the trees joined together. I'd seen it last time too. It hadn't acquired a color nobody had ever seen simply because I'd returned alone. Dark nearby, pale in the distance, almost white where it caught the light. I put my hand on the railing and lifted it again at once. The metal was colder than the wind.

Lumi would probably say, You brought all that water because you thought there wouldn't be enough here. I supplied the line for her in my head and laughed. Then I thought it didn't sound quite like her. Hearing my actual description, she might ask which part was brightest. She might still be unhappy about something else. I couldn't have a voice I'd invented answer me all along the path, then claim she'd been here too.

Somebody ahead glanced in my direction. I stopped smiling and walked on. I didn't need to explain whom I'd just been thinking of.

My shoe felt slightly cramped at first. When I reached a bench, I sat, loosened the laces, and smoothed the sock over my toes before tying them again. My left heel wasn't hot as it had been last time. Still, I stood and walked a few steps, checking that things felt better before continuing. A second visit didn't need to take me farther in compensation for the places I hadn't reached on the first.

The wind strengthened at the viewpoint. Someone held on to a hat and turned aside to let other people through. I waited until they'd moved, then stood in the free space, where I could see the shoreline bending away. A distant black speck kept moving across the water. I couldn't tell what it was and didn't give it a name. Close by, below the rail, a leaf flipped over in the wind, then flattened against the stone again.

I stood there quite a while. When nobody nearby was speaking, the sounds of the water weren't as orderly as I'd imagined at home. A small wave struck something hard at the edge with a brief noise. Before another patch could answer, a child was calling from farther away. This wasn't a place arranged to switch off human voices. I was glad I'd come anyway.

Leaving the rail, I found a stretch of bench where eating lunch wouldn't obstruct the path. The rolls were intact. The apple hadn't suffered either. My first mouthful of bread was dry enough to need water before I could swallow. I decided not to describe lunch afterward as though it had been invented especially for the waterside. But the second bite tasted normal, and I was hungry enough to finish it all.

The person at the other end put an empty wrapper into their own bag and left. I tucked the paper sleeve under my bag so the wind couldn't take it and ate the apple slowly. A little skin caught between my teeth. I spent a while looking down and dealing with it. When I raised my head, the brightest patch on the water had moved.

That might give Lumi more of today than the joke I'd composed for her earlier. But I didn't hurry to arrange the account in my head. I wasn't even certain which part would occur to me first at home: this, or the curled corner of the temporary bus notice.

After eating, I packed the core and wrappers away, wiped my hands, and rested. A passing man raised his wrist to check his watch. I called to him and asked the time. He stopped and told me it was just after one. I thanked him. He continued toward the entrance without asking why I couldn't check for myself. I looked again at the two departures I'd copied. Allowing for a slow walk back to the entrance and the temporary stop, the earlier one still gave me enough time. The paper corner softened between my fingers. I put the sheet back in its sleeve without adding our exchange to the blank space. There was enough afternoon left. I didn't need to leave immediately, nor walk every branch of the route simply because I'd come this far. I only meant to spend a little longer on this open, level stretch before heading toward the entrance in time for the bus I'd noted.

When I stood, the whole bench beside me was empty. I put the bag back on and didn't say Let's go to that vacant place. The water was ahead. Wind pressed my jacket against my leg. I pulled it away a little and continued along the open path beside the way I'd come.
