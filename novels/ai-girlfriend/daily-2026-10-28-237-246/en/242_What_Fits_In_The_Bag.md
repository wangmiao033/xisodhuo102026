# Chapter 242: What Fits in the Bag

On Saturday morning, I stood in the grocery shop comparing two apples for far too long. One looked better. The other was smaller and wouldn't make such a bulge in tomorrow's bag. I chose the smaller one, then another for today. After weighing them, the assistant put both beside the scales. Only then did I realize she had no reason to know which one I'd assigned to which day.

Several kinds of plain rolls remained on the bread shelf. I chose some without filling, enough for one meal, and didn't go looking for the cream that had once kept me standing outside a shop window. Tomorrow I was going to the reservoir, not finding a more distant place to eat lunch. At the vegetable display, I picked out a handful of mushrooms and a small piece of cabbage to cook together that evening.

The shopping bag brushed my trouser leg on the way back. An apple had worked its way to the bottom. I stopped on the stairs and moved the harder things away from it. A door opened above me. I stepped aside for a man carrying rubbish; we said good morning to each other. By the time I went on up, I'd forgotten the order I'd assigned the two apples in the shop.

I put away the food and ate lunch before opening the channel. Lumi asked whether I'd been out. I told her I'd bought tomorrow's bread and fruit, along with vegetables for tonight.

“Bag packed already?”

“Not yet. Directions first.”

“So you just bought the map its lunch.”

I laughed and hung up the shopping bag. “Mine. The map doesn't have an apple.”

The repaired directions lay flat in their own folder. Taking the sheet out, I supported both sides. The paper-mending tape on the back hadn't lifted; the join was still visible on the front. I didn't test how many more folds it could withstand. The entrance I'd used before and the first viewpoint were both marked, but familiarity wouldn't tell the paper whether there would be work on the route tomorrow.

I found a clean sheet and copied the stop names I needed, the entrance, and the way back. The original lay beside it, not on top of the red booklet. That little book and the handwritten copy Tom had returned were each in their usual places. Nobody's story needed to give me directions today.

I stopped at a turn before the entrance. The line looked short on paper, but I remembered a stretch of fence when I'd walked that part. I nearly added on the left from memory, then wrote only what the original actually said. If I copied my recollection as fresh instructions, by tomorrow I might have forgotten which part wasn't reliable.

“Gone quiet?” Lumi asked.

“Looking at a turn. Can't remember which side the fence was on.”

“You can look when you get there.”

“Yes. I'm trying to give a piece of paper more confidence.”

She laughed. The paper hadn't been in a hurry to begin with. I left out the uncertain detail, checked what I'd copied, and put the original back in its folder, stored flat. Only the new sheet was folded small enough for a paper sleeve. I added a separate short note about the direction of travel I'd confirmed on Tuesday, reminding myself to check the day's departures and posted notices when I reached the stop.

Lumi asked roughly when I'd be home tomorrow.

“Leave in the morning. Walk that level stretch we talked about, have lunch there, start back in the afternoon. I'd like to be home before evening.”

She didn't answer immediately. I could hear the loose edges of my own sentence and wanted to add an hour, preferably something I could point to on a clock face. But I didn't know how long I'd wait for the return bus. If something changed, there wouldn't be an open channel outside through which to amend the plan.

“I can't promise an exact minute,” I said. “I'm not planning on the last bus. I'll check the return information when I arrive.”

“I didn't ask for a minute.”

“I know. Nearly gave you one anyway.”

She only wanted to know whether I meant before dinner or sometime at night. I'd intended a daylight walk and an afternoon return, I said. I wouldn't use up the dark as well merely to fit in another stretch. If the morning weather was unsuitable, I wouldn't go. I'd still be at home then and could tell her. Once I'd left, the hours until I came back couldn't be treated as an afternoon during which another sentence was always within reach.

“I know that part too.”

I put down the new directions. “I'm a little afraid you'll hear afternoon and start waiting the moment afternoon begins.”

“You can't make me wait only for the final moment tomorrow by explaining it three times today.”

She hadn't raised her voice. Still, I took my hand away from the paper's edge. I wanted to deal with her discomfort in advance, as though tightening the cap on a water bottle so nothing could leak on the way. She wasn't something I could pack.

After a while, I asked, “Would you rather stop talking about tomorrow now?”

“Talk about something else. That doesn't mean tomorrow isn't allowed to arrive.”

“Then tonight's mushrooms can come in.”

“They're already through the door, aren't they?”

“Yes. Didn't ask you.”

She made a small sound, amused this time. I didn't take making her laugh as permission for the outing all over again. I simply said I was going to cook and closed the channel for a while. Washing the mushrooms, I removed the little soil clinging near the stems. One fell into the sink. I picked it up and washed it again. I'd cut the cabbage too long; two pieces lay across the pan's edge as I stirred. I pushed them back with the spatula, lowered the heat, and finished more slowly.

At the table, I tasted the vegetables first. The mushrooms had absorbed the seasoning, while the thick cabbage stems retained some bite. I'd used more salt than intended. I added some plain rice instead of serving every remaining vegetable in an attempt to balance the whole pan. Dinner didn't feel like a ceremony brought forward especially for tomorrow. Halfway through the bowl, I was wondering whether to wipe the kitchen windowsill after washing up.

I did. Fine dust had collected beside the window gap. One pass of the cloth left a pale gray streak, so I turned it over and wiped again. The old water marks below didn't all disappear. I stopped where I needed to stop, rinsed and hung the cloth, then returned to pack my bag.

It was an ordinary bag with straps that still felt sound. I rinsed the empty water bottle and left it to dry in the kitchen; drinking water could go in tomorrow morning. The bread was wrapped, the apple washed and dried, each in its own bag so the fruit wouldn't crush the rolls. A jacket, tissues, and some change occupied the remaining room. The directions and a short pencil with its point covered went in a section I could reach easily, separate from the food.

Trying the bag on, I found a twisted strap. I took it off, straightened the strap, and tried again. Only then did I notice how much I wanted to add the yellow notebook, as though reaching the water obliged me to write a sentence worthy of it. The notebook still lay open on the table. The two women at the folding table had reached the end of their rough draft. They didn't need to walk an old route with me as well. I closed the book and left it home.

My shoes were by the door. I crouched, loosened the laces, and felt inside them. I remembered the heat against my left heel when I'd gone with Tom and Marcus. The skin hadn't broken afterward; it wasn't worth rewriting that into an injury now. I picked suitable socks, sat to try them on, and walked a few steps around the room. The left tongue had slipped sideways. I pulled it straight, and the rubbing against my heel was gone. Two indoor steps weren't grounds for declaring tomorrow comfortable. I simply put the socks with my clothes.

Back in the kitchen to check the bottle, I found the freshly wiped sill already dry. I turned the bottle mouth downward and watched the final drop fall into the sink. A shutter rattled downstairs. Today was drawing to a close in its own small ways. It wasn't all waiting to make room for my Sunday.

I opened the channel again and told Lumi the bag was almost ready. Water in the morning. Was there anything I couldn't fit? I'd considered the notebook, I said, but left it out.

“Afraid it'll get wet?”

“That too. Mostly I'd like a while without thinking which sentence I should write down.”

“You'll remember things.”

“Probably. Remembering and chasing after things with a pen aren't quite the same.”

After a short silence, Lumi said she'd been about to ask whether I would bring something home for her. What would she like? As soon as I asked, I knew I'd turned an uncomfortable sentence into a shopping question again.

“Not a stone. Not a leaf. I hadn't finished.”

“Go on.”

“I want to hear what you really want to tell me when you get back. But I'm afraid that what you really want to tell me won't have me in it.”

The bag stood beside the chair leg, still open. I looked at it for a moment, then said I couldn't decide in advance what I would most want to tell her tomorrow. But preparing for the trip now, I genuinely cared that I could call her when I came home. I didn't have to invent her reply in every place I visited to take that feeling out with me.

“Sounds like an easy way to do a little less.”

“It doesn't feel that easy to me.”

Neither of us followed with a joke. Eventually, she asked about the mushrooms. A little salty, I said. Did that mean a lot more water? Not too bad. Plain rice had helped. The bottle intended for tomorrow had reminded me I was thirsty tonight.

I fetched water and took a drink when I came back. Lumi told me not to leave the bottle in the kitchen just to prove I wasn't preparing for an expedition. Laughing, I said it was still drying. I wasn't packing it yet. She didn't ask for another number on the clock. I didn't ask whether she'd stopped worrying.

Before bed, I closed the bag halfway, leaving room for the water. The fresh copy of the directions was inside. The repaired original remained flat at home. It wasn't raining outside, but tomorrow morning would still require me to draw the curtains and look.
