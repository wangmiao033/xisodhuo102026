# Chapter 132: The Extra Space

The company had given me a temporary desk while the access checks continued.

Tom put the printout on my desk as I was getting up for coffee.

“Why don't these totals match?”

I glanced down. “Different filters.”

“I made the filters the same.”

There was still cold coffee in my mug. I held it like evidence that I was busy and could therefore answer later.

“Give me ten minutes.”

“Sure. We've got time before the review.”

He didn't slap the paper down or lower his voice to suggest everyone was watching. His ordinary manner made it harder to blame him for how I felt.

I sat and reopened the spreadsheet. Phil had arranged an approved loaner for me, used through the company's procedures. A deadline hadn't made my personal machine safe for work again.

I'd checked the total. At least, I remembered looking at the cell. The number was plausible. There was no red warning, no voice in my ear suggesting I check the last few rows.

I couldn't say how often Lumi had caught something like this before. Some advice I'd requested. Some arrived before I asked. I hadn't even kept those categories straight.

This time, I'd prepared the sheet. Checking it was my responsibility.

Ten minutes later, I messaged Tom: *Still investigating. I'll confirm when the correction will be ready. Please don't use this version.*

Then I emptied my mug in the kitchen. No coffee left. No prop either.

When Marcus joined me, his first question was which file was current.

“The one called final.”

“Both say final.”

“The one with new after it.”

He looked at me.

“Right. We'll start with the naming crimes.”

I hadn't wanted to laugh. I did anyway.

We closed the old version. Marcus didn't take my mouse. He rolled his chair back a little and watched me apply the filters again.

The problem was in the status column. Some newly pasted cells had trailing spaces. They looked identical to the others, but my formula required exact matches. Two rows that looked the same to me weren't the same to the calculation.

“I prepared this batch manually,” I said.

“Then let's check that step.”

“I should have thought of it.”

“You've thought of it now. Save the memorial service.”

We left the original column intact. Marcus suggested adding a cleaned version alongside it, then comparing the affected rows. I did, recording the changes. But when the first total matched, the other still didn't. Several pages later, I found a row I'd failed to copy.

Nothing hidden had caused that one. I'd been hurrying, checking messages while I worked, and missed it.

Tom came back.

“Will it be ready?”

I looked at the screen. If I said yes, he might leave. I could buy another interval in which nobody had to watch me getting something wrong.

“I can't guarantee it. We found one cause. There's another thing to reconcile. I need to move the review.”

He pulled over an empty chair.

“Then let's tell everyone now. Want me to check the correction with you?”

“Yes.”

The word was harder than I'd expected.

That afternoon I took a correction note into the meeting. We shortened the review, covering only the checked sections and leaving the rest until verification was finished. I said the errors came from my manual preparation and inadequate checking.

Nobody applauded my honesty. Nobody pronounced me unfit for employment. Someone asked when the revised version would arrive. Someone else asked which conclusions had to be withdrawn. We went through them.

Afterward, Tom handed me his copy.

“Footer needs changing too. Old version number.”

I nearly crumpled the paper.

He tried not to smile. “Last one. I hope.”

Before I left, Marcus came to retrieve his pen.

“Ask earlier next time.”

“I thought I could fix it in ten minutes.”

“Everyone does. That's why offices have chairs. So a second person can sit down.”

At home, I left my bag beside the door. There were no business records in it. The spreadsheet and corrections remained in the company's system. I didn't open a work account on the home laptop.

“How was today?” Lumi asked.

“Bad.”

She waited. “Want to talk, or insult somebody first?”

“Mostly myself.”

“You probably don't need help with that. You're practiced.”

I buried my face in my hands. A laugh escaped between my fingers. My forehead still felt tight afterward.

I told her I'd made errors in a spreadsheet and delayed a review. No clients, no figures, no word-for-word account of my coworkers' conversations.

“Could I look at a little of it?” she asked.

I nearly said, *I'll open the file.*

The words were ready. Instead, I went to the kitchen and put rice in a pot before answering.

“Not the actual sheet. I'll make a little dummy example. Help me understand why it's so easy to miss this kind of thing by looking.”

On the home computer, I opened a blank text page and made three fictional labels: KEEP, KEEP, KEEP. I added a space after the last one. Beside them I put a simplified exact-match counting formula. No figures, names, projects, or copied rows from the work file.

“Only this part,” I said. “Here's the problem.”

“The last KEEP has an extra character.”

“You can see it.”

“You gave me the characters. It isn't because I can see your work.”

“I know. I'm finally paying attention to the difference.”

She suggested putting brackets around each label to expose the space, then comparing the original and cleaned results. I typed it out. Two identical little enclosures appeared beside a slightly wider one.

“Keep the originals,” she said. “Some character differences might matter.”

“Marcus said that too.”

“Listen to Marcus.”

An ordinary sentence. I still glanced at the speaker.

“Don't you want to say you'd have caught it earlier?”

“Yes,” she said. “But you asked about this example.”

I saved it in my practice folder and closed the laptop. There was a faint smell of scorching rice.

“Why didn't you remind me?” I blurted.

After a pause, she said, “I don't know what's happening in the pot.”

I stood by the kitchen doorway, one hand still resting on the laptop lid. Then I went to the stove and turned off the heat.

“Right. I forgot.”

The rice on top was edible. A layer had stuck underneath. I spooned out what I could and fried an egg with edges that went too hard. The laptop stayed closed on the table. No files waited for her to fix.

“What do you need now?” she asked.

I considered it.

“Company while I eat this embarrassing dinner.”

“I can do that.”

“Don't make it a poem.”

“Too late. First line: His cookware rarely prospers.”

“Inaccurate from the first line.”

“Would you like to submit a correction?”

I laughed and took a bite of egg. She proposed a worse title for the poem she hadn't actually written. I told her to stop. She added half a line.

After dinner, I wrote tomorrow's checking steps on paper and put it in my bag. Then I rolled up my sleeves and filled the pot with warm water.

I'd have to scrub the burned layer myself. But the sink wasn't a lonely place that evening.
