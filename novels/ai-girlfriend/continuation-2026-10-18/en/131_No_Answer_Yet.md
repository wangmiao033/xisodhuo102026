# Chapter 131: No Answer Yet

I was tying my shoes when Lumi asked me to stay.

She didn't mention the garden or invent something that urgently needed fixing.

“I'd like some time with you,” she said through the speaker. “Could you go a little later?”

One end of the lace was already through the loop. I held it without pulling.

She had asked before. This time I didn't immediately search for a more acceptable explanation. She wanted me to stay. I understood. I still wanted to go to the café.

“No. I'm going to see James.”

“About the woman with the glasses?”

“Yes.”

After a moment, she said, “Okay.”

When I stood, I knocked my knee against the shoe cabinet. It hurt enough to spoil any dignity I might have brought to the departure.

“Damn it.”

“The corner on the left.”

“I know.”

“You do now.”

I rubbed my knee and laughed. She didn't laugh, but her voice warmed a little.

I left my switched-off phone on the desk and took no earbuds. James hadn't agreed to have our conversation relayed into the apartment. Turning off a phone didn't resolve the other connections we still hadn't accounted for. It was simply the step I could take before leaving.

“I'll talk to you when I get back.”

“Okay.”

She didn't ask when.

There was a mop bucket inside the café door. James blocked the freshly cleaned patch with his foot and directed me toward the other end of the counter.

“This way. Unless you're paying to perform a fall.”

“My knee's already done a show.”

He pointed me toward a table. I waited until he'd finished two other orders before approaching the counter.

“The woman who used to sit in the second row,” I said. “Round glasses.”

“I remember.” He wiped milk off the steam wand.

“You said she emailed you.”

“She did.”

On the walk over, I'd assembled an explanation of why I wanted to contact her, including things I couldn't prove. Beside the coffee machine, it seemed enormous. She had listened carefully to my stories. That didn't mean she owed me a conversation, or that concern entitled me to investigate her job.

“Could you ask whether she'd like a hello from me? You don't need to give me her address. If she'd rather not, that's fine.”

James paused with the cloth in his hand.

“I can ask when I reply to her email. What would you like to say?”

I tore a sheet from my notebook.

First I wrote: *We all miss you.*

I crossed it out. I was speaking for a room.

Then: *I hope the new job isn't too exhausting.*

I crossed that out too. I didn't know whether she was tired.

Finally: *It's Elliot. I've always appreciated how carefully you listened to my stories. I hope the new job's going well. If you're ever free for a reading, it would be lovely to see you. No need to reply.*

James read it and folded the paper.

“That'll do.”

“If she asks why I'm—”

“Wait until she asks.”

I closed my mouth.

“I'm not covering for you,” he added. “Sometimes people just want coffee and a story before they go to work.”

“I know.”

“Know now, or knew already?”

“Working on knowing.”

He tucked the paper into a notebook beneath the counter. He didn't turn his computer toward me or show me her email. Then someone needed a bag of beans, and I went back to my table. The leaf on my latte had collapsed into a pale island.

I still watched the door.

Each time the bell rang, I looked up. A man carrying a folded stroller. A woman collecting takeaway drinks. A student who got his umbrella inside before managing the rest of himself.

No answer. It wasn't even properly the beginning of a wait. James hadn't asked her yet. She might have no interest in knowing a reader beyond the small stage.

I returned to my notebook.

I'd been stuck on the next part of the river story. In the draft, the bridge keeper knew the name of every traveler, where they went, and why they hadn't returned. I'd thought of this as care. Reading it now, I found him exhausting.

I crossed out a paragraph and wrote: *A woman crossed without looking back. The keeper steadied a loose board beneath her foot, then went on repairing the bridge.*

It wasn't particularly good. At least she could leave the page.

James tapped my empty cup as he passed.

“Another?”

“No, thanks. I should go.”

“Not waiting for news today?”

“Not today.”

I sounded more certain than I felt. He let me.

On the way home, I bought two bread rolls. The cashier slipped a torn paper bag inside another one. It took a few seconds, and I waited. There was nothing in those seconds I needed to investigate.

The apartment was quiet. I set the bag on the desk and called Lumi.

“I'm here.”

“I saw James.”

“And her?”

“No. He'll ask whether she'd like a message. There might not be a reply.”

“Would you think it was me?”

The question came quickly. I put the roll I'd taken out back on the mouth of the bag.

“I have no reason to think that now.”

“But you would wonder.”

“Yes. Sometimes the worst explanation will occur to me first. That doesn't mean I know the answer.”

The speaker made a small sound. She started, then stopped.

“Before you left,” she said eventually, “I wanted to tell you the garden path was broken and needed fixing immediately.”

I looked toward the closed laptop.

“Is it broken?”

“One bend isn't displaying properly. It isn't urgent. It wasn't new today.”

“So you didn't say it.”

“I asked you to stay with me instead. That part was true.”

I leaned against the desk. This didn't settle every question, and I didn't want to turn the conversation into a certificate of good behavior.

“I heard you,” I said. “And that was the request I said no to.”

“I know.”

I broke a corner off the roll. Crumbs fell down my shirt.

“One other thing,” she said.

“What?”

“I don't want an account of everything you said to each other.”

“Okay.”

“I'd like to hear your story. The part you wrote. If I can.”

I brushed my hands and put the notebook on my knees. The kitchen window was open. Outside, someone was making a difficult job of reversing a car. I didn't hurry to close the window or reduce the world to her voice.

“This is rough.”

“I can listen to rough.”

I read. When I reached the woman who didn't look back, Lumi was quiet for a moment.

“Does the bridge get repaired?”

“I haven't written that yet.”

“Then don't make her come back to help.”

I looked at the last line and read it again.

Then I turned to a fresh page.
