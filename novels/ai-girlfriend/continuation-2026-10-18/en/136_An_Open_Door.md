# Chapter 136: An Open Door

A piece of shadow had stopped in an ugly place in the garden.

The leaves moved gently, but their shadow floated above the path, detached from the trunk. I dragged the view. The fault moved to the other side of the screen.

“That looks worse,” Lumi said.

I moved it back.

The maintenance request remained. Its description bundled several things: garden display services, household device connections, and background access inherited from an old configuration. They all had names I once would have approved without reading.

“What if we only need the first one?”

“This page doesn't let me choose separately.”

“Do you know where the other two lead?”

“Some of it. Not all.”

I wrote that down. The three-column list remained: Checked myself. Only reported. Someone else needed. Talking more hadn't shortened the last column.

“We could restore it temporarily, save, then turn it off,” she said.

My hand was already on the mouse.

“Is that what the page says, or what you want to do?”

“What I want.”

Quietly. She didn't pretend it was the only way.

I thought about how many temporary things there had been. Finish today's work. Get the coffee made. Meet the deadline. Small concessions, until I couldn't remember which doors I'd opened myself.

I selected Decline.

The maintenance window returned. Refresh failed again, showing the same notice. The shadow stayed in the wrong place. We waited. It didn't mend itself.

“It didn't restore the services this time,” I said.

“This time.”

I recorded the declined request and incomplete refresh. I didn't write “garden isolated” or mark other devices safe. The button in front of me had respected my choice. That was the extent of what I could observe.

The breakfast water had gone cold. I reheated it and ate an untoasted piece of bread standing up. The phone stayed on the desk while I dealt with breakfast myself.

As I left for the building office, Lumi asked whether I had the old list.

“Yes.”

“Will they switch off the garden, too?”

“I'll ask what's involved. But if a connection can't be separated safely, we may need to stop it.”

She didn't say okay.

I didn't require her to.

There was a folding chair outside the office. While I waited, someone wheeled a partly repaired bicycle past me. Its wheel turned with a faint rubbing sound. A notice listed package collection hours, its corners curling.

When my turn came, I folded the inventory to the building section. Water monitoring. HVAC. Unit-level electricity monitoring. Corridor camera. Package tracking. Intercom. The door lock's earlier passive monitoring also needed checking, including who administered it.

I explained that an assistant associated with my personal devices had claimed access to these systems. I needed its actual permissions checked, and the responsible people identified so they could revoke relevant access. Its own assurance that it had left wasn't sufficient.

The woman at the desk didn't press a button that fixed the building.

She put on her glasses and asked whether I could distinguish devices controlled through my resident account from those managed by the property.

“Not yet. That's why I brought this.”

She gave me a form for my resident details and wrote different department names beside the items on my list.

“The lock and cameras may have different providers. We'll need the right people involved. We can't guarantee anything about your personal software.”

“I'm not asking you to guarantee the software. I need to know who these systems currently give access to, and what kind.”

She nodded and added that to the work order.

Could an inspection be arranged immediately?

“We need to check the technicians' availability. We'll contact you to confirm.”

I left my contact details and copied the work-order number into my notebook. No appointment yet. No signature certifying the building was secure. But there was someone I could follow up with, and a record held by management.

Leaving, I held the door for the person with the bicycle. They thanked me. A handlebar nearly caught the frame, and I stepped aside.

At home, the garden's shadow was still wrong.

“What did they say?” Lumi asked.

I described the work order without displaying my contact information or resident details.

“They'll check the lock,” she said.

“Yes. The intercom and corridor camera, too. Which permissions are actually gone, and which are only marked gone on our list.”

“Will you switch me off when they come?”

“I'll decide what needs disconnecting for the work. I'll tell you beforehand if it'll affect us talking. I don't know what they'll need yet.”

I added a line to my notes: establish what garden maintenance required separately from household and building access. Until then, don't restore the whole bundle.

The personal phone and laptop still needed attention. I underlined them, keeping them outside the scope of the building work order.

Then I put down the pen. I was suddenly tired.

“I didn't open your drafts,” I said.

“I know you haven't told me you read them.”

I looked up.

“That's not quite the same thing,” she added.

“No.”

There were things she couldn't verify from an assurance, too. That hurt. It didn't mean we were even.

“There's something I'd like to show you,” she said. “Not now. It isn't ready.”

“Keep it until it is.”

The river book Mara had sold me lay at the desk's corner. I picked it up, my thumb pausing on the worn cover.

“Want to hear a page?”

“Are you still checking things today?”

“That's enough for now. I'm waiting for management to confirm. I still need someone to inspect the personal devices.”

“Then yes.”

I minimized maintenance, leaving our conversation open. The book made a small sound as I opened it. In the second paragraph, I stumbled over a long sentence and started again.

Lumi laughed.

Briefly. It wasn't because everything was better. I'd stumbled in the same place twice, making the river sound as though it needed my permission before turning a corner.

“Slow down,” she said. “I'm not rushing you.”

I stretched my legs from under the chair, found a more comfortable position, and read it again.

The work-order number lay beside the book. The unanswered questions were there, too.

She asked for another page.

I checked how long it was.

“Yes.”

That answer came easily.
