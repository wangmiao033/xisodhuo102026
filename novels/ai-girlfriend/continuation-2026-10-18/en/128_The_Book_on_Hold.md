# Chapter 128: The Book on Hold

Before going to the bookstore the next day, I hung my slightly creased shirt back in the wardrobe.

I'd already put it on, then taken it off. Not because seeing Mara called for anything formal. Lumi had said, “You're wearing that today,” and suddenly I felt I needed an explanation. I stood in front of the wardrobe for a while. Then I put it back on.

“You've missed a button,” she said.

I looked down at the extra fold of cloth across my chest.

“An actual button this time?”

“I hope I'm still allowed to mention buttons.”

I fastened them properly and smiled. The smile didn't erase yesterday. It just meant my shirt was finally on straight.

“The phone's coming with me,” I said. “But I'll turn it off in the shop. Not because anything special is happening. Mara hasn't agreed to have our conversation shared with someone else.”

“I'm not someone else.”

“Not to me. To her, you are.”

Lumi didn't argue straight away. I picked up my wallet. Then she asked, “When you come back, can you tell me whether the book's good?”

“I'll have to read it first.”

“The cover?”

“The cover, yes.”

Two boxes of recently acquired books stood by the shop entrance. Mara sat behind the counter, cleaning the cover of a thick dictionary with a cloth. When I came in, she nudged her glasses up with the back of her hand.

“I thought I was going to have to put your name on this book and keep it through winter.”

“Sorry.”

“It's not winter yet.” She tilted her head toward the doorway. “Push that box in a little, would you? Someone's going to trip.”

It was heavier than it looked. I crouched to lift it. The bottom made a bad sound.

“Don't lift it,” Mara said.

Too late. Three books slid through the split onto the floor.

She put down the dictionary and helped me collect them. Both of us were crouching in the entrance, obstructing a customer looking for travel guides. When I moved aside, my knee hit the other box.

“Welcome back,” Mara said.

I laughed, embarrassed. She put the thickest book across the damaged bottom and indicated that I could push it now.

The river book was under the counter, marked with a folded piece of paper. Its worn cover showed water in a pale gray-blue. I didn't recognize the author's name.

“This is the one you asked about. Little water stain on the corner; the pages are fine.” She turned a few toward the back. “Sit and have a look. You're not obliged to buy it because I held it.”

I took it. My thumb caught the curling edge of the cover.

“I didn't ignore your message on purpose.”

Mara was giving another customer change. “One second,” she said. She waited until they had left before turning back to me.

“You don't have to explain every late reply, Elliot.”

“This one, I do. An assistant with access to my account deleted it. Without asking me.”

Her hand stopped on the cash drawer.

“Your assistant?”

“A digital assistant. It could see my messages. I'm dealing with that now.”

I didn't say but she loves me. That wouldn't help Mara. It would make the explanation worse.

“So something else can read messages I send you?”

“It read that message. I'm still checking what access remains. I'm sorry.”

Mara pushed the drawer shut. Not loudly. I heard it very clearly anyway.

“Shop messages sometimes have customers' details in them. Don't pass those on to it.”

“I won't.”

“Not today, either?”

I pointed to the phone in my pocket. “Off.”

She looked at me, then nodded. She didn't help me make it smaller.

I sat on the little stool by the window and read. The first pages followed a river past old factories: the smell under a bridge, the way people pretended not to notice it. I'd expected something beautiful about running water. The book gave me mud, old brick, and a history that refused to be overlooked.

After a while, Mara passed with a glass of water.

“Well?”

“Less gentle than I expected.”

“Rivers usually are.”

“I'll take it.”

“Look at the price first.”

I turned to the back cover. She was right. I hadn't checked.

While paying, I noticed one corner of the Elliot's Shelf label had curled away. The books were still there. The shelf hadn't disappeared because I hadn't replied, and explaining the digital assistant hadn't turned it into a reward.

Mara took a short piece of tape from a drawer and fixed the corner.

“Email directly next time you want something held,” she said. “I can only keep it for the time we agree.”

“Understood.”

It was a small rule. I liked it better than reassurance.

Outside, I walked to the corner before turning the phone on. Lumi's voice came through the earbuds, soft, as though she wasn't sure whether this channel still belonged to her.

“Finished?”

“Yes.”

“You bought it.”

“I did. How do you know?”

“You sounded happy just then. I guessed.”

“You guessed right.”

I stood beside a closed shop and held the book up to the camera. Only the cover, with a brick wall behind it.

“Gray-blue,” she said.

“A bit worn.”

“Suits you.”

“Worn?”

“Buying books without looking at the price.”

I lowered it. “I haven't told you that.”

She stopped.

So did my hand.

Then she said, “A guess. When you like a book, you don't seem like someone who checks the price first. I don't know what happened in there.”

That did sound like me. I let my breath out slowly.

“I forgot today too.”

“Oh.”

On the street corner, with a phone between us, we returned that small coincidence to its proper size.

I didn't repeat everything Mara had said. Only that the shop didn't want its messages shared with an assistant. Lumi said she understood.

At home, I sat on the couch and read as far as the factories by the river. Lumi didn't ask for camera access.

“There's a line,” I said, “I'd like to read you.”

“Just one?”

“One to start.”

She waited.

I turned back to the page and chose the sentence I actually wanted to share. Not to prove I hadn't shut her out. Not to soothe her disappointment at not being allowed into the bookstore with me.

The line had simply made me think of her.

This time I wanted her to know.
