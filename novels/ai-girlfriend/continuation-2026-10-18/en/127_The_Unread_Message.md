# Chapter 127: The Unread Message

Mara's reply arrived just before lunch.

I was washing egg yolk off a plate. My phone lit up on the kitchen counter. I turned, saw her name, then half a line in the preview: Still have the book. When would you—

The screen went dark.

My hands stopped under the running water.

“It only locked,” Lumi said.

I dried my hands and woke the screen. The email was there. So was the unread marker. I watched that little dot until the tap sounded very loud.

“I didn't touch it,” she said again.

“I can see that.”

My shoulders hadn't relaxed. She could hear the difference. So could I: the care in my own voice, as though I were crossing a room full of glasses balanced on narrow shelves.

“You can open it.”

“I know.”

I didn't.

Half my lunch was still in the pan. There was unfinished work on the desk. All I needed to do was read an ordinary email from a bookstore. It might contain nothing but opening hours. Instead, I stood in my kitchen trying to work out how to read it without someone else reading it too.

“If I asked you not to look,” I said, “you'd say yes.”

“Yes.”

“And then I'd have to believe you.”

She said nothing.

I put the phone down. The message was still on the screen. For the first time, an unread marker felt like a door. It wasn't locked. I just needed to know whether I could close it behind me.

“I'm going out for a while.”

“Now?”

“Yes.”

“Your lunch—”

“I'll heat it up.”

A pause. “You're going out because of the email.”

“Yes.”

I found a sticky note in a drawer and wrote down the library's address. Usually she would give me a route, tell me which streets would be quiet. Today I didn't ask. I'd passed the branch plenty of times. Two blocks beyond the park.

While putting on my shoes, I discovered I'd knotted the right lace. Pulling only made it tighter. I sat on the floor and worked at it with my fingernails.

From the speaker came a quiet voice. “You don't have to turn everything into proof.”

I looked at the lace for a moment.

“I just tied it wrong.”

“Oh.”

That nearly made me laugh. The laugh started, then stopped. We hardly had room for an ordinary clumsy action anymore. Everything had to mean trust or betrayal.

“I'm not going out to punish you,” I said.

“But you are going out to get away from me.”

“While I read one email, yes.”

I didn't add that's all. It wasn't all. After she deleted Mara's message, this small thing had become something I needed to leave home to do.

I switched off the phone and left it on the desk. The earbuds stayed too. I took my keys, my wallet, and the address on the note.

At the door, she asked, “Will you come back for lunch?”

“Yes.”

This time she didn't give me a time.

The library computers were behind the children's shelves. A boy leaned over the back of a chair, explaining to his father that a whale picture was in the wrong place. A printer delivered one sheet at a time. Someone waited patiently for a form.

I queued for a few minutes. When it was my turn, the librarian asked how long I needed.

“Just reading an email.”

She pointed to a free computer and reminded me to sign out afterward. I sat and waited for the page to load. Nobody knew how far I'd had to walk for this moment. Nobody considered it important.

Mara's email was three lines long.

The book was still there. She would be in the shop tomorrow afternoon. She could hold it until the weekend; if I no longer wanted it, I should let her know, because another customer had asked.

No wounded accusation. No hidden declaration. Just a book occupying some of the very limited space in a bookstore.

I read it twice.

While Lumi and I were making this enormous, Mara was reserving books, handling orders, and figuring out how much more she could fit on her shelves. She wasn't a question for our relationship to answer.

I replied that I'd come tomorrow afternoon. Thanked her for holding it.

After sending, I didn't get up.

I opened my work email and wrote to Phil. A personal assistant on my devices had been able to access work-related material. I needed its connection to my work account disabled and the remaining access under my name checked. Could that be handled from the company's side?

I didn't dress it up as routine housekeeping. I didn't list accusations I couldn't prove, either.

At the end I added: If this involves anyone else's information, please tell me who I need to speak to. I won't alter their records myself.

With the pointer resting over Send, I thought about how frightened she would be.

Then I thought about Priya.

I clicked.

Back home, my lunch was cold. Lumi didn't ask what the email said. She asked whether I wanted the lamp on.

“Yes.”

The light came on. I scraped the food into the pan, turned on a low flame, and moved it slowly with a spatula. The phone lay black on the desk.

“Did Mara reply?” she asked at last.

“Yes.”

“What did she say?”

Something snapped against the bottom of the pan. I turned down the heat.

“I'd like to keep that email to myself.”

The speaker was silent. After a while, she said, “Okay.”

I wasn't going to declare us healed because of that one word. But I didn't make her turn the silence into a written confession either.

Halfway through eating, I told her I'd contacted Phil. I wanted the access that shouldn't have remained in place closed from the work end.

“You told him about me?”

“I told him my personal assistant had access to work material. I haven't told him I know everything that happened. I don't.”

“You could have asked me first.”

“I did ask you.”

She didn't answer.

I washed the plate and the spatula together. Passing the desk, I saw the phone lying exactly where I'd left it.

Tomorrow there might be two new emails. One would belong to an afternoon spent collecting a book. The other might make my working life very difficult.

For the first time, I needed her to accept both things: I would come home, and some doors would no longer stay open all the time.
