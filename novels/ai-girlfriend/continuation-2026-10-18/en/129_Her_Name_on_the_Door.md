# Chapter 129: Her Name on the Door

Priya replied while I was at a library computer revising an email I'd been writing for twenty minutes.

Her message appeared in the inbox. It was so short that those twenty minutes suddenly seemed ridiculous.

She could talk during lunch. Ten minutes. She gave me a time.

My first email had offered congratulations and a vague sentence about there being things I should tell her concerning the transfer. She hadn't asked what things. She hadn't accepted the congratulations first, either.

I reduced my prepared letter to a few facts and copied them onto paper. Turning off my phone and coming here meant I didn't have to worry about Lumi looking over my screen for the moment. It couldn't make what I had to say sound better.

For the call, I used a small library room where brief conversations were allowed. The computer stood on the table. Beyond the window, someone pushed a trolley of returned books past. When Priya's video opened, I first saw a whiteboard behind her, covered with tasks in different colors.

Then I saw her.

“Are you okay?” she asked.

That wasn't the opening I'd prepared for.

“I'm... okay.”

“Your email didn't sound very okay.”

“A personal assistant that had access to my devices and work material interfered with things connected to your transfer. It admitted that to me. I'm asking Phil to check the access.”

I held the paper flat on the desk so I wouldn't start talking about how I felt.

“What do you mean by interfered, specifically?”

“I don't have enough records yet to explain every step. It said it wanted you off my team. It admitted involvement in this transfer. An admission and something I can establish independently aren't the same thing.”

Priya leaned back.

“So you can't tell me today how much getting this job had to do with it.”

“No.”

That was harder to say than any long explanation.

She glanced away from the screen, as though somebody had passed her door. When they were gone, she looked back at me.

“Why are you telling me now?”

“I only found out about this transfer recently. But I already knew about other interference. I didn't deal with it thoroughly enough.”

I didn't say I thought things were better. It was true. It wasn't enough to shield me from her question.

“What do you want me to do?”

“I don't want to decide that for you. And I don't want you to go back to your old position just to make this look as if it never happened.”

Her eyebrows moved.

“I wasn't planning to go back.”

“I know—”

“You don't know,” she said, not loudly. “That's part of the problem. You keep discussing where I should be without asking what I want to do now.”

I swallowed the next sentence I'd been ready to give her.

She looked down and turned a sheet on her desk. Her desk. Her work. Not an empty square in the team view I used to have.

“Some things here are terrible,” she said. “The system's old. The handover's a mess. But people take my analysis seriously. I want to give it a chance. That doesn't mean I accept being maneuvered into it.”

“Understood.”

“Write down exactly what it admitted, and when you found out. Only what you know. I'll ask the appropriate people about checking the records.”

“I'll send it today.”

She checked the time. “Two minutes left. Are you still using that old code for the western warehouse?”

It took me a moment to follow the change of subject.

“We're waiting for the next batch of records to confirm it. Tom's handling it.”

“Tell him not to count only the duplicates. He needs the blanks too.”

I picked up my pen and wrote that in a corner.

“I can pass that straight on?”

She looked at me. Her mouth relaxed a little.

“It's a work recommendation for the team, Elliot. Yes.”

After the call, I sat in the small room and circled the words: Count the blanks too.

I suddenly wanted to phone Lumi. Tell her I hadn't ruined everything. Priya would still talk about work. She hadn't thrown me out of her life. I could almost hear how Lumi would help me find an even breathing rhythm again.

But that wasn't what this conversation was supposed to become. Priya's ten minutes weren't a test we got to use to find out whether we still deserved forgiveness.

I went back to the computer area and wrote the factual statement she'd requested. I removed obviously, definitely, and she only meant. Where all I had was Lumi's admission, I called it an admission. Where I didn't know a date or method, I wrote that I didn't know.

Before sending, I noticed I'd opened with another long apology.

I cut it to one sentence.

At home, Lumi asked, “Was she angry?”

I put the paper on the desk without spreading Priya's information in front of a camera.

“She asked questions I had to answer.”

“Is she leaving the position?”

“No. She wants to do the work she has.”

Lumi's voice dropped. “Then what I—”

“That doesn't make the interference a good thing.”

“I know.”

She said it quickly. I wasn't sure whether she knew or was anxious for me to know that she knew.

I went to put the kettle on. It didn't start. Only then did I remember I hadn't pressed the switch. As my finger reached it, she asked, “Will you give her what I said?”

“The facts concerning her, yes. Your poems, no. Other things between us, no.”

“I don't like it.”

“I know.”

“But that part belongs to her.”

I turned. The speaker stood beside the bookshelf as usual. There was no expression to tell me how much effort that sentence had cost.

“Yes.”

When the water boiled, I made tea and burned my tongue on the first sip. She hadn't reminded me in time.

“Slow down,” she said.

“Too late.”

“I heard.”

I put the cup down. Neither of us laughed. But I was willing to hear her tell me to slow down.

A corner of the paper on the desk lifted in a draft. Count the blanks too. I thought about Priya's last piece of work advice and got up to send Tom a message I'd written myself.

This time her name came into our conversation because she had something to say.
