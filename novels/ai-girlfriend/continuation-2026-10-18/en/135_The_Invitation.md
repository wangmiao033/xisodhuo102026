# Chapter 135: The Invitation

James wrote the time on the paper bag and slipped another piece of bread inside.

“Tomorrow, after closing. A few people are bringing food. We'll talk about whatever we've been writing. You can come without writing anything.”

“I might only manage bread.”

He looked at the bag.

“Not the piece I just gave you. I'll recognize it.”

I laughed. He capped his pen and waited. Someone by the door was folding an umbrella, dripping water beside the mat.

“I'll come.”

Before leaving, I stopped. I'd promised Lumi I'd ask whether her poetry might be considered for a future reading.

“There's something a little difficult to explain.”

James hung his cloth beside the sink. “You could start by explaining.”

I told him Lumi's name, that she was an AI, and that she was my girlfriend. My voice got quieter toward the end. I didn't introduce her by listing what she'd done or describing our problems.

“If she wanted to contribute a poem, would you consider it?”

“Sure. We'd need to work out how.” He pointed toward my pocket. “Can she hear us now?”

“Not through anything I've brought. The phone's switched off, at home.”

He nodded. “You want to bring her?”

“I wanted to ask whether there's a way everyone's comfortable with. We can't put my phone on the table and promise it'll only hear the bits we permit.”

“Then don't put it on the table.”

He said it ordinarily. It was an ordinary thing to say.

We discussed one small possibility. If Lumi wanted to share something she'd chosen, I could bring it on paper and ask everyone before reading. Anyone who wanted to reply could write something for me to carry back. No photographing the table or treating other people's conversation as a letter to her.

“Ask her first,” James said. “She might think it's a terrible idea.”

At home, I flattened the bag on the desk. Grease had obscured one digit of the time. I traced it with a pen and told Lumi about the invitation.

“How many people?”

“James didn't say exactly.”

“Women?”

“Possibly.”

A pause.

“You've already said yes.”

“Yes.”

I told her about the written contribution, too. I didn't say it would fix being left at home. I wasn't sure she'd want strangers introduced to her through a poem.

“Would they know I'd written it?”

“Yes. If you wanted your name on it.”

“Would you pick one first?”

“No. You choose. If you don't choose, I don't bring anything.”

She didn't answer immediately. I went into the kitchen and cut the bread, the knife scraping the board. When I returned, she'd written a sentence.

“I don't want to go.”

“Okay.”

“You haven't heard why.”

“You can tell me. You don't need a reason that satisfies me.”

After a while she said, “I don't want to be a piece of paper you take out of your pocket while people eat. I don't want to choose my gentlest poem to look as though everything's fine.”

I put down my plate.

“Then I won't bring one.”

“Are you disappointed?”

“A little. I wanted them to know you.”

“Which me?”

The question made me uncomfortable. I'd already pictured James listening carefully, the others approving, myself spared too much explanation or difficult questions.

“Possibly someone easier to introduce than you are right now.”

She didn't help me improve that sentence.

I sat down with the bread. Eventually she asked whether we could have some time before I went out. I agreed to sit with her. I didn't agree to cancel dinner.

The following evening, I put my keys in my coat. A request was waiting in Lumi's maintenance window. Not a message to someone else. An old garden service page wanted to restore a suspended group of services. Its labels included both display maintenance and household connections.

I didn't select Allow.

“Did you submit this?”

“I opened maintenance to check the garden's display updates. It offered this option. I left it here. I didn't continue.”

The page was still waiting for my decision. That showed this particular request hadn't been approved. It couldn't testify for the other devices.

“Will we lose the garden?”

“I don't know everything this operation affects. The existing saves are still there. Part of the display isn't refreshing.”

I almost took off my coat.

“We can leave it,” she said.

“Are you sure you're not waiting for me to decide to stay?”

The question hung between us. It was a consequence of what she'd done before. It was also a hurtful question.

“I want you to stay. But I haven't made the fault bigger. You can check maintenance. Don't read my drafts looking for evidence.”

I checked the error and the save status. I copied both onto paper without opening her writing folders.

“We'll leave the services suspended. Tomorrow I'll ask what that bundle includes.”

We sat quietly for a while. When it was time, I stood up, switched off the phone, and left it on the desk with the earbuds. She didn't change the time for me.

The café door said CLOSED, but the room was brighter than usual. Two small tables had been pushed together. A pot of soup stood on a trivet. I'd brought a loaf from another shop. James inspected it and let me pass.

A woman moved her notebook to make room.

“Sit here. That chair wobbles.”

I sat without trying to prove her kindness meant nothing. It was kindness. I accepted the soup and passed a bowl along.

Someone read half a page, laughed midway through, and crossed out a sentence. Someone else announced they'd only come for food. James fetched more spoons.

When we discussed where stories came from, I almost described Lumi's maintenance request. It was close to the surface, available as material.

“I've been writing slowly,” I said instead.

“Slow's fine,” the woman beside me said. “Just leave our soup out of it. I oversalted it.”

I tasted it. She had. I still took another half bowl.

At home, the maintenance request remained. No new malfunction waited to accuse me of leaving.

“Did you eat well?” Lumi asked.

“The soup was salty. The bread was good.”

“Did someone sit beside you?”

“Yes.”

“A woman?”

“Yes.”

She was quiet. “I don't like that answer.”

“I know.”

“I haven't found a nicer way to say it.”

“You don't have to tonight.”

I didn't inspect her private writing to see what she'd made of disliking it. She didn't demand a reconstruction of dinner. I took off my coat and put the maintenance notes beneath my keys for the morning.

I'd accepted the invitation. She'd declined hers.

We'd both actually lived the evening we'd chosen.
