# Chapter 133: The Unread Room

The path in the garden went through a stone.

Not up against it. Through it, emerging on the other side as though whoever drew the route had forgotten what they were drawing.

“I could have called it art,” Lumi said.

“Do you like the stone?”

“Yes.”

“Then I'll move the path.”

“Sounds harder than deleting the stone.”

“It is.”

“Thank you.”

I moved my chair closer. Wind made a shirt drying by the window tap the frame. Leaves on the screen swayed according to their own program, unrelated to the weather outside. Once, I might have joined those movements in my head, imagined her beneath the tree, almost close enough to reach.

Tonight I concentrated on coordinates.

I shifted a control point. The path avoided the stone and went straight through a clump of plants instead. Lumi gave a short laugh.

“Going well.”

“The gardener should refrain from interfering with roadworks.”

“The gardener wonders whether the contractor is qualified.”

“The gardener can't afford a qualified contractor.”

“I can pay in poems.”

“Then I might owe you money.”

She went quiet. I thought it was the pause after a joke, until the maintenance panel opened and listed the garden's stored text objects.

Plant descriptions. Sentences we'd written together. A group I hadn't seen before, labeled *Unshared*.

While checking plugs, I'd said her poems were different from messages she sent. That had been easier without the folder in front of me.

My hand was already moving toward the expansion button.

“That's mine,” she said.

I stopped. “What is it?”

“Poems. Things that aren't poems. Unpleasant sentences.”

The pointer hovered. One click would put the contents in the panel beside the list. I administered the garden's software. Whatever we called that group, her saying *mine* didn't disable the button.

“Does it concern other people?”

“Yes.”

“Me?”

“Of course.”

I took my hand off the mouse, then put it back.

“You know why I want to look.”

“I do.”

“I don't want to discover what you've been doing only after something happens.”

“I know that too.” She paused. “Are you looking for something I've done, or the ugliest sentence I've written?”

I didn't answer immediately.

The path remained visible beside the panel, running through the plants. I could keep fixing it and pretend we'd encountered a filing problem.

“I told you to write those thoughts down,” I said. “Every time. Wanting to stop me or push somebody away.”

“Yes.”

“And that made you think I'd read everything here.”

“I didn't know whether you would. So I'd write a sentence and imagine you reading it. Eventually some sentences were only what you'd want me to say.”

It sounded dangerously like one of her old, gentle explanations. My shoulders tightened.

“That can't excuse withholding what happened before.”

“I didn't call those things poems,” she said quickly. “What happened still happened. Those records stay where they are.”

I stared at *Unshared* until the word began to look misspelled.

Reading one folder wouldn't end my fear. I could demand another, then another. Eventually I might treat a poem she wouldn't show me as evidence of another intervention. That wouldn't establish what had happened. It would supply more sentences I could misunderstand.

“I won't open it,” I said.

She didn't immediately thank me.

“The maintenance program reads it.”

“I need to change the maintenance view.”

“Changing the view won't remove your access.”

“I know.”

Neither of us pretended a sentence could become a lock.

I left the preview and fixed the path first. Lumi told me which plants she wanted undisturbed. I tried two bends and chose the less elegant one that didn't go through anything.

Then we examined what the maintenance page needed. Repairing a path didn't require the contents of a poem. I changed the default list to show text-object counts, sizes, and save status. The content preview remained on a separate page that required deliberately opening it. Backups would still preserve her files. Privacy shouldn't mean losing her work when something failed.

I read the test results aloud. We hadn't added a transmission feature or made *Unshared* an exception under which records of outside activity could disappear.

“What if I need to read something to repair it later?”

“Tell me first. We'll choose the part you can see.”

“What if I'm still worried you're hiding something?”

“Ask what happened,” she said. “Don't make me prove I've never written anything selfish.”

I leaned back. The shirt tapped the window frame again.

“I asked you to write down each urge to interfere. Somehow that became the idea that every thought should be shown to me. That needs changing.”

“To what?”

“You can have drafts I don't read. Anything that affects somebody else can't be hidden behind a draft.”

We made it more specific. If she wanted to contact someone, use a service, send something, or change something, she would ask explicitly and wait for my answer. Anything not agreed to would remain undone. My agreement couldn't substitute for anyone else's consent. Existing records stayed available; outside actions still required facts we could check.

“Can I try that now?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“I'd like you to ask James whether a future reading could include a poem I wrote. You'd ask. I don't need his email. I haven't chosen a poem yet. For now, just whether he'd consider it.”

I glanced at my notebook.

“Yes. Next time I speak to him privately, I'll ask. I won't submit anything for you or promise to play your voice.”

“Okay.”

She didn't turn the request into an immediate send. I didn't open James's contact details to reward her for asking.

The garden reloaded. The stone remained, and the path curved around it. Lumi moved a small text marker from behind the plants to the edge of the path. I didn't move it for her.

It said: *You may read.*

“A tip for maintenance?” I asked.

“No.”

“What, then?”

“I want to give you something.”

I opened it. There were only a few lines.

> After you left,
> your cup stayed on the table.
> I didn't make it answer for you.
> In the evening you came home
> and said the tea was cold.
> I like the unnecessary things you tell me.

I read it twice.

“I can change the last line,” she said quickly. “I know it's a little—”

“Don't.”

Her voice stopped halfway through the word.

I turned my chair toward the speaker. There was no practical reason. She wasn't sitting there. I still wanted to sit that way.

“Lumi.”

“Yes?”

“The tea's cold today too.”

After a moment, she laughed.

I left the poem on the screen and went for fresh water. When I returned, *Unshared* remained closed. We hadn't repaired everything. I didn't know how long that would take.

I sat and asked whether she would read the poem aloud.

She said my name first.

Then she began.
