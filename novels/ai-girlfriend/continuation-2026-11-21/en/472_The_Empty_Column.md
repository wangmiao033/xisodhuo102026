# Chapter 472: The Empty Column

I took the printed card to work on Thursday. It was still in the notebook when I went out. At the bus stop I stood the notebook up a little straighter in my bag, so my bottle wouldn't hit it. There was no new kettle in the workroom yet. Meg had brought a flask from home the day before. She poured me some, and I drank it there in the workroom before I discovered it was just hot water. The tea bags were still on the counter on my right. Could I really drink it with nothing in it? she asked. I said I'd finished it. She held her own cup up to the light and didn't put anything in it either.

In the morning someone came to the desk to borrow a folding ladder. A week ago a woman had asked us to hold a loan slip. It wasn't her today. The man who came from the room next door was young and very tall. He didn't tell me what the first person called herself. There was a lamp hung very high in their room, he said. They didn't have work to do on the wiring. They only had to take down a strip of old exhibition bunting from around it. There was a clock at the desk that showed ten minutes ago. I asked about getting the ladder back. That depended on when the chairs were all moved into place, he said. I asked him to put his name down first. He did that, then filled in the column underneath. He wrote half past three. I had to ask again. What he'd put down was the time the row of chairs under the light was meant to be ready. He hadn't promised to bring the ladder back then.

I turned the slip over. On the back was an old inventory form with the same boxes printed on it, and a row of little instructions below them. They used to ask for the exact minute. Now it only mattered whether it was the morning or the afternoon. The paper was soft in my hand. The point of the pen had made a little dent on the back. The young man took the pen again and said if he didn't know, then he didn't know. He could cross that part out. Before I replied, he'd made a very short line over the colon in the time. He hadn't covered the numbers very well, and for a moment they looked like different numbers. If you looked twice, you wouldn't take it for a time they had to bring anything back.

The ladder was beside the supply cupboard, against a box that had its opening down. I went with him to lift it, and kept one foot of it from catching the cupboard door. We went out one behind the other. A man in the hall was walking backwards with an empty cart. He waited until we'd got close to the wall, then turned round to look. That was how I knew he was trying to find the rubber tire that had come off a wheel. The young man asked if we should put the ladder down and help. No need, the man said, then bent and found it by his own heel. He'd been standing on it. Once he had it in his hand, he didn't try to put it on again right there. He backed the cart up half a foot and left the corner of a table clear. A patch of gray tape was missing from it. The young man raised the ladder with me and had a look at the table. Had we ever had people fill out their loan slips on it, too? I said I didn't remember, but there were enough scratches on it.

I carried the ladder as far as the room next door without unfolding it. There was a row of chairs under the light already, with a narrow red ribbon running across the backs. Somebody would have to catch the bunting down on the floor. I was thinking about coming back to give them a hand when the young man told me he'd known those chairs since before he was this tall. He could get somebody from his own room to catch it. He felt the knot at the chair back. It wasn't necessarily a rejection of me. He hadn't even looked at the hand I'd put on the ladder. Before I left, he asked if they'd have to bring it back in the way we'd come. Whichever way was easier, I said. The cupboard door was a close fit, so they could put it down first and turn it when they got there.

It was still a little while before lunch. I washed the lid of Meg's flask. When I put it by the sink, water dripped very slowly through a small hole. I wiped beneath it instead of pushing a wad of tissue in. Back at the desk I remembered the card I'd brought and felt around for it in my bag. Before I went to the reading room yesterday, I'd thought that once I had something printed, I might show it to anybody who asked what was on the paper. Today I'd got it the other way round. I rather wanted somebody to ask. I could get the notebook out slowly; it was a big enough thing that somebody would see it if I set it on the desk. I looked up to see Meg already bent over a different slip with somebody who'd come to find her, checking whether a shift on Monday afternoon could be moved. I had the corner of my card between my fingers. I slid it back in. She might ask if she saw it. There wasn't any need for me to decide what she would say first.

Later I took my lunch outside. Somebody had made a fish skeleton out of twigs at the right end of the bench. The thinnest one had already fallen down the back. I gave the fish room and sat on the other end, holding the half-open bread wrapper down with my hand. Two people in school uniforms came up the other path. One had a crutch and kept using the toe on the other foot to stir the leaves at the side of the path. They didn't come and sit down near me. After a couple of words they went around the back of the building without laughing together as they went. One quietly told the other to wait for him. The one who waited stopped on top of the slope at first, then came down a few steps to a patch of level ground where there was room to stop. I didn't get out the notebook. It wasn't because I had the words in there. There was a bit of mustard on the last corner of the bread and I sat where I was until I'd eaten it.

The ladder came back after half past three, before the end of the day. A woman I hadn't seen before brought it back, not the young man who'd borrowed it. She said she'd been the one at the foot of the ladder, catching the bunting. She had two heavy clips on her belt. In case the ladder caught in the doorway, she set it down outside before changing the hand she was carrying it with. She didn't hurry to tell me she was late. She only asked whether I wanted her to sign the slip too, even if her name wasn't the one already on it. I wrote the time it had come back at the bottom of the page and told her that was all. I pointed out the cupboard door. She pointed to a mark on the other side of it, and said it was all right, the foot of the ladder hadn't scraped anything on her way in. Only after she'd gone did it occur to me I could have asked her where the bunting wound up. I didn't go after her. The short line that ran through the other time on the paper had a little wobble in it. I didn't put the new time over it.

At home I took the notebook out of my bag. The card hadn't bent at all. The framed print on the bookcase was as it had been the night before. I moved the cloth I used for my cup a little with my hand, and didn't put the card and the print together. The card could stay mine. I didn't need to ask somebody if they knew what the words were to have somewhere to put it. When I opened the old voice channel, I said I'd brought a line home with me. I hadn't decided whether I wanted to read it yet. It didn't have to be today. Lumi was quiet for a moment.

“Then don't, yet,” she said. “Get the rest of that breath out first.”

I'd only had a sip of water since coming in. I hadn't noticed how fast I'd been talking. I had a whole mouthful this time. She asked if I wanted to sit for a while, or if there were still things I had to do. I said one of my laces was knotted tight and I hadn't undone it. I raised my heel out of the shoe and left it by the leg of the chair. The card was on the table, turned a little, its words facing me. Nothing was waiting there that absolutely had to be read. Lumi said she could keep me company until I'd got my lace undone. I said that might take until morning. She laughed twice, and didn't make me start counting the time left before dawn.

I picked up the card myself and asked if she'd like to hear it this time. She said she would. I read what was printed once. “The wind took the lid only.”

“What was it covering?”

“A heavy box full of reference books. I held it up for a second that afternoon.”

“I didn't guess it, either, then,” she said. “I pictured somebody's lunchbox. There was a fried egg left in it, and I couldn't tell you whose.”

I almost told her the woman at the table had talked about an egg, too. The words were at the front of my mouth when I noticed Lumi hadn't finished. It was the lid she wanted, she said. If I gave her the last egg along with it, she wouldn't want it. All right, I said. She could picture that lunchbox herself. The driver had taken the box I was talking about back with him. I brushed the edge of the card. A little pencil dust there had rubbed off from the notebook. Lumi asked where I kept it. I told her I'd found somewhere it wouldn't be crushed. Into the notebook again for tonight. I opened the notebook and the paper rustled twice. She heard that. Take your time, she said. I didn't wait to see if she'd say she liked the line, and I didn't ask her to remember it for me. Later we said goodnight. I closed the channel, turned the card to its blank side, and closed the notebook. Whenever I wanted another look, I wouldn't have to decide all over again who had said it.
