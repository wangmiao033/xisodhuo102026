# Chapter 474: The Other Side of the Board

On Saturday morning the reading room's glass door was open just wide enough for one person. Only as I went in did I see that what was holding it open wasn't a wedge. It was a two-sided chalkboard. Marnie was cutting stiff paper at the far end of the counter and couldn't come over. A man I knew by sight was on his knees inside the door, gathering up bits of chalk with a folded newspaper. I stood on the threshold with a loaf I'd just bought. By my foot was a piece of pale blue chalk shorter than my little fingernail.

“Come on in,” the man said. “Don't step on the blue. The white's a lost cause.”

I put one foot on a tile without dust on it and brought the other in after it. He volunteered when things were going on in the inside room. Today he was taking turns with another volunteer at the secondhand book table inside. He said the chalk box had slipped and he'd kicked the board toward the door when he tried to catch it. Somebody was waiting to come in, and he still didn't want to look up.

“Do you want me to take the paper so you can get up?”

“What did you buy?”

“A loaf of bread.”

“Find somewhere for the bread first. I'm not the one who broke.”

I took the bag behind the counter and asked Marnie for a small dry cloth. She asked if we needed to rescue the yellow one. I told her I hadn't seen any yellow. She pointed at an illustration on the newspaper with the tips of her scissors and went back to her cutting. It was a sheet she'd put aside for wrapping change the month before. An advertisement showed a taxi, with the yellow going right down into the fold. I folded the cloth into a square. By the time I looked around the volunteer had got up at last, with one white knee.

We lifted the board a little farther from the door. A young woman came in carrying an illustrated book with a cover that curled up. She looked at the chalkboard and asked if the secondhand books had all been cleared away. They hadn't. There was supposed to be a sign, but in our hurry to move the board we'd lifted it upside down. There was a very long green tail at the bottom. The volunteer took a look at the book, handed it back and showed her where to find the person taking donations in the inside room. He stood back from the board. She hadn't gone far before she looked over her shoulder and smiled. It looked like a fish swimming upside down, she said.

After she'd gone inside, he stood the board the right way up against the wall and showed me that side of it. The fish's eye was crooked; there was a white dot on its lips. The tail went all the way down to the frame. Seen from here, there were only two lines of lettering, saying TURN OVER FOR TODAY'S SCHEDULE, and the fish took up most of the rest. Two children had helped with it yesterday, he said. One had left to get a book halfway through the picture, and the other had finished the lower half of the tail. Nobody had asked him to save it. Today he'd meant to wipe the entire side so he could put both the buying and the donations on the same face. The other face had once said LOOK INSIDE, but a folding leg had rubbed two of the letters off when the board was put away.

“Couldn't we leave the fish side?” I asked.

“Tell me where you'd put the new words.”

I reached up to see how much blank space it had. Set upright, there was only a strip just above the tail. I could have written two words there. Anything longer would go right through the fish's back. The volunteer didn't say yes. He took what was left of a stick of white chalk and measured it across his palm. “Somebody's going to look at this side and nothing else. They still need to know where to go in.”

We turned the board over. The note above the fish could stay. On the other face, besides the half-erased letters, there was an unfamiliar telephone number at the upper right, with only the last three digits left. I asked if it was the reading room's number. He didn't know. We shouldn't make somebody else's number into ours, he said. I pushed the remains of the old letters toward the frame with the dry cloth, then folded it over. The old writing was still faintly visible in the light. Did we need a damp cloth, he asked. He could try writing over it, I said, just not in too thin a line. He drew an arrow at the bottom first. From outside it didn't look much like a direction. It looked as if you'd turn in and have to double back. I held the frame while he rubbed it out and replaced it with a line of straight lettering: SECONDHAND BOOKS IN THE INSIDE ROOM. People could read it as soon as they'd come through the door. We didn't need to put up a separate sign outside.

Once it was out of the way, I set the piece of blue chalk in the tray. The volunteer picked it up and said it wasn't nearly enough to draw a fish. He waved the little piece toward me before he put it down again. I got the dust from around my shoes and folded the dirty half of the newspaper inward. Somebody at the sale table asked the price of a dictionary. The volunteer answered that somebody else knew instead of staying to help me count pieces of chalk. Marnie handed me my loaf. She'd rolled the bag shut, she said. The corner sticking out had brushed a few crumbs onto the papers on her counter. She hadn't put the loaf in a new bag. I opened it to check. The ring of hard crust on the top was still there.

I was there when the woman with the illustrated book came out of the inside room. She had two folded sheets in her hand instead of the book. As she went through the door, she pointed out the fish to her companion. It faced into the room now, so you saw it on your way out. She remembered it being the other way up and wanted to know why it had turned itself around. Her companion said a fish's head didn't have to be up to begin with. After they left I looked at it for a little while. The fish had just one eye. There was no need to give it another unless we meant to count the other face of the board as the other half of the fish. I took the same bag home and cut a slice across the loaf. The knife caught a little at the edge. When I rolled the paper down again, I didn't do it quite the way Marnie had.
