# Chapter 473: A Chair Out of Line

When I got to the reading room on Friday, Marnie was on her knees with half her body inside a cupboard. She was pushing a low stool out ahead of her. Every time it moved, the glass candy jar on top of the cupboard rang a little. I shifted the jar toward the middle. There were two candies inside, one yellow and one that had melted and set again in its clear wrapper, like an odd bit of ice.

“Don't sit down yet,” she said. “I don't know if that stool will hold you.”

“I wasn't going to.”

“Then tell me where you'd put the chair. I know the chair will hold.”

She meant the padded wooden one against the wall. There was a long window in the room with a strip of metal where the panes met. In a little while the afternoon sun would move slowly along the baseboard. That bit of light was the reason I'd wanted to stop for a minute when I came in to return my book. I pulled the chair out and turned it at an angle. It faced away from the row of tables, toward the dry cleaner's across the street. Half the letters in the sign had gone dark. The half that were still lit made it look less like a store than a small notice asking people not to get something dirty.

“Who's sitting there?” Marnie asked.

“Anybody.”

“And who's standing here?”

She was by the door, her arms folded. I looked at the space left between her and the back of the chair. A person could turn sideways and get through. A cart wouldn't. I'd come that way to return a book once and made a woman with both arms full move for me. She'd propped the books on her elbow and waited to put them down until I was out of her way. My hand was still on the chair. Without really deciding to, I started to put it back. With four chairs all in a row, nobody would have to tell me which one was wrong.

Marnie dragged the stool clear and worked a loose rung back and forth with her foot. “I'm asking if you can move it a little. I didn't say you have to face it the other way.”

I took two steps along the window. There was a space by the curtain where the stool had been. If I shoved the chair into it straight, the back would snag the curtain. I got down to untie the cord. Marnie said there was no hurry to untie it; that knot belonged to where the curtain had hung before. I ran my hand along the fabric and found it was easier to push the chair into the space from the inside than to pull from the door. I set the back at a slant, leaving a gap for the hem. Marnie didn't say if she liked it. She fetched the wide book cart from outside and pulled it into the room. One wheel of the empty cart slipped on the first tile. Then it went between the chair back and the door handle without touching anything else.

“Can you still see the street like you did before?” she asked.

I sat down to try. The window frame hid one more letter on the dry cleaner's sign, but not the door. Somebody brought a long coat out of the shop with the hook of the hanger wrapped in their hand. The wind blew the plastic cover up against their face. They didn't hurry to fix it. They held the door for somebody else first. I drew a foot out from under the chair and turned my knees a little toward the window. “Yes. You can see who's coming, too. If this were under a table, the only one who couldn't see somebody coming in would be the person with their back to the door.”

“Don't go writing out what the chair's for,” Marnie said. “I only asked if you could see the street.”

She picked up the stool and took it toward the inside door. I laughed a little too soon. I still had the tiredness from work hanging on me. I started to take the stool from her and stopped just as she crossed the threshold, seeing that she was holding it with the loose rung out, neither end about to drop. When she came back, she asked if I wanted water. I did. I put my book on the counter. It was an old travel book by a writer whose other book I'd bought before I'd started working. I remembered pushing that one into a friend's hands and opening it at a page I'd folded before he could look at the cover himself. None of the corners of this book were folded. Marnie turned to the pocket at the back and shook out an old address label. There was a number on the back of it.

“Did you put this in?” she asked.

I shook my head. We both looked at it and neither of us worked out what the person who'd written it was counting. She didn't throw it away. She put it under the jar with the number facing down. Outside a car turned and washed the glass white for a moment. Someone who'd come to return a book asked if he could wait for his companion. Remembering what Marnie had said, I only told him there was a chair by the window. He tested the seat with his bag, then gathered it into his lap and sat down. He sat sideways, with one hand on the chair back. Instead of looking out the window as I had, he could see the inside doorway. A few minutes later the person he'd been waiting for came out of that room, not in from the street.

On the way home I didn't put the story of the chair in order in my head. I bought a carrot. The one on top was thin and I put it back and took the short, stout one next to it. The person selling it said he was making soup himself that day and wouldn't peel his. I hadn't bought the carrot to make his soup, but I did ask what he used to get the dirt off. A little brush, he said. Two rows of bristles were missing from his. I paid and stood for a moment with the paper bag in a crowded stretch of sidewalk while somebody finished tying their shoe. The Friday bus came early. I didn't sit by the window and there was no book in my hand that I'd have liked one more page of.

I used half the carrot for dinner. I covered the rest with a saucer on the cutting board and put it in the refrigerator after washing the pot. Before I turned out the light, I saw that the chair I'd had for years wasn't straight at the table either. There was an old scratch between its legs and whenever I cleaned the floor I put the front feet at either end of it. This time I left the chair slightly off. The place got cooler at night; I got a jacket from the passage and laid it over the back. Neither sleeve reached the floor.
