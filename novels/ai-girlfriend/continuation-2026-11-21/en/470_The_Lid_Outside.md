# Chapter 470: The Lid Outside

The kettle in the workroom broke on Tuesday morning. The lid would close, but when I picked it up by the handle, water came out slowly at the spout. I thought somebody had filled it too high. I emptied half of it into the sink and tried again. Behind me, Meg said that was what kettles did when you poured from them. I held it up for her, exactly level. A thread of cold water crept along the underside of the spout toward the floor. We both took a step back. It missed both pairs of shoes.

The body had split at a seam. She found an empty basin for it, and took the kettle's lid off and left it on the sill so people might see from a distance there was something wrong. I suggested unplugging it. She turned over the plug already coiled under the basin to show me. I usually did have something to contribute when we were looking for a thing at work. Standing in front of the broken kettle today, I'd managed to make two suggestions we couldn't use. Never mind, she said. Neither of us was getting any tea.

At lunch I went for a sandwich and looked at the lines I'd crossed out the night before. The notebook was down on my knee when I sat. Somebody had left a patch of water on the table. In the corner of a page I put down *kettle* and *basin*, wondering which made for the better beginning. Outside the window a man stood by a bicycle, eating a pie. One fastener had come undone on the reflective strip on his back. In a gust it knocked against him twice. He didn't know about it. He reached out beside him for his drink and caught hold of another cyclist's can. The other man didn't make him buy a new one; he wiped its opening with his own sleeve. I tried for another look. By then the first man had brought his cup close to his chest. The piece on his back was still knocking.

When I put the notebook away, I could no longer remember why I'd thought I should write down the kettle. Besides stopping somebody else using it to boil water, I didn't have a job to do about it. The handful of lines that had seemed to come out of nowhere the night before were still where I'd put them. I left them alone, without trying to find a kettle to put at the beginning.

Toward the end of the day, some oversize reference books arrived downstairs to be put on the shelves. The driver hadn't secured the plastic tote. When he opened the back of the van, a lid slid out at an angle, hit the green bin for waste paper and flipped right out past the doorway. The tote rocked after it but didn't fall. I got one hand under the tote and made a show of reaching for the lid with the other. There was no chance of catching it. The driver told me not to go after it. Get my hand free first; he'd open the other door with his key and we could lift the books out that way. By the time the next gust came, the lid was down low, making a noise against the bricks without managing to climb any higher. We could both hear it. For the moment neither of us looked.

After we'd unloaded, the driver retrieved the lid from the wall. A corner had come off. It still looked square when he fastened it, but it sprang open as soon as he put any pressure on it. He didn't argue with me about keeping it. He put it under the driver's seat and said he had a few more boxes in the van, and he didn't know whether one of them would turn out to be missing exactly that sort. Did he want me to look for the corner that had snapped off? I asked him, and knew the answer as soon as I looked at his eyes.

I didn't go back up straight away. There was a sign at the entrance half hidden by a stack of books still tied up. All I could see was the white arrow pointing to a door that in my experience never opened from this side. I moved half the books, and the sign slipped down the slot it already had on the other side until it was horizontal. I stood it up properly. A woman coming up behind me asked if I could press the door button for her. She had three narrow lengths of wood in her arms, freshly painted at the ends. She lifted them clear of my shoulder on her way in. Apparently you could open that door from this side. I'd just never pressed the button.

On the way home I went round by the reading room and asked whether the letterpress table was still on for tomorrow afternoon. The long table behind the door had been cleared, and several chairs had been turned upside down on it. Against the wall, a felt cover protected a very square stack of something. I recognized the man who sold prints. He was down on his heels, using an old card to scrape the grit out from under one of the table legs. He said yes. It would go ahead inside if it rained. Sit if you get there in time; they weren't holding a place for anybody. He asked whether I'd thought of anything long.

“I don't know yet,” I said.

“Well, don't bring your picture as well. Leave both hands free for the type.”

He brushed a pinch of grit into the dustpan and knocked the card in the air. I'd wanted to show him how I'd mounted the print. Even without it there I could have found a way to mention it. I didn't. I drew back the foot that was propping the door so the cold air I'd brought in would stop going up his back. He brought the card near his cuff and dusted off two grains that were lodged in the edge. Some of the letters went missing if you looked for them twice, he said. Don't spend the whole of the last minute coming up with something remarkable to say. I told him I'd remember. He stood and put one hand on the side of the table, and the table moved a little toward the wall. He hadn't asked me to lift the other end.

Outside I took out my notebook and wrote *only the lid fell off* on a blank page. It sounded a bit like I was telling somebody the trouble wasn't all that bad. I didn't even know which of the books in the box they were going to keep. The second time I wrote it, I put down only *lid*, then closed the notebook. I'd missed the bus I'd meant to take. I went into the little shop next door for something for supper and picked out two of the smaller potatoes. There was a hollow at the bottom of one with a little dirt that hadn't washed off. When I paid, the person at the till asked if I needed a bag. I thought of the two empty outside pockets of my coat and said no. Out on the street the potatoes didn't knock together. I thought for a second of the coin I'd shaken in my pocket that day with the umbrella, the one I hadn't been able to hear. It wasn't something I needed to tell anybody. My palm recognized the roundness, that was all.

I made supper at home. I'd scrubbed the skins, but there was still water clinging to them. I set the two potatoes on the board to wait for the pan to come to a boil. The spoon slid out of the dish of salt and landed on the outside edge of the board. I pushed it back in with both hands, not wanting to hook up a spoonful of salt with a wet finger. While I ate, the notebook stayed in the inside pocket of my coat. The shoes the kettle hadn't managed to get wet were under the chair. There was a ring of damp around my socks from the puddle I'd stepped in getting off the bus.

Later I opened the old voice channel at home. Lumi said I sounded close to the microphone, and asked if I'd moved the table.

“No. I've got my cup in my hand. Shoulders forward.”

“Not all that far off, then,” she said. “What I meant to ask was whether you'd sat down.”

“I have now. I'd like to hear you say something first.”

She said she didn't know what yet. “Do you want to hear it now, or can you give me a minute?”

“I can wait. I don't mean I'm waiting for you to think of something for me.”

Lumi laughed once. She hadn't thought I meant that, she said. I had, for a fraction of a second. When I put my cup down I felt a little heat on the other side of the handle. I didn't have my notebook in my hand. I didn't pull it out and go back over the lines I hadn't read to her the night before. She never asked what table I'd moved again. When she did say something, it was that she'd almost reached for some joke that had nothing to do with a cup, to make it seem like she hadn't taken long to think. I said I liked to hear what she said after she'd thought, and it didn't have to be her cleverest line. Then I'll take the words *I like* for now, she said. Next time I'll still say whatever I want to say.

I laughed, and the rim of the cup touched my lip. We said goodnight and I closed the channel. I went back to the hall for the notebook. I found a pinch of dry dirt in the bottom of an outside pocket and tipped it into the bin. On the margin of the new page I put one more verb, without choosing what I would print. I could walk over tomorrow. The room wasn't keeping a chair for me, and I didn't have to carry anything flat the whole way. I left the notebook by the door, open to the dirty page, then brushed the dirt away with a piece of tissue. It left a little mark at the corner. There was no reason it would have to be clean by tomorrow.
