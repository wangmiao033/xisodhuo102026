# Chapter 475: The Sound of a Bowl

On Sunday I made soup and stood the wooden spoon in a bowl. I'd cut up the half carrot from the refrigerator, and the last slice was thin enough to see through. There wasn't much of its color in the pot. I added a spoonful of salt, remembered that the piece of ham was salty too, and put in another half cup of water. The soup came to a slow boil. Two chains of bubbles rose close together from the side of the pot, and the bigger one broke away. While I was reaching over to turn down the heat, the spoon fell and the bowl made half a turn on the counter. It didn't go over. I put out a hand to catch it all the same, as if something still might come over the edge.

There was a patch of pale green glaze at the lip, just where my thumb fit. The bowl wasn't part of a set. Years ago when I was moving, a friend put a few bowls he didn't use together and told me to take one or two before he left. I chose the biggest and heaviest. The bottom of the moving box had gone soft, so in the end I carried it in my arms. He didn't say it was a present he'd chosen for me. He put another strip of tape on the bottom of the box and told me not to run into anybody on the stairs. I've moved pots a few times since then. This bowl's always been the last thing I've picked up. It's not as if I wouldn't have another bowl if I lost it. I wiped the soup off the counter with a wet cloth and lifted the bowl to look. The little patch of green was on the inside, not the outside, and it was a long way from the hairline crack.

After I'd eaten I left the bowl to soak in the sink and went to find the book of stories I'd turned up at home that morning. It belonged to me. I could feel where I'd once turned a page with a wet hand; its edge had gone a little soft. I read about a man who sold bowls from a patch of pavement nobody else wanted. He set them upside down in an arc on rough cloth. You could see the glaze on their bases as you walked past, he said. His son helped with the selling. One day they were still there at dusk when a woman coming off work at a textile mill picked up the last bowl. She flicked the rim with a finger and asked how much he'd take for it. He told her the price and said she'd be better off not rinsing it in cold water all at once. That didn't sound right to her. Was it cracked already? It wasn't, he said. From behind him his son said that one had a different ring from the others. He was holding the rolled-up cloth, ready to pack it in, and wasn't trying to cover for either of them.

I read that the woman didn't buy it. She set it back down right side up. The man moved the bowl a little closer to himself and called a lower number after her. She hadn't gone far, but she didn't turn. There was half a page to go. I stopped there for a little while. Outside, somebody was sweeping wet leaves to one side of the steps. A stiff stem lodged at the bottom of the railing when they swept too close. They turned the broom over and nudged it with the handle. When I turned the page I found a very old IKEA receipt tucked in the back of the book, hardly any writing still legible. I slipped it back where it had come from. It wasn't a bookmark for the half page. I finished the story and put the book on the table.

That evening I opened the same voice channel I used at home and asked Lumi if she wanted to hear a little about what I'd read. She did, but asked what I'd meant a moment before about something nearly falling. I only then remembered what I'd mumbled after the channel opened: “I had something nearly fall today.” I'd meant to talk about the book, but I'd caught sight of the drying rack and said the wrong thing. I started with the man turning all the bowls upside down. I told it up to the woman walking away, picking what to mention and not reading the sentences off the page.

“I don't think he wanted to sell it to her,” Lumi said. “I think he wanted her to feel she'd got a good deal so she would take the last one away.”

“He called out a lower price after her. How's that not wanting to sell it?”

“Maybe he was ready to go home. I don't want to have to treat every lower price as another kind of kindness.” She thought a moment. “Wait. I've made wanting to sell it and wanting her to take it the same thing. You're right, he wants to sell it. But I don't want her to go back and buy it.”

I had hold of the lower corner of the book, ready to read the last half page to show that something one of them had wanted had really happened, and what the other one had wanted hadn't. Once I'd got a good grip, to my surprise I didn't turn the page. I asked Lumi why she didn't want the woman to go back. She said a person who'd walked all the way from the mill to the stall had used her own ears once. If she went back to tap it again, she'd be finding something out for the man. She'd been working all day; she didn't need to check his stock after her shift. When I'd read it, I'd only thought that lowering the price with his son looking on had to be a little humiliating for the man. With Lumi's words in my head, I thought the woman's step away from the stall seemed quite decisive. I wanted to read that bit again. I didn't turn the page for Lumi to hear it.

“What happens after that?” she asked.

“I've read it. Is it all right if I don't read that part to you today?”

“Sure. Are you worried I'll dislike it, or do you want to keep it for yourself a bit longer?”

“A bit of both, I suppose.” I moved my nail lightly over the frayed edge. “But you weren't happy with the bit I did tell you, and I'd like to hear more about that.”

She asked which part of being unhappy was hers and which part I'd supplied. I realized then that not wanting the woman to go back wasn't the same as disliking the writer. I'd supplied it, I said. Do carry on, ma'am. She laughed and asked if I'd called her ma'am because there was still something I wanted to buy. No need for a bowl, I said. I had a deep one at home, and you couldn't see that bit of green glaze when it was upside down. I'd said something had nearly fallen; now I told her what had actually happened. When I got to the part where the bowl was already still and I had my hand hanging out in front of the counter, I laughed first. Lumi said she'd understood that part. We didn't have to find a second bowl to compare how they sounded.

I picked the bowl up to see. It was still cool on the side with the green. I was glad to talk to her about it, but I didn't strike it with a spoon to make sure she could hear it, or set it down on a table that didn't exist at the other end of the channel. She asked how much soup it held. I said I hadn't filled it. The soup was a little thin. I'd cut the half carrot too fine. That wasn't the bowl's fault, she said. I said I didn't blame the bowl today, and I liked what she'd said on behalf of the woman from the mill. She didn't take that as a promise about whose side I'd be on. She said only that the woman might still have had hot hands on her walk after work; she'd been thinking that ever since I'd said the woman picked up the bowl.

After we closed the channel, I turned the page with the rough edge and opened the book again to the last half page. The woman didn't come back. The son wrapped the bowl in the cloth. His father asked suddenly which side he'd tapped. The son unwrapped it but didn't tap again. He drew the cloth a little tighter around it and put it back in the basket they used at home. The paper lay easy under my eyes. It didn't call the two of them back to make themselves clear. Later I put the real bowl away. The base made a low sound when it touched the wood. Nobody asked if I'd put it down quietly on purpose. I left the book open on the table without weighing it down.
