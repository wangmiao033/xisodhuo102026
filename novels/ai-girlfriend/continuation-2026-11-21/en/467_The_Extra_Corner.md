# Chapter 467: The Extra Corner

The Saturday sun came around the roof across the street without much strength left by the time it reached my window. The print had lain there all night. The bottom right corner was standing up, though no higher than it had the night before. I found an old frame in the drawer and took out the faded blue-gray mountain. It had come with the frame. I'd hung it for nearly a year once, mostly because I was too lazy to find something else. A very small price was printed below the mountain. I couldn't tell whether it was meant to be read standing upright or upside down. All I could make out was a nine.

The riverside print was a little wider than the mountain. I tried shifting it down and to the left behind the glass. That hid the white crease behind the bottom of the frame, but took the bricks to the left of the bridge with it. A stretch of railing looked sawed off. When I moved the print back, the bent corner pushed up against the glass. I supported it from behind and pressed with a fingertip. The paper flicked, and for a second it was as flat as the mountain in my hand. As soon as I let go, the corner lifted again. I laid the frame down and supported the print as I slid it out. The other three corners were straight. The fourth wouldn't quite open out, and looked almost like an extra one.

I went out with a strip of paper the width of the print. I didn't take the print itself. Taking something I'd just got dry back to the same street didn't sound like an idea I'd had myself. On the doormat downstairs there was one wet footprint already worn down to the heel. Outside it wasn't raining. I passed the bus stop from the opposite direction to the one I normally took when I got off. Coming round the back of a bus in broad daylight, I saw the empty bench on its other side. There was a tuft of dead grass underneath it and a bright stud earring right at the end. I was going to pick it up when a woman carrying two bags stooped to feel around by the leg of the bench. I pointed instead of touching it. She was looking for her car keys, she said, and did I mind if she took the earring? I hadn't picked it up, I said. That was for her to decide. She had a look at me, laughed once, and found her keys looped around the handle of one of her bags. The earring stayed under the bench.

The folding table wasn't where it had been on Thursday. Two shop fronts farther along, the man who sold prints sat on a step with one shoe on a table leg to keep it from rocking. He recognized me and asked whether the paper was still wet.

“It's dry. I pressed it once after it dried.”

“Did it work?”

“The paper's dry,” I said. “The crease is still there.”

“I meant, did it dry?”

He put his foot down and the right side of the table dropped a little. Just as I went to help, he put his foot back. An envelope folded twice was tucked under the leg. He saw me looking and said once it reached him the envelope didn't have another job. I didn't ask who it was from.

A man half a table away from me was trying to choose between two river scenes of about the same size. Neither was the one I had. In one, the bridge didn't make it halfway across the picture. The other had the tall side of a boat in the foreground. The man asked which view was worth going out of his way to see. The seller said it depended where he was coming from. The man actually told him his route. They traced two routes with their fingers on the table and agreed, in the end, that he shouldn't go back across the bridge after work. He didn't buy either print. As he left he nudged the envelope back under the table leg with his shoe. The table stopped rocking. The seller looked down and called thank you after him.

When I could get up to the table, I laid down my strip of paper. I said I wanted a bigger frame and didn't want the glass to press on the print. He drew a clean piece of board from behind the smallest picture on the table and held it up for me. He hadn't cut the block for the river scene, he said, so he couldn't tell me whether the person who had would want it square. But the sheet of paper was mine. I didn't need to ask anybody which way to put it. He stuck four paper photo corners to his sample board, leaving about a nail's width of room in the one farthest to the right, and slid the smallest sample print from the table into them. The corners themselves were attached; the sample print wasn't. He stood the board up a little while. Nothing moved at the bottom. The glass still needed its own clearance. I said I had another, deeper frame at home, an empty one and would have to measure it. Measure before cutting anything, he said; if I didn't have what I needed, a frame shop would do. I didn't need to ruin a print or make a special trip back to him. He had a few sheets of ordinary heavy card under his table. He let me pick one, and I paid for the card. I could take four paper corners, too. He had several more for the sample board anyway.

“I won't take money for fixing it,” he said. “I haven't fixed anything.”

“I didn't bring it for you to fix.”

“That works out nicely, then.” We each turned something over in our hands without touching.

While looking for the corners he'd turned up a stack of wide white strips. One had a slight bow in it and two square ends. He started to show me the width, then stopped and turned the strip blank side up as he handed it to me. I could write down the size to cut the card on the strip I'd brought myself, he said. The white strip wasn't for framing. It was a guide to the size they'd be using at a little letterpress demonstration on Wednesday at the old reading room. People would set a short line a little at a time and pull one impression. It was already somebody's event; he was going along to help. I didn't need to watch his table for him. If I wanted to try, I could bring a short line. Twenty characters with spaces made things easier. The white paper was offcuts. They wouldn't be taking commissions for cards. He pulled the other stack in a little to keep the wind from getting under it.

“I don't know how to set type.”

“I didn't tell you to set it at home.” He pointed to the blank side of the strip. “An idea will do. If you get there and don't feel like printing, you can watch.”

I asked if I could think of something at the last minute. Of course, he said. He didn't ask who I meant to write it for. I held the board against my chest and carried the guide in my fingertips as I walked a little way with the wind at my side. A few steps from the table I stopped and turned the outer face of it back towards me. There was nothing written there. That little curve I'd noticed only showed again when I eased my grip. It wasn't a form waiting for an answer. In my hand it really was a different thing from the strip I'd brought out to measure the print.

At home I measured the empty frame. There was enough depth, but a little had to come off the card. I held the ruler firmly down. The first pass of the blade didn't go through. The second found the same groove, and the edge came free. I fitted the four corners, keeping the paper off the glass and leaving a little room on the right. I slid the old mountain back into its empty paper sleeve, upside-down nine and all. I set the framed river on the inside end of the bookcase where I could see the bit of railing I'd almost hidden. Two steps back, the crease might have been there or not. Close up it was still behind the glass, where nothing had rubbed it out.

I passed the bookcase later without stopping. I thought about opening the voice channel to say something. Then I picked up my cup and realized I was thirsty. I poured some water and sat and drank it until there was only a little left in the bottom. I didn't open the channel that day. I put the guide under my notebook with just the white end showing. Before I turned off the lamp I took the cup away. There was no second corner on the table waiting to be cut off.
