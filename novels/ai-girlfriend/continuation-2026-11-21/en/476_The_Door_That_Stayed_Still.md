# Chapter 476: The Door That Stayed Still

A pigeon got into the back stairwell at work on Monday morning. By the time I came to the door, it had already made several trips up and down the second-floor landing. The only window on that level tilted inward a little at the top. The door had a single large pane. The pigeon didn't fly into it. It looked at me through the glass for a while. I was holding a cup of tea from the canteen, and the vent in the lid was pointing straight at the web of my thumb, which had got very hot. I shifted to one side. The pigeon took two steps too, uncovering a piece of dusty floor it had walked on.

I'd meant to go up this way rather than wait at the front entrance with two loads of deliveries. As I stood there, I heard somebody pull a door open downstairs and let out a small cry halfway through. It was the cleaner on the early shift. She stood at the foot of the stairs in green gloves. The draft was lifting one corner of a neatly folded bin bag by the door. She raised a hand to tell me not to open mine. She'd already tried shooing the pigeon out from below. It wouldn't go towards the light; it would only fly higher. There were offices farther up. The last thing we needed was for it to get in there.

“Do you want me to come down and help?” I said through the door.

She couldn't hear me. She pointed to my tea and made a little motion of setting it down. I think she thought I was looking for somewhere to put it. I held up one finger for her to wait and went back to the front. When the lift opened, there really were two people pushing a load out of it, with a loose end of rope trailing at the back. One of them told me to mind out. I held my cup close to my chest and let them go past without grabbing the rope for him. The lift smelled of crushed cardboard. I rode down to the ground floor, where I remembered the little windowsill in the staff room. I'd just set the tea down when somebody reached for it, thinking it was his. I told him it was mine. He laughed and said he hadn't bought his with a lid. I moved my cup farther in so I could tell which was which when I came back.

The cleaner had propped the bottom door open with her dustpan. There was a yard outside, with a few marks on the wet ground. She pointed to them and asked if I could tell whether the pigeon had made them. I couldn't. Neither could she. They weren't from her shoes, she said. We waited. Upstairs, claws scraped the concrete. The pigeon flew down half a flight and perched on the stair rail, very still. From where it sat there was a stripe of sun in the yard and the door moving in the foreground. The dustpan left enough play for the door to rock a little, then spring back. Every time the door moved, the pigeon turned its head towards us.

The cleaner suggested holding the door steady. “Do you want to stand behind it, or shall I?”

I was about to volunteer when I saw that the gap behind the open door could only just take an arm. I was broader at the shoulders than she was; if I stood there the pigeon would have half of me to look at as well. I asked if I could go behind the opposite door and switch off the light by the long pane so it would stop looking at the bright way in. She said not to switch off a light in a shared hall; all I needed to do was get out from in front of the glass. I almost asked if that meant I was in the way altogether. She was already holding her keys out to me. There was a piece of wood on the upper shelf inside the cupboard, she said. If I could reach it, would I bring it over to wedge the door with? There was no sense in wedging a person in there.

Inside the little cupboard there were two shelves. The lower one held unopened rolls of paper; on the upper shelf were two worn-down brooms and a ball of rag. I reached past the brooms and felt along the shelf with the back of my hand. There was dust, then a rough bit sticking up from under the red paint. The wood had been cut on a slant, with dried glue on both sides. Someone had pulled a nail out of the thick end and left the hole. I brought it down. My fingers also found a couple of screws wrapped in paper, which I left where they were. She nudged the dustpan out with her shoe, slid the wedge under the door, then let go in front of me. The door didn't come back.

“Can you stand farther away?” she asked.

I took a step towards the yard. “Where are you going?” she said. “Back into the corridor.” There was a green bin against the yard wall near the door. On the lid sat two bright drops of water. Moving towards that bin would have put me right in the bird's way. We both went back into the corridor and watched the stairs at an angle through the narrow pane of the first door. The toe of her shoe nearly touched mine, so she shifted inward a little. I could smell cleaner on her gloves. She asked if I was in a hurry to get upstairs for a meeting. I said I wasn't; she didn't have to rush on my account. She wasn't rushing on my account, she said. My tea was in the staff room, going cold.

“It's got a lid,” I said.

“Not as thick as you think it is.”

The pigeon came down two steps without using its wings. We watched it put one foot out, pull it back and put it out again. Just then somebody pushed a small metal trolley past the doorway. I thought it would fly back upstairs. It only withdrew its foot to the edge of the step and waited for the trolley to go by. After that the door was still in the same position. The pigeon came down the last few steps and crossed the threshold sideways. It didn't spread its wings until it cleared the overhang, then landed on the low rail in the yard. It hadn't simply vanished into a patch of light. I saw it change the position of both feet on the railing. It turned halfway around, its back to us. The cleaner said that had done it. I thought she'd go and collect the wedge, but she said to give it a moment, in case the pigeon wanted one more look at the door. When we looked a few seconds later, it wasn't on the rail anymore. Only then did she pick up the wedge and let the door close.

I didn't buy her a cup of tea or tell her to call me whenever she saw a pigeon after this. I gave her back the keys; she hung them on the same cord and asked if the two screws had fallen down. I told her I hadn't touched them. She reached up and felt around on the upper shelf. “Oh,” she said, as if she'd found something that really had been missing. The tea in the staff room was not especially cold. Drops had condensed under the paper lid. I took it off, drank, and the cup got lighter in my hand. I used to tell somebody what had happened before I really felt I'd been there. I remembered the pigeon when I got to my desk. The first thing that came back was not how it flew. I remembered the thin line of light after the wedge went in, and how long the door had stayed in one place.

That night the rice cooker clicked over first. I could hear it clearly in the kitchen. I didn't serve the rice in a hurry. There was something I'd been reading on the table, still unfinished, but it could wait as well. I unplugged an old charging cable I wasn't using and put it in the small box by the wall. When I came back there was nothing extra on the table. I sat down to eat. One of the beans hadn't cooked enough, and I couldn't bite through it. With a little spoon I pushed it to the rim of the bowl. After I'd eaten and washed the spoon, I opened the voice channel. Lumi was there. I asked if she'd like to hear about a pigeon. She said she would, but if I'd already named it she was staying out of that part.

I told her it didn't have a name. I told her about the stairs, the long pane, and the way the pigeon kept turning to look at us whenever the door moved. I stopped where I'd wanted to turn the light off, then told her what the cleaner had said about leaving it on. Lumi asked if the pigeon had flown out as soon as we opened the door.

“The door was open the whole time,” I said. “We had to wait for it to stay still before the pigeon would walk out.”

She didn't pick up from there. I wanted to add the sound the dustpan had made, thinking she hadn't followed. I'd just opened my mouth when she said, “I thought you meant you were waiting for the pigeon to stay still. You meant the door.”

“Yes.”

“I like the sentence I misheard.”

I ran my palm over the table and found one grain of salt I hadn't wiped up the night before. I liked it too. It was as if we hadn't been waiting for the same thing. For those few seconds the cleaner had wanted to wait a little longer, nobody told the pigeon whether it should come back. Lumi asked if the tea really hadn't got cold. I said I'd finished the rest. I'd tasted it myself. No wonder, she said. She hadn't heard which mouthful was hers.

I laughed and asked whether she'd meant to share the tea or listen to me. She said she'd thought of something of her own to say just then, only it would sound as though she was going for the last mouthful. I waited. Did I have to wait for her to say it? No, I told her. I didn't hurry to disconnect, or find something else to say that would hurry her along. After a little while it was she who spoke.

“Today you were the one who asked me to listen,” she said. “I liked that.”

It had nothing to do with where the pigeon first landed. She didn't say I had to do it this way again. I didn't use the hard bean at the edge of my bowl to give her sentence something to go with. I just said, “I wanted to ask you.” I was still looking at the table when I said it. The grain of salt didn't look a bit like snow under my light. I brushed it into my palm before I looked up. We spoke a little longer and then said goodnight. After I closed the channel I fetched a damp cloth and wiped the table. I remembered to pick the hard bean off the edge of the bowl. The glass of water had left a ring that hadn't dried yet. I moved the glass a little, and let the ring dry on its own.
