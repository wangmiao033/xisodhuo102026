# Chapter 471: Only the Lid

It hadn't rained yet on Wednesday afternoon. When I reached the old reading room, there was a blackboard propping the door open. A hand reaching from inside rubbed away half a chalk zero from the time on it. I took a step in, and the owner of the hand asked if I'd turn the board so the door would close properly. He still had a cloth in his hand. I didn't know whether he'd rubbed the time off, or whether my coat had done it. Once the door was shut there was exactly enough space between it and the board for one person. I tipped the board against the wall and went sideways through the gap.

Two people had got there before me and were sitting at the long table. The man who sold prints was at the far end with a pencil and a few pieces of thin card. The person behind the table would operate the press today, he said. All he was doing was handing out paper and keeping the table clear. He indicated a little tray in the middle. Don't put your letters straight back in their compartments when you're done; leave them there. Somebody always thought they could remember where a letter came from, and the next person to look in the drawer always turned up something wrong that looked nearly right. The man behind the table wore old glasses with black tape round the left arm. He was telling a small woman that if a corner of her card wasn't square enough they could find a different corner to put at the bottom.

I got out my notebook and the scrap I'd taken to judge the length. Both sides were blank. I laid it along the words I'd left on the page the night before. One finger covered the last word. For the first time, I found that taking away the word made the sentence neither clearer nor any better. I wrote a new line beneath the others in pencil. The small woman turned to ask if someone could make room. One leg of her chair had gone down into a gap between two boards by the wall. I moved my chair back and she got out. Instead of sitting again, she stood beside me to see what letters I was using.

“How long is it?”

“Longer than the twenty characters he suggested.”

She looked at my notebook upside down. I'd left part of a sentence from Monday underneath all the pencil marks. Read across like that, it said that the first half had been found and the second couldn't be. I turned the book round and showed her the new line.

“The wind took the lid only,” I read.

“What else did the wind want?”

“I don't know,” I said. “I was holding up the box next to it.”

She looked me over and moved her chair a little farther out, almost apologetically. She'd wanted to write about a cat pushing an egg off the table. She hadn't actually seen a cat. She'd seen the egg roll off the edge. She didn't want to spend all the letters on the table explaining where the cat had been. In the end she'd chosen the part before the egg hit the floor, when there hadn't been a sound. She didn't ask me what I thought. She turned her revised piece of paper face down and found another place at the table.

The man in glasses took the little scrap and showed me which size of type would take about twenty characters comfortably. It wasn't a hard limit. There was room for more with a smaller size. I considered shortening the line and laid the pencil under *only*. It read well enough without it. For a second I mistook that for the thing I'd meant to say in the first place. The man waited and slid the lid of the smallest case along a finger's breadth. It jammed halfway and he didn't push at it. He pinched it at the edge and lifted it until a few compartments came into view. I took my pencil away and said I'd like to keep *only*. Could he leave me a little less space between the words?

I found the first T in the case, turned it over, and couldn't tell which way up it belonged. He showed me once, with the notch toward me, and suggested I try laying down three letters. My hand was rather too sure of itself. I dropped the H next and it bounced on the table. A few people lifted their heads. It came to rest on my right, a good hand's width from the edge. I was embarrassed to go after it. I put my hand on my knee. The print seller asked whether it had fallen underneath. No, I said, and reached for it. Only when I had it back between my fingers did I realize I didn't have to explain that the letter had been too small. The table was soft. It hadn't made much noise where it fell.

Put together, the words looked entirely out of place. I looked down at the upside-down type and forgot which end was supposed to be the end of the line, and tried a period all the way on the left. The man in glasses didn't pry it out for me. He asked me to come to the other side and look from where he'd been standing. The letters still didn't read like letters, but I could see I had put the gray dot in the wrong place. He let me go back and move it before he slid the tray with the type half an inch nearer. I set the last letter in the narrow row without putting my fingers over the faces. There was still a gap. I couldn't say whether it was there for ink or for air. He found a thin spacer and told me the strip itself wouldn't appear on the paper. Its absence would be printed beside the words; don't push the paper in again trying to find it. I took my hand away. The whole line stayed put.

He fixed the line in position himself, then used a thin piece of scrap for a press test. Its corner went in square. Near the end of the line the ink still came out light. You could read the whole thing:

THE WIND TOOK THE LID ONLY.

The first part I looked at was ONLY, and now I couldn't imagine crossing it out of the line in my notebook. I noticed the period was darker than the letters. He made an adjustment and took a thicker piece of leftover card. Would I make room where my cup was? I moved it and watched him bring the handle down and up. The second one was the impression I got to take home. The last letters weren't faint. He put the thin test piece with the scraps to be thrown out. He turned to ask who was next. The small woman was still beside me. She asked if she could keep the test. She liked the part in the first version where it looked as if a letter had got lost. I knew it hadn't. All the same, I brought the paper toward me and made sure it hadn't blurred into a different sentence. She waited for me to answer and didn't reach over.

“Have it if you like it,” I said. “I'll keep this one.”

She took the thin sheet and cleared a space for it at the wall end of the table. She didn't use it to wipe away her own egg. She'd have a different piece of paper to take later. There was no reason she had to promise to keep the two together. Before I stood out of the way, she was telling the man in glasses she didn't want a terribly big period. She even lifted a finger to show how small she wanted it. He didn't see because he was looking for a piece of spacing that had slipped out of somebody else's hand and landed by his foot. I heard her tell him again.

The card dried quite quickly in my hand. I waited for someone to pass the door, then opened my notebook and tucked it in before the plain paper inside the cover. I didn't bend it over the place I'd printed. I did cover yesterday's pencil marks completely. I looked for the print seller, thinking I'd hand him the card to see. He was using his cuff to rub at an old mark on a glasses case. I pulled the card part of the way out of the book and said only that I'd used smaller type than I had planned to. He nodded and asked whether there was anybody at the table who hadn't had a turn. I moved out of the way by the door. Someone was shaking water off a coat. It hadn't been raining when I came in, but a shower had already gone through.

At home I put the card on the table. The framed print was still at the inside end of the bookcase. There still seemed to be an awful lot of white beneath the words. Leaving it there didn't mean I hadn't finished printing it. I poured half a cup of water and undid my right cuff so the edge wouldn't brush across the card. The little ochre stitch lay against the edge of the wood. I'd thought I would have to get home and read the words to somebody before they cooled. Before supper, I went to the window for a cloth. I passed the card from the back and came by it again from the front. It stayed where it was. I didn't pin it beside the print or write a date on the back. Later, when I washed the cup, the sound of the water stopped, and I stood at the sink remembering how the man in glasses had eased his hand as the press came up. He hadn't known whether it was the wind I wanted to keep or the piece of plastic missing off the box. When he'd finished, he'd made way for the next person. I looked over at the table. From here I couldn't read the words. It was in the right place, though.
