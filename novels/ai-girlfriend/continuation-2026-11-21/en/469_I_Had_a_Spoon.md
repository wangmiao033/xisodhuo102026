# Chapter 469: I Had a Spoon

On Monday at lunch I took the beans left over from yesterday to the little table by the stairs. When I lifted the lid, I found that a thin layer of oil had set around the edge. They were still cold. One end of the container was wider than the other. I'd always thought the reason to design a thing like that was so that, after you dropped it, you could tell at a glance which end had hit first. I got my spoon out, put the lid back, and went looking for somewhere to heat the beans.

There was a note by the microwave door asking people to remove their things if someone else was waiting. Underneath, somebody had added in blue pen: EVEN IF NO ONE IS WAITING. While I put my box in, a man from work stood with his head down cutting very thin slices off a pear. I'd expected him to have bread as well. On Mondays he usually brought a piece broken off at home, and once he got to the crust there were crumbs all over the floor. Today he hadn't. He gestured with the knife at the empty spot when I asked. He wanted to finish the pear first, he said. Otherwise he'd get home and run into the same pear again. That last bit was for himself. I waited for my box to turn where I could see it; through the glass, I saw that the white bit along the edge had melted.

I'd planned on only sitting for ten minutes or so. When I got back with the hot box, I set it where the sun reached the table. Half the lid lit up. I got the white guide strip and my notebook out of the bag. Nothing was written on the strip. On Saturday, when the man had said twenty characters, all I'd thought was that it left no time to get anything right. I pushed the strip away from the container so it wouldn't pick up oil. I thought I'd start with something that didn't sound quite right.

The first thing I came up with was I DIDN'T FORGET. Once I'd written it down, I thought it didn't sound like anything a person would say to you under ordinary circumstances. Somebody takes a piece of paper out of his bag, looks at you a couple of times, says he didn't forget, and then what? If you don't know what he didn't forget, all you can do is wait for him to get to the rest of it. I drew a line through the words. The ink went a little farther than the words did. I didn't want to use the twenty characters to settle something I owed somebody. Nobody had said I owed them anything. A person came down the stairs and crushed a dry leaf under a shoe. After they passed, I saw part of the window reflected in my spoon. It was upside down, all bundled up in that very small metal belly.

I'd only brought the spoon in case I couldn't find one. I'd already got to work before I remembered there were always teaspoons in the staff kitchen. If you opened the drawer you could hear the whole stack knocking about. But I'd brought my own. When I'd scooped up the last of the beans I could take it to get something to drink without asking where anything was. I wrote I HAD A SPOON. Literally true, of course. Put it on a good piece of pressed paper, though, and it sounded as if someone had told a witness to stop getting ahead of himself and explain where the spoon came from. I tried a little period at the end. Now somebody had come in to report it missing. I covered the period with a small square of ink and gave myself room to laugh. I'd left the spoon on the lid with a bit of it sticking out over the table. It moved when I breathed out. I took it by the handle and finished the beans.

When my colleague came downstairs he asked who I was writing something so short for. I said there was a little letterpress demonstration and I was making a list of letters I might use. He caught sight of the ink square. They might not have a ready-made little square for me, he said. Why did he think that? He'd had a go at cutting a stamp once, before he got married, he said. He had a page of letters all saying FROM THIS DAY FORWARD and had forgotten to leave room between the words. I forgot to ask who'd had use of the stamp in the end. He was blocking the stairs with his handful of pear, and somebody needed to get by. I washed the spoon. When I came back he'd only got one slice left and was eating it slowly. He probably wasn't going to run into the half he'd worried might still be there when he got home.

There was still a little while before I had to get back to work. I wrote three or four more lines. All of them went in the notebook; I didn't use the strip. In the last line I squeezed the date down and saved a blank space beside it. Funny thing: whichever line my eye went to first, the first thing I heard in my head was always the spoon. When I put away the pen, I still hadn't circled anything. I didn't need to hold the food box sideways to get it into the bag, either. A group of people had come down the stairs by then. The sun on the table had shrunk to the size of a little saucer for a spoon. I left it for whoever might sit there next.

After work I took the washed spoon to the kitchen to dry, and passed the bookcase without turning on the light. I opened the voice channel myself and told Lumi about Wednesday's letterpress demonstration at the old reading room. Then I asked if she wanted to hear a line I'd written and hadn't decided on. Did I want to know what she'd heard, she asked, or did I want to know whether I should use it?

“You can just listen first,” I said. “You don't have to decide. I don't know if this one's for printing.”

“I'll listen, then.”

I read the one about the spoon, plainly, one word at a time. She didn't pick up at the pause. When I'd finished, she said the first thing that had barged into her head wasn't a dinner table. It was a door with a security checkpoint. The person in front of her in line took a spoon out of his pocket and tossed it into the little tray. I laughed, because I'd started out imagining myself as the one being questioned. She said the spoon hit the tray and made a sound just a little louder than whatever the man was saying. Neither of us read the sentence out again. I really didn't know whether I still wanted to put it on paper. I hadn't said I wouldn't use it, either. She asked if I was still going on Wednesday. I said I'd probably go and look. I didn't know if I'd set anything. She said okay. She didn't ask where the room was or trade my sentence out for one that sounded right to her.

“Do you want to hear another one?” I asked after a while.

“Not today,” she said. “I still have the spoon in the tray.”

I'd already turned over to the second line and had my finger on it. I shut the notebook. I asked if she wanted to stay like this for half a minute. She said she needed to go first. But she liked that when I'd asked, I hadn't slipped the line in. I said all right. After she'd said goodbye I closed the voice channel. The real spoon was still standing in the kitchen with a drop of water below its handle. I took a dry cloth to it. Not because I couldn't stand the sight of the drop, or because there was more of myself I needed to write down. I just wanted to put the spoon away so I could find it if I wanted to take it tomorrow.
