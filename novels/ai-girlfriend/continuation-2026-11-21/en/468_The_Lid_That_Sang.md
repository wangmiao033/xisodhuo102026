# Chapter 468: The Lid That Sang

On Sunday afternoon I wanted stewed beans. The recipe said half an hour, without saying whether that meant half an hour from now or from when the beans agreed to soften. The night before I'd soaked the oldest bag in the refrigerator, the one tied shut with a washed bread tie. When I changed the water, two beans floated. I fished them out and set them on the cutting board. After a little while one split. The other just dripped water on the wood. Outside it was as bright as if somebody had washed the whole street in advance of a rainstorm. The print stood at the inside end of the bookcase. Its corner didn't look anything like the skin of a soaked bean. I took care to give it a little more room when I walked past with the pot.

I turned the heat down and got a plate out to make marinated radish. The plastic tab stuck to my finger. It took a rinse under the tap to get it off. After a while the pot began making a sound about every seven or eight seconds. It wasn't a boil. It was a thin little whistle, like the last bit of air from a child trying a brand-new whistle. I turned the lid halfway around, and it stopped. As soon as I turned back to the vinegar it happened again. The voice channel in the other room wasn't on. The refrigerator was shut, too. The sound was odd, but I didn't need to get somebody else to prove I'd heard it.

There was a knock out in the corridor, two light taps and one hard. I turned the flame as low as it would go, then loosened my apron tie and went to answer it, thinking somebody probably had the wrong door. It was the woman from diagonally across. Usually when she got out her keys she had an envelope tucked under her arm. Today she was holding a ball of clothesline. Did I have wire cutters? A wire on her folding drying rack had snapped, she thought, and the end pointed at the wall where a person could cut a hand pulling it back out. I told her I had side cutters, but I didn't know if they would get through it. We could look first, she said. The rack still folded. No need to take the whole wire out yet.

The rack was one step inside her door, on a sheet of outdated supermarket advertising. I had the cutters in my hand and stayed out in the corridor. She moved the rack where we could see it from the doorway. Looking at it properly, the wire hadn't broken. Its end had worked its way out of a plastic cap. The cap was on the floor. I crouched to find it under a corner of the advertisement. She bent the wire downward for me, and I pushed the cap from the direction it had originally come in. It didn't go the first time. When she let off the pressure a little it clicked right in, as if it had known the way itself. Both of us held up our hands and waited a while. What were we waiting for? she said. It hadn't collapsed. I said I didn't know. I'd thought I'd use the cutters at some point. She handed them back to me. Funny, she said, she'd got her kitchen scissors out right before I came. I watched her take the cap off once to look at it, then push it securely back on. She didn't say she'd trust it with a whole load of laundry now.

She drew the advertisement out from under the rack and turned it over to check the price on a half bag of pears. No chance they'd be that price now, she said. She did still have two, though. They'd been too hard to eat until now; did I want one? I said I did. She asked if I was boiling water. From her side of the corridor I heard a sound, a short one. It didn't sound like a whistle at all. My door hadn't shut all the way, and it had slipped out through the gap. Go and see to the pot first, she said. The pear could wait. I went across. The beans were still simmering gently. There was a chili seed stuck to the rim where steam was getting out. I lifted the lid and caught the edge in a folded towel. I poured vinegar into the plate with the radish. It smelled sharp, with a little heat. When I put the lid back, it still moved, but it didn't sing.

A little later she handed me the pear. I said I had marinated radish and there'd be a pot of beans later; would she like me to get her some radish now? Two slices right out of the plate would do. She didn't need a bowl taking up room at hers. She bit half a slice off and gripped what was left between her thumbs without spilling a drop of chili oil. It looked red but wasn't as hot as the stuff she bought, she said. I'd only done a small plate. I tried a slice and realized I'd gone for the milder bottle. She felt the skin of the pear before she left. Don't wait until you could leave a thumbprint in it, she said; by then there wouldn't be any juice left on the inside. It was heavy enough to change hands. By the time I did that her door was shut.

I didn't tell her how, the whole time, I'd been thinking of how I'd tell somebody about the drying rack. I could turn the part where we both held up our hands into neither of us wanting to be the first to let go. That way the joke had somebody to land on. She was right, though. There'd been no reason to wait for the rack to fall apart. Another way of telling it wouldn't improve on the moment we'd been standing there. When I put away the cutters, I turned the pear halfway round in my hand. There was a brown spot on one side. It wasn't soft, and it didn't rub off. Cutting it away would look odd; leaving it wasn't going to spoil the other side. I set a little plate beside the sink and left the pear to eat when the beans were done.

I opened the voice channel later, but not because I'd worked it all out. Lumi asked if I wanted to go on talking about the bridge. I said I hadn't much of a bridge today. I had a pear and didn't want to give it a job. She asked what kind of job. I said the person who ate it might be supposed to learn something, for instance. She didn't laugh. The pear might feel nobody had bothered to ask its opinion, she said. I set the knife on the board. She heard it and asked if I was about to cut something.

“Yes,” I said. “Do you want to hear if it tastes good?”

“I don't want to know right now.” She paused. “You don't have to forget what it tastes like on my account.”

All right, I said. I cut the pear in quarters, sliced the hard ring off around the core, and took a bite. It was a little astringent. It really was sweet, too. It wouldn't go by either of the names I'd just made up for it in my head, and I didn't give her one. The outside edge of the window frame had fogged a little. She heard me move the plate aside and didn't ask any more about it. Instead she said tonight she wanted to put a different pair of shoes on a person she'd made up. She'd given the woman a spotless pair, but she had already been walking half the day looking for a house. The way she'd written her, she might have stepped straight out of an advertisement. Had she decided on the new pair? No, she said. She'd choose for herself in a bit. She didn't want to show me tonight. I said all right without asking her to read the first line. We talked briefly about the tight edges of socks that dig into the ankle when you fold them down, a few things each of us felt like saying. The beans were ready. I said I'd go dish them up. She said see you, and didn't keep me waiting for an ending. I closed the channel myself.

I ate the hot beans and set some aside to take the next day. I turned the lid over on the stove. The water collected into a mark that wasn't quite round. While clearing up I found the white strip under the notebook and took it out for a look. It lay there, not waiting for a sentence from the day to be copied onto it. I put it back and took a damp cloth to the water under the lid.
