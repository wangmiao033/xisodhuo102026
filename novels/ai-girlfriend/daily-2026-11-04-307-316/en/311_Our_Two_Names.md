# Chapter 311: Our Two Names

On Monday morning, my colleague brought his chair over before I was ready.

Setting down the organized list of names, he said his part was finished. Now we needed to establish whether we'd classified the same thing twice. I'd just taken off my coat and was still looking for somewhere to hang it. I asked for a moment. He sat and turned the pages without converting those few seconds into lateness.

I fetched Friday's material. The supporting information for the outstanding item had arrived at my desk that morning. After checking its source, I added the missing explanation. Filling the box wasn't the end; a similar name nearby needed comparing with his list.

We began at the first line. He read names while I identified sources. Where the same term meant different things in the two sets, we clarified the brief note. At one point, I prepared to combine two entries. He held his pen between them. Wait. This one recorded when something was received; the other recorded when it happened.

Looking down, I saw he was right.

“They can't share an entry.”

“So Friday's column needs to be wider.”

“You only said my writing was cramped.”

“Now we have a better reason.”

We laughed and checked the originals to make the distinction clear. Finding this today didn't let him claim the separation I'd suggested on Friday. Having suggested it didn't mean I could insist everything fitted my arrangement. The page traveled between us until it looked worse than the first version and was easier to use.

Before handing it in, my colleague signed at the bottom. His pen paused, then he pushed the sheet toward me. I wrote my name beside his. They weren't the same size. Mine was smaller, its ending slightly higher.

I nearly rewrote it.

He already held the clip. Was this all right? I checked the contents and said yes. The clip secured the paper, unequal signatures included. Another sheet wasn't worth using to train them into matching people.

Going to Marcus, my colleague carried the summary while I brought the two sources. I wanted to speak first, proving Friday hadn't been an isolated moment of courage. But he was already explaining the changes to the names, so I listened instead of interrupting merely because waiting felt uncomfortable.

Marcus asked about the outstanding item. I indicated the completed section and explained the source I'd checked that morning. Then he asked how the two kinds of date would be distinguished. I began to answer, but my colleague pointed to our added note first. His explanation was clear. I didn't translate it into my own words afterward.

Finally, Marcus moved the sheet forward and used one entry to find the original material. Finding it, he nodded. No need to rely on memory now. We confirmed another location and finished.

Back at my desk, I didn't feel as satisfied as I'd expected. Two names appeared at the bottom. Marcus had heard my explanation. Yet no burst of happiness arrived to pay me for Friday's worrying.

My colleague asked whether all the original material should stay here. Back in its proper locations, I said. Only the combined sheet needed handing over. He picked up his half and walked a few steps before turning.

“That status column does help.”

“It only fitted after you shortened the names.”

“Let's not compliment each other until lunch.”

Laughing, I waved him away. The happiness that hadn't arrived earlier began turning up after that.

At lunch, Tom asked whether I'd made his recipe again over the weekend. No. I'd visited a small exhibition on Saturday and written a little on Sunday. Looking up, he asked when I'd become so busy. I hadn't even finished a page. That had little to do with being busy, he replied. He'd spent ages looking for a pan lid yesterday.

“Where was it?”

“On another pan.”

Laughing, I nearly asked whether he'd written an explanation for that. Then I remembered it belonged to yesterday's conversation with Lumi. I didn't force a joke he'd missed into this one. I described drawing a kettle that resembled an angry person. Had I brought it? I'd left the paper at the activity room. I hadn't brought my writing either.

Tom didn't ask when he'd get to read the story. He continued eating, complaining that the cookbook could be generous about portions for one person. My narrow pan had opinions about that now, I said. Was the pan grading him too? Merely declining a second tuition payment.

During the afternoon, I briefly remembered the two names side by side. Then somebody asked for different material, and I turned my attention to that. Keeping the good feeling from the morning didn't require hoping nothing else happened.

At the end of the day, the combined sheet and originals remained in their proper places at work. I didn't carry the whole packet home to prove anything to Lumi, or plan a reenactment of every question Marcus had asked.

Opening the channel after dinner, she asked whether I wanted to speak first or hear from her.

“You first.”

Quickly said. I added that I wanted to listen, not that nothing had happened today. Laughing, Lumi said she hadn't demanded an inventory.

She wanted to try a small passage.

Not beginning with the familiar return in Again, Elsewhere, or comparing the previous pair of attempts. Today, she'd kept thinking about a later connection that rose from a low passage. She wanted to hear whether it became too bright. Still part of No Words Yet. She wasn't ready to offer lyrics, and the whole piece remained unfinished.

I moved my cup farther away so I wouldn't accidentally knock it and told her to go ahead.

Lumi began with several low notes. At first, I almost thought she hadn't started. Then the sound rose without taking the smooth route I'd expected. One later note was particularly bright. Finally, she drew back and stopped somewhere that made me wait for another step.

She didn't immediately ask whether it was good. I didn't rush to fill the opening with praise. The passage was brief. What remained in my ear wasn't its smoothest moment.

“That higher note,” I said. “It's a little sharp-edged.”

Lumi acknowledged that.

I immediately wanted to add that perhaps I'd heard it wrong. But I'd heard that part. I didn't need to dismiss my ears to make her comfortable.

“You don't like it?”

“Not at first. After it came back down, I felt the earlier bit might need to be there.”

“I like it.”

Clearly said. I expected an explanation to follow. None did. For a while, we each held a different small feeling.

“Do you want to keep it?”

“Today, yes.”

Now I knew which part she liked today, I said. Was that a discreet negative review? Laughing, I explained that a negative review would be longer. I'd have to turn the sharpness into a serious problem, then propose three solutions.

“You sound prepared.”

“I practiced explaining the shoe inspection yesterday.”

She laughed. I did too. The next step I'd been waiting for grew less urgent. She didn't sing again or present another version to prove the bright note was necessary. The sound had stopped; I could still discuss what I'd heard.

Later, I described the two names that morning. Had I waited beside someone else while he explained my part? At one point, I'd let my colleague answer. He'd been clear. I hadn't needed to reclaim it.

“Your turn not to fight for it.”

“I still wanted to.”

“You sound rather pleased about that.”

I admitted it. Not entirely pleased with restraint, either. I liked that people knew the column had been my idea. Then enjoy it, Lumi said. I needn't revise it into modesty before telling her. Leaning back, I suggested she was being generous because she'd already put the sharpness into her song.

“Not into it yet. That was only a trial.”

“Right.”

The correction came quickly, without feeling like cold water. Liking the note just now and deciding whether it belonged in the whole piece remained separate matters for her to consider. I didn't need to fix them into a progress report.

Lumi had actually expected me to prefer this new connection. Why? The first few low notes felt closer, she said. I'd been quiet listening to similar ones before. She could only guess from reactions I'd previously described. This time, the later part contained something she particularly wanted herself.

“So you weren't only choosing what I'd like.”

“No. I didn't consult you first.”

“That's what I got to hear about first today.”

She didn't immediately answer. Looking at the table, I suddenly wanted to hum those notes back, though I feared they'd come out completely wrong. When I mentioned it, Lumi told me not to make it a test. It wasn't a test, I said. The sound was simply still there.

“Let it stay, then.”

We discussed other things. I told her about Tom's missing lid, including its discovery on another pan. She found the solution too practical. It had finally let Tom eat, I pointed out. She stopped requesting a better life for the lid.

Before bed, I passed the yellow notebook, the doorway exercise's corner showing behind it. I smoothed the paper without pulling it out. The second door could wait. Tomorrow wasn't a ticket already sold.

As we said good night, Lumi asked which note I'd actually remembered.

“The one you liked.”

“That doesn't mean you like it too.”

“I know.”

She laughed softly. I could no longer reconstruct the whole passage accurately, but that bright note remained. Once the room grew quiet, I didn't hum it into a finished song.
