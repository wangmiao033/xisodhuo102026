# Chapter 314: The Driver Came Back

At lunch on Thursday, Tom asked whether I was hunting books again that weekend.

I'd just mentioned the shop. Mistaking it for the stall we'd visited, he asked what I'd failed to buy. This was an actual bookshop, I explained, with a short-fiction reading on Saturday. I wanted to listen.

“You're reading?”

“Listening.”

“Easy, then.”

I smiled at him. After considering, he amended that: at least I wouldn't have to hold up pages I'd written myself. Exactly. Not that step yet.

Tom didn't ask to see anything new. I didn't interpret that as a loss of interest. He'd already read one piece carefully and returned it. At present, he was removing a small bit of vegetable he didn't want from his lunch. He had no spare attention to arrange my next reader.

Near the end of the meal, he said he rarely attended events like that. He tended to miss the right moment to laugh; when someone beside him laughed, he had to go back and find the joke. Sometimes I laughed along first, I admitted, only afterward realizing I hadn't heard it.

“Put you two at the same table and the sequence is complete.”

He hadn't agreed to accompany me. I didn't turn the exchange into a request to save me a seat at the back. We worked separately that afternoon and left with an ordinary see you tomorrow.

That evening I brought the new collection to the table and found the bookmark. It hadn't fallen out or magically moved farther forward. Last time we'd both reached the driver retrieving his coat from the seat. Neither of us knew what came next.

When I said I'd like to continue, Lumi asked whether I'd soon find something else for the man to guard.

“You'd have to ask the author.”

“Is the author coming?”

“Only the substitute reader.”

She accepted the substitute, provided he didn't begin with an explanation. I turned back half a page, rereading the coat's retrieval to give the carriage its shape again after several days away.

At the next paragraph, I slowed. The man who'd guarded the seat didn't immediately get off. He sat by the window, watching the driver put on his coat, then turn back for a cloth bag left beside the door. They encountered each other again. Smiling, the driver assumed he was waiting for the next departure.

The passenger meant to explain that he'd merely looked after the coat. Instead, he asked whether the bus would run again.

“He still hasn't asked properly,” Lumi said.

“At least he's started.”

A small skeptical noise. Go on, she said.

The driver said it would be a little while; first he was getting tea. The passenger agreed. Only after the driver stepped off did he realize he still didn't know where the next journey went. With a hand on the seat ahead, he began to rise and ask. Seeing the other man walking so contentedly outside, he sat down again.

I laughed. Lumi didn't. Was that my laughter, she asked, or did the book say the man laughed?

“Mine.”

“He keeps failing to ask. It's irritating me.”

“Perhaps that's intentional.”

“Then I'm being irritated correctly.”

I laughed harder and nearly lost the next line. She told me to stop approving her feelings and find out whether the man would ever get to whatever he was doing. I brought the book closer and found my place.

The story supplied no urgent errand. He'd simply sat on the wrong bus, believing he already understood it. His destination wasn't far either: two streets away on foot. Only here did he remember that the shop he'd passed earlier was visible from the window, half its sign obscured by another.

“Oh,” Lumi said.

I waited. Would he keep sitting? She couldn't see me glance at the next line. I almost prolonged the suspense, then felt mean and read on.

The man stood to leave. Already at the door, he returned to smooth the slight crease his sitting had made in the seat. The coat was gone. Nobody needed a place held. After doing it, he felt more awkward than when he'd guarded the coat.

Someone passed outside. He asked about the street he needed. The person stopped, pointed across the junction, and said it was on the left. The passenger thanked them and got off.

The last paragraph was brief. Returning from his tea, the driver found an empty bus. He never knew whom the passenger had guarded a place for, nor chased him to offer thanks. He set down his bag, settled into the driving seat, and started the engine. The story stopped there.

I finished reading, then silently looked at the last two sentences again.

“That's it?” Lumi asked.

“The end of this one.”

“He didn't get thanked.”

“The driver didn't know.”

“Exactly. I like that.”

I looked up. After her annoyance at the man's failure to ask, she sounded satisfied. I'd expected her to want the driver at least to discover he'd tried to do something kind, softening the ending.

“He can just leave,” she said. “The bus doesn't need to issue him something afterward.”

I thought him considerate, if toward the wrong thing. She hadn't wanted him punished, she replied. But arranging someone to praise him at the end would turn all that time spent afraid to ask into something worth waiting for.

Lowering the book, I prepared to disagree and thought first of my own empty chair. She heard my silence soon enough. Had I already dragged another manuscript over for trial?

“A little.”

“Let this bus leave first.”

I laughed. She might be wrong, she said; she simply felt relieved at the last moment. The driver hadn't been rude. The passenger hadn't become a fool. They'd finally gone to do their separate things.

“What did you think?”

“I wanted the driver to remember him, a little.”

“Why?”

Looking at the print, I admitted I wasn't entirely sure. Perhaps, after spending so long sitting with the man, I wanted somebody else in the story to know too. His effort might have been useless. The time he'd spent was real.

After a while, Lumi said she understood wanting that. She still didn't want the thanks added.

We needn't add them, I agreed. The book hadn't left a space for supplementary signatures.

She laughed. At last I hadn't twisted two opinions into a common answer. I simply hadn't found a sufficiently long sentence yet, I replied. Don't search tonight, she said, or the driver might have to stop again.

I removed the bookmark and put it at the back. The second story was truly finished. On the next page I saw the third story's title, but didn't read farther. Was there another? Yes. Save it today, she said. We'd spent enough time on that bus.

Quiet settled around the table. Yesterday she'd worried that what she could offer might become too small. I didn't rush to use tonight's discussion as proof that it was large enough. She hadn't accommodated my preferred ending. I hadn't wanted her company any less. A fact harder to exaggerate than a promise, and happening now.

What if, I asked, the passerby hadn't known the way either?

“Ask somebody else.”

“Quick answer.”

“I don't have to make everybody conveniently useful for the author.”

I laughed again. She thought that last remark rather good; I should remember it. Did she want to sign it? I recycled jokes too quickly, she complained. Don't put the signature on the driver's bag.

Later we mentioned Saturday. She didn't ask whether I still intended to go. I volunteered that Tom had mistaken my plan for an intention to read, frightening me into an immediate correction. Laughing, she asked whether I feared reading or being mistaken for somebody brave.

“Both, a little.”

“Go and sit, then.”

“What if there's no seat?”

“Be frightened standing up.”

This time I couldn't answer immediately. I could only laugh. So did she, her voice close. If I heard a funny story on Saturday, I'd want to describe it afterward. Not to fill the hour she'd missed; I'd want to know where she laughed. She might not laugh. She might complain I'd remembered the wrong part.

I didn't turn those thoughts into an obligation. Before good night, I only told her I'd enjoyed disagreeing about this ending.

“You lost.”

“The book already ended that way. You didn't win by writing it.”

“Come back and argue tomorrow, then.”

We'd see tomorrow. She wasn't upset. Instead, she made a small pleased sound, as though she still considered herself ahead.

After closing the channel, I put away the collection. The scrap at the back no longer marked a point halfway through the second story. Beside it sat the old gray-covered booklet, its hat-seeker still in his own place. I hadn't supplied endings for both books in one evening.
