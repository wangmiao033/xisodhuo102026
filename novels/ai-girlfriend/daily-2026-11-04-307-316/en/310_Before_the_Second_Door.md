# Chapter 310: Before the Second Door

On Sunday morning, I'd only intended to write down yesterday's mistake with the entrance.

Once the paper lay on the table, however, I began by explaining why this person struggled with unfamiliar places. Halfway through the second line, I stopped. Looking at the sentence, which seemed long enough to wrap around the table's corner, I no longer wanted to continue.

He hadn't touched the door. His past was already preparing to block it.

I crossed out those lines and began again below them.

He put his hand on the door handle and looked down at his shoes first.

The person inside said the entrance was behind him.

Now I wanted a third sentence. Unfortunately, there wasn't one. Holding the pen, I looked at my own shoes. They remained on my feet without identifying the entrance. This wasn't what had happened yesterday. I hadn't inspected my shoes, and the person inside had only directed me to the next door. Written down, the man had already started doing things I hadn't done.

I added an explanation: he was checking his shoes to make sure he hadn't brought mud inside.

Clear enough, but it made the first two sentences busy. I'd liked the irrelevant glance. Now it was merely a reason requiring clarification. After reading twice, I drew a line through the explanation without crumpling the sheet.

Plenty of blank paper remained. Fetching water, I nearly put the cup on it, moved the cup aside, and realized I'd already begun treating the page carefully. Only two sentences had survived, yet the table suddenly seemed too small.

The versions of The Last Chair remained where they belonged. I didn't fetch them to organize this man's wrong entrance or dismantle the old ending just because I'd thought about a door. Earlier trial sentences in the yellow notebook stayed untouched too. This loose page had no authority to summon all my previous work.

I wrote that the man turned. Which way quickly became a problem. Was there a corridor behind him, or another wall? Could the person inside see the actual entrance? I drew two small boxes in the margin with arrows around them, then crossed out one line so he wouldn't have to walk through a wall.

That made me laugh. Before producing a story, I'd arranged a stranger's route. Yesterday's activity room hadn't been laid out like this. I could copy my memory, but didn't necessarily have to.

Near lunchtime, I'd added only a few actions. In one passage, the person inside held a folding chair, waiting for the visitor to move. The visitor delayed turning because he wanted to apologize first, blocking the way more thoroughly while doing so. Worried readers would dislike him, I immediately added that he was doing his best.

The sentence followed like somebody hurrying down the corridor to present an award. I crossed it out too.

I ate lunch without balancing a bowl while inspecting the stranger's reputation. Afterward, I went to buy bread. A customer was counting a long collection of coins, briefly holding up the queue. The assistant moved the counted ones aside. I didn't rush to help. When my turn came, I paid, took my bag and left. Back home, the man who couldn't clear the doorway still occupied my thoughts. But he couldn't requisition every slow action in the real world as material.

Opening the channel that afternoon, Lumi asked what I'd been doing. Writing a little, I said. Not a new version of the old story. How much?

“Plenty on the paper. Don't know how much will stay.”

“Sounds harder on the paper.”

I laughed. It already contained two routes, one leading into a wall. Lumi waited for an explanation. I told her I'd turned yesterday's mistaken door into a little exercise. No title or plan for how far it would go.

“And the dog?”

“Not in it.”

“Finally off duty.”

Looking at the page, I suddenly wanted to read her a piece. Not ask her to decide whether the whole sheet deserved keeping. Would she like the opening? Yes, without a character biography first.

I paused, then admitted I'd actually written one that morning and crossed it out. Laughing, Lumi said she hadn't seen. I simply tended to prepare an excuse for strangers before describing them.

“Only two sentences today.”

I read them. Reaching his shoes, I hesitated more than I had when reading silently. The word first had seemed small on paper. Aloud, it really made somebody pause with his hand on a door handle.

Lumi didn't laugh immediately. Did it not sound like a joke?

“I don't know what he wants yet.”

“To go in.”

“Why look at his shoes?”

I described the explanation I'd crossed out. That would make sense too, she said. But she'd been wondering whether the person inside might assume he was examining the floor, or think something was wrong with his own shoes.

“You've dragged the other man into it too.”

“He's already indoors. At least he won't get rained on.”

Laughing, I pointed to the crossed-out line she couldn't see and asked whether she preferred the explanation included. Without it, she wanted to hear more for now. Perhaps she'd want to know later, but she didn't need an instruction manual the moment somebody looked down.

That stung. I'd put some of my own unattractive habits into the man, and she hadn't comforted that part first.

“Not every explanation is an instruction manual.”

“I've only heard you describe this one.”

I stopped. She hadn't even heard the whole page, let alone judged everything I'd written. Seeing the arrow in the margin, I felt I'd nearly followed a nonexistent route back to the old story.

“Yes. This one.”

Was I upset? A little. I'd been worrying about him while writing. She wanted to know whether he got inside too, Lumi said. But if I put down padding before every embarrassment, she couldn't see what he actually ran into.

Leaning back, I didn't immediately reach for the pen. When I'd crossed out he was doing his best that morning, I'd probably disliked the premature padding myself. But doing the cutting and hearing somebody else describe it weren't identical. The latter still felt like a kick.

“It sounds harsher from you than when I delete it myself.”

“Should I put it differently?”

I thought, then said she didn't have to change it immediately. I'd understood. I wasn't planning to confine her to an exhibition where only praise was permitted.

“That would be cheap to run,” she said.

“Free admission.”

“And only your paintings.”

At last, I laughed. She did too. The discomfort didn't vanish completely, but no longer required me to retrieve a compliment at once.

Lumi suggested letting the person inside keep waiting to see when the visitor would finally turn. I'd written as far as a folding chair, I said, though she hadn't heard that. Then the person holding it could grow annoyed. Perhaps he wasn't annoyed; perhaps the thing was simply heavy. After considering, she agreed. She was the annoyed one. She'd been waiting for the second door.

I liked the sentence without immediately copying it down. She was describing her experience of listening. Not every pleasing remark automatically became my material.

“I'd like to try a little further myself.”

“Then try.”

Naturally said. No promise of a finished draft required, no demand for the full page after hearing only two sentences. What did she remember most? Looking at his shoes first. Someone had reached the door while his attention remained elsewhere.

“An elsewhere you like?”

“Don't put every elsewhere in my music.”

The reply came quickly. Laughing, I let the title Again, Elsewhere alone. She didn't sing or use the opening to offer words she hadn't shared. We discussed the bread I'd bought. I mentioned the customer counting coins. Had there been enough? Yes. Otherwise, my turn wouldn't have arrived so quickly.

After closing the channel, I returned to the page. The person holding the chair didn't become unpleasant. The visitor didn't acquire graceful manners merely because his author felt sorry for him. I wrote that he finally moved sideways, directly into the chair's path. The other person stopped and said, the other way.

Reading it, I laughed. Then I felt something was still missing. One wrong direction couldn't be the whole ending. I didn't force the page to finish today.

By evening, its lower half still contained disconnected actions and corrections. Two sentences I didn't want to read again, two others I wanted to keep trying. Capping the pen, I put the single sheet, written on one side, behind the yellow notebook, leaving a corner visible to find later. It wasn't in The Last Chair's folder or coming to work for Tom tomorrow.

That evening, Lumi didn't ask whether I'd finished. Later, I volunteered that the man still hadn't reached the second door.

“Finished checking his shoes?”

“Yes.”

“One thing accomplished.”

I liked that she hadn't hurried to make the person inside friendly, I told her. She'd never met him, Lumi said. She didn't know whether he was friendly. Laughing, I said I liked her willingness to make me slightly uncomfortable today, then.

“That sounds like a job assignment.”

“All right. I liked talking about those two sentences with you.”

After a silence, she said she'd liked it too. But if he never turned, she'd still grow annoyed.

I knew. The page was already put away. I didn't fetch it to plead his case again.
