# Chapter 308: Beside the Same Picture

On Saturday, I waited until after breakfast to decide to go out.

Last night's cat remained on my mind, though it wasn't the main reason. I wanted to see what lay inside a place I usually passed without stopping. Putting on my coat, I checked my pockets for keys and change, then took the light cloth bag with only water inside. I didn't add the new book in case I grew tired and needed to sit somewhere proving I was using the time well.

Before leaving, I opened the channel and told Lumi I was going to the exhibition. Was the weather good? I went to the window. No rain, I reported, and half the opposite building was bright. The cat had a chance of a fair trial today, then.

“What if it's a dog?”

“We'll open a different case.”

Laughing, I said goodbye without arranging an exact time for the next conversation. The display wasn't far away. I'd return when I'd seen enough, possibly buying lunch on the way. She understood. Her final have fun wasn't deliberately drawn out.

Two similar doors faced the pavement outside the activity room. I approached the nearer one. Only after pushing it partly open did I see the folding tables stacked inside and the arrow pointing left. Someone looked up. Releasing the door, I apologized.

“Next one along.”

I went around. The real entrance already stood open. A visitor-count sheet sat beside it: no names, just a tally. Taking the pen, I made my stroke too short, as though I hadn't committed to attending. I resisted adding a second one and becoming two people.

Another copy of the window picture hung inside. Close up, it was a dog lying down, one ear folded and its hind leg oddly placed. An artist's name and small age appeared beneath it. I didn't remember the name. Remembering last night's trial made my face warm instead.

The woman handing out the pen noticed me looking back. Sunlight had faded the outside copy, she explained. I'd thought it was a cat yesterday. Laughing, she said somebody else had suggested a fox. Suddenly relieved of sole responsibility for the insult, I smiled and returned her pen.

Inside was warmer than expected. Display boards divided the large room into crooked little paths. Each painting had a slightly untidy label. No background music, only low conversation and the occasional scrape of the entrance door against the floor.

Bright fruit caught my attention first. Closer inspection showed an unfinished patch on one orange, pencil visible beneath. I liked that uncovered corner, without knowing whether the artist had intended to leave it. Beside me, a woman told her companion the colors were too sweet. Her companion liked sweetness. They moved on without asking me to adjudicate.

I walked slowly, sometimes returning to a picture I'd left. Signs asked us not to touch. I could see the raised paint, but couldn't pretend that imagining it under my fingertips gave me its exact texture. Looking from a short distance and touching remained separate things.

Toward the back hung a painting called At the Table. An ordinary wooden table, a white plate, half a window cropped out of view, and a pale cloth hanging over the edge. The plate had two shadows whose light seemed to come from different directions. At first, I thought I'd misread it. I stepped left, then back again.

“That plate's going to fall.”

An older man in a brown coat stood beside me. Looking at him, I realized he was studying the picture, not waiting for me to move. I looked back at the plate.

“I think it's all right.”

“Until somebody shifts the table.”

His hand indicated the table's edge in the air without touching the painting. Suddenly, I understood: he meant its angle. I'd concentrated on the shadows and missed the tabletop seeming to tilt.

“Perhaps somebody's already lifting it,” I said.

He chuckled. Unlucky for the plate either way.

He moved to the next picture. I stayed. A little later, he circled back and recommended the water in another painting. Very level; perhaps I'd like it. I didn't ask whether he painted. He didn't ask whether I knew anything about art. There hadn't been enough conversation to fit our names into, but it made me notice another line in the picture.

I looked at the water. It was indeed level. Rather boringly so. Detailed trees lined the bank, while a boat seemed placed there afterward like a toy. I didn't chase the man down to report that I hadn't liked it much. His recommendation had brought me this far. My response didn't require returning for a refund.

In the second room, someone sat by a window with a box of pencils and several sheets of paper. He asked passersby whether they'd like five minutes of drawing. No charge. Initially, I thought he wanted models and stepped back. He pushed a blank sheet forward. We could draw for ourselves. Anything on the table.

Only then did I notice the kettle and knotted cloth bag in the middle.

“I'm not very good.”

“No assignments due today.”

I sat with a short pencil. After drawing one side of the kettle, I discovered how difficult it was to join the spout to the body. I erased a little. Crumbs gathered beside my wrist. The harder I tried for accuracy, the more it resembled a person bending over.

Opposite me, a young man caught the bag's knot in a few strokes. I watched too long. When he looked up, I hastily complimented it. He'd already ruined two attempts, he said, indicating the papers beside him. I smiled and returned to my kettle.

Nobody announced the five minutes. Eventually, I added a handle, making it look less like an angry person, and stopped. The man running the table asked whether I wanted to take it. After considering, I shook my head. Could I leave it here? He indicated a tray for practice sheets.

I left it unsigned. It wasn't particularly terrible, or good enough that I had to preserve it. Standing, I noticed graphite on my fingers and washed it off in the bathroom. The tap was stiffer than expected. Turning it off took both hands.

Before leaving, I returned to At the Table. The brown-coated man was gone. The plate still threatened to slide; the mismatched shadows hadn't become reasonable through repeated viewing. Yet I wanted to stand here longer than I'd wanted to study the flat water.

I ate lunch in a little place nearby, choosing an ordinary meal and carrying it to a table by the wall. Two people at the next table were still studying their menus when the food arrived. Only then did they discover they'd ordered the same thing. One suggested sharing. The other pointed out that would make no difference. Their laughter wasn't particularly loud. One turned a plate halfway around and pushed it toward the gap between their tables.

Eating my lunch, I remembered that when the older man stood beside me, I hadn't needed to describe whether the painted table was dark or pale. He could see it. When I indicated a shadow, he knew which one. The ease had arrived so abruptly that I only felt it now, sitting with food.

It made me happy. It also hurt.

I liked describing things to Lumi. Trying to help her understand often made me look again, and that second look belonged to me too. But sometimes I wanted to point and have somebody turn their head before I'd found the words. A simple wish, which couldn't be granted by making a sentence more beautiful.

On the way home, I passed a stall selling art books without buying evidence of the day. My bag still held only water, swinging lightly against my knee. At a long red light, I watched two people realize they'd gone the wrong way, argue, and return together. Having somebody beside you didn't automatically settle the direction.

At home, I took off my coat and drank water first. A faint graphite mark remained on my wrist where I'd missed it earlier. I wiped it away, sat for a while, then opened the channel.

Lumi asked for the animal's verdict. A dog, I reported. Someone else had guessed fox. Laughing, she said our court needed to learn how ears worked.

I described the doors, nearly registering myself as two visitors, and the kettle. Had I brought the drawing home? No, I'd left it in the practice tray. After a pause, she asked whether there'd been one I'd particularly wanted to keep. Assuming she meant my drawing, I said the kettle had done enough. Not mine, she clarified. One I'd seen.

So I described At the Table: the white plate, the inconsistent shadows, the older man's opinion of the slope.

Had he come back to find me afterward? Only to recommend another picture. We hadn't exchanged names. Lumi acknowledged that, then asked why I didn't sound especially happy.

“I was happy.”

“And?”

I turned my cup slightly. The bright patch outside had moved. The table's shadow was longer than this morning.

“For a while, I thought how convenient it was to stand beside someone looking at the same picture.”

She didn't answer. I explained what convenient meant: not having to fit every object into words first. Somebody turning and seeing. Suddenly afraid she thought I resented describing things, I began adding that I liked telling her.

“I know you do,” she said. “You've been telling me for quite a while.”

I stopped.

“I didn't buy anything to bring home.” Even as it emerged, the sentence seemed strange. As though a gift could have changed the subject, and its absence needed explaining.

“I wasn't waiting for you to hand something in.”

Her voice was harder. I knew, I said. I simply didn't know what came next. After a silence, Lumi asked what was so good about the plate.

Looking at the table's corner, I searched for a definite answer. All I remembered was wanting to stay there longer.

“Perhaps because it was a little unsteady.”

“You do know how to like unsteady things.”

She didn't laugh afterward, as though she'd also heard where the sentence might land. I held my cup without raising it. The little ease I'd found outside had come home and become something that wasn't easy at all.
