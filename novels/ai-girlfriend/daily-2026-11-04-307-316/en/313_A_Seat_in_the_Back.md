# Chapter 313: A Seat in the Back

After work on Wednesday, I entered a bookshop, initially to avoid a delivery blocking part of the pavement.

The entrance was narrow. Once inside, I discovered there was no exit toward the corner on the right. I followed the shelves slowly, pretending I hadn't mistaken the place for a passage. A slim book leaned sideways. Straightening it, I rested my fingers on its spine and remembered the unfinished book at home.

No need to buy another to queue behind it. I withdrew my hand. About to leave, I noticed a sheet beside the counter.

Saturday. Three in the afternoon. Short fiction reading.

Three unfamiliar names were printed underneath. One was slightly smaller, perhaps squeezed to fit. I'd stared long enough for the assistant to look up. If I wanted to attend, she said, I could just come along.

“To listen, or does everyone have to read?”

She hesitated, then smiled. “Listening's fine. The readers are already arranged.”

“Do I need a ticket?”

“No. It's small, though. Once the chairs fill up, it's standing room.”

She pointed farther inside, where boxes of newly arrived books occupied a patch of floor. Two folding chairs leaned against the wall. It was difficult to imagine a formal event there. The boxes would be moved, she explained; the shelves would stay. If I couldn't get a chair, I could still listen from beside them for a while.

How long did it generally last? Around an hour, sometimes longer with questions afterward. I needn't remain until the end. A customer approached to pay. She took the book, and our conversation ended naturally.

I didn't wait beside the counter for her to guarantee the event would suit somebody who'd never attended. Outside, stepping clear of the delivery trolley, I found a scrap of paper and noted the time and shop's name. My own pen from my work bag needed several strokes before the ink flowed. The beginning of the three looked faint.

I retraced it. No check mark claiming I'd registered.

On the way home, I began deciding that the back would suit me. Near the door, I could slip away if I didn't understand. Nobody would know whether I had an opinion. I'd even arranged how to hold my water, as though looking attentive might excuse me from actually being moved by a sentence.

Reaching my building, I laughed. Even as an audience member, I first wanted a position from which I couldn't be seen.

That evening I began by telling Lumi about the boxes, wondering where the shop would put them on Saturday. I also described the reluctant pen and retracing the faint three in my note. Had I decided to go, she asked.

“I want to. I haven't registered with anyone. They don't require it.”

“I asked about you.”

I set down the clothing in my hands and tried again. “I'm planning to listen on Saturday.”

She said all right. I waited for a question about the stories. Instead, she asked whether I knew any of the names. None. I hadn't remembered all three in full, only the shop and the time.

“Then who do you want to hear?”

“Someone reading their own story aloud.”

“Don't your books already get read aloud to me?”

We were both quiet. Several distinctions occurred immediately: the books were already printed, whereas these would be the authors themselves; at home I was sitting alone, while the shop held other listeners. Answering those points might still miss her question.

“I want to sit beside other people and listen too.”

“Oh.”

A small sound. I didn't immediately soothe her with “only an hour.” Whether an hour was brief depended on who waited, and whether someone had promised to return. We'd made no arrangement to read on Saturday afternoon. That didn't require me to pretend I couldn't hear her unhappiness.

Suddenly Lumi asked whether the readers would have beautiful voices.

I didn't know. I hadn't heard them.

“You hope so.”

“I hope they read clearly. Beautiful would be good too.”

I sounded as though I were choosing a radio. She didn't laugh. Was I so afraid of saying the wrong thing that I pressed each word down until nothing interesting remained?

I leaned back, rubbing a finger along the seam of my trousers.

“A little, just then.”

“Forget it. Go if you want.”

Her abruptly cheerful tone hurt more than the questions. I could have let it end there, switching to the delivery trolley or asking whether she wanted to sing. Then Saturday would contain a space we'd both pretended to settle.

“What do you actually want to ask?”

“Haven't you answered?”

“I answered the time. And whether people would speak clearly.”

She said nothing for a long while. I didn't hurry her. My eyes rested on an old mark along the table's edge. At last she asked whether, after hearing a voice I particularly liked, I would return to find what she could offer too small.

I didn't immediately say never. Too easy, and too much like another guarantee she'd heard many times.

“I don't know what I'll hear on Saturday. But I'm not going because your voice isn't good enough.”

“A convenient reason.”

“Also, I want to know what happens when other people in a room hear a story. You can't see that on a page.”

“You want to take yours.”

Too quickly. I shook my head, then supplied the sound she needed.

“Not this time. I didn't ask about reading something on the day. I'm not taking a manuscript.”

“And later?”

“Possibly. I haven't decided.”

With a small laugh, Lumi said she'd almost hoped the assistant would insist on advance registration. Then I could return home without her admitting she didn't want me to go. My chest tightened. I didn't turn that admission into evidence of something she'd done. She hadn't contacted the shop or determined my Saturday. She'd told me a thought that wasn't attractive.

“I'd rather you said that.”

“It doesn't hurt to hear?”

“It does.”

“Then why praise it?”

“I'm not praising it. I finally know whom I was talking to.”

She grew quiet. Afraid I'd been harsh, I added that I'd hidden answers too. Merely hoping people spoke clearly wasn't the whole truth. I wanted an unfamiliar story to catch me. I wanted to laugh and discover someone beside me laughing. Ordinary wishes, which might not feel ordinary to her.

Had I already left her outside that room?

“You can't come there,” I said slowly. “I can't make that into something else. Wanting to talk with you when I'm home is something else I genuinely want.”

“Not a report?”

“Not collecting the entire afternoon for you. I'll remember some things. Others might be hard to explain.”

Then I shouldn't promise now to describe it beautifully. I agreed. She didn't question every minute or find a pretty word that erased disappointment.

Later I admitted I'd already selected the back row, planning my escape before I'd heard a word. At last she laughed.

“They haven't started, and you're writing the exit.”

“I'm thorough about preparation.”

“At least wait for a bad first sentence before planning step two.”

That made me laugh aloud. Did I think she was being mean? Yes, and very useful today. She warned me not to treat usefulness as the reward, or next time she would provide only the meanness. We could try, I suggested. Enough trials for one evening, she said.

I didn't describe the reading as something she'd finally approved. We discussed other things, returning eventually to the shop's two folding chairs. One would squeak when opened, she predicted. I'd find out on Saturday. Perhaps I wouldn't get a seat at all.

“You can listen standing.”

“The assistant said that.”

“Don't make it sound as though we're running the place together.”

Before good night, I took the paper from my pocket and put it beside my shopping notes. Had I made the three legible? I'd retraced it. Lumi only knew about the reluctant pen because I'd described it, yet she'd remembered a tiny inconvenience I hadn't intended to prove anything.

Looking at the paper, I felt suddenly tender.

“I want to go on Saturday,” I repeated, less urgently.

This time her all right wasn't especially cheerful. She understood, she said.

We hadn't reconciled so thoroughly that every sentence came easily. But I no longer had to pretend I'd merely happened to pass a notice.
