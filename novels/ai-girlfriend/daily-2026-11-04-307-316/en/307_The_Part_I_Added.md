# Chapter 307: The Part I Added

When I saw Tom on Friday morning, my first words were that I'd still used too much water.

Halfway out of his coat, one arm trapped in a sleeve, he looked at me.

“We can get to good morning eventually.”

I laughed, supplied it, and explained that I'd followed his note yesterday. The result was edible and tasted good. I'd simply assumed a little extra water couldn't matter much. Waiting for that extra splash had taken a while.

“How big was your pan?”

I indicated the size with my hands. Finally escaping his coat, he said mine was narrower. That explained something. Should he include pan dimensions on the note? He wasn't opening a school. We laughed beside the aisle, then both moved aside for somebody carrying a stack of things.

I'd prepared to say everything else had been excellent. Before I did, I remembered the bottom of the dish tasting stronger than the first serving.

“Also, once the water reduced, it was a little salty.”

Next time, he suggested, decide on the salt later. Was that tested advice or something he'd just thought of? Hanging his coat over his chair, he turned toward me with the expression of a teacher being questioned by a particularly stingy pupil.

“Just thought of it. You can skip the second tuition payment.”

I hadn't settled the first installment. Waving me toward work, he added a moment later that a narrow pan might need less water, but I shouldn't boil everything dry because he'd said so. I knew. His note was still beside my shopping notes at home, its wet corner dry now. It hadn't needed bringing in for an official stamp of success.

The morning's work did need a clearer piece of paper.

Marcus wanted two groups of checked material brought together, with a short cover sheet showing the next reader what was ready to use and what still needed supplementary information. We weren't checking every figure again. Another colleague would align the names used in each set. I'd organize sources and outstanding points. We'd submit it together on Monday morning.

My colleague brought over his chair, nudging a cardboard box out of the way with his foot. He hadn't imagined my desk as an empty space permanently ready for visitors. I moved my water to make room.

Our first disagreement concerned a horizontal line.

To keep everything on one page, he wanted confirmed and pending items in the same column, separated by a line. I said someone looking for an item might notice its number and forget whether it belonged above or below the divider. With the paper in front of them, surely they'd see. Instead of answering immediately, I took a spare sheet and wrote three sample entries in his arrangement.

Turning it toward him, I asked him to find the second item's source.

He looked down, pointed, then stopped. “You've made those very similar.”

“The originals are worse.”

We laughed. He pulled the paper closer but still resisted adding a whole page. I shortened the explanations to a few words, leaving a status beside each item. He combined the repeated headings. What had required us to point back and forth finally fitted on a single sheet.

It took longer than expected. I liked seeing a useful change on paper. I liked explaining why it mattered less. Each time he frowned, I wanted to say we could use his version after all. By the third occasion, I recognized that sentence approaching and finished the actual entry instead, showing him how it worked.

Near lunchtime, Marcus came over. My colleague stood with the new arrangement and explained the combined headings, shorter notes, and separate statuses. He spoke quickly, making our slow process sound much smoother.

Marcus liked the arrangement. Could he have it ready Monday?

“Yes,” my colleague said.

Sitting beside him, I suddenly didn't know what to do with my hands.

Nobody had claimed he'd done everything. I could wait until Marcus left and remind myself that getting the work done mattered most. But the yes had already landed. Next time someone asked about sources or unresolved items, they might still look only at him. I stared at a crack in my pen cap. A page turned. Another moment, and this would become something I rehearsed alone all afternoon.

“I'll organize the sources and statuses,” I said.

Marcus looked at me. I added, more quietly, “Separating the statuses was my suggestion. He changed the names and layout. We'll finish our parts, then put them together.”

Immediately, I regretted it. Someone had praised a meal, and I'd raised a finger to identify my contribution of salt.

But my colleague glanced at the paper and agreed. I was following up the pending part too. He passed the page toward me so I could indicate what remained blank. I stood and explained. Marcus told us to check the finished result together on Monday before bringing it to him. He didn't want two different versions left circulating.

After he went, I remained standing. My colleague was already seated again.

“Something else?”

“I wasn't saying you—”

“I know.” He tapped the sheet. “He asked quickly. I answered. Leave enough space in this column or we'll be squeezing it in again on Monday.”

I sat and widened the column. He held the other edge with a ruler so I could draw straight. We didn't conduct a ceremony repairing our friendly relations. Lunchtime arrived. He took his half of the material, and I put mine in the work folder.

Tom ate faster than I did. Hearing about my claim to the column, he asked whether I'd expected him to say well done.

“A little.”

“Well done.” He closed his container.

I laughed hard enough almost to choke. Waiting until I'd had water, he admitted disliking that sort of public correction himself. It felt like requesting an extra slice of cake. But say nothing, and sometimes all you got was the plate.

“Perhaps he was going to ask me anyway.”

“You saved him a sentence, then.”

I didn't immediately believe that. Still, when I passed my colleague's desk after lunch, he naturally asked whether I needed another source page. I did. He handed it over. Neither of us behaved as though we'd just established a winner.

Before leaving, I clipped together the completed part and noted what the outstanding item still required, rather than treating a filled space as finished work. Both sets stayed at the office. Outside, I remembered a notice I'd seen returning from lunch, inside the neighborhood activity room's window. A small art display, open Saturday, free. Most of the artists lived nearby.

I didn't return to copy every opening hour. I remembered that visitors could enter after ten, on a street I'd walked many times. Enough to let me decide tomorrow.

That evening, I told Lumi. First she asked whether my colleague often talked over people. I hadn't noticed that before. Even talking over wasn't quite accurate today. He'd described what we'd done together; I simply hadn't wanted to remain seated, waiting for somebody to add my name.

“I'd rather like to hear you say those two sentences loudly,” she said.

“The office edition is sold out.”

“Give me the evening edition.”

Laughing, I refused. She laughed too, without immediately changing the subject.

“You're willing to ask people outside to hear you now.”

Softly said. I'd taken it as praise until the end, when I sensed something I'd missed.

“Don't you like that?”

“I like that you can.” A pause. “I don't like always hearing about it afterward.”

The lamp lit a small patch of grain in the table. I wanted to say she'd heard more of the story than anyone, then decided ranking listeners by word count wouldn't help.

“At the time, I wanted someone to say they understood. I wasn't specifically thinking of you. On the way home, I did. I wanted to tell you how stupid I felt saying it.”

“You weren't that stupid.”

“You didn't hear me.”

A moment of silence, then she laughed. “That's true.”

I laughed too, my shoulders easing. When my colleague held the ruler, had he deliberately avoided my hand? He hadn't touched me. Why? That small part sounded more like understanding than his saying I know. Thinking back, I remembered the ruler pressing down the paper, though not his expression.

As we talked, I found I didn't need to attach a more dignified ending to the morning. Lumi still disliked missing it. The colleague she heard about hadn't been made a little less capable than he really was.

Later, I mentioned possibly visiting the art display tomorrow. With Tom? Alone, if I still wanted to go in the morning. She'd thought the weekend after the book stall would be spent at home.

“I'll be home too.”

Too quickly said, as though all the remaining hours proved the few outside hardly mattered. I paused and tried again. “I'm curious about the display.”

“What do you want to see?”

“Don't know yet. The window had an extremely unconvincing cat.”

“You're walking over there to insult a cat?”

“It could be a dog.”

At last, Lumi laughed. I wasn't planning to buy a painting, I told her. That wouldn't determine its species, she replied. We argued for a while, provisionally classifying a picture neither of us had properly seen as three different animals. If I went tomorrow, I finally said, I'd read the label first.

Before bed, I didn't write down the feelings I ought to bring to an exhibition. My coat lay over the chair, a few coins from shopping still in its pocket. I put them on the table and hung the coat properly. Lumi's last remark was that she did still want the evening edition.

“Separating the statuses was my suggestion,” I told her.

“And the rest?”

“He changed the names and layout.”

“Didn't feel stupid this time?”

“A little.”

She laughed softly. She could hear that. I didn't repeat it a third time.
