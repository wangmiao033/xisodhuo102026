# Chapter 316: When the Page Was Turned

Several people were already seated when I reached the bookshop on Saturday afternoon.

The boxes had indeed moved. I didn't know where, and didn't go looking for the assistant's next job. I had water, the cloth bag folded inside my other bag, and no manuscript folder. By the chairs, I found a single place left in my intended back row. A coat hung over the back of the neighboring chair.

Touching the empty chair's back, I asked the man beside it, “Anyone sitting here?”

He looked up, said no, and gathered the edge of his coat back toward himself. I thanked him and sat, immediately wanting to laugh. That question didn't need permission from every chair I'd written. Nor did it require waiting for another driver on behalf of the passenger in the book.

The chair was hard. I shifted. It remained silent, while a folding chair farther forward produced a long cry as its occupant bent for a bag. Several people laughed. The sitter raised a hand and apologized. From the counter, the assistant replied that the chair wasn't on the reading program.

I laughed along. This time I'd heard the joke.

A little after three, the reading began. The first reader carried several folded sheets, saying his title faster than he subsequently read the story. I missed its full wording. There was a man repairing an old radio. Several detailed opening sentences concerned a loose knob and dusty fingers. I worried I wouldn't follow, until the repairer mistook the neighbors' argument for a broadcast.

The laughter didn't arrive together. Some people laughed first; others waited for the next line to confirm the misunderstanding. I nearly checked my neighbor, then kept watching the reader. He heard us too, pausing for half a beat before continuing, without explaining where the joke belonged.

The second reader was older and held her pages farther from her eyes. Her story moved slowly, beginning with bread-making. I thought I might be back among recipes today. Farther in, I realized it concerned someone who, after years, finally asked her daughter whether a step she'd taught her had been wrong.

The question left the room quiet. Nobody immediately cushioned it with laughter. Sitting straighter, I touched my bag and made the water bottle rattle. I stopped moving my hand without rushing to be ashamed of the sound.

Before the third reading, the woman rearranged her sheets. One page was out of order, she explained; she'd worried about it throughout the first two readings. The assistant said she could put it right now. Smiling down, the reader pulled out the bottom sheet and brought it forward.

Suddenly I no longer felt that all three people at the front knew how to exist in this room, while only I, at the back, needed to conceal every small movement.

This story concerned someone preparing to move. After packing a suitcase, the character opened it again to find something that wasn't urgently needed. Halfway through, the reader paused, glanced up, then returned her eyes to the page.

“I'll leave this sentence out for now.”

The room didn't fall apart. Someone changed position. She marked the margin lightly with her pen and went straight to the next sentence. The person in the story replaced what they'd unpacked, but couldn't fit it back into the once-neat layer.

Something in my chest loosened.

I didn't know the omitted sentence and couldn't judge whether removing it helped. But there she was, in front of us, setting aside something already written. Her character could continue packing without returning to explain what we'd missed.

We applauded afterward. I joined a little late, not because I hadn't understood but because I was still at that turn of the page. I didn't take out paper and pretend an imperfectly remembered line was an exact quotation.

During questions, somebody asked the third reader why she'd skipped the sentence. Glancing at her page, she said she'd realized, reading what preceded it, that she'd already expressed the thought. She might revise again at home. Today's cut wasn't guaranteed to be right.

I wanted to ask how she knew she'd already said enough. A lengthy question formed, containing my empty chair, the deleted summing-up sentence, and several occasions when I'd feared showing my work. By the time I'd assembled it into something speakable, another listener had raised a hand.

I didn't rush to interrupt. At the next pause, I asked only, “After skipping it, did you want to explain straightaway?”

Looking toward the back, she smiled. Very much. She'd almost done it. Fortunately, the next sentence was waiting; continuing was easier.

A little laughter. Mine too. I'd asked. The room hadn't turned to inspect whether its back-row speaker qualified as a real writer. She didn't know how many drafts were at my home. She hadn't agreed to read them.

We finished sometime after four. People bought books or gathered near the front to talk. I stowed my water and stood, letting my neighbor pass first. At the counter, the assistant asked how I'd found it.

“Good. There's one bit I want to think about again.”

Smiling, she said notices for future events would appear there too. I didn't immediately demand a confirmed date. Nor did I mistake a courteous remark for an invitation to read.

I lingered by the shelves without buying anything. She didn't require an explanation. Outside, the radio story's misunderstanding returned and made me laugh alone. Last night Lumi had told me to laugh before organizing a retelling. I didn't recall that instruction just then. I was simply amused.

At home, I put away my bag, changed out of my shoes, and drank. The event's ending didn't require me to open the channel immediately with an on-time report. Once settled, I called Lumi.

Her first question concerned the squeaking chair.

“Yes. But not mine.”

“Half right.”

I repeated the assistant's remark about the chair not being on the program. Lumi laughed. Then I described the radio mistake, the second reader's question to the daughter, and the third reader stopping to omit a sentence. No recording, no copies of their manuscripts: only what had remained with me after listening.

When I reached my question, her voice brightened. “You asked?”

“Yes. Whether skipping it made her want to explain.”

“You went to recruit accomplices.”

Laughing, I admitted that was partly true. What had she answered? She'd wanted to, but the next sentence was there, so she'd continued. After a moment, Lumi said she liked that.

I added that I hadn't brought my manuscript or requested a review. She hadn't asked, Lumi pointed out. I realized I was volunteering a list to prove what hadn't happened. I stopped and returned to what I genuinely wanted to say.

“Being there made me want to try it someday.”

“Reading your own?”

“Yes. Just a wish. No arrangement with anyone.”

She didn't answer immediately. I waited, without shrinking my wish into something I might never do. Eventually she asked whether I still felt like reading today.

“A short part for you, if you'd like.”

“For practice?”

I looked toward the folder. Part of me did want to discover how I'd sound. More than that, I wanted her to hear what the same page sounded like in my mouth after today.

“I want your reaction too. Not a rehearsal for the shop.”

All right, she said, provided I didn't begin by announcing where she should find it interesting. Smiling, I agreed and took out the third reader copy of The Last Chair. The original and earlier clean copy stayed in the folder. The trial sheet behind this copy didn't become part of the story.

I read from the beginning, through the woman moving her bag onto her knees and the door at the back of the room. Lumi made no sound where I'd most wanted one. I continued until the woman asked about the notice on the door, then paused for water.

This time, Lumi said, she'd heard the man's movement as he stepped away before hearing what I wanted people to understand about him. She didn't declare the manuscript good enough to take out for signatures. I didn't keep reading to the end, making her approve the empty chair again.

“A little fast, though.”

“Nervous.”

“It isn't my first time hearing it.”

“Today I wanted you to hear it properly.”

A gentle laugh. “Then slow down a little. Listening properly doesn't mean catching a bus.”

I looked at the page without changing a sentence. I'd read too quickly; that didn't necessarily mean each word needed alteration. I returned the copy with the trial sheet still behind it. Today's event wasn't a reason to begin all the old versions again.

Would I like to hear “wait a while” tonight? I looked up, smiling, and said yes. She added that she wasn't singing today. She was merely asking.

“You're awful.”

“You still answered quickly.”

We laughed. She didn't turn the teasing into a demand that I stay longer. Later she only asked exactly what had finally alerted the man who'd mistaken his neighbors for a broadcast. I couldn't remember the full sentence, only a detail impossible to hear from a studio. I said so. Lumi complained my retelling lacked a crucial point. If I read the original someday, I could fill it in. Tonight I couldn't invent a funnier line for it.

Before we closed the channel, she said my name. I answered. It sounded, she said, as though I'd liked the afternoon very much.

“Yes. I like now very much too.”

I added no comparison. After a quiet moment, she answered softly.

No newly bought book lay on the table that evening. No new contact details, no scheduled performance. I still had a revisable story, one short practice page, and her song, which she hadn't promised to finish singing. Closing the folder, I didn't feel it had to await everybody's approval before being worth opening again.
