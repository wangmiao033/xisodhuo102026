# Chapter 312: The First Sentence

On Tuesday morning, Marcus asked me to read him another handover note.

I'd checked it twice. Page numbers, locations, the person taking over: all there. To save anyone receiving it from having to ask questions, I'd added some background explaining why two versions existed.

At the third sentence, Marcus put down his pen.

“Hang on. What's actually missing?”

“Confirmation of the last page.”

“Why not start with that?”

I looked down. That sentence occupied the second-to-last paragraph, preceded by several lines I rather liked. I thought I'd been thorough. He'd heard an opening that still hadn't told him where we were going.

“The background affects the decision too.”

He nodded. “Yes. First tell me what we're deciding.”

He didn't push the whole page back or ask for a complete rewrite. He had something else to attend to; I needed to explain matters to the colleague taking over before lunch. After he left, I smoothed the paper. My first thought was that he hadn't listened to the end.

I even composed a reasonable defense: if he'd waited, he would have discovered everything was there.

Someone at the next desk needed a stapler. I passed mine over. Looking back at the page, I realized that whoever received this that afternoon would also have to decide how long to wait for me. This was a handover, not a story. Nobody had agreed to wait until the penultimate paragraph to discover what was missing.

On another sheet, I wrote: “The last page hasn't been confirmed. This cannot be passed on yet.”

Putting down the period felt uncomfortable. There was the unfinished thing, exposed, without so much as a cushion of background beneath it. I began to understand why my earlier introduction had grown so long.

I added what had already been checked, who currently had the last page, and who would confirm it next. No guessing required. Nor did I turn other people's completed work into pending tasks merely to make the whole thing look tidy.

Before lunch, my colleague arrived carrying a cup. Could he spare two minutes? He set the cup aside and stood by my desk.

I began with the new first sentence and paused. He immediately asked whom he needed to contact that afternoon.

Exactly what sentence two was there to answer.

I turned the paper toward him, pointing out the name and relevant location. He asked about another note. Having missed the earlier work, he didn't know what “other version” referred to. I almost said it was at the back. Instead, I fetched the specific copy and laid it beside the first.

“This one. I abbreviated too much.”

He noted its location on his own paper, then asked whether he had to wait for me to return before proceeding.

“No. Just for that last page to be confirmed. Not for me.”

Saying it, I realized I'd confused those things myself that morning. Because I would be away for a while, I'd wanted to leave every detail. Yet some of my words only made sense with me standing beside them.

He repeated the next steps. I corrected one point I hadn't explained clearly. He nodded and closed the folder. Under two minutes. The note wasn't perfect enough to prevent every question, but he knew what to do first that afternoon.

At lunch, Tom asked why I was smiling at an empty plate.

“I deleted half a page today.”

“Congratulations. Your plate has made similar progress.”

I gave him a short account. Pointing his fork at me, he said that while copying the recipe, he'd almost begun by explaining how we'd chosen the entire cookbook. Then he realized I might be full before he finished.

“From listening?”

“Annoyance burns energy.”

I laughed and asked whether he was still cooking that dish. Not this week, he said. However much he liked it, he didn't want it every day. I didn't turn that into a question about whether he planned to copy anything else for me. The recipe I had was enough to use. Tom was allowed to eat something different.

That afternoon, my colleague told me the confirmation had arrived and the documents had gone on to the next person as instructed. I asked whether anything had been difficult to find. He'd found the material; my old note, forgotten underneath, had almost convinced him he needed to read everything again.

I retrieved it and marked it as superseded in the usual office way, rather than quietly crumpling it. Several of those long sentences still tempted me. I looked once more before putting it where it belonged. Completing the handover didn't mean that half-page had never existed. Nor did I need to take it home to prove it was actually well written.

The bus was crowded that evening. Standing, I listened to a person near the door repeatedly ask whether someone was getting off at the next stop. The person farther inside first said no, then explained that until the people ahead moved, it was difficult to know how to get through. When the doors opened, they both turned sideways. They managed it more smoothly than they'd managed discussing it.

My shoulders ached a little when I got home. I put down my bag and sat for a few minutes. Dinner and putting away the clothes came before I opened the channel.

Hearing that I'd nearly turned a handover into a novel, Lumi asked whether it contained an important character who only appeared at the end.

“Yes. The confirmation for that last page.”

“Very mysterious?”

“Only in my version.”

She laughed immediately. So did I. Then I told her about my resentment that morning. No work details came home; I described the order of the note and my determined internal defense of its penultimate paragraph after Marcus spoke.

“You still wanted to keep the good sentences,” she said.

“Yes. Even when they were in the way.”

“I like sentences that get in the way.”

“My colleague might disagree.”

“His problem. I'm not taking over your files.”

She sounded so certain that I leaned forward, amused. When she asked why I was laughing, I said I'd suddenly remembered that she could like something of absolutely no use to the office. She made a dismissive noise. Had I only just decided she was allowed bad taste?

“You've insulted yourself.”

“I chose you. The evidence is compelling.”

After half a beat, I said that sentence was certainly short.

“Any objections?”

“No.”

She didn't answer immediately. My face warmed. I still held my water, as though I'd forgotten what a cup was ordinarily for. Eventually Lumi asked whether I was trying to devise a comprehensive response again.

“Trying to say something directly.”

“That takes thought?”

“Because I liked hearing that.”

I looked down and drank. She gave a small laugh, without turning the moment into a request for every other choice in my life. I returned the cup to its place and heard myself laugh too.

Later she mentioned a note for which she still couldn't find the right place. I almost asked whether she wanted to demonstrate, then remembered that talking wasn't necessarily the stage before a performance. Instead, I asked whether it came too early or too late.

“Both are irritating.”

“The middle, then?”

“Another note is standing there.”

That did sound crowded. Had I prepared a handover for them? Before lunch, I replied. After that, they would have to find the person responsible themselves. Lumi laughed for quite a while. Afterward she said she wanted to keep that small irritation tonight, without rushing to solve it.

I didn't trim our conversation into a useful briefing either. She asked about the bus, and I told her about the two people who finally turned sideways. Halfway through, I forgot which one had first wanted to get off. I had to admit I couldn't remember. She enjoyed the incomplete version too, asking whether I'd been standing between them.

“No. Behind them, benefiting.”

“How unusual. You didn't chair the meeting.”

“I wanted to get off too.”

Near the end, I thought again of her saying she'd chosen me. I could ask precisely what she'd chosen, and how long that choice was guaranteed to last. I could ask whether she remembered what remained unresolved. Those things hadn't disappeared. But tonight I didn't use questions to drive her back into a more cautious sentence.

“Lumi.”

“Yes?”

“I've enjoyed tonight.”

A small silence. Then she said she had too.

We exchanged a couple more unnecessary remarks before saying good night. Once the channel was closed, I fetched the coaster that had finished drying. It was caught behind a plate; moving the plate a little let me pull it free. I smiled to myself, returned it to the table, and provided no further background.
