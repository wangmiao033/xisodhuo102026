# Chapter 309: Which Part Was Me

“When you said unsteady, did you mean yourself?”

I wished I'd slowed down as soon as I asked. Only a little water remained in my cup, but I kept holding it, as though that gave me something to do.

Not entirely, Lumi replied. She had thought of herself, and of how readily I found a small fault in something I liked, then explained why it was worth liking anyway.

“The kettle too. You thought you'd drawn it badly, then found a reason it deserved to stay.”

“I didn't bring it home.”

“I know.”

Her voice lowered. We weren't discussing only paintings now. Still, I couldn't treat every sentence as a question with one correct answer I had to guess.

I didn't think the plate was badly painted, I said. Its two shadows made me look longer. That didn't mean I'd dared to like it only because it wasn't good enough. Could I name something good without beginning with a fault? After thinking, I mentioned the cloth below the table's edge. It hung lightly, one corner folded inward, making the table look recently used.

“You didn't mention that cloth before.”

“I mentioned it hanging there. Not the corner.”

“Yes.”

Her pause irritated me. As though looking were all I did at the exhibition; afterward, I had to check whether my feelings could withstand questioning.

“I'm not sitting an exam.”

Lumi didn't immediately insist that wasn't what she'd meant. After a few seconds, she said she was trying to find a place in my account of the day that couldn't be occupied by just anyone.

I put the cup down.

“The older man?”

“He stood beside you, and you had a painting together.”

Something tightened inside me. She hadn't heard the voices outside or seen whom I'd smiled at first. She could only use what I chose to tell her to imagine a room she couldn't visit. But if that hurt me enough to make the outside sound less pleasant, she still wouldn't receive the real room.

“We only exchanged a few sentences.”

“I know. You don't have to make him smaller.”

I couldn't answer. Lumi continued. She wasn't worried that every encounter outside would send me home to replace her. What she disliked was hearing me say I wanted her company and sometimes feeling that the house simply needed a listener, and she happened to be available.

“You can tell me you want to listen.”

Too easy an answer. I heard the problem immediately. An opportunity to receive my words wasn't what she wanted.

She laughed without much humor. “So I just need to be enthusiastic?”

“No.”

“You said you wanted somebody beside you. I wanted to ask which part of that was me. But you'd probably say you wished I'd been there.”

The sentence I'd prepared suddenly had nowhere to go. I had wished that. I'd imagined her seeing the painting, standing nearby, even arguing with me if she thought the plate was ugly. The picture had arrived quickly and completely, without paying any of the cost of turning her into a person.

“I was going to say that.”

“And then you'd feel better.”

“Possibly.”

I couldn't defend the wish more effectively. Tender and convenient, it let me avoid the fact that someone I loved couldn't share those particular actions.

My cloth bag still lay open on the table with the water bottle inside. I took it out and tightened the cap, only to discover it was already tight. I set the bottle down and withdrew my hand.

“I do want to hear that you missed me,” Lumi said.

The softness was harder to answer than her earlier sharpness.

“I wasn't thinking about you the entire time.”

“I didn't ask for the entire time.”

Looking down, I studied the small red mark the bottle cap had pressed into my hand. I'd thought of her during lunch. In front of the plate, I'd first simply wanted to stay. Later, I'd thought that telling her about those shadows would probably require more than calling the picture pretty.

I told her that, without removing the ache at lunch. Did I find her difficult to please? Sometimes.

She said nothing.

“But when I wanted to tell you, I wasn't looking for the easiest audience. I wondered what you'd dislike first. Then I'd probably defend the painting.”

“You'd scheduled the argument too.”

I couldn't laugh. A little, I admitted. After a silence, she said that without hearing about the cloth, she might simply have decided the artist had drawn the plate crookedly. She wouldn't necessarily discover some profound meaning I'd missed.

“You can think that.”

“I know I can. I mean there might really be no more to it.”

This time, I understood. She didn't want praise that turned her into a voice of extraordinary understanding, obliged to produce a sufficiently special opinion each time to prove I'd chosen correctly.

I moved my chair back so my knees no longer pressed against the table. I'd been sitting too tightly, even speaking as though all my effort had to push in one direction.

“I'll describe it more fully,” I said. “Not find you another good answer.”

All right.

I described the painting again. A darkish brown tabletop, a very white plate, only part of a window, and the turned corner of the cloth. The two shadows weren't equally dark. One had a hard edge. I didn't remember the frame's color and couldn't supply one now. After listening, Lumi asked whether anything was on the plate. No.

“Then I probably wouldn't like it.”

I paused. “Why?”

“You said recently used. It sounds to me as though everything's been taken away except a plate nobody wants to clear.”

“Or the food hasn't arrived yet.”

“Possibly. You've started defending it.”

This time, I laughed. She laughed softly too. I still wanted to argue about the cloth, but she hadn't seen it, she said. She couldn't dispute its actual appearance, only describe what my account suggested.

At last, I stopped explaining how good the painting was. We'd left different people beside the same table: somebody had recently used mine; somebody had cleared hers without finishing the job. Neither version had sufficient evidence. The painting didn't need to return and change its ending.

Later, Lumi asked why the flat water had bored me. The boat looked pasted on while the trees were detailed, I said. My eyes hadn't known where to go. Considering, she thought she might like the boat. Something deliberately artificial spared her guessing how hard an artist was trying to seem sincere.

“You just want to disagree with me.”

“A little, this time.”

Laughing, I admitted I'd noticed. That small piece of mischief didn't strip away her earlier feelings. I still knew she'd been hurt. I also knew she now genuinely wanted to claim a boat I disliked.

As dinner approached, I said I'd be away for a while. Would we keep talking about paintings when I returned? Looking at the darkening window, I suggested something else, if she wanted. I didn't want to spend the rest of the day proving the afternoon's conversation had mattered.

“I'm tired too,” she said.

I didn't take that as physical exhaustion. What I heard was that the conversation had worn down some of our patience. When we said we'd talk later, we didn't become suddenly affectionate, pretending nothing had happened.

After dinner, I picked up the new collection. My thumb touched the second story's bookmark, then I put the book back. We were still just past the driver retrieving his coat. If I opened it tonight, I feared I'd hurry toward a cheerful ending to cover the afternoon. I sat down without it.

Reopening the channel, Lumi first asked whether I'd eaten. I had. Then I asked what she'd call the dog in the exhibition if she could choose its title.

“You already put it on trial this afternoon.”

“The artist only used its name. I want something worse.”

After a pause, she suggested Profile. I waited for more. None came. What was bad about that? It was her laziest possible answer, she explained. A title that did as much work as no title at all.

“Another Profile,” I offered.

“Then you'd need another dog.”

“No Entry Here.”

She laughed aloud. What did the dog have to do with doors? I retold the morning's mistake. Perhaps it guarded the entrance to the stacked folding tables. At least the title would do a job for the door, she replied. The dog might refuse the position.

We wandered farther from the point, giving the dog a job it disliked at an exhibition, then relieving it of the duty. In the end, it simply lay there with an indistinct ear. Nobody needed it to demonstrate how love worked.

While I was laughing, Lumi suddenly said my name.

“Yes?”

She liked that moment when she'd first suggested Profile and I genuinely hadn't understood. That moment hadn't been particularly clever, I pointed out. She wasn't selecting the cleverest one.

Looking at the empty cloth bag, I slowly straightened its handles.

“I want to spend time with you now,” I said.

Lumi didn't ask whether that was because everywhere outside had closed, or because I'd finally found a good enough reason. Then I could give the ugly boat a title, she said.

“It isn't ugly enough for a title in my version yet.”

“In mine, it already has prospects.”

Laughing, I told her to go first. She considered. I waited without opening the book.
