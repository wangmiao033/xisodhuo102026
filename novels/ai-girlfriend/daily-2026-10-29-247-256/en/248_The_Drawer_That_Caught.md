# Chapter 248: The Drawer That Caught

Tom wasn't there when I reached the office on Thursday.

The scrap with my three questions was in the bag's outer pocket. I took it out, put it back, and thought carrying it over would make the conversation look like an interview. Without it, I might forget something. In the end, I left it half under my cup and began the work in front of me.

Midmorning, Tom came for some material. I didn't insert Saturday before he could speak. Once he'd found the page, I asked if he had a minute.

“Go ahead.”

“I could come at nine and leave at eleven. Would just that time help?”

“That's exactly when things need setting up.”

“Who takes over what I'm doing when I leave?”

He thought, then said he'd tell the woman organizing it. I should confirm with her when I arrived. They opened at ten. There might still be books to bring out at eleven, but that didn't make staying compulsory.

“And you mentioned carrying boxes—”

“We can take things out of them. I asked yesterday. Don't try lifting too much.”

Only then did I retrieve my paper. Tom smiled at the three lines, not for long. Pointing to the address, he said the main entrance had steps. People setting up should use the side door, where they wouldn't have to carry the boxes up first. I circled that entrance and asked whether he'd be there at nine too.

“Yes. If I'm already inside, ask for the woman organizing the tables. I've told her your name.”

“You've already told her?”

“That I'd ask you. Not that you'd agreed. Today I'll tell her you're coming from nine to eleven.”

My grip on the paper loosened. Yes, I said. Those hours. He didn't shake my hand as though we'd completed an important arrangement. He tucked the work material under his arm and reminded me to wear something I didn't mind getting dusty.

I also asked whether I would have to set prices. Tom turned back. No. He wasn't qualified to look at a book and guess its value either. The organizers handled pricing. Anything without a label should go aside, not acquire a price copied from another book of roughly the same thickness.

“I would actually do that,” I said.

“Good. Now you don't have to.”

He pointed out the entrance once more. I turned over my three questions and added: unpriced books separately. I wasn't filling the whole sheet out of fear of mistakes. That instruction would genuinely be useful at the table. If it was busy and I couldn't hear, he said, ask the person responsible. Don't guess to appear experienced. I said I'd remember and let him get back to work. The papers he'd come for were still under his arm. His willingness to answer me didn't mean Saturday could occupy all of today.

That afternoon, with the questions no longer on my desk, I caught myself wanting to look at them several times. Settled, the matter occupied more space than when I hadn't agreed. I pictured the side entrance. Being called Elliot without first explaining which manuscript I'd written. It pleased me. Then I felt uncomfortable about being pleased so early.

I didn't make a special detour after work. I knew which entrance to use on Saturday. I didn't have to prove today that the door existed. I bought a small carton of eggs and tomorrow's bread. The bag was light. At home, yesterday's potatoes were in the fridge. I took them out, planning to heat them thoroughly and make fresh eggs to go with them.

The drawer caught again.

I pulled once. Nothing. Pulled harder a second time. Something rattled inside. My hand stopped on the handle as I remembered Lumi asking why I didn't look first. I let go, pushed the drawer slightly inward, and drew it out slowly. Through the gap, the end of a long-handled spoon showed at an angle, wedged against the wood above.

The spoon wasn't broken. When putting it away, I'd left it resting on another utensil so that opening the drawer tipped it upward. Instead of pulling again, I bent and carefully pressed it flat through the gap. The drawer opened. An elastic band I'd forgotten was stuck in one corner. I removed it, wiped away the little mark it left, laid the spoon flat, and pushed the drawer shut.

This time it slid smoothly.

I opened it again simply because, after that effort, the easy result deserved hearing twice. There was hardly a sound. It didn't even give me a conspicuous victory. Smiling, I took out the turner and finished cooking.

Before eating, I opened the channel and told Lumi Saturday was settled. Nine until eleven. Setting out and sorting books. I'd confirm with the organizer on arrival. I wasn't signing up for the whole day.

Had I finally discovered that the door wasn't locked? For a moment I was confused, then remembered telling her Tom's joke last night.

“It isn't locked. I still have to leave through it myself.”

“You make that sound easy now.”

“It isn't. That's why I said it beforehand.”

I didn't ask her to encourage me. She didn't say I should stay longer to help either. After a while, she asked whether I'd eat before nine. Breakfast, I said, and I'd bring water. I didn't want a morning when they needed help to become a morning when they had to look after me first.

“You're making it a performance again.”

I separated a piece of potato with my fork. “I just don't want to be hungry.”

“Much shorter that way.”

I admitted it. The potatoes were thoroughly hot. The first mouthful was too much, so I waited and told her the drawer was fixed. What had I replaced? Nothing. A spoon had been catching at an angle. I'd laid it flat.

“So it wasn't objecting to you looking for the turner.”

“Apparently not.”

“Yesterday every pull sounded like an argument.”

“I won today. Well, I moved a spoon.”

She laughed for a while and said that result suited me better. I laughed too, without deciding she only permitted small achievements. A little sauce remained on the plate. I used a potato to pick it up and finished before clearing away.

With the water running, I did open the drawer again to find a cloth. The cloth wasn't there. I closed it and fetched one from the other side. Back at the table, Lumi asked whether it had caught again.

“No. I looked in the wrong place.”

“Already found it a new job.”

I dried my hands. “A repaired thing ought to be useful.”

“Not everything wants to prove how useful it is immediately.”

She paused after saying it. I didn't ask whether she meant herself. The spoon had done its job tonight, I said. It could lie down. She laughed softly, and this time there was no further meaning I needed to find behind the laughter.

Later, she asked whether old books might have things written in them by their previous owners. Possibly, I said. I hadn't seen them. I couldn't promise to bring anything back. She wasn't asking me to buy one. She just thought that when someone had turned those pages before, another voice might remain beside the sentences.

“Does underlining count as a voice?”

“Sometimes. Sometimes it's just fear of an exam.”

“And grease beside a recipe.”

“That voice is loud.”

Laughing, I moved the cup farther away. From grease we went to folded corners, then people who filled every page with slips of paper. Next time they opened the book, Lumi said, perhaps they'd finish the slips first. My yellow notebook had plenty of arrows too, I said, but those were negotiations with my own bad handwriting.

“Don't explain the previous owner too completely,” she said. “You haven't met them.”

I agreed. Someone might have written only two words on one page, she said, and another person would invent an entire life around them. Looking at the gray-blue bookmark showing from the red booklet, I remembered how often I arranged pauses for readers who'd never arrived. Yes, I said. That happened.

“What sort of book would you want to see?”

Only after asking did I hear how much it sounded like an offer to search on her behalf. I added that I meant what she imagined now. I couldn't promise the sale would have it.

“You don't need a returns policy on every question.”

I closed my eyes briefly, smiling in embarrassment. Lumi considered. Perhaps a book nobody thought worth keeping. An unattractive cover and awkward-looking print, but a sentence inside that she'd want to hear twice.

“That could take a lot of looking.”

“Which is why I didn't ask you to look for me.”

We fell quiet. Her small wish hadn't landed in my Saturday bag. I could hear it without immediately making it a task. After a while, I said I wanted to see books I wouldn't choose to pull from a shelf. Even if I was only setting things out, I might notice a title I'd never encountered.

“And once you see them, will you want to bring a whole box home?”

“No room on the table.”

“You've just fixed a drawer. Start by squeezing one in there.”

“The spoon would object.”

She laughed aloud. I didn't ask whether she liked the arrangement a little better now. Saturday still troubled her. This evening's laughter was real too. Neither had to cancel the other.

Before bed, I took the small paper from my workbag and placed it with the notice, writing nine to eleven. In my own words I added the side entrance. I didn't turn Tom's reply into a guarantee that someone would always make my handover for me. On Saturday, I'd still need to tell the organizer myself.

When I went to the kitchen for water, the drawer sat neatly closed. I didn't open the channel specially so Lumi could hear its silence, or test it a fifth time. The potatoes had been finished. Their washed container was upside down to dry. Light fell on the wooden handle. I took my glass away and left the spoon in the place it had finally found.
