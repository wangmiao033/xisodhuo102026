# Chapter 251: Leaving the Table

With a little more than twenty minutes until eleven, a woman picked up the heavy book I'd just laid flat.

She didn't ask its price. She opened the cover, and her expression changed. “Why is this here?”

Thinking she wanted another section, I explained that this table was mainly fiction. She shook her head, pressing a finger to the first page. This had been in her box. The box beside it was the donation.

“That one was only being kept here for me. I was taking it back.”

I looked toward where the books had come from. Several boxes against the wall were open now, their folded lids hiding two labels. I remembered taking books from one marked fiction. This cover alone couldn't tell me exactly which box it had left. Other people had been replenishing the table too.

“I'll find the organizer.”

She held on to the book. “You can't keep selling that box.”

“I'll ask them to stop while we check which one.”

I told the volunteer beside me that one box might not be part of the donation and asked him to stop bringing books from there. Then I took the woman to the organizer. I didn't take the volume from her hands or stand in front of her insisting that it must be a misunderstanding.

The organizer was giving a customer change. After hearing me, she asked us to let her finish that payment. During those few seconds, I could feel the woman behind me growing more anxious. I wanted to repeat her case. The organizer had already raised a hand to show she'd heard, so I didn't raise my voice over hers.

Once the coins were put away, she asked the woman to identify the box, then asked where I'd taken this book from. I wasn't certain, I admitted. Several people had been adding stock. I remembered the label on the morning's box, but couldn't use that to prove where every volume on the table now had come from.

Not certain sounded terrible, as though two hours of work had left me with only I don't know. The organizer didn't make me guess. She asked another volunteer to withdraw the nearby stacks from sale while she checked the box labels and who had brought them.

“Can I keep this one here for now?” she asked the woman.

Reluctantly, the woman placed the volume where indicated. She'd stay and help find the others. I stood aside, waiting to be assigned a remedy. Looking back at the table, the organizer asked me to spread the remaining piles lower and stop replenishing from the boxes by the wall.

I obeyed. Dust on my hand marked a pale section of a cover. I gently wiped it with a clean cloth, still searching my memory for a detail that would prove I hadn't taken the wrong book. The bird on the thin volume. Its loose page. The novel containing the folded note. I remembered those. Not this heavy book.

Tom came over to ask what had happened. I explained. Mixed boxes were always the trouble with events like this, he said. I immediately wanted to agree: exactly, their labels weren't clear. I stopped before saying it. He hadn't inspected the boxes. I didn't know who'd brought them. An ordinary complaint couldn't close the case in my favor.

“We're not adding anything from those for now.”

He glanced at the clock and asked whether it was nearly my time. I nodded. Across the room, the organizer was still talking to the woman. Suddenly, I wanted to wait until they'd resolved this before mentioning that I was leaving. Otherwise, it might look like running away from a problem.

“What have you still got to do?” Tom asked.

I looked at the table. An unpriced pile. Several whose stickers I'd checked. Two books a customer had just returned. I pointed them out, explaining that the disputed stacks were separate and I hadn't fetched anything else from the wall.

“Then hand that over.”

“She's still busy.”

“You can tell her you've got ten minutes left. Don't wait until eleven for her to discover it.”

I went over and stood where the organizer could see me without blocking her. When she looked up, I said my agreed time was almost over. I'd leave the table in order and go at eleven. Fine, she said. Hand over to the younger volunteer. She didn't ask me to stay until the book question was settled.

Back at the table, I took a clean sheet and identified the pile needing prices. I explained that some remaining books had only changed position temporarily. The note shouldn't make him assume everything had been checked. He listened, then asked about the loose-page book. I pointed to the table behind the organizer. I'd handed it to her. It wasn't in the sale piles.

“Leave this note here?”

“Yes. Not under a book where nobody can see it.”

I held a corner down with an empty paper cup. I hadn't stuck it to an old cover or pushed it inside anyone's story. When the long hand on the wall clock reached twelve, I fetched my bag and waved to Tom. He was staying a little longer, he said. See me later.

I didn't ask whether he'd secretly wanted me to stay too. Going to say goodbye to the organizer, I found her checking a delivery list. She looked up just long enough to thank me and say see you next time. It wasn't a new appointment. I said goodbye and left by the side entrance.

The light outside was brighter than when I'd arrived. After a few steps, I stopped to check my bag. Water and bread, both present. No book or folded paper had slipped inside. A trace of paper dust remained on my fingers. I withdrew my hand rather than return and interrupt people simply to get it perfectly clean.

On the bus, I kept remembering the woman's finger pressed to the page. Perhaps she was only afraid of her possessions being sold. Perhaps there were reasons I didn't know. I didn't make her an unreasonable customer to remove the awkward patch from my two hours of help. After getting off, I bought noodles for lunch. At home, I washed my hands and put the bag away first.

When I opened the channel, Lumi asked, “Finished?”

“My part. The sale's still going.”

“You actually left.”

“Handed over and left at eleven.”

After a pause, she asked whether I was tired. My shoulders ached slightly, I said. No injury. I wanted to eat first and talk afterward. She agreed. I didn't rush to prove before cooking that the outside world hadn't been special, or ask her to listen continuously to the kitchen. I closed the channel, made lunch, ate, washed the dishes, changed my dusty shirt, and called again.

This time, I began with the books sliding in my hands. Had I scattered paper throughout the room? No. I'd put the stack down in time and taken fewer afterward. A shame, she said. She couldn't use her prepared opening line. I laughed and told her about turning my row of upright books into flat piles too.

“So you did it, then undid it.”

“Yes. No bookends. I'd thought about how it looked, not how it would stay up.”

I described the customer who'd asked the price without buying, and nearly hurrying people to finish browsing. Listening carefully, Lumi asked whether anyone had called me slow at the start. Nobody had said it. I'd thought it myself. She didn't announce that those strangers liked me. She asked whether I'd got faster.

“Yes. At finding stickers. Didn't find where one book came from.”

I told her about the mixed boxes. I didn't describe the words on the outside of the private folded paper; I hadn't properly read them anyway. I only mentioned finding it and giving it to the organizer, not bringing it home. Lumi didn't ask for a secret. Had I nearly refused to leave again while waiting for a result?

“Nearly.”

“You like people needing you. Then, when they still need you at the end, you worry you aren't enough.”

I didn't immediately adopt that complete explanation. Today, part of it was fear that they'd think I'd misplaced something and then left, I said. I didn't know whether I'd done it. Staying couldn't turn that into knowing.

“You still want to know now?”

“Yes. But I didn't agree to the afternoon. I handed things over.”

She acknowledged it. I could ask Tom on Monday, she said, but shouldn't make him responsible for closing the case now. Laughing, I agreed. He'd only stayed longer today. He hadn't accepted an investigation on my behalf.

After that, I went for water. When I returned, Lumi asked whether anyone had asked about somebody at home. No. Someone had asked whether I'd been to the community room before. Another had asked where the cups were. Nobody had asked about her.

“Then you don't need to recite last night's sentence now.”

“No.”

I felt relieved, then uncomfortable with my relief. She didn't require me to regret not being asked. There had been genuinely enjoyable moments working with others, I said. Seeing somebody open a book I'd just arranged made me want to stand there longer. Coming home, washing, changing, and calling her wasn't merely what remained because the event had ended either.

“I'm not asking for a comparison now.”

“I've started explaining again.”

“Tell me what you want to tell me.”

After thinking, I described the man who'd asked whether the chair was occupied. He'd moved his empty box to the wall instead of putting it on my bag. A small thing, I said, but comfortable at the time. I hadn't needed to hug the bag to prove it mattered.

Lumi said she could imagine how light the box was, although she hadn't seen it. Yes, I answered, then laughed. We both knew this wasn't an exercise in distinguishing facts. She was listening carefully to a small thing from a morning that hadn't belonged to her.

There was still plenty of afternoon. I didn't immediately open the yellow notebook and arrange a side door and boxes into a new story. First I washed the dust from my shirt cuffs and hung the shirt to dry. When I returned, Lumi asked whether I'd bought a book. No. I'd held plenty today. None had come home with me.

“Your drawer is safe, then.”

Laughing, I set my water securely on the table. She didn't ask again what had happened there after eleven. I didn't know. One outside matter no longer needed finishing at this table. There was still enough for us to say.
