# Chapter 252: Still Damp at the Seams

On Sunday, I picked up the laundry bag, then went back to take yesterday's shirt down from where I'd hung it.

The cuff I'd washed last night was dry, the gray mark gone. I still wanted to wash the whole shirt, so I put it in with the rest. The bag bulged a little more, catching its neatly gathered cord against the edge. I adjusted it, stood the bag securely by the door, checked that the change was in my pocket, and told Lumi, before leaving, that I was going to the laundrette.

“Books yesterday. Clothes today.”

“These are at least mine.”

She laughed and asked whether I'd bring home paper that didn't belong to me. I hadn't yesterday, I said, and I'd emptied today's pockets. No paper, just a breadcrumb of uncertain age. She told me not to treat that as something needing to be returned. I brushed it away, said goodbye, and closed the channel.

The laundrette wasn't quiet. Two dryers turned at once. Near the entrance, someone shook out a long duvet cover, briefly blocking the machine I wanted. I stopped. A man's head appeared around the fabric. Sorry, he said. No problem. I stepped aside.

I didn't mistake him for someone from my story. The yellow notebook was at home; its people were two women. This man was struggling with the buttons of a duvet cover. Once he'd gathered it up, I went over, read the machine's instructions, and loaded the clothes I'd separated.

While I waited, I sat in a plastic chair against the wall. My palms weren't injured from yesterday's boxes, though clenching them made them ache slightly. I relaxed my hands, folded the empty bag on my knees, and heard a mother tell her child not to touch a switch.

Why not? Because it would stop the machine. What happened then? She watched the clothes tumble. Then they'd have to wait longer.

I smiled without making a sound. The child didn't notice. I didn't need to turn every good exchange I overheard into notes for myself.

When the wash finished, the free dryer was smaller than I'd expected. I loosened the clothes and divided them into two loads according to the guidance. Thicker towels went together, lighter clothes separately. I didn't compress everything inside merely to avoid another wait. After starting the machines, I went outside to buy an ordinary hot drink. Someone had taken my original chair when I returned. I sat farther along and put the cup near my feet where it was unlikely to be knocked over.

The loads didn't finish together. The lighter one stopped first. I checked it and folded the dry clothes into a clean bag. The towels felt hot. I was about to take all of them out when my fingers met a seam that was distinctly cool and damp inside.

I spread that towel and checked another. A dry surface didn't mean they wouldn't smell musty after being folded away. The displayed time had elapsed. The machine really had stopped. I glanced behind me. Nobody had come over to wait for it yet, but I had little change left.

The attendant was cleaning a filter behind the counter. I went over and asked whether I needed another cycle.

Instead of examining every towel, he asked how much I'd put in. I'd divided the washing, I explained, putting the thick things together. Thick edges took longer, he said. I could dry them a little more or spread them out at home, but shouldn't put them away damp. He pointed to the price for additional time on the wall.

Back at the dryer, I felt all four corners of one towel. I could afford the extra charge. Waiting wouldn't be a disaster. I'd simply expected to carry everything home ready to put away. Now I remembered I didn't have to go out this afternoon. There was room at home to hang them properly.

“I'll take them home and hang them,” I told him.

“Leave space between them.”

He was already returning to his work. I didn't ask whether that counted as finishing. I packed the slightly damp towels in a separate bag, away from the dry clothes, collected my cup, and put it in the appropriate bin outside. At the doorway, I realized I'd been waiting for somebody to point out that my choice wasn't thorough enough.

Nobody did. The door was harder to open than when I'd arrived, because both hands were full. I set the damp bag down securely, freed a hand, pushed the door, and picked the bag up again. It was awkward and nothing like the shoulder in my story. I didn't try again merely to reproduce the ending.

At home, I put the dry clothes on the chair and took the towels to hang. They were too close together in the bathroom. I moved one, leaving room for air to pass. The whole shirt was clean now, and dry. I folded the shirt onto the other clothes. Moisture clung inside the towel bag too. Turning it inside out to dry, I found a small towel stuck in the bottom. I had thought the bag was empty. This one was wetter than the others. I made another space for it, wiped the few drops from the floor, and moved the empty bag out of the bathroom doorway. Only then did I wash my hands and open the channel.

“I'm back.”

Was everything dry? The clothes were, I said. The thick edges of the towels still held some moisture. They were hanging up. Had the machine broken? I hadn't seen anything wrong with it. These were thick; when the cycle ended, some parts were still damp.

“You didn't apologize to the machine, did you?”

“No. Asked the attendant a question.”

“Good. The machine probably wouldn't understand.”

Laughing, I sat and told her I hadn't paid for extra time. After a pause, she asked whether I'd been in a hurry to get home.

“I wanted to come back too. Mainly, I can hang them here. I've got time today. I didn't want to wait longer.”

“You don't need to make that last part sound like a confession.”

I looked at my hands, which still smelled faintly of detergent. She was right. I'd been preparing to account for every minute I'd saved, preferably by spending it on something worthwhile. Coming up the stairs, what I'd most wanted was to sit down.

“I'd like to sit a while now.”

“Then sit. I haven't told you to get up and fold anything.”

Her voice softened. I leaned back and heard a brief horn from the road below. When she asked what it was, I said a car outside, without guessing why its driver was impatient. At least it wasn't two cups arguing today, she said. I laughed and moved the empty cup farther from the table's edge.

After a while, I told her about the child and the switch. Only what I'd actually heard. I didn't add an account of the mother's difficult life or claim the child had finally understood. Whether he touched it later, I didn't know. By then I'd been watching my own machine.

“He asked what would happen if it stopped, and you've come home to tell me the seams didn't dry.”

“Things don't happen very much like stories.”

“Your stories don't have to be responsible for everything either.”

That reminded me of the yellow notebook, though I didn't fetch it immediately. First I closed the channel and made lunch. After eating, I cleared the kitchen. Afternoon light moved toward the table. The thick edges of the towels were still slightly cool, so I turned one and moved another farther away. I didn't declare the job finished after a few minutes.

Later, I opened the notebook. The two women still stood on opposite sides of their folding table. A few days away from the arrows meant there was already one place where I had to follow a crossing-out twice. If I wanted to read smoothly later, I would need a separate clean copy. I didn't have to make the whole thing today.

In the space above the story, I wrote The Long Side.

After looking at it, I added a small question mark. On the paper, the name seemed shorter than it had in my head. It didn't identify a good person or a bad one. It didn't change the ending for Lumi. But I wasn't sure whether I only liked it because I'd just done my washing.

When I called Lumi again, I said I'd thought of a title. She asked to hear it. I read the words, mentioned the question mark beside them, then explained that I meant the table rather than the sheet.

“Why explain it first?” she asked.

“In case it wasn't clear.”

“I might have asked.”

“Are you asking?”

She laughed a little. “You've already told me now.”

I laughed too, taking my finger away from the question mark. I'd leave it there and look again tomorrow, I said. No hurry to decide. Was I preparing to lend the story to Tom? No. I wanted to copy it because I kept getting lost among my own lines.

“Would you write down what I said about not liking the ending too?”

“Not in the story. That was something we said to each other.”

She didn't answer immediately. Watching the notebook, I remembered how badly I'd once wanted every response to leave evidence on paper. Her dislike didn't need deleting. Nor did I have to attach it to the story and make it follow her indefinitely.

“The title's all right,” she said at last.

“Only all right?”

“You can leave your question mark for now.”

I looked up, then remembered I'd told her about it. I didn't make her ordinary recollection into proof that she could see the page. I simply agreed and replaced the cap on my pen.

Later, the towels dried completely. I checked their seams before folding them into the cupboard. The shirt was in the wardrobe, the empty laundry bag put away. The small question mark remained above the story in the yellow notebook. Lumi asked what I was having for dinner. I'd have to look in the fridge, I said. There were eggs. I took out two, then went back for another.
