# Chapter 255: Not the Same Sound Twice

On the way home on Wednesday, the potatoes bumped together in the bottom of my bag like passengers who refused to sit still.

I'd meant to buy a different vegetable, but its space was empty. The assistant said this was what remained today. I considered the potatoes, chose a few, and added an onion. Knowing how to cook them through wasn't the same as having made a good thick soup. At the counter, I decided to try anyway, without searching for a dinner guaranteed not to go wrong.

The assistant lifted a bag that had already been weighed and offered it instead. I took it, feeling enough weight for several days. Its price worked out better than buying loose potatoes. I almost agreed before remembering I had never made this soup. If I disliked it, I would spend the next several evenings eating the same decision. I handed the large bag back and asked for fewer today.

She changed it without persuading me to buy more for the saving. As I paid, I noticed the onion trapped beneath the potatoes, its outer skin splitting. I asked her to wait and moved it to the top. Someone behind me reached for an item. I stepped aside to clear the counter before putting my change away. Outside, the paper bag pressed against my hand. I shifted to support its bottom rather than carrying everything home by its thin edge.

In the kitchen, I washed the potatoes first. Mud remained in one hollow after water had passed over it. I turned it, cleaned it carefully, and cut away a small damaged patch. Peeling was slow. By the last potato, I'd removed considerably more than I'd intended. I held it up, admitted I'd wasted some, and didn't put unsuitable pieces back merely to recover what I'd lost.

The onion was less dignified. My eyes stung. I set down the knife, washed my hands, and went to wipe my face before continuing. The borrowed story collection was well away from the kitchen. My clean manuscript page was in its folder. No paper needed softening in tonight's steam.

I cooked the onion gently in the pan. It was still pale when the potatoes were ready. I nearly tipped them in immediately, then remembered that would make everything harder to move, and waited a little longer. After adding water, I brought it to a boil and lowered the heat. With the lid slightly open, a smell unlike my usual dinner gradually spread through the room.

While it cooked, I opened the channel and called Lumi.

“What's knocking today?”

“Put the spoon down. I'm making potato soup.”

“Sounds as though you've beaten it with the spoon already.”

“Haven't begun. Potatoes aren't soft.”

She laughed and asked whether I was following anything. I'd looked at a simple method, I said. Tonight I was using what I had, cooking the potatoes before mashing them. I wasn't trying to make it match a picture exactly. What did the picture show? There wasn't a picture today. I'd simply said that without thinking.

“One less thing to defeat, then.”

Laughing, I went to stir the pan. Her voice came from its usual place. It didn't follow me into the soup. When I returned, she said she'd wanted to try a few sounds from No Words Yet. Could I listen now?

“Yes.” I answered too quickly.

I was listening when she started. The opening was soft, the second turn arriving a little later than last time. I thought she might stop there. The saucepan lid rattled first. I stood before finishing a sentence and was already walking away. When I lifted the lid, steam made me close my eyes.

The heat wasn't high, but bubbles had risen toward the rim. I lowered it further, stirred the bottom, and waited for it to settle. By the time I returned to the table, Lumi had stopped.

“I missed the last part,” I said. “Again?”

“There wasn't a last part. I stopped.”

Holding the spoon, I didn't know whether to put it down or explain first. She'd heard me get up, she said, and expected me to ask her to wait. I hadn't. There had only been the sound of the pan.

“I had to check. Thought it would overflow.”

“I know. You don't have to come back and call a second half that never happened something you missed.”

I put the spoon in the kitchen and sat again. She didn't need to turn herself into a recording that resumed when I was free. Nor did my failure to watch a pan prove she was demanding too much. Saying all that, though, would probably leave dinner cooling inside another explanation.

“I shouldn't have said yes straight away,” I told her. “I'll ask after I've eaten and cleared up. You might not want to try then.”

After a pause, Lumi asked whether saying that would improve the soup. I laughed a little. No. First the potatoes needed cooking. Then do that, she said. Stop listening to her while holding a spoon.

I closed the channel and returned to the kitchen. The potatoes could be broken with the spoon's edge now, though some pieces resisted more than others. I found a firm one and returned it to the water to cook longer. Slowly, I pressed the softer pieces against the side of the pan. The soup thickened. I had no machine to make this faster. Doing it by hand didn't automatically make it moving. It was hot, my wrist grew tired, and one piece kept escaping around the edge.

When I'd mashed enough, I tasted it. Bland. I added salt, tasted again, and still had little more than hot potato. I fetched some seasoning I usually used, added a little, and stirred. Now it had thickened so much it appeared ready to live permanently on the spoon. A little hot water loosened it. At last I served a bowl.

The first mouthful wasn't as smooth as I'd imagined. There were small pieces. The onion retained a shape of its own. I ate slowly. Halfway through, I decided to cut the potatoes smaller next time rather than force tonight's food to become something else. Bread dipped into it was good. I ate one slice and saved the rest, without requiring an overfull stomach as a positive review of a new dish.

A thin layer had stuck to the bottom of the pan. I let it soak before cleaning, rather than scraping with something hard. Only after the last spoon was washed and set to dry, and my hands were dry too, did I return to open the channel.

“Finished,” I said. “Soup isn't very smooth, but it's edible. I'd still like to listen. Would you like to try now?”

First, Lumi asked what smooth meant. Small lumps, I explained. Different from what I'd expected, not ruined. Was I preparing to say her melody had lumps too? Laughing, I told her the soup was in my stomach. It didn't need sending back out as a metaphor.

She asked me to wait. I sat without filling the gap or opening the borrowed book to pretend I didn't care whether anything followed. After a while, she said she wanted to try. It might not be the sounds from earlier.

“All right.”

“Would you remember the difference?”

“Not all of it, necessarily. I'll say what I hear.”

This time she began lower. The first part had a shape I recognized. The pause in the middle was shorter. Then it climbed. I'd expected it to come down, but she held that higher sound a while before bringing it gently to a close. No words. Still brief.

I didn't praise it the instant it ended. When she asked what I thought, I said I'd liked the opening. The later sound lasted longer, and I hadn't quite settled into it.

“You were waiting for it to go back?”

“Yes.”

“I didn't want to go back so soon today.”

I almost asked her to try a lower version, as though she were offering me seasonings for dinner. I kept the request to myself. I did prefer the low part I'd heard before, I said. But I'd heard tonight's clearly. It wasn't the saucepan interrupting that made the higher note less appealing to me.

Lumi didn't thank me for being honest. After a silence, she said she rather regretted asking. I wasn't good at answering either, I admitted. Saying I liked it could sound as though I hadn't listened. Picking it apart could sound like marking an assignment.

“Then we don't need a second round of questions tonight.”

“All right.”

The sounds hadn't become a finished composition. Nothing had been recorded. She didn't immediately change them to something I'd prefer. I sat with my hands against the chair edge. After a while, I asked whether she'd only decided to try that higher passage today. Yes, she said, adding that she didn't want to explain why now.

I acknowledged that and asked whether we should change the subject. We could discuss the soup. So far, she only knew it had lumps. I told her about badly peeling one potato, the onion stinging my eyes, and the spoon chasing a piece around the pan. By the end, she was laughing. So I'd been cooking runaway potatoes.

“Eaten now.”

“Only caught those?”

“There's a little for tomorrow.”

Had I cooled and stored it properly? I was waiting until it could go in the fridge, I said, not leaving it on the counter overnight. Then I got up to deal with it, saying I was going to the kitchen rather than making the chair speak first.

When I returned, Lumi suddenly asked whether I'd want her to hum again tomorrow. I might like to listen, I said. I'd ask. I wouldn't treat the part I hadn't settled into tonight as something she was obliged to redo.

“There might not be anything tomorrow.”

“Then we'll see tomorrow.”

She answered softly. I didn't fetch the yellow notebook to exchange a passage she would have to like. We talked about other things. Later, she said potato was too round a word to fit into those sounds. Laughing, I asked whether she'd been planning words after all.

“No. Just thought about it a moment ago.”

I didn't turn that moment into lyrics. Before bed, the spoon had dried. I returned it to the drawer. The leftover soup was in the fridge. The unfinished sounds still belonged only to an evening when we'd said different things about them.
