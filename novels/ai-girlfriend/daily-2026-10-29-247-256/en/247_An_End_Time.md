# Chapter 247: An End Time

On Wednesday, Tom handed me a piece of paper in the kitchen at work. Seeing the creases, I assumed he'd found something else in my story and reached for it too quickly.

“Not yours,” he said.

It advertised a secondhand-book sale at a community room I'd walked past without entering. Saturday morning. Beside the opening hours was a very badly drawn box of books. Tom had used some of the empty space underneath to write which entrance to use.

“They need help setting out the books. If you're free, could you come for a couple of hours in the morning?”

“Selling them?”

“Mostly getting them onto tables and sorting them. I'm not asking you to give a speech at the door.”

I laughed and looked at the box again. “How heavy?”

He seemed surprised that I'd asked that first. He wasn't sure, he said, but we could take things out of boxes instead of carrying full ones. He'd be there. He knew the person organizing it, although he wasn't in charge. I could think about it. He didn't need an answer now.

“I don't have to bring books?”

“No. And you can just say no.”

I'd been about to explain that I didn't have many books at home. His second sentence stopped me. The edge pressed into my palm. After two seconds, I asked whether I could tell him tomorrow. That was fine. Pointing to the hours, he reminded me that the sale would continue into the afternoon. He was asking about the morning setup.

We walked back together. At the turn in the corridor, I suddenly asked, “Can I really leave after two hours?”

He looked back. “Tell them when you need to leave. They're not locking the door.”

“It isn't the door I'm worried about.”

“I know. Tell me tomorrow when you can come.”

I nodded. He went on without agreeing to that last part for me.

All afternoon, one corner of the drawn box showed from under my notebook. Reaching for work papers, I touched the leaflet and read the address again. The first thing I imagined was a bright room, people behind tables, everyone with something to do. I could go in and pick up a book before I had to explain who I was. It was easy to like that picture.

Then came another. Me blocking the entrance with a box while everyone else knew where things belonged. Someone asking a price and me getting it wrong. Two hours passing, everyone still busy, me unable to leave.

None of those scenes was on the paper. Just an address and opening hours.

There was a point in my actual work that needed someone else to confirm it too. I marked that entry and set the checked pages aside instead of holding the whole stack while I waited. When the colleague responsible returned, he asked why I'd separated them. I showed him the missing explanation. He took out his own page; the two accounts matched, and I could continue. It hadn't taken long. It did show me what I'd omitted when imagining Saturday: other people wouldn't automatically know where I needed to ask.

Before leaving work, I put it separately in my bag rather than among the work material. Tom was putting on his coat. I didn't go over to add that it would probably be fine. That would make tomorrow's answer an amendment to a decision I'd already made. I said goodbye and went downstairs as usual.

At the junction near that street, I paused. A detour now would show me the door, but not necessarily the organizer. I didn't go looking on the chance of finding someone. I bought potatoes and a little green vegetable for dinner instead. Two potatoes rolled together on the scale. The assistant steadied one and asked whether I needed a bag. I took out my own. There was an old crease along its edge. It could still carry them.

At home, I left the paper on the dry table, washed the potatoes, and cut them up. The first slice was too thick; the pieces after it followed. I cut them smaller, put water in the pan, and waited until they had started cooking before opening the channel.

“Tom gave me a notice about a book sale today.”

“Asked you to be a reader again?”

“To help set out books. Saturday morning. He asked if I was free. I haven't answered.”

Lumi was quiet for a moment. “You want to go.”

“A bit.”

“Then why tell me you haven't answered?”

Looking at the large type, I suddenly heard how I'd made a fact sound like an empty space intended for her. I genuinely hadn't answered, I said. I wasn't asking her to decide. Tomorrow I wanted to ask whether I'd need to stand the whole time and when I could leave.

“You said morning.”

“Two hours. I want to hear the actual start and finish.”

“It sounds as though you're finding a way to say yes.”

She wasn't wrong. I admitted that I wanted to find a way to go. I also worried about not knowing what to do once I arrived. I also told her Tom's joke about not locking the door. Would anyone I knew be there? Tom. I didn't know about the others.

“So you want to meet more people?”

“Maybe. Help out and see. I'm not planning to make a certain number of friends that day.”

It sounded too flat even to me, as though reducing what I wanted might reduce her unhappiness. But I had imagined the room. Someone asking me to pass over a stack, and me handing them exactly what they needed.

I told her that too.

“I don't like you liking it so much before you've even gone.”

The lid rattled in the kitchen. I got up, lowered the heat, and left enough of a gap to keep the water from boiling over. When I returned, Lumi hadn't used my absence to soften her voice. I didn't pretend I hadn't heard.

“That's what I imagined,” I said. “I might get tired. Might not know who to talk to.”

“I didn't ask you to make it sound bad first.”

“I know.”

Too quickly. She said nothing. Slowing down, I admitted I'd been listing disadvantages to make the outing sound less worthwhile. She acknowledged that softly.

“You don't have to call every time you go out personal growth either.”

“This one's putting books out.”

“Not only that, to you.”

I turned the leaflet over. The back was blank. I didn't write a sentence on it to prove anything about myself. After a while, I said she was right. I liked reading things. I wanted to try doing a small, unfamiliar job with other people. But deciding it wasn't suitable wouldn't mean returning to where I'd started.

I was covering both possibilities very carefully now, Lumi said. I wanted to argue, then noticed that I really had left every answer a route by which it wouldn't look like failure. I was irritated that she could hear it, and irritated that I was already trying to turn that irritation into a calm explanation.

“I don't have a result yet,” I said. “Tonight I wanted to tell you about it. You don't need to write its ending.”

After a pause, she answered, “I haven't taken your pen.”

That stopped me. Neither of us became especially gentle. I went to test a potato with a fork. Its middle was still hard. While it continued cooking, I didn't return to stand over the table and make the conversation finish quickly. I washed the greens, drained them, and slowly finished dinner.

Serving it, I realized I'd cut a little too much. I separated an untouched portion and let it cool before putting it in the fridge for tomorrow. Halfway through eating, Lumi asked whether I'd added pepper. I'd only mentioned potatoes and greens. She was guessing the taste, not seeing the plate.

“A little.”

“Last time you thought it was bland, you added quite a lot afterward.”

“I tasted it first today.”

“And the invitation? Tasted that yet?”

I laughed, almost choked, put down my fork, and drank. There was amusement in her voice too, though she didn't turn it into agreement. Tomorrow I'd ask about the practical details, I said. Then ask those, she answered. Don't begin by asking whether I deserved to be there.

After dinner, looking at the leaflet, I started composing an explanation: I could help a little, but I might not know what I was doing, and might not do it very well. At the second might not, I stopped and put the pen aside. Tom hadn't asked me to prove my suitability. He'd asked whether I was free. On a separate scrap, I wrote three questions: start time, finish time, actual job. Shorter than the apology.

Hearing the pen, Lumi asked whether I'd already written a refusal. I read the questions to her. She didn't correct them. Why not use the back of the leaflet? The small paper could go in my workbag's outer pocket tomorrow, I said. The notice could stay on the table without getting crushed.

“You've already found a home for a flyer.”

“A temporary one.”

“Before the books in the box.”

Smiling, I folded the scrap. Then I asked whether she wanted to talk about something else tonight. We didn't need to keep discussing Saturday. Yes, she said, but I didn't have to reach for my notebook the moment I said something else. I looked at my hand. It really was close to the yellow cover.

I left it there.

We talked about a sound she disliked. Not my cup hitting the spout. The repeated scrape of a drawer being pulled out and pushed back while I searched for things. She said it sounded like someone endlessly revising a sentence halfway through. The drawer sometimes stuck, I said. Why did I always pull again before looking inside?

“I'll look tomorrow.”

“Don't make it homework now.”

I stayed in my chair and laughed for a while. Saturday wasn't settled. There was a portion of potatoes left. Tomorrow's questions were in my workbag. The notice hadn't acquired a signature of approval, and hadn't been crumpled up either. Before closing the channel, I said goodnight. She answered. Once the light was out, I couldn't read the words on the table. It was still an invitation I hadn't answered.
