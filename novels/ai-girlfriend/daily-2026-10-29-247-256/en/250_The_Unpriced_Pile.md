# Chapter 250: The Unpriced Pile

On Saturday, I ate the bread over a plate. Crumbs still reached the edge of the table.

When I told Lumi two had escaped, she asked whether I wanted to withdraw last night's most reliable arrangement. The plate had caught most, I said. I'd wipe up the rest. She laughed, then asked about the water. I tightened the bottle cap. Filled, I said. No notebooks in the bag.

“I wasn't checking your luggage.”

“I wanted to tell you.”

After a brief silence, she told me to go. Before closing the channel, I glanced at the clock without asking whether she was holding back something she needed to say. We'd said goodbye. Leaving couldn't always require the other person to prove nothing had been omitted. I closed the channel, took my bag, and pulled the door shut behind me.

The bus left me a short walk from the community room. I followed the street name on my paper. There were steps at the main entrance, as promised. Inside, someone was dragging a table. The little door at the side stood open, an empty cardboard box blocking half of it. Rather than step over, I called in.

“Here to help with the books?” a woman asked.

She wore a gray top, no blue apron. I said I was Elliot. I'd arranged with Tom to help from nine to eleven. She nodded toward the interior. Tom was moving tables. She was in charge of the books. I could leave my bag under the chair over there, out of the way.

“I do need to go at eleven.”

“All right. Start with these two tables, then. Tell me where you've got to before you hand over.”

Her answer was so ordinary that, for a moment, I didn't know how to continue thanking her. The wall clock was a few minutes short of nine. I put my bag away and asked which box to open first. With her toe, she gently indicated one against the wall, labeled fiction. The lid was already loose. I crouched and took the books on top.

They were more slippery than I'd expected. A large one with a glossy cover underneath began sliding out as I lifted the smaller books. I stopped, put what I held back down, and used both hands to make a smaller pile. The woman didn't laugh. She cleared space on a table. Put them here first, she said. Not near the edge.

After moving his table, Tom came over, his hair a little untidy. Had I found the place? Yes. I pointed out my bag too. His would go there as well, he said, then went for another box. He didn't need to stay beside me while I got acquainted with every book.

The tabletop hadn't been wiped. I lifted my first stack, set it down again, and asked where to find a cloth. The organizer handed me one she'd already wrung out. Wipe it, then let it dry. A dark old mark ran through the grain. It wouldn't disappear. I didn't keep grinding the damp cloth into it. Once the surface felt dry, I began arranging books.

Large ones flat, smaller ones standing. Returning, the organizer steadied the upright row. There were no bookends. Once they opened, someone could knock them over. Better to lay everything flat. My face warmed. I hurried to hold them, but she'd already gone to deal with something else. I dismantled my attractive row into piles. The titles looked less impressive. They didn't require rescuing every few seconds either.

Price labels to one side. Unpriced books separately. The stickers weren't in consistent places. Some were on the back, others in a corner of the rear cover. I turned them slowly, neither peeling old labels off nor guessing a price by thickness. At first, I was slow. Once I knew where to look, my hands grew surer.

A thin volume had a bird on its cover and yellowed edges. As I lifted it, a page came loose and fell to the table. At first I thought it was an inserted sheet. Looking inside, I found that the page numbers didn't join and a line of broken glue showed along the spine. I didn't tuck the page back in and place it among the priced books. I wrote loose page on a blank slip and set it on a separate pile.

The organizer looked when she came past. Don't sell that yet, she said. We'd ask the donor when they returned. She moved it to the table behind her, without requiring me to decide whether it was worth keeping.

“I've only seen this one page,” I said. “Haven't checked the whole book.”

“Then writing that one page is loose is enough. Don't certify it complete.”

I nodded and continued. A younger volunteer beside me asked whether labeled books could go straight onto the display. I repeated what the organizer had said, mentioning the labeled one I'd just separated because of its page. Neither of us declared every book satisfactory. We set aside the problems we actually saw.

The younger volunteer hesitated with a thick cookbook. Should it go with household books or the large volumes? Looking at its size, I almost said here, then asked how the organizer wanted things grouped. Adjusting a sign at the other end, she said subject first. Large books could go on the lower part of the display. Neatness shouldn't make them impossible to find. Together, the volunteer and I shifted a small stack to make room. As a child, he said, he'd thought that if a family bought a cookbook, they had to eat through every page. I laughed. With one that thick, they couldn't change books for a year.

“That's why I wouldn't choose a thick one.”

He turned another volume over to find its price without waiting for me to supply meaning to his childhood joke. I remembered where cookbooks belonged. I also noticed that when he laughed, he looked first at the book rather than at me to check my response. When the next box opened, we each took a few. That hadn't made us partners required to stay together all morning.

After half past nine, the passages through the room narrowed. Some people carried boxes. Others put up signs. The front table still hadn't made space for taking payments. Halfway across with a small stack, I met someone turning unexpectedly and nearly collided. I stopped, the paper corners against my chest, instead of forcing my way between two people.

“Excuse me. Going to that table.”

One person moved, and I passed. Tom glanced up from the other end, then returned to arranging his own books. Nobody stopped to congratulate me on saying those two words. Only after setting down the stack did I notice how tightly I'd held my shoulders. Releasing them brought a little ache, not pain.

I fetched my water from under the chair and drank where I wouldn't block the passage. Nobody asked why I was taking a break. A man carrying an empty box asked whether anyone was sitting there. No, I said, but there were bags underneath. He put the box against the wall instead of on top of them. I tightened my bottle, went back, and straightened the top book of a leaning pile.

Before ten, the organizer asked us to move all empty boxes to the wall. The sign at the entrance was turned around. Two people were already waiting outside. Watching them enter, I suddenly understood that I wasn't arranging this only for my own eyes. Other people's hands were about to undo these tidy piles.

The first visitor at my table asked about travel books. I pointed toward another table, then realized I wasn't certain. Geography-related books were being put out there, I said. The organizer could help him look. I couldn't promise they were all travel. He nodded and went over. I didn't pull out any volume containing a map simply to make my answer more useful.

Later, a woman asked the price of a book. I turned it over. No sticker. It had got into the displayed stack. I asked her to wait and took it to the organizer. Once a price was written, I brought it back. The woman looked, returned it to the table, and said she'd keep browsing.

My empty hand felt unexpectedly disappointing, as though those extra steps ought to have made her buy it. Then someone wanted to see a book underneath. I moved the top layer and forgot the disappointment while helping. Finding the price was my task. It wasn't her promise to purchase.

A battered novel held a small folded paper. When I turned the book to check its label, the paper slipped into my palm. Two handwritten lines showed on the outside. I saw that it was handwriting and didn't unfold it. The organizer was surrounded by people asking questions. I stood holding the slip for a moment, almost putting it in my pocket until she was free. Then I realized how easily I could turn away and carry it home that way. I looked for an empty envelope.

There wasn't one, only clean sheets. I wrapped the little paper in one and placed it with its book on the pile awaiting questions. When the organizer was available, I called her.

“This was inside. I haven't opened it. Don't know whether it needs returning.”

She looked at the book and asked which box it had come from. I showed her. She took both book and paper away to keep until she could ask. It wouldn't be sold with the book. I nodded without asking what the note said, or inventing an important history for its absent writer.

Back at my table, two stacks I'd separated had become mixed. I reached to rearrange them, then stopped. Some were simply books a customer had put aside while looking, not mistakes demanding immediate correction. I waited for the older man to finish and return what he didn't want. Then I filled the space he'd left.

The wall clock had passed quarter past ten. Paper dust marked my hands. Wiping one against my trousers left a pale streak. At last, the old clothes I'd chosen had a use. From behind another table, Tom asked how I was doing. All right, I said. I'd expected carrying to be the busy part, not waiting for people to finish looking.

He laughed and told me not to hurry them. I hadn't, I said, though my hand had almost reached out. We returned to our separate tasks. I moved the unpriced pile inward, leaving visitors somewhere to set down what they held. It wasn't eleven yet. There was still work I could do beside this table.
