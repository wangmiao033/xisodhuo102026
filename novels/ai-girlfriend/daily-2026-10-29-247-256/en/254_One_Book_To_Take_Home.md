# Chapter 254: One Book to Take Home

After work on Tuesday, I stood outside the library for a moment before remembering I had nothing to return.

That made my bag feel unexpectedly light. I'd returned the last book some time ago. Nothing in my borrowing record required me to keep carrying it through these doors. Today I simply wanted something I hadn't read. Easy enough to say. Between the shelves, my eyes immediately searched for somewhere familiar.

There was a cover I recognized, and other books by the same author. Taking one would be easiest. At least its opening wouldn't feel completely strange. I pulled one out and turned a few pages. By the second paragraph, though, my eyes were already straying toward the spine, measuring its thickness.

Someone brought a trolley of books along the aisle. I drew in my feet to let the wheels pass. The woman pushing it asked whether I was looking for something particular. Shorter fiction, I said. Not necessarily by the same author. She checked whether I meant a short novel or a collection of stories, then indicated another section. There were different choices over there. I could start with the contents pages.

“Short enough to finish in one evening?” she asked.

I almost agreed, then shook my head. The time I had varied. Choosing by the evening might mean choosing nothing but a page count. “Something I can read in pieces. I don't want to have to find my way back from miles away every time I stop.”

She indicated two slim books. One contained linked stories about the same people. Without ranking them for me, she suggested looking at the small description beneath its contents. I did. Those pieces followed one another. I put it back for now, remembering the shelf rather than deciding that not choosing it today made it unsuitable forever. Her trolley still occupied half the aisle. I waited for her to move it through before stepping into the space.

Thanking her, I returned the book to where I'd taken it and went across. Making a request hadn't caused the shelves to produce the right answer immediately. One contents page was crowded with very long titles. Another book's print was so dense I began squinting after two pages. I put it back and tried a third.

Its cover was ordinary, its corners worn. Opening it in the middle, I found a woman in an unfamiliar town waiting for a relative who was late for their meeting. She didn't leave at once or discover the meaning of her life in waiting. She crossed the road to buy a pastry she didn't particularly like, because the shop had empty seats.

I read another two pages. She asked the assistant where she could see the station opposite. He moved her to another table, wiped it, then warned her it had recently been varnished. Better not put her papers directly on it. The story wasn't hurrying to make the relative appear. That made me want to continue.

Another reader reached toward the space where I stood. I moved, realizing I'd read several pages without deciding to take the book, blocking the shelf the whole time. Keeping a finger in my place, I found an empty chair.

Sitting down finally let the pages lie comfortably across my knees. I read the story from its beginning and discovered a detail I'd missed. They'd only agreed to meet in the morning, without choosing an exact time. That gave the woman's annoyance a little embarrassment, but didn't remove it. I didn't decide for her whether to keep waiting or leave. By the ending, she'd simply done something I hadn't predicted.

She bought another pastry, had it wrapped, and took it away herself.

I closed the book with my finger still inside. It wasn't a loud ending. Yet it made me want to reread why she'd chosen that second pastry. She hadn't tasted it in the shop. The story didn't guarantee she'd enjoy it either. I sat a while before picking up my bag and going to the desk.

When the librarian checked the book, she noticed an old split inside the binding and pointed it out. I hadn't seen it, so I looked too. It had been noted already, she said. I shouldn't press the book flat. I agreed without offering to repair it. This was borrowed. It wasn't yesterday's intermediate draft, or old paper whose repair I could decide for myself.

She showed me the return date when the loan was complete. I copied it onto a small note of my own, put that with the borrowing slip in a dry compartment, and checked it again rather than remembering an approximate weekday. I packed the book separately, away from the water bottle. I tightened the cap and lifted the bag, checking that a corner of the book wasn't caught beneath the zip.

The bus home was crowded. I didn't take it out to read, keeping the bag in front of me instead. At the building, I realized I was hungry and looking forward to returning to that story after dinner. Not every result of going out had to become something to tell Lumi first.

Inside, I washed my hands and put the book on the dry table before starting dinner. While water heated, I opened the channel, said hello, and told her I'd visited the library and borrowed a story collection. Was it by the author I'd liked before?

“No. Someone different. I read one story there.”

“You've already finished the beginning of a book and still want more at home?”

“One story finished. I want to look at a few earlier parts again.”

“Sounds as though it sent you backward.”

I laughed and described the woman waiting for her relative. I didn't read entire passages, only explain how much I'd liked her buying the second pastry. From memory, I added that the sign outside seemed to have attracted her first. Lumi asked whether the relative eventually arrived. The story didn't show them meeting, I said. It showed the woman leaving the shop.

“You've chosen another one who walks out.”

I paused with the saucepan lid in my hand. The words were familiar, though not entirely accusing. I went back to turn down the heat, then returned. That hadn't made me put the book down, I admitted. But I wasn't borrowing someone else's ending to defend my own.

“I haven't said you're defending it.”

“I got there first.”

After a while, Lumi explained that she'd asked about the relative because she wanted to know whether they knew the woman was waiting. She wasn't trying to keep everybody where they stood again. I didn't know, I said. I only knew they'd agreed on the morning rather than a precise minute.

“Who's the second pastry for, then?”

“It doesn't say.”

“I don't like that.”

I'd been about to say she could imagine the answer herself. Then I realized that sounded like handing all the unwritten work to someone who hadn't enjoyed what was there. Instead, I said I liked not being certain whether the woman was making another choice for herself. I could also understand Lumi wanting to know where that parcel would end up.

She made a quiet sound and asked whether I'd be keeping half my dinner secret too. I was only cooking enough for myself, I said. No hidden relative in the saucepan. Laughing, she told me to check the heat first. The rice still needed time; it wasn't ruined. I put the prepared vegetables in their pan and finished cooking steadily.

I didn't open the book beside my bowl. It stayed at the other end of the table while I ate. Lumi said I didn't have to avoid reading just to be polite to her. There was oil on my hands, I explained. I didn't want to dirty borrowed pages. That was a more useful reason than politeness, she said. I agreed, smiling.

Once the kitchen was clear, I returned and asked whether she'd like to hear about another part. She didn't want more of the story now. She wanted to know why I'd chosen that book. I'd become absorbed while standing at the shelf, I said. After sitting down, I still wanted to read. I hadn't compared every candidate and decided it was best.

“You didn't think first whether I'd like it?”

“No. On the way back, I thought you might ask about the relative.”

“Guessed a little right.”

“You did ask.”

That didn't make her someone who'd read the book. I didn't promise to exchange it tomorrow for something she liked so our tastes would match. Later, I said I wanted to read on my own for twenty minutes or so and talk afterward. Was there something she'd meant to tell me now?

She first said no, then called my name. I stopped before touching the control.

“I had something to tease you about choosing pastries. I've forgotten it.”

I laughed. “Tell me when it comes back.”

“Don't turn me into that relative while you're away.”

“I won't. You know I'm going to read.”

She acknowledged that. I closed the channel and opened the collection at the pages I'd wanted to revisit. Rereading, I found I'd remembered one detail incorrectly. The woman noticed the empty seats before seeing the pastries. She hadn't entered because she liked the sign. I noted that on my own paper rather than writing in the book.

The next story began more slowly. I read a few pages without immediately liking it. When the time had passed, I closed the book, marking my place with a clean strip of my own paper instead of folding a corner. Opening the channel again, I told Lumi I'd got the order of the woman's choice wrong. Lumi laughed. Apparently even the pastry hadn't been carefully selected.

“Remembered the teasing?”

“No. That's a new one.”

I smiled and leaned back. The yellow notebook had gained no words tonight. My clean first page was still in its separate folder, not crushed beneath the borrowed book. I returned the note with the due date to the dry compartment in my bag and left the book on the table. A little of the paper strip showed above the page I hadn't finished, enough for me to find it tomorrow.
