# Chapter 253: The Copy I Had Not Checked

On Monday, Marcus put a sheet beside me and asked where he should look up the number in its lower corner.

I recognized the form. We'd merged two entries earlier, retaining their original numbers afterward. I'd made a point of checking them. The answer was almost ready before he finished asking. I pointed toward the later column and said he could match them there.

“This copy doesn't have that column.”

My hand stopped. There was only one page in front of me. Its heading was familiar, but the explanation below was missing. It wasn't the final page I'd handed over. It was an intermediate copy I'd kept while checking the order. Looking up at Marcus, I first asked where he'd got it.

“The pile beside your desk.”

“I didn't tell anyone to use that pile.”

My voice was too loud. Someone nearby glanced up. I drew my hand back and saw there was nothing on the paper saying it shouldn't be used. It had simply been left neatly somewhere people could reach, close to the material they actually needed.

Marcus didn't argue. Which copy should he use now? Give me a moment, I said, and went to fetch the checked version. Opening its storage place, I briefly wanted to tuck the other sheet away so it would never appear again. Instead, I brought it back with the new one.

“This is an intermediate draft,” I said. “Kept it to compare the order. I didn't separate them. You didn't pick up the wrong thing.”

He took the new page, checked the numbers, then the heading. “Can this one go across?”

“Yes. Let me compare the attachment again.”

“Then I'll wait until you've done that.”

He left both copies and returned to his place. That wait made my face hot. It wasn't a reprimand, but it prevented me from finishing with It should be fine. I took out the corresponding attachment and compared each entry. The final copy was right. What the intermediate page lacked was precisely the explanation the old numbers led to.

I turned to the final attachment and noticed that both copies had the same printing date. Checking only that would still have made them look identical. I took the last page to Marcus and asked who needed the material, and whether they wanted the merged table or the original itemized explanation. He pointed to the relevant sentence in the task instead of letting me choose from memory. Back at my desk, I put the explanation behind the table and checked the page count.

Most of the intermediate sheet was correct. That, rather than a page full of errors, had made me reluctant to doubt it. After labeling the comparison folder, I went through the outgoing pile too. One sheet contained calculations for my own use. I moved it away from what Marcus needed.

When I finished, I went over with the complete set and told him it was ready. He added his own processing mark on top and asked what I'd do with the old one. Keep it separately, I said, away from material ready to be handed over. I kept a hand on the corner until he had the set securely. He nodded and started on the next item, without adding a reassuring speech to my admission.

Back at my desk, I collected the checking drafts I needed to keep in a separate folder. Not every old sheet needed throwing away. Nor did every page need huge warnings. I marked the folder For comparison, checked that nothing inside needed immediate delivery, and put it somewhere different from the finished work.

One sheet slipped along the desk edge. Bending to catch it, I nearly hit my forehead on the underside. Someone told me to be careful. I thanked them. Sitting up again, I almost laughed. Admitting the morning's mistake hadn't stopped me being clumsy afterward.

Before lunch, Tom came to borrow tape. He asked whether I had any, then glanced at the newly separated folders. I handed him the tape. He thanked me for helping on Saturday.

“What happened to the book they took off the table?”

“Still for the person in charge to check. Not my decision.”

“Right.”

“I was saying thanks. Didn't come to give you a final case report.”

I laughed and told him I'd left an intermediate draft in the wrong place. I'd spent the morning arguing with a pile of paper. That explained why I looked ready to verify the tape's origins too, he said. I didn't repeat every sentence from the morning. Instead, I asked whether his arms had ached yesterday. He raised the tape. He could still lift this, at least.

After he left, I took out my lunch. Some of the rice had stuck together. I separated it with my spoon. Halfway through eating, I realized I was no longer rehearsing how I should have opened the morning's conversation. I had work of my own for the afternoon. It wouldn't all automatically become evidence because I'd made a mistake earlier.

On the way home, I passed a small stationery shop. I'd only meant to replace a pen that was almost empty. At the shelf, though, I saw packs of ordinary lined paper. I picked one up, felt its thickness, and put it back. There was room left in the yellow notebook. Copying a story didn't necessarily require a whole new pack.

The assistant asked what I wanted. A pen, I said, indicating the plainest kind. She invited me to try it. In one corner of the test sheet, I wrote my name. The ink ran smoothly. I drew a short line as well, without trying ten possible story titles in that small space.

I took the pen home. When I opened the channel, Lumi heard the packet rustle and asked what I'd bought. A pen. The old one was nearly empty, and I wanted to copy the opening of the folding-table story tonight.

“Title decided?”

“Not yet. The question mark's still there.”

“You can write the story first.”

True. I put down the pen and made dinner. It was a simple meal. Afterward, I took the bowl to the kitchen and waited until the water had stopped before telling her about the morning. I began with Marcus picking up the intermediate copy, then paused and changed it. I'd left the intermediate copy beside the final ones. He'd taken it from there.

Lumi asked how much of it had already been used. He'd asked before passing it on, I said. We'd checked the complete set afterward. I hadn't found evidence that the intermediate sheet had gone farther. It wasn't a major disaster. That didn't mean nothing had happened.

“You told him?”

“Yes. Started by saying I hadn't asked anyone to take that pile.”

“As though a sheet of paper had made an appointment to be somewhere.”

“I really thought everyone would know.”

She didn't keep teasing. After a while, she suggested what I disliked most was having checked carefully and still managed to go wrong here. Yes. The copy I'd checked had genuinely been checked. The problem was the one I hadn't intended anybody else to use.

“So tonight you're going to make the story into a piece of paper nobody could possibly mistake?”

I'd been reaching for the yellow notebook. My hand slowed.

“No. Not originally, anyway. I wanted to copy it yesterday.”

“I know you wanted to yesterday.”

Her voice was level. It wasn't the sequence she was reminding me about. It was how quickly I wanted to prove the two things entirely unrelated. I set down the notebook and saw the crowded arrows in its margins. Even I had to search in places. That was what copying could actually address. It didn't also need to turn me into someone who would never mix up documents.

“Just the beginning tonight,” I said. “As long as I can follow it.”

Would I change the ending to one she liked while I was at it? No. She wasn't disappointed in a way that required explanation, or pleased enough to rescue me. She simply told me to write.

After closing the channel, I took a few clean sheets of my own, laid them flat, and tried the new pen. My first sentence was larger than usual, crowding the next. I took another sheet and opened up the spacing. The first, with only two sentences, could be used on the reverse for ordinary notes. I didn't tuck it into the story folder as though it were one of its pages.

At the place where both women reached out, I omitted bag strap. Copying onward, I suddenly lost the movement. What did she let go of? I compared it with the original and found what I'd missed. Adding the words made the new page less clean. I watched the small insertion mark and nearly started another sheet.

In the end, I kept it. The next line began where it should. I went on until the second woman put her bag on the opposite side. Then I stopped, checked the page I'd copied, and numbered it underneath. Clear handwriting didn't make it automatically correct. One addition didn't make the entire page a failure either.

Opening the channel again, I told Lumi I'd only written a page and already altered it. Was the new pen disobedient? The pen was fine, I said. I'd missed something while copying.

“Then don't return it.”

“I won't. It's written my name already. Knows too much.”

Lumi laughed. Afterward, she asked whether I'd read the first two sentences, not the whole story. I did, without explaining the omitted words first. She said I'd read more slowly than when telling her about the office. Paper didn't rush to contradict me, I answered. Those two women might, she said.

Looking at the new page, I suddenly wanted to keep going. But it was getting late. I placed it in a separate, ordinary folder and returned the notebook to its own place. If I continued tomorrow, I'd begin on page two. I wouldn't copy today's effort all over again.

Before turning off the light, I noticed the pen's packaging still at the table corner and cleared it away. The space it left wasn't especially large. Enough to put down my cup, and to take out that first page again tomorrow.
