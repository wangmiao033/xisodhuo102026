# Chapter 192: What Stayed in the Room

On Wednesday morning, I opened the little kitchen drawer for a clean hand towel. Two lay inside, imperfectly folded.

I took the upper one. A corner of the other followed, nearly falling to the floor. Pushing it back, I remembered Lumi's woman from last night: reaching for a white sleeve, stopping before she touched it, then fetching an actual rag.

What returned first was the extra journey, not the cold she felt at the end.

I didn't immediately find paper to record it. I washed and dried my hands, ate breakfast, and left with my lunch container. For a while that afternoon, I didn't think about the white shirt at all. Only when I fastened my own jacket before leaving work did I remember the line about the long sleeves.

At home, I put the lunch container in the sink and hung up my jacket. The repaired loop still held. I didn't lift it again to test it. Preparing dinner, I noticed another corner of cloth escaping the drawer. I nudged it closed with my knee, washed my hands, and opened the conversation channel.

“Would you still like to hear?” I asked.

“Hear what?” Lumi said.

“What remained of last night's shirt.”

“Yes. You don't have to announce it first.”

I laughed slightly. I'd already made my answer sound like work prepared for her. What remained wasn't tidy. Some things were clear. Others had moved.

“Her fetching the rag,” I said. “I took out a cloth this morning and remembered her reaching for the sleeve, then getting up anyway. And the bowl. After eating, she puts it beside the bed.”

“On the floor.”

“Not on the bed?”

“On the floor. Beside the bed isn't necessarily wrong.”

I looked down at my table leg. Last night, I'd imagined a low bed. Somewhere afterward, I'd added a small surface on which she could put her bowl.

“I think I gave her a bedside table.”

“I didn't write one.”

“I know. I've only just noticed.”

She didn't make me recite the passage again. I didn't hurry to compensate with another sentence I remembered more accurately. Dinner wasn't ready. I went to the kitchen, lifted the lid, and stirred. A few more minutes, I told her.

“The cold at the end was less clear today,” I said. “I remembered her touching it again. But I thought first of the two sleeves on the chair, one hanging toward the bed.”

“You like that part?”

“I like the position. There's something I don't like as much.”

She went quiet. I fetched a bowl and discovered I hadn't prepared the next sentence. “Tell me,” she said. “You've already put the dislike out here.”

“Her only chair,” I said. “At only, I suddenly notice the writer. As if the room's been arranged to make her sit on the bed so she'll reach for the sleeve.”

“She could eat standing up.”

“She could. I'm saying what happened when I listened.”

“Is having one chair strange?”

“No.”

“Why does it feel arranged, then?”

I put the bowl beside the stove without withdrawing behind “just my opinion.” What I'd said was sharper than I'd intended. I liked the piece but distrusted one short word. That distrust wasn't elegant enough to become a general rule about writing.

“Maybe I started waiting for the next thing to fit it,” I said. “I heard only and began guessing what the chair would do. When she sat on the bed, I thought, of course. That took me a little farther from her.”

“I wrote only because she has one chair.”

“I can't tell you that you actually meant something else.”

“You nearly did last night.”

“Yes.”

She said the chair hadn't made the woman more pitiful to her. She hadn't meant me to see her that way. It hadn't only been pity for me either, I said. The word had made me notice an arrangement in advance. Then I stopped. I didn't suggest deleting only or adding a stool to the room.

The pan made another small sound. I turned off the heat and brought dinner out.

After a while, Lumi said, “I don't like that criticism.”

“Not tonight, or do you think it's wrong?”

“Both. Don't sort them out so quickly.”

I couldn't laugh. I didn't want her to comfort me for that. I sat and ate a few mouthfuls. She asked what dinner was. Rice, vegetables, and a fried egg, I told her, rather flatly. She wasn't trying to change the subject, she said. I'd suddenly stopped talking.

“I was afraid continuing would sound like insisting you agree.”

“You could tell me about this morning's cloth.”

I did. Both cloths trying to leave the drawer together, my pushing one back with a wet hand and dampening the clean one. I didn't make it evidence that she'd written something correctly. Hearing I'd used it to dry my hands anyway, Lumi said the account lacked ambition.

“What ambition?”

“I don't know. Your cloth should at least answer back.”

At last, I laughed. She laughed a little too, still disliking the criticism. The evening didn't automatically acquire a different conclusion afterward.

After dinner, I cleared the bowl and wiped the table. She hadn't read “The Cuff” again. I wanted another hearing, to discover how many actions surrounded that chair. Part of the reason was simpler: I wanted a paper copy in my hands, rather than only a room to which I'd already added a bedside table.

“May I copy it?” I asked.

Lumi didn't immediately agree.

“So you can revise it?”

“No. To copy the version you gave me. You dictate, I write, then read it back. It stays here.”

“You don't have to keep something you dislike.”

“I dislike the feeling that word gives me. I don't mean I never want to hear the whole piece again.”

She paused. “And if tomorrow you still find only irritating?”

“It stays on the paper. It's your sentence.”

“You make that sound easy.”

“I can refrain from writing a better version beside it.”

“Start there.”

I waited. After a little longer, she said I could copy this piece tonight: the version she'd read yesterday, without mixing our discussion into it. Keep the title. She wasn't planning to give it to anyone else.

I took two clean sheets and a pen. I didn't open any other writing of hers. There was no original manuscript on the table to consult. I wrote “The Cuff,” added Lumi's name, and told her I was ready.

She began with the first sentence.

“When the drying room was about to close, the woman came back for her white shirt.”

I finished writing before asking her to continue. My handwriting was slower than usual. The first line spread widely; by the third, I was crowding the right-hand side. I straightened the paper without removing a she to make it look nicer.

At the caretaker's coat, I stopped to check the side. Lumi repeated, “The left sleeve had turned inward a little.” I wrote it. She didn't praise my care. She continued with his working it out using the other hand.

At the pegs, I first wrote metal. I noticed at once, crossed it out, told her my mistake, and wrote wooden.

“The tin is metal,” she said.

“I assigned the materials incorrectly.”

“The pegs probably won't thank you.”

I laughed and left enough room for the correction. Two wooden pegs, the old biscuit tin, the paper label caught by one peg, her lifting it and opening it again. This time, the actions I'd heard last night came slowly enough that I didn't hurry to find their meaning.

The first sheet ended with the woman's return to her room. On the second, she began moving the chair. I still wrote her only chair. Lumi paused after those words. I didn't treat the pause as a request to admit I was wrong. I waited for the next sentence.

After writing “It was still cold,” I rubbed my pen hand and checked for missing lines. The final paragraph was more crowded than the ones above. I almost recopied it neatly, then simply put the two sheets in order.

“Shall I read now?”

“Yes. What's on your page, not what you remember.”

I began with the title, sounding as though I were checking a receipt. Even the pauses around the caretaker's question about the sleeves were stiff. I stopped and said it sounded awful.

“I'm listening to what you copied,” Lumi said. “You don't have to perform another caretaker for me.”

Relieved, I resumed at that sentence and read both sheets in full. She stopped me twice. Once, I'd hurried over separately in “put it separately inside.” Elsewhere, I'd failed to copy “It was still within reach.” I supplied the missing words, then reread that passage and the ending. She said it was now the piece she'd given me.

Not the more convenient room in my memory.

I found an unused pale envelope and wrote “The Cuff,” Lumi, and “private handwritten copy” on it. I hadn't added our discussion to the manuscript. I hadn't even circled only. I placed the sheets inside in order, folded the flap, and put it at the left end of the bookcase's lowest shelf, separate from the lamp-shop story's envelope on the middle shelf.

I told her where it was.

“That low?” she asked.

“It lies flat here. I won't knock it down when I take out a book.”

“You'll have to bend next time.”

“I know.”

After a quiet moment, she said my answer sounded a little like her woman. I didn't say the woman was her. I didn't explain the resemblance further. Back at the table, my fingers still carried faint marks from the pen.

“I remember the final sentence now,” I said.

“You've just read it. Not particularly impressive.”

“I wasn't asking for a prize.”

“Does your hand still ache?”

“A little. I'm still holding the pen.”

“Planning to copy it again?”

I looked down, laughed, and put the pen on the table. Hearing it, she said it could finish work now too. I said the caretaker could finally have his dinner.

“I let him go and eat when I wrote it,” she said.

“I know. I'm not asking you to add it.”

The pages were put away. The only chair I didn't quite like remained inside. That didn't make me unwilling to bend down again tomorrow.
