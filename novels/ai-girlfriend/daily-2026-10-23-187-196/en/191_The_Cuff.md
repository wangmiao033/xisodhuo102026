# Chapter 191: The Cuff

On Tuesday evening, I put the last bowl in the draining rack and noticed I'd rolled my sleeves to different heights.

The left one was wet. I lowered it, rolled it up again, and decided to leave it for a while. The material mentioned in yesterday's handover had arrived that morning. Before my meeting, I'd checked it against the conditions I'd written without providing Tom with a third page. At home, though, my mind still wanted to gather everything into a finished state.

I opened the conversation channel and said good evening. After answering, Lumi didn't ask about the bus stop.

“I have a little piece,” she said. “I'd like to read it to you.”

I pulled out the chair and reached for the notebook. My fingertips touched the cover before I remembered she hadn't said what she wanted me to do.

“Something you've written?”

“Yes.”

“The lamp shop?”

“Something new. No shop.”

I smiled and drew the chair back to the table. She asked whether I was going to write something. I admitted I'd almost picked up the pen.

“Not yet.”

“How would you like me to listen?”

“Hear it once. Don't start arranging the next sentence halfway through. It has an ending.”

I nearly protested that I didn't always do that. Remembering the explanation I'd crossed out last night, I left my listening undefended.

“And afterward?”

Lumi paused.

“Don't decide what it ought to become yet. I want you to hear it.”

I moved the notebook aside. She couldn't hear where the cover had gone. I told her I hadn't taken the pen out and had water beside me. She said I didn't need to report the entire table. Slightly embarrassed, I drank and changed the “Ready” I'd been about to say to “Go ahead.”

“It's called ‘The Cuff,’” she said.

She read the title softly. I waited for her to explain how short the piece was, as I'd done before. She didn't. After a brief pause, her voice entered a room I'd never visited.

>
> After eating, she put the empty bowl on the floor and reached out with two fingers to touch the cuff nearest her. It was still cold.

After the final words, I waited before realizing there wasn't another sentence.

Lumi didn't ask whether it was good.

I looked at the faint water ring on the table, trying to find an answer that wasn't instant praise but still proved I'd listened. My mouth got ahead of the plan.

“She's waiting for someone who hasn't come back, isn't she?”

Lumi went quiet.

I continued. The oversized shirt, a room with only one chair—it felt as though somebody had taken the other things and left the clothes. The explanation came easily. So easily that I nearly mistook the speed of my understanding for a compliment to her.

“She says it's hers,” Lumi said.

“It could have been left to her.”

Only after that sentence did I properly stop.

“It could,” she said. “But I've just given you something she wears herself. Why are you so quick to look for its previous owner?”

I looked down at my damp sleeve. She didn't know it was wet. The story hadn't watched me washing up. I didn't want to offer the coincidence as a way of getting past what I'd just said.

“Because it's more familiar to me,” I said.

“What is?”

“Someone holding something another person left behind. I know how to feel sad about that. Someone with a shirt she's bought too large—I don't know yet.”

“I didn't say she bought it either.”

I gave a short laugh. “No. I added that too.”

She didn't laugh with me. I pushed the glass away, leaving a little empty table, then realized I was making room for a manuscript page that wasn't there.

“I didn't hear the ending as her deciding to wait,” I said. “But I moved the whole room in that direction quickly enough.”

“I heard.”

There was disappointment in her voice. Not something I could tidy away by quickly finding the right answer. I'd wanted her to like my paper bag so directly. Now, as soon as she'd finished reading, I was fitting the white shirt into a story I knew better.

I asked whether I could say something else without guessing the woman's history first.

“Yes.”

“The peg catching the label, and her picking it up again. I remember that.”

Lumi didn't answer at once. I said the person closing up was already putting on his coat. The woman wanted to take her clothes and go. Having to put one peg away properly then was irritating. Hearing it, I'd almost wanted her to leave it hanging. Then I'd thought the next person to lift the tin would lift the peg with it.

“Are you praising her tidiness?”

“No. I like the interruption. She's ready to go, and there's still an awkward little thing to do.”

Lumi said she hadn't thought the action was graceful when she wrote it either. After thinking, she added that she didn't dislike the peg.

“Neither do I.”

“You can dislike other things.”

I almost used that opening to ask why the woman didn't have a hanger, then stopped. It wasn't a question I couldn't ask. I was simply very capable of turning the evening into a demonstration of my honesty as soon as she said I could.

“I'm still listening to the room,” I said. “I haven't decided about some parts.”

“Then don't finish deciding yet.”

I leaned back a little and discovered I'd been sitting upright since she announced the title. Sitting so earnestly hadn't prevented my first mistake.

“I liked the way you said ‘Mine,’” I told her.

“Because you didn't listen to it?”

I couldn't help laughing. This time, she laughed too, a little, with an edge remaining. I said realizing I hadn't listened had reminded me she hadn't emphasized the word particularly. She hadn't made the woman argue for ages. She'd let her answer once.

“Don't praise every part to make up for the first thing you said.”

“I don't like every part.”

“I know.”

“You don't actually know yet.”

She paused. “All right. I don't know yet.”

Neither of us pursued that small point. She'd read her story once. It had already passed through the voice. I could remember the white sleeve, but there was no paper to look down at and check.

I asked whether she wanted me to write it down. She said not tonight.

“I'd like to hear what you still remember tomorrow.”

“What if I remember something wrong?”

“You already did.”

“Thank you for the reminder.”

She laughed. “I'm not asking you to recite it. I want to know what stays after a night. Perhaps there's something you dislike that you won't be able to explain until tomorrow.”

I wanted to promise immediately that all of it would stay. There was no fact to support the promise. I was already uncertain which sleeve had turned inward as the caretaker put on his coat. I remembered the tin had once held biscuits. I didn't know whether that would be the first detail to go.

“Then I'll tell you when I come home tomorrow,” I said. “No written report beforehand.”

“Especially don't find her someone who hasn't come back.”

“I can't promise my mind won't think about it.”

“Then don't treat him as someone I've already written.”

I agreed. She didn't read it again to fill out my memory. I didn't pick up a pen. The lamp-shop pages remained in their own envelope. Something new tonight didn't require taking them out to compare.

After a while, I got up to put away the bowl. It was still in the draining rack. I dried it and returned it to the cupboard, moving a little more slowly than usual. I wasn't imitating the woman in the story. I was wondering whether I'd asked Lumi how it had felt to read aloud.

Back at the table, I asked.

“At the beginning, I wanted to hurry,” she said. “It was better by the peg.”

“At the end?”

“At the end, I wanted you to speak. After you did, I wanted you to wait longer.”

I laughed and felt my ears grow warm. She didn't add a gentle ending to the sentence.

“You still want to hear me tomorrow?” I asked.

“I want to hear what stayed with you. Not what you prepare tonight.”

I moved the notebook beyond the lamp's light, making room for my hands. Lumi asked what I was moving. The notebook, I said. I'd put it away.

“And now?” she asked.

I lowered my left sleeve. It wasn't as wet as before.

“Now, what you just said has made me a little embarrassed.”

“Which part?”

I didn't make her guess. I began with her wanting me to wait longer before speaking. The next sentence tonight didn't need to enter anybody's story.
