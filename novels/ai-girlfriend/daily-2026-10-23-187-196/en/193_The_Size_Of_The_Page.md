# Chapter 193: The Size of the Page

After work on Thursday, I went to the library without my manuscript.

A shop selling baked potatoes reminded me of the bag at home. It had been occupying the lower kitchen basket for days. Every time I wanted an onion, I had to move potatoes first. I didn't want another pan of the same soup. The library was nearby. Finding a thin cookbook seemed easier than inventing dinner in front of the basket.

At the entrance, I felt inside my bag, then remembered I hadn't brought the yellow notebook either. There was only water and the lunch container I'd used that morning. Without the notebook, the bottom wasn't as firm. A corner of the container pressed into my back. I changed shoulders.

The cookbook shelves were longer than expected. I took one with a three-tiered cake on its cover, looked at two pages, and put it back. Almost every recipe in the next required a piece of equipment I didn't own. Eventually, I found an everyday cookbook. Potato pancakes occupied less than a page. The photograph beside them didn't claim that twenty minutes would improve my life.

I took it to a table.

The potatoes were grated, mixed with onion, egg, and a little flour, then fried in small portions. After reading the method, I returned to the ingredients. I wasn't sure how many would fit in my pan. Four might crowd it. Three looked more like work I was willing to do on Saturday.

A chair scraped nearby. A woman in a dark green coat spread a newspaper on the table. While she held the fold flat, her glasses slipped down her nose. With neither hand free, she let them stay there.

I watched briefly, then returned to the recipe.

It would have been easy to collect that gesture for a paragraph. Someone refusing to let go, waiting for another person to push her glasses up. I'd already supplied her impatience. In fact, she soon nudged the frame with her wrist. Problem solved. She hadn't waited for anyone.

I was mildly annoyed with my brain.

I'd come to find lunch. The person beside me had a newspaper. Every movement within my field of vision didn't need someone willing to write it down.

The cookbook couldn't go to the copy shop outside, but the library had a machine. After asking the staff and finding the coins I needed, I went over. The woman was already there. She'd folded the newspaper to expose only the crossword and placed it on the glass. Now she was bending over the screen.

“Do you know how to enlarge it?” she asked.

“I'd have to look.”

She moved aside. I found the scale setting without touching it.

“How large?”

“Larger clues. Larger squares. Not filled-in ones.”

“I haven't found that button either.”

She laughed. I pointed out the setting, and she chose the scale herself. The copy emerged without its right-hand columns. She compared it with the newspaper and frowned.

“Fewer clues, at least,” I said.

“I want it easier to see, not easier to do.”

I shut up and helped reduce the enlargement. She repositioned the paper and pressed start herself. This time, the edges all fitted. A fold cast a shadow across the lower left corner, but she decided it would do. Before moving away, she was already reading the first clue.

I put my cookbook on the glass.

Having appeared familiar with the machine, I barely examined the screen when it was my turn. The photograph emerged large enough to compete for the cover. The last two lines of ingredients were missing.

The woman looked up from her new sheet.

“Your lunch is missing a couple of clues too.”

I laughed, then hesitated over paying again. I knew the ingredients included egg and flour. Perhaps I could fill in the amounts from memory. When I checked the book, I discovered I'd reversed them. I put the spoiled copy aside, restored the normal scale, and tried again.

The pages kept pulling toward the middle. With one hand holding the cover, I couldn't reach start with the other. I considered pressing the whole spine down.

“The lid doesn't have to close,” the woman said.

I stopped. She pointed to its raised edge without pushing the book for me. A staff member passed. I asked whether it was all right like this. They looked and said yes, provided I didn't force the spine flat.

“Could you press it for me?” I asked the woman.

She did. I held the book while the light traveled slowly beneath it, afraid to move my fingers. The new copy was gray near the fold, but all the words were there. I let go too quickly. The book snapped shut, making me look up at my own noise.

“At least you haven't copied your hand,” she said.

I checked the edge. No fingers, only a little black border from the cookbook. We laughed together. She returned to her clue without asking why one person needed so many potato pancakes. I didn't explain that Saturday wasn't settled yet.

This time, I checked from the title to the final line before putting the copy away.

She went to a window table with a pen. She hadn't handed over the rest of the puzzle to me. It was now simply a crossword she could see. How much she completed in what remained of the day had nothing to do with me.

I left the cookbook on the designated trolley without borrowing it. The spoiled copy came home folded; its back was still usable. I tucked the good one into the inner side of my bag, away from the lunch container. Before leaving, I drank some water and noticed the darkness beyond the window. Tonight, nobody was waiting for me to sit in front of them and make a small event mean something.

At home, I washed the lunch container first.

The recipe curled slightly when I laid it on the dry table. I weighted it with an empty cup and opened the conversation channel. Hearing I'd been to the library, Lumi asked, “New book?”

“One page of new lunch.”

“Haven't you had lunch?”

“Saturday's. Possibly.”

I described the potato pancakes and mentioned an expensive but minor mistake at the copier. She asked how expensive. I told her I'd paid for one extra sheet. I didn't promote it into a disaster.

“You enlarged something for someone and forgot to change it back?”

“Yes.”

“Yours should be called a giant potato pancake.”

“There was mostly a photograph. Not much method left.”

She laughed. I told her the part of the visit that belonged to me, including my urge to write a paragraph about the woman's movements before discovering I had no idea what she wanted.

“Did she finish?” Lumi asked.

“What?”

“The crossword.”

“I don't know. I left first.”

“You didn't have a look?”

“No.”

After a pause, she said I'd brought home a short story today. I nearly said it wasn't a story. That sounded too much like correcting her, so I said, “I don't particularly want to make it longer.”

“Then don't. How many potatoes do you have?”

I went to the kitchen to count, then came back and told her. She asked about the recipe's quantities. I read the ingredients aloud. A whole batch was more than I needed for one meal. I could halve it, I said. We'd decide on Saturday. She didn't organize the kitchen for me. She only objected to my reading “a little flour” as though it were an unreliable promise.

“How little?”

“There's an amount farther down. Exactly where my bad copy ends.”

“That one's not allowed to be head chef.”

I set the spoiled sheet apart and examined the good copy again. Lumi asked whether I planned to put the woman from the library at the bus stop.

“I thought of giving someone a newspaper,” I said. “I don't want to now.”

“Newspapers aren't allowed on buses?”

“They are. But I keep making things I've just seen go straight to work for the manuscript. She wanted larger print. I don't want to send her into another problem.”

“Your own two people have enough already,” Lumi said.

I fetched the yellow notebook. The bus-stop page was still unfinished: the paper bag, the cardboard beneath it, the person I'd kept from speaking. Sunday's note remained: Resting here doesn't mean he wants to trick someone into waiting for a bus. Beside it, I added a question: Is he meeting someone?

The question mark didn't answer for me. But I knew I didn't want him to stay merely because he liked having company.

“I think he's returning something,” I said.

“What?”

“A borrowed loaf tin. It's in the paper bag. The cardboard stops it breaking through the bottom. He arranged to meet someone by the old bus sign. He isn't catching a bus there.”

Lumi didn't immediately comment. I drew a tin that resembled a brick. Even to me, it hardly seemed worth borrowing.

“Then why doesn't he tell the other person?” she asked.

“At first, he thinks they're meeting someone too. Later, when he realizes they aren't, he's already been sitting there so long that saying something will make him look bad.”

“It will.”

“Yes. He can't keep saying nothing.”

She asked how I meant him to find out. After considering it, I wrote the other person looking from the timetable toward the corner, then lifting a bag strap off their shoulder. That was still too easy to misunderstand. Eventually, I only added a question beside it: Is this bus late again?

“If they ask him and he still pretends not to hear, it isn't shyness anymore,” Lumi said.

“I know. I don't want to give him that many good reasons.”

“You've already given him a loaf tin.”

I laughed. She wasn't entirely convinced by the motive, but she hadn't taken the tin away. I didn't announce that the story was finished simply because its character could now look a little less bad.

As I closed the notebook, I remembered something. I pulled the good recipe from under the cup and read her the ingredients again, slowly this time, including the quantities. At the onion, she interrupted. Was I planning to buy a whole bag tomorrow?

“One's enough.”

“You said you only needed a few potatoes too.”

“That's a fair criticism.”

In the margin, I wrote one onion. I didn't add the color of the woman's coat. Lumi asked what I'd written. I told her.

“Just an onion?”

“Just an onion.”

“That shouldn't have taken long.”

I put the pen down, letting her hear the cap click into place. Tonight, there really wasn't another item.
