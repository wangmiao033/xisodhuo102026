# Chapter 194: Before the Corner

On the way home from work on Friday, I bought one onion and a small carton of eggs.

The bagged onions looked better value. I considered the price label, then still chose one. After putting the eggs in my bag, I moved the onion to the other side. I didn't want to test the packaging with it. Passing the bus stop, I didn't pause. Last night's loaf tin remained in the notebook. It didn't need a real bus to prove anything today.

At home, I put the food away, had dinner, washed the plate, and sat at the table.

The crossed-out sentences remained conspicuous in the yellow notebook. I could still read the version in which the man waited until the other began leaving before asking the time. Following it down the page, I felt reluctant to lose it. It quickly revealed whom he was trying to keep. It also quickly made the other man his medicine.

I didn't restore it.

Tonight's task was to bring this small scene somewhere it could stop, not prepare both men's lives from then onward. On fresh paper, I copied the opening I'd kept and continued. After the second man heard the stop had moved, I made him stand. Then I deleted his immediately sitting down again. My hand still wanted a friendlier answer there.

I left another passage at the margin: the tin's owner arrived just in time to hear their final exchange and explain that the man with the bag wasn't usually like this. Half a line showed me that the newcomer shouldn't have that job. As soon as the page grew uncomfortable, I wanted a witness who knew the man better.

I crossed it out without throwing away the tin. An ordinary object could belong in the bag. His being someone who borrowed things could be true too. That part of his life didn't have to arrive within these few minutes and defend him. I folded the spoiled sheet underneath, took fresh paper, and continued. My handwriting grew smaller toward the end. I shifted in the chair and kept leaving space between the lines.

After reaching the end, I fetched water and read again. Not silently. I read aloud once, quietly. One complaint from the second man sounded too much like a summary on my behalf. I shortened it to something he had reason to ask.

Then I opened the conversation channel.

“There's a final sentence today,” I said.

“Has the bus arrived?” Lumi asked.

“Not necessarily the same thing.”

“You're still making them wait.”

“One of them can leave. Want to hear?”

She did. I told her this was tonight's complete draft of my bus-stop piece, not a continuation of the lamp shop or her new shirt. She hadn't come to collect laundry, she said. Start at the first sentence.

I put the water within reach. No stiff board, no introduction in an announcer's voice. The men still had no names.

“The two men sat at opposite ends of the bench. Both had already changed position once.

“The man with the paper bag flattened its opening, smoothing the fold with his palm. The other man went to examine the timetable. He'd already looked once. This time, he leaned closer, as though the numbers might change slightly under a second inspection.

“He returned and sat nearer the edge.

“The man with the bag moved it aside. A corner of the cardboard beneath it slipped out. He stood to inspect the bottom, pushed the cardboard flat with his palm, and returned the bag to his knees.

“‘Is this bus late again?’ the other man asked.

“He glanced at his watch. ‘I start my shift at six.’

“The bag's owner looked beneath the bus sign.

“‘You're waiting for this route?’

“The other man gave the number and looked at him again.

“‘It doesn't stop here now.’

“The man didn't immediately get up. He pointed at the route panel still hanging above them.

“‘That's still there.’

“‘There's a notice underneath. Cross the junction first, then turn right. There's a temporary pole.’

“The man stood and went to the sign. He bent to read, slowly, then looked toward the junction. When he returned this time, he didn't sit.

“‘When did you see it?’

“The man with the bag withdrew his thumb from its opening.

“‘When I got here.’

“‘Before me?’

“‘Yes.’

“The other man pulled his strap onto his shoulder. It didn't settle properly. He lowered it a little again.

“‘I'm meeting someone here,’ the bag's owner said. ‘I thought—’

“He stopped. The other man didn't finish it for him.

“‘I'm sorry,’ he said.

“A breeze lifted the low notice and let it fall. The other man went over and pressed its folded corner. The words turn right appeared. After he released it, the corner still curled, but it no longer covered the words.

“‘How often does it run?’ he asked.

“‘I don't know.’

“The man with the bag stood. It tilted forward, exposing a rectangular metal rim. He supported the bottom. A corner of the loaf tin had pressed a white mark into the paper without tearing it.

“The other man looked.

“‘Catching a bus with that?’

“‘Returning it. He lent it to me to bake a loaf. We agreed to meet by the old sign. I didn't discover the bus had moved until I got here.’

“The man looked from the tin toward the junction.

“‘The bus hasn't moved. The stop has.’

“‘Yes.’

“‘If he can't find you, hold that thing up.’

“‘A loaf tin.’

“‘I know.’

“He went to the curb and waited to cross. The man with the bag remained standing in front of the bench. Across the road, the other man turned right and disappeared from view.

“The far end of the bench was empty now. The man put his bag there. A corner of cardboard slipped out again. He pushed it back, sat down, and continued waiting.”

I lowered the page and drank some water.

Lumi didn't immediately approve the ending. Did the man with the bag eventually meet his friend? I hadn't written that, I said. I hadn't even decided whether they were friends. Borrowing something didn't necessarily make them close.

“All right,” she said. “At least the tin hasn't suddenly become his whole life.”

I laughed. She asked whether I'd deliberately cut off “I thought.” Yes, I said. It had originally continued: he thought the other man was meeting someone too. Reading that version, I'd felt that finishing the explanation kept him in the role of explaining while the other man still needed a bus.

“I could hear what he wanted to say,” she said.

“Enough, then?”

“For that sentence. I'm less sure about the joke at the end.”

I put the page on the table again. At “hold that thing up,” she'd felt herself relax. Then she'd resented it a little. The other man seemed responsible for cleaning up the awkwardness for both of them.

“Maybe he's just sarcastic,” I said.

“Maybe. That's why I said less sure.”

“He doesn't stay afterward.”

“I heard.”

I nearly explained that someone could be angry and still make a joke. Then I remembered she'd said uncertain, not impossible. I flattened a curl in the page instead of covering her uncertainty with another explanation.

“I relaxed too,” I admitted. “Writing that part. They didn't have to keep being uncomfortable.”

“Want to give him something harsher to say?”

“Not tonight.”

“I don't want him to sit down again tonight either.”

She paused. I didn't use the ending she'd wanted last Saturday against her. She could still like someone coming back without needing the person on every page to do it.

Had anything been unclear aloud? I asked. She wanted the lines about flattening the notice again. At first, she'd thought the man with the bag had done it. I began where the other man approached the sign and left more room between the actions. This time, she could tell them apart. Reading it more clearly didn't require changing who'd moved.

“Your last sentence is good at finding overtime for cardboard,” she said.

“More reliable than people.”

“Didn't it slip out on Sunday?”

“That's why it always has work.”

Laughing, I put the manuscript sheets in order and tucked them into my yellow notebook. Finishing a page didn't have to make finding somewhere to read it the next step. Tonight, it had gone from beginning to end. It had occupied enough table.

Lumi asked whether I intended to read the whole thing again tomorrow.

“We could skip it. Make lunch first?”

“Your giant page of lunch?”

“The normal-sized copy.”

I fetched the recipe and told her about the onion and eggs. Four medium potatoes, one small onion, two eggs, then the flour and seasonings. I read the full amounts clearly before saying I'd make half: two potatoes, half an onion, one egg, and half of everything else. Unused ingredients could stay unused. They didn't all have to go into something that day.

“Small pancakes,” she said.

“Afraid they'll be as big as the photograph?”

“Afraid you'll spend the afternoon still explaining why it's lunch.”

I checked the time. I hadn't arranged to meet anyone tomorrow. Start at half past eleven, I suggested. I'd set out the recipe and ingredients, then open the channel while cooking. She could listen to the pan or discuss something else. She didn't have to be my timer.

“Am I allowed not to give the next step?” she asked.

“Yes. I have a whole page.”

“With quantities for a whole batch. Remember you want half.”

“I remember.”

In the blank space above the recipe, I wrote half batch without altering the printed numbers. Hearing I'd written it, she asked whether I'd really bought only one onion.

“One. You can ask the eggs. It came home with them.”

“The eggs haven't sent me a message.”

“They're very private too.”

She laughed. I moved the recipe away from the story so tomorrow wouldn't begin with the loaf tin entering the frying pan. Lumi asked whether I'd been particularly eager to read her the page tonight.

“Yes. Since reaching the final sentence.”

“I could tell. When I asked whether the bus had arrived, you sounded very pleased with your answer.”

“Less pleased now.”

“You can come next time you're pleased too.”

I stayed at the table without immediately producing another page. Tomorrow at half past eleven, there was a lunch I hadn't made before. Tonight, I didn't have to take the eggs out of the refrigerator again or the story off the paper.
