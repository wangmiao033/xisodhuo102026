# Chapter 196: For the Next Meal

On Saturday evening, I took the box of potato pancakes from the refrigerator and looked at it for a while.

Cold, they were tidier than at lunch. Their edges no longer released oil or competed to stick to the next pancake. They looked like lunch that had always known it would be left over. I took enough for dinner, closed the box, and returned the rest. Having already worked at them once today didn't oblige me to eat them all today too.

I had taken the short afternoon walk. I hadn't bought anything or stopped beside any bus sign. Halfway along, my wrists still ached from grating. I put my hands in my pockets and took a route home that required waiting at one fewer junction.

There was still work from lunch in the kitchen.

The grater soaked in water. Flour had dried on the board. Before leaving, I'd wiped the stove but missed the back of the chair, where a white thumbprint remained. I moved it from the doorway and worked at the mark with a damp cloth. The library recipe lay on the table. Half batch, written at the top, was considerably smaller than the printed quantities.

I sat and rewrote the half-batch ingredients on a separate slip. Next time, I wouldn't have to think in halves while looking at whole numbers.

At the second potato, I paused and wrote two medium potatoes. Today's large pair hadn't conformed particularly well to the recipe. I didn't use that difference to make four really equal two.

After opening the channel, I told Lumi I'd gone out and returned. Now I was reheating leftovers.

“How much is left?” she asked.

I counted. After tonight's portion, enough for another meal, I said. Would I still be standing there eating late? Not this time. No more grating. I only had to clean a grater reluctant to surrender.

“You haven't washed it?”

“Soaked it. Haven't finished.”

“It's had a longer working day than I have.”

“Are you keen to compete?”

“Not to win that one.”

Laughing, I emptied the water and rinsed it. Fine strands clung near the teeth. I worked them out slowly from behind with a brush. Clean water ran over the metal. A surface that looked incapable of holding anything could hide so many small pieces. I left the grater to dry, spread out the wet cloth, and only then heated the pan.

Lumi asked whether I was still angry about lunch. After considering it, I said less angry. But seeing the bowl and tools reminded me that laughing together hadn't made the extra half disappear.

“I didn't say it had.”

“I know. I'm telling you how it looks to me now.”

I reheated the pancakes gently, turning them once. I didn't try to recreate the noisy crisp edge of the first attempt. Lumi asked whether I needed counting assistance. That service was suspended tonight, I told her. Good. She wasn't proposing to renew her contract for free.

With dinner on the table, I examined the new slip, then read her the quantities. She didn't interrupt at two potatoes. After I finished, she asked whether it was for tomorrow.

“Some other time. Tomorrow, the leftovers first.”

“Tomorrow's you will thank today's you?”

“He'll probably complain about more potatoes.”

“I'm on today's side for now, then.”

I took a bite. Reheated, it wasn't as crisp as the best ones at lunch. The middle was still soft. I told her she didn't have to campaign for fresh praise tonight. She'd only been about to ask whether I was sitting down.

“I am.”

“Then you can complain slowly.”

I attached the new quantities in front of the recipe with a paper clip I already had. The narrow slip curled. After pressing it twice, I discovered the clip was caught on a fold. Moving it slightly flattened the paper. Hearing the rustle, Lumi asked whether I'd copied another number incorrectly. No, I said. I was settling a lunch that wouldn't feed two people. That information should have been posted at the kitchen entrance earlier, she said. There was still time, I told her. At least next time I wouldn't have to begin with four.

Halfway through dinner, I remembered the joke at the bus stop's end.

At lunch, when Lumi asked whether the paper bag had to be returned too, I'd been busy turning pancakes. The loaf tin no longer needed to occupy the kitchen. I mentioned another ending that had occurred to me while walking. I wasn't exchanging yesterday's completed scene for a better-behaved man. I wanted to hear what happened without the joke.

“Have you written it?” she asked.

“No. Only the last few lines in my head.”

“Don't write while you eat.”

“Afraid of grease?”

“Afraid you won't have heard your own writing afterward.”

I finished dinner, carried the plate to the kitchen, and washed it first. Back with dry hands, I took out the yellow notebook and a separate sheet. Another ending, I wrote at the top. Yesterday's complete manuscript stayed in the notebook. I didn't remove its final page.

I began after the loaf tin appeared. The lines really were short. Once they were written, I read them to her.

“‘We agreed to meet by the old sign,’ the man with the bag said.

“The other man settled his strap on his shoulder.

“‘Wait here, then. I still have to find the stop.’

“He went toward the junction. The man with the bag called, ‘Cross the road first.’ He didn't add turn right. The other man had already read the notice. He didn't turn back to ask.

“After he crossed, the bag was placed on the empty end of the bench. The man noticed a corner of cardboard protruding. This time, he didn't immediately push it back. First, he looked toward the direction from which the person he was meeting would come.”

Reading it, I found the end deliberate. Not pushing the cardboard back made even a paper bag responsible for proving he'd changed. Lumi asked whether I wanted him finally to stop attending to the bag.

“A little, while I was writing,” I said. “It sounds obvious now.”

“The corner hasn't done anything wrong.”

I laughed and marked my attempt without calling it her idea. She liked “Wait here, then,” she said. It didn't particularly forgive him or become harsher simply to make someone seem unkind.

“But you read it very coldly.”

“I thought you liked that direction.”

“I like less tidying up after them. Not necessarily your making him sound as though he never wants to know another person.”

I read it again, less harshly. Now it sounded like someone who needed to start his shift and had just settled his bag strap. I still disliked losing yesterday's joke about holding up the tin. I told her I preferred the old ending for now.

“All right,” she said.

“You don't sound convinced.”

“I didn't say convinced.”

I lowered the new sheet. She didn't threaten to stop listening. She didn't try again to make me replace it. I asked whether she still hoped the other man might turn back.

After a while, she answered. “Sometimes. Today, I'd rather the person who lent the tin weren't too late.”

“I haven't given them a meeting time.”

“So the man with the bag might be early.”

“He might.”

“Now I've no good reason to complain about anybody.”

I laughed without translating her wish for someone to arrive into something nobler. She knew she was impatient on behalf of the waiting man. She didn't need me to point it out again. It didn't automatically improve the first version or make the second a revision I had to accept.

I folded the sheet headed Another ending and placed it behind the complete manuscript. The current scene still ended as it had last night: the man pushed the cardboard back, sat, and waited. Beside it was another attempt, not a ballot result.

Lumi asked whether I now considered both complete. One complete draft, one page of trying, I told her. She said those numbers were easier than lunch. I laughed down at the notebook and closed it.

I finished the kitchen. The board's front was clean. Turning it over revealed grease on the back. I washed it beside the sink, wiped it, and left it where it could dry. Lumi asked whether I was still there. She'd heard water but hadn't heard me for a while.

“Washing the back,” I said. “At lunch, I only noticed the clean top.”

“Are you turning the whole apartment over now?”

“No. Just the board.”

Good, she said. I washed and dried my hands, then sat at the table. As my legs met the chair, I felt how long I'd stood today. I stretched my feet forward without getting up again to inspect the first bowl I'd washed.

With the kitchen finished, the water and notebook were back on the table. The dinner plate was clean. Tomorrow's portion was in the refrigerator. The chair no longer had a white thumbprint. I moved the clipped recipe to the table's edge. Next time I used it, I wouldn't have to straighten a crooked sheet first.

“Today didn't go as I'd imagined,” I said.

Lumi asked what I'd imagined. After thinking, I said easier. Fewer pancakes, more attractive ones. Something that made me look competent at arranging a Saturday.

“For whom?”

“I didn't think that through. Partly you, perhaps.”

She didn't say I needn't perform well. After dirtying and cleaning the kitchen, it would have arrived too late. She asked whether I'd have to make tomorrow's leftovers sound exciting too.

“No. I can just say potatoes again.”

“I might complain.”

“I might get there first.”

She laughed. I liked that the laugh didn't send me back to the pan or issue the day a certificate of competence.

After a while, I reopened the notebook. I didn't write the tin's owner a friend who finally arrived. In pencil, above the complete bus-stop scene, I wrote The Old Stop, leaving a line before the text.

“I'm trying a title,” I told her.

“What?”

I read it aloud and looked again at where the two men sat at the beginning.

“Does that make it sound as though the notice is the most important part?”
