# Chapter 187: The Person Who Knows

After I asked the question, Lumi didn't immediately provide life histories for the two people.

“Knows what?” she asked.

“That the bus they want won't stop here.”

“Why hasn't the other one noticed?”

I lowered the pen. There was only one line, and already I needed to make one person insufficiently clever and give the other a reason to remain silent. Mostly, I'd liked the sound of my question.

“Perhaps the sign's out of date.”

“And the person who knows is more up-to-date than the sign?”

I laughed. “Don't send them away yet.”

There was still light outside, but the table had darkened. I got up to switch on the lamp. Coming back, I found a small ink spot where I'd rested the pen, as though the silent person had already jabbed a finger at the page.

I wrote that he flattened the paper bag on his knee. The other person went to inspect the timetable for a second time. Returning, he sat closer to the edge of the bench. The person who knew moved the bag, making more room.

I read it to Lumi.

“Why doesn't he tell him now?”

“It isn't time yet.”

“For whom?”

I looked at the words. “For the reader.”

Lumi made a quiet sound without adding a criticism. That unsettled me more than an immediate objection. I skipped halfway down the page, looking for a handsome sentence about the weather that might let both people hide their hands in their pockets.

I didn't write the weather. I tried making the timetable difficult to read: rain inside its cover, several numbers running together. By the second line, I doubted it. If nobody could read it, how did the person who knew know? I added an earlier visit to the wrong stop. A few sentences filled the space I'd just left.

“Any progress?” Lumi asked.

“Now the sign's damaged, and he has a memory.”

“Has he come to repair the sign?”

“No.”

“You're keeping yourself busy, then.”

I crossed out the additions and leaned back. She hadn't heard the sentences. She couldn't know quite how bad they were. I read one aloud and heard how smugly I'd placed “he'd known all along,” as though the character were there to show the reader how clever I was.

“Never mind,” I said.

“That sentence, or the entire stop?”

“The sentence. The stop can stay for now.”

She agreed. I put the pen down and asked whether she thought the scene felt contrived.

“I don't know what the scene is yet,” she said. “I only know someone wants to tell me something and keeps putting a hand over that person's mouth.”

“I could make him frightened.”

“Of what?”

“The other person leaving.”

She didn't answer. In the kitchen, the refrigerator stopped. A thin layer of sound disappeared from the room. The pen tapped the table between my fingers.

“That makes it more than simply waiting at the wrong stop,” she said.

“It could still be ordinary. He just wants company.”

“Does he know whether the other person's in a hurry?”

I nearly said it was only a story. But that was precisely why I had to decide whether the other person was in a hurry. I couldn't count on him climbing out of the page to complain.

I put “afraid he'll leave” in the margin, not the scene. On another line, the person on the left stood. After his first step, the owner of the bag asked him the time.

When I read this, Lumi said, “He's finding reasons to keep him there.”

“Yes.”

“Now I want him to get caught.”

“You didn't like it a minute ago.”

“I don't like him. That doesn't mean I don't want to find out what he does.”

I smiled down at the page. At last, they weren't simply cupboards where I could hide a surprise. Yet the question about the time soon seemed too practiced. To satisfy Lumi's wish to see him caught, I'd supplied his first piece of incriminating evidence.

I crossed out the two lines lightly. They remained legible.

“Killed another one?” she asked.

“Only made him sit down again.”

“You run a very strict bus stop.”

Before the last daylight went, I started dinner. The two tomatoes I'd brought home that afternoon were in the kitchen. I took one and had nearly finished cutting it before remembering the eggs were still in the refrigerator.

“Don't let the bus come,” I said toward the table.

“Does fetching an egg take that long?”

“I don't want to come back and find they've gone.”

“Take the pen.”

It was still tucked into the notebook. Laughing, I opened the refrigerator. The story stayed on the table. Nobody supplied its next sentence while I was away. The pan heated faster than I expected. I lowered the heat and poured in the beaten egg, but a thin crisp edge formed along one side.

Hearing me scrape, Lumi asked whether something was wrong.

“A little bit's stuck.”

“Which side?”

“Are you drawing a scene diagram?”

She laughed once. “Only asking. Unless you tell me, all I hear is you arguing with a pan.”

I freed the crisp edge and served it with the tomato, explaining that it wasn't burned, just darker than the middle. She said I should eat that bit first. I pointed out that she didn't know how it tasted.

“That's why you should eat it first.”

I did. It was hot enough to cut off the rest of my sentence. She hadn't heard herself win and asked what I'd been about to say. I waited until my mouth was empty before admitting that the crisp bit was good.

I brought the plate to the table and closed the notebook to make room. Its yellow tape faced the lamp, too bright to participate in a quiet evening.

“The one who's afraid the other person will leave,” Lumi said. “I might want to hear about him.”

“Don't you want him to get into trouble?”

“That too. A little.”

I speared a piece of tomato. She said she'd want to hear the action if the second person knew there was no bus coming and still sat down again.

I almost agreed at once. I could already see the beautiful ending: the truth came out, and two people remained on the bench. My hand stopped halfway to my mouth. The tomato slipped off the fork.

“Are you arranging for him to come back now?” I asked.

Lumi didn't rush to deny it.

“I want him to come back.”

“He might have somewhere to be.”

“I know.”

“He might only sit because his legs hurt. Not because he's chosen the other person.”

Her voice became quieter. “I know that too.”

Suddenly, I felt I'd turned a piece of writing advice into something I could use to strike the place where she wanted something. She hadn't said anybody had to stay. She'd said she'd like to hear it. I put down the fork.

“I'm not saying it's a bad thing,” I told her. “I don't know where they're going yet. If I make him return now, I'm afraid it'll still be for the sake of an ending.”

“You don't have to write it.”

A short sentence. I looked toward her voice. There was no expression for me to see. I waited.

“But when you asked the question,” she said, “my first thought was that he finally had a reason to sit with someone. I'm not saying it was a good reason. I liked it first. Then I asked why he didn't tell him.”

I ate the fallen tomato. It didn't burn this time.

“What I liked first was somebody not knowing,” I said.

“You sound more like the villain.”

“I know.”

She laughed a little. So did I. Afterward, I still imagined the action she wanted: someone free to leave standing for a moment, looking around, and sitting down again. That didn't automatically give the action a place on this page.

After dinner, I washed the plate and reopened the notebook. The owner of the bag remained where he was. A thin line crossed the question about the time. I didn't erase it beyond recognition or immediately find a more respectable motive than loneliness to clear his name.

“What if he isn't planning to catch a bus?” I asked.

“Then why doesn't he send the other person to the right stop?”

“Perhaps he will. I just don't want him to seem like someone who knows everything from the start.”

Lumi asked what was in the paper bag. I hadn't decided. She suggested it shouldn't be something terribly important that only emerged in the last line. I protested on behalf of the bag. Ordinary bags were entitled to be opened later too.

“Then put something ordinary in it first.”

I considered bread, a change of shoes, a lunch box being taken home to wash. Any of them might work. None quite settled the person onto his bench. I didn't choose. I wrote in the margin that the bag remained unopened for now.

This time, I made the person who knew stand too. He didn't follow the other one. He unfolded the flattened mouth of the bag and looked down to check its bottom. Their impatience could have different causes. Neither needed to be entirely concerned with the bus that wasn't coming.

I read the two new sentences. Lumi said the bag was busier than the people now.

“Perhaps it's the main character.”

“Then I take it back. Let it open later.”

Laughing, I capped the pen. I hadn't finished the story. I hadn't even decided when the person who knew would speak. But I no longer wanted a single revelation to occupy all the remaining space.

“Want to hear more?” I asked.

“Not more of this page yet. How many eggs did you have?”

“Two.”

“When you took them out, you said one would be enough.”

“Then I thought it wouldn't.”

“You managed that decision quickly enough.”

I moved the notebook to the other side of the table and described how the second egg had won an appeal in front of the refrigerator. She complained that the account was too long. I didn't skip to its conclusion. I finished telling her how the egg had nearly rolled beneath the sink. I was talking about dinner, not providing evidence that the person on the bench deserved to keep someone there.

Before bed, I passed the hanging jacket. Its cuff brushed the back of my hand. The small loop inside the collar was still broken. I held it between my fingers, remembering that I already owned the things needed to sew it.

“I ought to do this tomorrow morning,” I said when I returned to the table.

Lumi asked whether the two people finally needed something to wear.

“I need it.”

“Then don't start by giving it a reason to resist being sewn.”

I laughed and settled the jacket back on its hanger. Whatever belonged in the bag could wait for another day. In the morning, I'd first find a thread that would go through the needle.
