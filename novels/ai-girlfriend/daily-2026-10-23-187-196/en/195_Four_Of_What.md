# Chapter 195: Four of What

Just before half past eleven on Saturday, I put the recipe on a chair outside the kitchen doorway.

It was away from the pan, where I could see it by looking down. Thursday's bad copy remained with the used paper. I took out the onion and eggs I'd bought last night, then chose potatoes from the basket. Two large, two smaller. They all needed cleaning. I put all four in the sink, intending to wash them and use only the large pair.

When I opened the conversation channel, Lumi asked whether the pan had started work. Still waiting for supplies, I told her. She said I shouldn't dock its pay.

I washed and peeled the potatoes, cutting away one ugly patch. Wet peel accumulated in the sink. The recipe stayed dry. As agreed last night, I was making half a batch. One egg and half the onion already waited beside the board.

“I'll start with four,” I said.

“Get all four ready before you touch the onion,” Lumi said.

I thought she meant I shouldn't do too many things at once. I agreed and picked up the grater. The two large potatoes went into the bowl, followed by a smaller one. Peeled potatoes were slippery. I left thicker pieces at the end rather than turn lunch into a contest between my fingers and the tool.

By the fourth, my wrist ached.

“These four pancakes might be impressive,” I said.

“Pancakes?”

“Yes. Larger than the photograph.”

She paused. “When you said four, you meant four pancakes?”

My hand stopped over the bowl.

“When you said get all four ready, you meant potatoes?”

“Yes.”

I looked at the recipe on the chair. Four potatoes for a whole batch. Last night, we'd agreed to halve it. With all of them within reach, I'd heard her entirely natural “four” and abandoned the next step I'd already decided on.

“I've prepared a whole batch of potatoes,” I said.

“You said you were only washing two.”

“I said using two. I washed four.”

“I thought you'd changed your mind.”

I put the grater down. The bowl didn't look like one person's quiet lunch, particularly now I was hungry.

“Then you should have asked.”

“Yes. You could have looked when you reached the third.”

That annoyed me more, because I'd been the one to pick up the third. I didn't hold the fourth up as further evidence against her. I fetched another egg. The remaining onion would have to go in too, with the full amount of flour. It wasn't beyond saving. The weekend in which I'd meant to do a little less had simply grown inside the bowl.

“I'm sorry,” she said.

I was cutting onion. My eyes had begun to sting, and I didn't answer immediately. She said my name again.

“I heard. Wait a moment. I'm not crying.”

“I didn't say you were.”

“Establishing that first.”

She laughed a little. So did I, not attractively. Wiping my eyes made me feel sorrier for myself. I washed my hands and returned to the rest of the onion. Lumi didn't use the interval to supply five more solutions.

The grated potato needed some water squeezed out. I wrapped a portion in a clean cloth and twisted with both hands. The first bundle was too large. A few strands escaped into the sink. I removed them and used smaller portions. More water ran out than I'd expected. By the end, my fingers understood what a whole batch meant.

I couldn't leave the cloth in a heap. I shook away the clinging strands, rinsed it, and draped it over an empty basin. More water had collected in the bowl. I poured some away. My newly dried hands were wet again. Lumi asked why I'd gone quiet. I told her lunch now required drying my hands twice.

“You can ask me for the next step now,” she said.

“Next step.”

“Read your own piece of paper.”

I laughed aloud. The recipe was still on the chair. Our argument hadn't made it harder to read. I mixed in onion, eggs, and flour, added seasoning, switched on the stove myself, and poured oil into the pan.

I still made four in the first batch.

They crowded together. Pushing them apart on one side brought them together on the other. Unwilling to admit that even the pan couldn't accommodate four, I hurried to turn the first when it began to color underneath. The spatula supported only half. The top split, like a pancake someone had tried to fold in a hurry.

Lumi heard me sigh and asked what had happened.

“I've made one into two.”

“So the quantity problem's solved.”

“There's a fee for laughing at me now.”

“That one was before the rule. Free.”

I really laughed. Afterward, I could continue. I pushed the broken pieces aside and turned the others slowly, no longer trying to make them match. I lowered the heat. The edges still hissed faintly. Lumi could only hear them. Were they crisp yet? They looked it, I told her. I'd have to eat one to know.

Once the first batch was on a plate, I tried the broken one. Cooked through, but soft in the thick places and harder at the edge than I liked. I told her. She asked me not to summarize it with “all right.”

“The onion's a little sweet. Inside, it's like boiled potato. Outside, something else with stronger opinions.”

“Could you make the next batch thinner?” she asked.

“Didn't you just tell me to get everything ready first?”

“The potatoes. I didn't say to crowd four pancakes into the pan.”

I held the broken piece without eating it. She hadn't said she'd known better all along. I'd already supplied the tone.

“I'm describing the taste now,” I said.

“What am I supposed to say, then?”

She didn't ask pleasantly. I wanted to say anything, though that apparently excluded what she'd just said. The pan was still hot. I put the plate down and added only three for the next batch, pressing them thinner.

“Let me finish this mouthful first,” I said.

She didn't answer. I chewed, drank some water, and returned to watch the edges. Once they had more color, I tried turning one. The spatula went under easily. A whole pancake flipped. I wanted to announce success and leave out the sentence in between.

“I made these thinner, as you suggested,” I told her. “One's turned over.”

“I heard.”

“You didn't hear it turn.”

“I heard you say as I suggested.”

I laughed, still reluctant. “I thought you were about to tell me you'd known all along.”

“I did want to. A little.”

“Good thing you didn't.”

“Good thing you didn't make me guess what you'd turned into two this time.”

I looked at the three pancakes. All intact. She didn't congratulate me. I didn't feel I needed that before carrying lunch away.

After lifting them out, I switched off the heat and took the plate to the table. I ate two. Hearing the fork touch the plate, Lumi asked about the sound. I told her I was fighting lunch for an edge that remained attached.

“Wouldn't a knife work?”

“It's beside the chopping board. I've sat down.”

“What a difficulty.”

I didn't stand to prove I wasn't lazy. Another attempt separated the piece. She heard me laugh and asked whether that counted as success. Yes, I said, though the size wasn't quite right. There was only my plate on the table, only my fork making noise. When I stopped, she could say my name. She couldn't continue eating for me.

I'd wanted the feeling of someone being busy beside me today. Once I was genuinely busy, I'd wanted advice only when it suited me. Otherwise, she should apparently just say the first batch was worth eating. I rested the fork and admitted part of my anger had been unfair. She didn't ask for a percentage. She said she should have asked what I meant by four too.

“Do you want to hear more about the taste?” I asked.

“After you've finished that mouthful.”

I did. She didn't fill the pause by explaining herself. She waited for me to speak again. This one was better, I told her. Thinner. The onion no longer hid in little clumps inside it.

The remaining mixture still needed cooking. Water had already begun to seep out again. I didn't want to leave a bowl of raw ingredients to solve at dinner. Lunch had occupied a comfortable little square in my imagination. Now it extended beyond both ends.

“These again tonight?” Lumi asked.

“At least I won't have to grate potatoes.”

“You could save some for someone else.”

“Who?”

She hesitated. “I only said you could.”

“Do you want me to?”

“Not particularly.”

I waited with my fork. She said she'd suddenly imagined that making too much food might turn today into another day I went out to find someone to eat with.

“I was already going for a walk this afternoon,” I said. “But I haven't arranged a guest. I don't want to start looking for someone because of four potatoes.”

“I know. It sounds tiresome aloud.”

“I'm not calling you tiresome. I'm still hungry. I can't also look after a person who hasn't been invited.”

She laughed quietly, with a tightness I still disliked. I didn't want to promise her my afternoon walk simply to remove it.

“After eating, I have to finish cooking and put things away,” I said. “Then I'll go out. Leftovers tonight. Would you like to keep me company for a less fresh meal?”

“You make it so tempting.”

“I'm not qualified to advertise lunch right now.”

“Tonight's fine. Don't promise to make it again.”

That was easier to agree to.

Back in the kitchen, I switched on the heat and turned the pan so its handle didn't block the bowl. The later pancakes finally stopped fighting for space. While I stood there, Lumi chose a useless subject: if the man with the loaf tin met the person he was waiting for, would he hand over the bag too?

“I haven't thought. It might be his bag.”

“Mean.”

“The tin isn't his.”

“Then why return the bag?”

I turned the last three pancakes. She had a point, but it didn't need settling now. I suggested discussing ownership when I wasn't holding a spatula.

The final batch emerged well after the time I'd intended. I switched off the stove, put the grater to soak, and wiped away grease spots. I spread the remaining pancakes to cool a little, then boxed and refrigerated them. I didn't set another place and leave the portion she couldn't eat growing cold opposite me.

I told her I was ready to go. She asked how long.

“About half an hour. I want to walk slowly.”

“I hope you've had enough to eat.”

“That I can confirm.”

Putting on my shoes, I found a dried strand of potato on my sleeve and threw it away. When I came back for my keys, Lumi called me.

“Elliot. Next time, say four of what.”

“We get a next time today?”

“Not today.”

I laughed and agreed. I returned the recipe to the table. Flour marks remained on the board; I'd finish cleaning tonight. I closed the conversation channel and went out without letting lunch take the whole day.
