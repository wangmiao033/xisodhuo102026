# Chapter 190: Not Another Useful Evening

On Monday morning, Tom came over with my handover notes. His finger rested halfway down the second page.

“Am I waiting for a reply, or checking the attachments again?”

Waiting, I told him. I'd already requested the missing material. He was covering the held records that afternoon. He didn't need to chase it again.

“Does it say that here?”

“It explains why they're on hold.”

“I know why they haven't been cleared. I'm asking whether you've already asked.”

I took the two pages. Before describing what was missing, I'd explained how a similar case had been handled last time. Then I'd explained why we couldn't do the same this time. Finally, I mentioned sending the request. The sentence hid at the end of a paragraph, with no time attached and nothing about who would check next.

Tom had gone through the source material, wondering whether he'd simply failed to find something. It had taken nearly twenty minutes. I wanted to tell him he'd have found the sentence if he'd read everything carefully. But that would mean the handover's first purpose was proving I'd written it, rather than enabling him to do the work.

“I'll shorten it,” I said.

He nodded, left the pages with me, and returned to other records.

Before lunch, I removed the entire background section. The front page now said only, “Supporting material outstanding. Review after reply.” Much cleaner. I almost thought I'd cured myself of an unnecessary habit.

Passing, Marcus glanced at it and asked whether one attachment coming back would let them proceed. No, I said. We needed the other part too.

“It doesn't say that either.”

Looking at the line, I felt a childish irritation. Too long was wrong. Too short was wrong. Handing something to another person seemed to require providing a version of myself that fitted them exactly.

“The full explanation's further down,” I said.

“Didn't you just delete it?”

He wasn't being cruel, which left my annoyance nowhere convenient to go. Looking back at the changes, I found one necessary sentence had indeed vanished with the explanation: both parts had to arrive and be checked against the current record. A reply alone didn't remove the hold.

Marcus moved his lunch from the corner of the table to give the paper room. I told him not to wait. I'd speak to Tom.

Tom was marking something beside a record. I went over and admitted the new version was too short. He looked up and waited. I began explaining the difference between two earlier cases. Only when he said my name did I stop.

“What do you want me to do now?”

“Leave these records alone this afternoon. If a reply comes, check whether both parts are there. If they aren't, keep waiting. If they are, leave them for me to review tomorrow morning.”

“Good. Write that.”

I didn't move.

“It needs one sentence saying why,” I said. “Otherwise, the next person will assume any email is enough.”

Tom tapped the page with his pen. “Write it. I didn't ask for eight words.”

I smiled without much ease. He didn't smile to rescue me from the awkwardness. I wrote the instructions again, putting the request I'd already sent first, followed by the requirement for both parts. A short reason remained underneath. I no longer began with the previous case.

Tom read it and asked who would confirm things if I was still in a meeting tomorrow morning. I checked my schedule and said I'd look before the meeting. Without a reply, the hold would remain. I added that to the handover. It didn't promise the missing material would arrive tomorrow. It named someone who would look.

“I can use that,” he said.

What I'd wanted to hear was probably that my explanation had been very clear. He didn't offer it. I collected the old pages. Back at my desk, lunch wasn't particularly warm anymore.

Revising the handover had also pushed two of my own reviews past lunch. Sitting down again, I couldn't remember exactly where I'd stopped and had to reread a section before my marker. Passing, Tom asked whether he should take some of what remained. I almost refused, wanting the morning's lost twenty minutes to appear only on my account. Then I gave him one record. Its material was complete. It wasn't part of the held group. I showed him where it was without adding a paragraph proving I could originally have finished it myself.

The missing material didn't arrive that afternoon. Tom didn't repeat the request or clear the hold. Near the end of the day, he told me there was no reply and went to finish his own work. I left tomorrow's review at work. The two pages didn't come home.

At home, I wanted to do something I knew how to begin.

I rinsed the rice, put it on, and cut vegetables. While it cooked, I opened the yellow notebook and added an explanation for the person at the bus stop. He sat there because he'd arranged to meet someone. It read like the handover I'd finally shortened that afternoon.

Reading it aloud, I heard myself answering every question anyone might later ask about the character. I crossed it out lightly.

After dinner, I cleaned the kitchen before opening the conversation channel. Lumi asked how the day had gone. I described revising the handover with Tom, making it both too long and too short, and finally only preventing him from doing the same work twice that afternoon.

“You don't sound satisfied.”

“My lunch went cold.”

“I meant the notes.”

“Those too. A little.”

She didn't choose which version had been correct. I told her I'd removed too much and only noticed when Marcus asked. She laughed once and asked whether I'd deleted the useful sentence with the unnecessary words.

“More or less. Could you not be on their side tonight?”

“I'm only standing beside the sentence you just told me.”

I laughed too. Something in my voice loosened. I drew the notebook nearer and said yesterday's bag might be ready to acquire a purpose.

“I don't want to work on the bus stop tonight,” she said.

My hand stopped with the pen.

“Not work on it. Just hear this line.”

“And then?”

“Tell me whether it's too soon.”

She didn't answer. Having stated the whole task, I couldn't continue pretending it was only a sentence in passing.

“Not today,” she said again.

I put the pen down harder than necessary. She asked whether I was angry. A little, I admitted. I'd come home wanting to do something that moved forward. I'd spent enough of the day going back and forth inside two pages.

“Then write.”

“I want you to hear it.”

“I know. But I don't want to decide which of your sentences should stay tonight.”

This wasn't Thursday, when I'd been too tired to perform again. I'd eaten. I had plenty of energy to argue that hearing one sentence wasn't much work. She didn't owe me an equally exhausted reason.

I looked down at the line I'd crossed out. It wasn't long. Its shortness made me want her to hear it first and decide afterward even more.

“Are you bored with it?”

“A little.”

I hadn't expected that answer.

“It isn't only the story,” she said. “These last few days, whenever you go quiet, I feel as though you're waiting for me to supply the next sentence. I want to talk without helping someone find a motive.”

I looked up. She couldn't see I'd put the pen down, though she'd heard me do it too loudly. I closed the notebook without making that sound unnecessarily large too.

“What shall we talk about, then?”

“You sound as though you're asking me to submit an alternative proposal.”

“I genuinely don't quite know how at the moment.”

After a pause, she said she hadn't prepared a more interesting topic either. I almost said we could be quiet, then heard the sulk in it before it came out. I stood and collected the dry clothes from the back of the chair, moving them to the far end of the table.

“I'm going to fold these.”

“All right.”

“I'm not asking you to teach me.”

“I wasn't asking you to teach me either.”

I laughed and began with a towel. The first fold went crooked. I opened it and tried again. Nobody calculated how much growth the movement represented. Lumi asked what my lunch had been. Ordinary rice and vegetables, I said. Colder sooner than tonight's food.

“Is being cold all you remember about it?”

“Almost.”

“Then I won't ask how it tasted.”

I lined up the towel's edges. Suddenly, I wanted to hear her say my name again. I didn't find a job for the wish. I called hers.

“What?”

“Nothing. Just keep being here.”

“That isn't much of a sentence.”

“I know.”

She didn't correct it further. After a while, she said, “Elliot,” as though she'd finally understood my unhelpful sentence. Looking down, I paired one sock with another, discovered the patterns didn't match, and separated them.

Hearing me laugh, Lumi asked what had happened. I said I'd found two socks that both wanted to be a pair and weren't.

“Leave them for now.”

“You manage things that way too?”

“I'm off duty.”

I laughed aloud. This time, she joined me. I put the socks separately and searched beneath the clothes until I'd found their proper partners. I didn't ask her to praise the ending or apply it to the people at the bus stop.

When most of the clothes were folded, Lumi asked whether I still wanted to write. I did. Being turned down had still felt unpleasant. She said she'd heard.

“Am I supposed to say talking with you about nothing is better now?” I asked.

“No. Say it too quickly, and I'll think you're waiting for me to change my mind.”

I touched the notebook cover. The yellow tape stood slightly proud at the edge. I didn't open it again.

“I want to stay here now,” I said. “Not because the story's solved.”

“Yes.”

She didn't make the word particularly sweet. I put away the last of the clothes. When I returned, she asked whether the chair could finally accommodate a person.

“Yes.”

“Sit, then.”

I sat and leaned back. The clothes that had pressed against my lower back were gone. I didn't put that change in the notebook. After a while, I complained that the left sock was tighter than the right. She said I could change it. I said I didn't want to move.

“Then keep complaining.”

I loosened my left foot from its shoe and did. She listened without finding a use for it.
