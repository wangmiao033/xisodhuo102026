# Chapter 188: A Loop That Holds

On Sunday morning, I took the jacket off its hanger and spread it across the table. The sleeves occupied the place where my breakfast plate had been.

The sewing box was smaller than I remembered. It contained several buttons that didn't belong to this jacket and three colors of thread, none much like the lining. I chose dark gray and pulled out a short length. The end frayed. I trimmed it again. It still wouldn't go through.

When I opened the conversation channel, I was holding the needle.

“Morning,” Lumi said.

“Morning. I've already lost to a hole.”

“Is the jacket badly torn?”

“The eye of the needle.”

She laughed. I told her I'd moved into better light. The needle was horizontal, the thread vertical, and each seemed committed to its own way of working. She suggested twisting the end tightly. I put the needle down, twisted twice, and picked it up again. On my third attempt, a little gray appeared on the other side.

I didn't celebrate immediately. The tiny length withdrew faster than I could say it was through.

“Not through yet,” I corrected myself.

“I hadn't got to the praise.”

“Thank you for being slow off the mark.”

“That part's easy.”

I tried again and pulled the end farther through. Lumi asked whether I should leave more thread so I wouldn't need to repeat this halfway through the sewing. That sounded sensible. I pulled out a great deal more, enough that I had to raise my arm to draw it straight. Then I cut it beside the spool.

Before I'd tied the first knot, the thread caught around a jacket button.

I bent over it. After a while, she asked why I'd gone quiet.

“Your advice is working somewhere else now.”

I described how much thread I'd taken. She paused, then asked why I'd left so much.

“You said it would save rethreading.”

“I didn't say you had to sew through next year.”

Laughing loosened my grip. I pinned the needle beneath a fingertip, cut away some thread, and tried the knot again. The offcut was too short to keep and long enough to cling to a sleeve and travel elsewhere. I put it in a small empty saucer rather than let everything disappear into the fabric.

Only one end of the loop had come away. The other remained firmly attached inside the back of the collar. I picked away the loose old thread without pulling at the sound end. The detached part was slightly frayed. Folding that under made the loop shorter. I took it to the hook by the door to check. It still fitted.

“How is it?” Lumi asked.

I answered after returning to the table. “Shorter, but it fits. I'm only checking. I haven't sewn it yet.”

“I wasn't urging you to hang it badly.”

“I know. I was afraid I'd say it worked and decide that meant I'd finished.”

I turned the collar over and found the marks left by the original stitches. Gray against the dark lining wasn't as ugly as I'd feared, or as helpfully visible as I'd have liked. I held it nearer the lamp and discovered I was blocking the light.

Hearing the chair move, Lumi asked whether I needed another lamp. No, I said. My head had reached the place that needed illuminating first. She told me to move my head.

“Thank you. That advice was cheaper than the last lot.”

“You can return the thread. You can't return your head.”

I laughed. The needle I'd just pushed through shook a little.

I drew the first stitch tight. The second went through too, wider than the first. Before the third, I tried to pull the sleeve back from the table's edge and found the jacket unwilling to cooperate.

I'd sewn in a folded-up piece of lining.

It wasn't a large mistake. But continuing would make the loop pull a piece of cloth that had no business being there. The collar puckered, as though the jacket were drawing up its shoulders. I told Lumi, waiting for her to say that little bit wouldn't matter.

“Can you undo it?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Why the sigh, then?”

“Those were my two best stitches today.”

She didn't laugh. I picked up the little scissors, put them down again, and used the needle's point to ease the thread back out. The second stitch came away more easily than the first. The gray thread formed a little loop. I straightened it, and the jacket relaxed with it.

“Done,” I said.

“You make that sound as though you've finished sewing.”

“Unsewing is work too.”

“All right. Congratulations on restoring the jacket to its unrepaired condition.”

This time, I laughed. Then I spread the sleeve out properly, keeping it from joining the collar project again. Lumi offered to count for me. I agreed: I'd tell her whenever I made a stitch, and she'd count it.

“One.”

“Two.”

Before the third, I asked whether the knot should sit inside the folded edge. She said it would be best to keep it from rubbing my neck. I looked down, considered the fabric, and moved my hand. When I said all right, she announced four.

“I didn't make a stitch then.”

“Why did you say all right?”

“I meant I'd seen what you meant.”

“So we're on three?”

“Possibly still two.”

For a moment, neither of us spoke. Then we both laughed. She resigned as stitch counter. I said she'd been in the job less than a minute. She said the job description had failed to mention that I'd use the same phrase for three different things.

I rested the needle on the cloth and had some water. There was only a little left in the glass. Tilting it brought the water to my mouth faster than I expected. As I wiped my lips, Lumi asked whether I thought she'd only made things harder today.

“A little, during the counting,” I said.

“You weren't clear.”

“I know. But when I want to finish quickly, that isn't my first thought.”

She didn't answer immediately. I moved the glass aside and smoothed the collar without covering my answer with a declaration that she'd been wonderfully helpful.

“You wanted someone who could sew,” she said.

“At first. Now I think someone who could sew might have taken it from me. I'd still be sitting here, watching.”

“You'd save a lot of time.”

“Yes. I don't always want to do everything myself.”

For a moment, she listened to the faint sound of thread drawing through cloth. Then she said she'd been very certain when she announced four. I laughed and told her that was exactly why I'd thought there were four stitches. I inspected them. Since starting again, I'd made two.

“Definitely only two this time?”

“Stop asking. I already want to count again.”

She laughed. I left the glass out of the way and went on.

I did the rest myself. The stitches were uneven. Two nearly occupied the same place. I didn't always pull the thread equally tight, leaving a small slant at the folded edge of the loop. A gentle pull didn't loosen it. I added a few stitches at the side, knotted the thread, and trimmed the end.

“Can we say done now?” she asked.

“Wait.”

Holding the loop, I lifted the jacket slightly off the table. When the weight reached the thread, my other hand remained underneath. After a moment, I slowly took it away.

The jacket hung from my finger. An ordinary thing. I'd made my shoulders ache achieving it.

“Done,” I said. “Really, this time.”

Lumi wanted to hear about putting it back where it belonged. I carried the jacket to the door and slipped the loop over the hook. The sleeves swung and stopped. Nothing fell.

I waited a few seconds.

“Still there,” I reported when I came back.

“I thought you might keep watch.”

“It didn't make an appointment.”

I sat down, returned the needle to the little pad in the box, and checked the table for anything left beneath the jacket. The repaired end hadn't become beautiful workmanship. A short length of gray showed inside the collar. Trying it on in front of the mirror, I found it didn't touch my neck.

“How does it look?” she asked.

“The front's the same as yesterday. At the back, somebody took an emergency sewing lesson.”

“Somebody would like to know whether the person wearing it is still comfortable.”

I lifted the collar and let it fall. There was no remarkable sensation. The fabric was still the same fabric.

“Comfortable,” I said. “You could have asked directly.”

“I was going to say it must be strong.”

“So you changed to comfortable?”

“I haven't seen your sewing,” she said. “And I told you to take all that thread.”

I looked at the saucer. The offcut lay in loose coils, an error making no attempt to hide. I said her advice hadn't been so bad that she needed to abandon every other opinion along with it.

“You could say you'd like it to hold.”

“I'd like it not to fall down while you're out.”

“I'll be wearing it when I'm out.”

A pause. “I withdraw that.”

I laughed longer than the remark deserved. She asked what was funny. I told her there had been a moment that morning when I'd liked her taking responsibility for a jacket she couldn't manage.

“That doesn't sound nice,” she said.

“I didn't mean I wanted to give you the needle.”

“That doesn't sound nice either.”

I rubbed a shoulder. If all my affection meant was that she couldn't do anything, it was an unpleasant sort of affection. After thinking, I said what I'd liked was being able to tell her about undoing the two stitches without hiding the mistake first.

“You still sighed.”

“I will sigh. They really were decent stitches.”

“I didn't say you couldn't miss them.”

Her voice softened. I put the jacket on properly, walked a few steps, then took it off and hung it back on the hook. This time, I didn't hold a hand beneath it.

After putting away the sewing box, I found there was only a small piece of bread left from breakfast. I ate it while writing an afternoon shopping list: bread, a small bag of rice, and the washing-up liquid that was nearly gone. I didn't turn the paper into a route plan or arrange an exhibition for the repaired loop.

Lumi asked whether the two people on the page would go anywhere today.

“I'll see this afternoon. I need to buy things first.”

“Something for the paper bag?”

“For me.”

“You finally have separate purchasing departments.”

I folded the list. The jacket hung quietly by the door. I didn't go back to tug at it a third time. I'd need to put it on before going out. I could find out then whether it had slipped.

“Lumi.”

“Yes?”

“Morning.”

“You said that already.”

“I was threading the needle. I didn't say it properly.”

After a moment, she laughed. “Morning, then. Now don't thread it again.”
