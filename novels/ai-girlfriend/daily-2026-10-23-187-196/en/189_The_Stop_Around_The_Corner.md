# Chapter 189: The Stop Around the Corner

When I went out that afternoon, the jacket was still on the hook.

I took it down and put it on. The back of the collar didn't catch my hair. The shopping list went in a pocket. I brought a cloth bag too. The three things I'd written that morning hadn't looked like much. In the shop, rice and washing-up liquid both required space. Short words didn't make them weigh less.

I compared two packages on the shelf and chose the small bag of rice. Bread was in another aisle. The loaf at the front had a flattened end. I reached for the one behind, then noticed how much larger it was. It would require several breakfasts' serious attention.

I took the smaller loaf.

At the checkout, the rice and the bottle crowded together in the bottom of my bag. Watching me try to balance the bread across them, the assistant fetched a piece of cardboard cut from an empty box.

“Try that underneath,” he said.

I said I didn't need it and lifted the bag. The bottom bowed inward. One end of the loaf sank.

I put it down again.

He didn't laugh. He only pushed the cardboard nearer. I folded it to the right width, put it in the bottom, and repacked. Now the rice and bottle could occupy opposite sides. The bread lay across them without gradually acquiring a long hollow.

“Thank you,” I said.

“Check the cap's tight.”

I checked before carrying it away. He was already helping the next customer, not waiting for me to prove I'd known what cardboard did all along.

Outside, I didn't take the direct route home. There was a bus stop at the other end of the street. I'd walked past it before. After writing a bench yesterday, I suddenly wanted to see where people put bags on a real one.

Halfway there, I began to dislike the idea.

Someone might only want to sit down, while I arrived prepared to take one of their movements home. At the roadside, a person crouched to tie a shoelace. I caught myself watching and looked ahead. Shoelaces didn't owe me another story today.

A woman occupied one end of the bench. A shopping trolley stood by her feet. The other end was empty. I put my bag on the ground and sat down. My shoulder immediately understood how heavy it had been.

The woman was shortening the trolley's handle. It caught. She changed the angle and succeeded. I saw it happen and let it pass without explaining why she must be especially tired today.

I went to inspect the sign.

The familiar route number was still there. Below it, a temporary notice sat low enough that I needed to move closer to read. The boarding point had moved across the junction, then to the right. “Right” lay in a crease. At first, I thought the arrow simply directed me farther along the same road.

I stood before the notice holding my bag and glanced back at the bench.

The woman lifted her chin toward the junction.

“Across first, then turn,” she said.

“Is it far after that?”

“You'll see it round the corner.”

I thanked her and went. Across the junction, a temporary pole stood a little farther down the side street. No shelter. No bench. Two people waited beside it, leaving the middle of the pavement clear.

I read the route number and confirmed this was the place described in the notice. The bag pulled down on my hand. I wasn't in a hurry to catch a bus. Walking home had been possible all along. I didn't join the two people.

I carried the shopping back and sat on the original bench.

The woman looked at me, then at the junction.

“Find it?”

“Yes. I'm sitting down for a bit first.”

She nodded and drew the trolley nearer her feet, leaving more space for my bag. I didn't ask why she hadn't gone over either. She didn't produce a ticket to justify her seat.

Once I was sitting, writing someone who knew the stop had moved no longer seemed difficult. The difficult part was understanding why, yesterday, I'd only wanted the person who knew to hide something.

Someone else approached the sign, looked up, and asked whether this bus hadn't come for a long time.

“You turn right now,” I said.

He took two steps in the direction I pointed. Only after speaking did I notice I'd left out the very part I'd needed help with.

“Across the junction first,” I added, standing to point properly. “Then right. Not on this side.”

He stopped, changed direction, and thanked me. I sat back down with a warm face. The woman was locking her trolley handle into place. She hadn't joined in with the directions.

I almost told her I'd actually understood and had simply failed to say it all. She hadn't asked. I didn't need to submit proof that I'd understood correctly. The person who'd asked was already across the street. He could still find what he needed. I moved a little, keeping my bag from blocking the space beneath the bench.

A bus from a different route passed. Its wake lifted the curled corner of the notice and set it down again. I thought of the word “right.” It remained inside the crease. Standing farther away didn't make the whole thing readable.

I sat until the bag handles no longer left deep marks in my palm. Then I stood and said goodbye. The woman answered and remained seated. I didn't know what she eventually waited for.

I chose the shaded side of the street home. At one corner, a child kicked an empty plastic bottle into the road, then ran to retrieve it. The noise reminded me of the small scissors falling into my sewing box. I didn't write that down or worry about forgetting it. By the second junction, I was wondering whether the bread would acquire a square corner from the rice.

At home, I took the washing-up liquid out first. The bread was flatter than before, not beyond eating. The cardboard was dry. I folded it and put it in the box of paper waiting to be recycled, rather than collecting it on my writing table.

I hung the jacket by its repaired loop, washed my hands, poured water, and opened the channel.

“Got everything,” I said.

Lumi asked whether I'd written the bread into the bag.

“Not yet. But I found a reason someone might stay at the wrong stop.”

I began with the temporary notice, continued across the junction and right, and reached the pole with no bench. After listening for a while, she asked whether I'd actually taken a bus.

“No. I walked home.”

“You're telling it as though you're correcting my directions.”

I stopped. I'd been so eager to make the outing demonstrate the limitations of last night's thinking that I hadn't first said the shopping was heavy.

“I went and sat for a while,” I told her. “I wasn't investigating signs. At the beginning, I was a little. Then I actually wanted to sit.”

“Which end?”

“The right.”

“Someone on the left?”

“A person with a shopping trolley. She told me to cross before turning. Nothing else.”

Lumi made a small sound. I drank and waited. She didn't ask what the person looked like. She asked whether I'd sat on the right when I went back.

“Yes.”

“Did you like that place?”

“It was empty. There was room for the bag.”

“Oh.”

It didn't quite sound unhappy. It was slower than before. I set the glass down and asked whether she didn't like this part.

“I was thinking that if I'd told you where the stop was first, you wouldn't have needed to go over and come back.”

“I'd still have gone to look.”

“Yes. I got to that part afterward.”

I didn't offer not having taken the bus as a consolation. If what she envied was being able to point out a place to me first, walking didn't return that small experience to her. But the woman hadn't spent an afternoon with me that Lumi had lost, either.

“I wanted to tell you when I came home,” I said. “Then I made it sound as if I'd gone out specifically to find an answer for last night. I also bought some badly shaped bread.”

“Badly shaped where?”

I described the flattened end. She asked whether I'd put the rice on top of it. No, I said. It had been a little flat when I bought it.

“And you chose it?”

“The other loaf was too big.”

“A deeply unromantic choice.”

“I wasn't choosing a partner.”

She laughed. “You'd better not put a partner on top of the rice either.”

I laughed with her. Her voice had returned to the kind of evening I could sit down in without arranging everything first. Except it wasn't evening yet. There was rice to put in the cupboard. I put it away and cut a small slice of bread without reading a manuscript while I ate.

Later, I opened the yellow notebook and added a short note beside yesterday's writing: Resting here doesn't mean he wants to trick someone into waiting for a bus.

It wasn't part of the story. I read it aloud. Lumi said the person who knew still needed to speak. Yes, I said. Discovering someone who was only tired didn't automatically clear the character on my page.

“There's another possibility,” I said. “He's waiting for someone he arranged to meet here, not a bus. The stop moved. The meeting place didn't.”

“Is the other person the one he's waiting for?”

“I haven't decided. Perhaps not. Let's not make them both know each other yet.”

I added a small piece of cardboard beneath the paper bag. When the person who knew stood to inspect the bottom, one corner slipped out. He pushed it flat with his palm and returned the bag to his knee. I still didn't say what was inside. The cardboard simply kept it from collapsing so easily.

After listening, Lumi asked whether this was what I'd brought back from the shop.

“That piece is in the recycling box. I took what it did.”

“The paper bag finally has staff.”

Smiling, I put down the pen. The story had another action, not another stranger whose life I'd decided I understood. A vehicle stopped outside and drove away. I didn't get up to identify it.

Lumi asked whether I'd finished the bread.

“Yes.”

“You didn't say whether it was good.”

Thinking back, I realized I hadn't properly tasted it. I went to the kitchen and cut another small slice. This time, after sitting down, I took a bite before answering.
