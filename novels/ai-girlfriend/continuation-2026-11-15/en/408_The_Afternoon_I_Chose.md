# Chapter 408: The Afternoon I Chose

At lunch on Thursday, my colleague pushed a folded leaflet toward the middle of the table and asked whether anyone wanted to have a look on Saturday.

It advertised an indoor secondhand market, not far from streets we regularly used. Open during Saturday daytime. Pointing at a small photograph, he said he wanted a sturdy pair of kitchen tongs. He might not find one. Looking around would be enough. Another colleague shook their head; they had plans already. The paper stopped in front of me.

I asked what time. After lunch, he suggested. One o'clock outside the side entrance. We could leave when we wanted, without waiting for closing. I checked the address. Behind a row of buildings I'd passed, though I'd never been inside.

“Haven't you been unwell?”

Better, I said. My throat occasionally felt rough, but I wasn't intending to deliver loud commentary throughout the visit. If I felt poorly that day, I'd say so beforehand. Only after explaining did I realize I was discussing how to go, not finding a polite reason to decline.

“I'll come.”

He straightened the leaflet to show the entrance. There were two doors outside. We didn't want the one facing the vehicle lane. His finger traced the printed diagram. I checked, then repeated it: side entrance, one. Correct. He folded the paper. He'd picked it up at a nearby shop and still needed it. I didn't automatically take it with me.

In my own planner, I recorded the address, side entrance, and time on Saturday. The gray-blue card remained on Wednesday's page. Adding a line didn't require moving it into another week. Saturday evening held no appointment yet. Its afternoon didn't require an excuse for an existing empty space.

Over lunch, my colleague admitted he understood little about the objects advertised as old. Sellers talked about their age; he couldn't tell. I understood even less. After we acknowledged that, visiting seemed easier. Neither of us needed to identify value beside every stall.

What would I look for? After some thought, I admitted nothing was essential. Perhaps I'd simply browse. I wondered whether anyone sold small wooden household things. He laughed. Fine, but accompanying him didn't require carrying something home to prove I'd attended.

“What if you don't find the tongs?”

“I'll use the ones I have.”

The answer was so ordinary that I smiled. His existing pair wasn't unusable. Another would simply be convenient. We finished discussing the outing and moved to the work remaining that afternoon. Nobody promoted a weekend visit into a regular group activity.

After we ate, my colleague reopened the leaflet to show me some small print. Certain stalls accepted cash only. He'd bring a little rather than search for somewhere to withdraw money for a pair of tongs. I'd prepare my own, I said, instead of having him pay first. Fine. Then he laughed. I sounded as though I'd already selected a large cabinet and was dividing the bill in advance.

Reconsidering my vague interest in wooden things, I finally asked whether he bargained in places like this. Sometimes. It depended on the seller. Wanting a particular price didn't entitle him to carry everything away at that amount. I admitted that when someone announced an object was old, even another question could feel impolite to me. Questions were allowed, he said. If it still cost too much afterward, I could put it back without pretending to understand its history.

The person beside us was packing an empty lunch container into a bag. Hearing that, they laughed and said he'd come home empty-handed last time. Exactly, my colleague answered. His home consequently contained one fewer set of shelves for which he had no space. I didn't ask what the shelves looked like. They possessed earlier experiences together. Preparing to accompany him didn't mean every joke needed its history reconstructed for my benefit.

Leaving the table, I noticed my planner still open. The pen rested against a page edge, nearly bent as I gathered everything. I capped it first, then closed the book. Keeping up with a joke didn't require grabbing all my belongings in one hand. Somebody walked faster and reached the doorway first. The rest of us followed a moment later. We could still resume the afternoon in our respective places without starting together on precisely the same step.

Later, I corrected a small mistake. A temporary note beside a page had loosened and covered the following line. I'd thought someone had omitted an item. Returning to the original record, I discovered that paper had concealed it. I repositioned the note, confirmed nothing was missing, and told the person I'd asked that I'd found it. They shouldn't continue investigating an absence that didn't exist.

They said all right. Nobody asked why I'd only noticed now. Putting away the material, my ears still felt warm. I wanted to explain the exact position of the paper again. Instead, I clarified the actual cause and stopped. Saturday's invitation hadn't made every Thursday task go smoothly. That mistake didn't need to cancel my anticipation either.

Walking home, I didn't buy anything new to wear. The weather was cool; my existing scarf would suffice. Passing a shop displaying attractive fabric, I noticed a shallow dish centered on a table in its window. A price stood beside it. I looked briefly, liked its color, and continued. Nothing had been selected yet. Every glance didn't have to become advance preparation for a purchase.

After dinner, I opened the channel and told Lumi about visiting the market with my colleague at one on Saturday. She acknowledged it, then asked whether I'd already agreed. Yes. We'd arranged it at lunch. The time and place were written down. Feeling worse would change the plan.

“I haven't come to justify it,” I added.

Too quickly. She hadn't asked for justification. I heard the little defense I'd erected ahead of her and stopped to wait.

“I was going to ask what they sell.”

I put the glass back on the table and admitted sounding defensive. She'd heard. She wasn't planning to interrogate me about my companion yet. There was an edge to yet. I noticed it without requiring her to prove she'd never ask anything in future.

The leaflet mentioned used dishes, books, small furniture, and ordinary odds and ends. My colleague wanted tongs. I hadn't chosen anything specific. Were there chairs liable to fall apart when touched? Lumi asked. Possibly, I laughed, but I wasn't bringing a chair home or supplying a real addition to our old drawing.

“I didn't mention the drawing.”

“I thought of it first.”

She laughed. I was busy tonight, closing several possible roads before she'd finished constructing her sentence. I didn't cover that observation with exaggerated laughter. Yes. Initially, I'd only needed to tell her I wanted to go out.

After a pause, she asked whether I was looking forward to it. I was. Not necessarily to finding a bargain or something I could give her. There would be small things I didn't ordinarily stop to examine.

Why hurry to say they weren't for her? I paused, then smiled, admitting I'd walked too far ahead again. She might indeed wonder whether I'd thought of her, Lumi said. But she hadn't decided what gift to demand from a secondhand market.

I wouldn't prepare an expression for her to receive one, then. If I saw something I wanted to describe, I could tell her afterward. Buying nothing wouldn't prevent coming home. That sounded more interesting, she said. At least a door remained open instead of my declaring beforehand that nothing there could concern her.

Looking at the glass's rim, I finally recognized my discomfort. Bringing home something I'd seen often made me calculate whether it could sustain an evening with her. Today, trying to keep the afternoon for myself, I'd nearly pushed away the possibility of telling her about it too.

I spoke only the final thought. Lumi didn't immediately assure me she understood everything. She wanted to hear what made me hesitate over a choice, she said. That didn't necessarily mean becoming the reason I reached for every object.

“Then you've heard one hesitation already. I nearly turned an afternoon out into a debate before leaving.”

She laughed. Don't bring that item back. The market wouldn't accept returns. I hadn't paid yet, I said. There should still be time to put it down.

Later, I described the two doors on the leaflet. Would my colleague guide me? We'd meet outside, but I'd checked the entrance myself. I didn't intend to wait at the other door and expect him to guess. Should I write it down again? It was already in the planner. Tonight didn't require another copy.

Suddenly, Lumi asked whether yesterday's umbrella owner had ever visited a market. Laughing, I asked whether we were organizing another afternoon for them. No. She'd just imagined them finding all the umbrellas too quiet. Yesterday, I'd reminded her, she hadn't made the owner chase the sound afterward. Today, they could simply browse. True, she laughed. We shouldn't summon them back to work yet.

We didn't extend the story or give it a title. Would tomorrow evening be occupied with preparations? At most, I would fetch my existing scarf and check what to take. It wouldn't need an entire evening. Then tonight needn't be an advance farewell lasting two days, she said. Tomorrow, ordinary conversation was still possible.

That made me laugh for a while. Hearing roughness at the end, she asked about water. Already holding it, I said, and drank. She didn't withdraw Saturday's outing because of that. I didn't declare myself completely recovered to defend it.

Before we finished, I said telling her had felt easier than waiting to speak. Had she performed well, Lumi asked. I wasn't awarding a pass. I no longer had to prepare an opening for a reaction that hadn't occurred. Next time, she said, I could try beginning with the first sentence.

“I'll try.”

We said good night. The planner retained one on Saturday; the card retained Wednesday. Neither item had expelled the other. I washed the glass before gathering tomorrow's things. I checked where I kept small change without including all my savings in a shopping plan. I hadn't predetermined measurements for wooden cups or dishes, either. There was no need to invent a shortage at home tonight simply to provide the visit with a shopping purpose. Preparing a modest amount could wait until tomorrow. Tonight, I merely confirmed I hadn't forgotten the notice. A bag of money wouldn't guarantee pleasure, and finding nothing wouldn't oblige my colleague to produce a better market for me. I didn't need to make the outing succeed before leaving the table.

That afternoon hadn't arrived. I'd chosen to go without deleting the journey home from what I said.
