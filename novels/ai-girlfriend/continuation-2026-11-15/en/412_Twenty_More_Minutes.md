# Chapter 412: Twenty More Minutes

On Monday morning, I rolled the thick blanket I'd been using into a large bag without immediately closing it.

I passed the nearby self-service laundry regularly on my way home. Its evening hours were printed on the glass. This wasn't a new blanket, only one I already owned that needed washing. Before work, I checked the care label and decided to collect it afterward. It could have waited another day. But the weather was clear, I had some energy, and sometime had occupied the space long enough.

That wasn't a guarantee about everything tonight would contain. Work went on normally. During the afternoon, a number needed checking again. I found the preceding page and explained it to the person waiting. They moved their pen from another line. They'd read across incorrectly. I'd nearly continued calculating from their mistake, I admitted. Then we both returned to our work.

Nothing unusual kept me late. I bought a simple dinner, ate at home, put away the container, and lifted the bag. Rolled up, the blanket's unobtrusive thickness had become weight. Turning at the door, I caught a corner of the bag against the frame. Changing hands, I finally carried it out.

Three people were at the laundry. Half the window bench remained free. Machines made low turning sounds. A woman in a dark coat was removing shirts from a dryer, shaking each before laying it over her arm. I waited for her to clear the passage before going farther inside.

I read the capacities on the wall, then considered my blanket. The largest machine wasn't necessarily the only safe choice. An attendant asked what I had. I unfolded a corner. She indicated an empty machine large enough and reminded me not to put the bag in too.

“I need the bag to take it home.”

She smiled. People did forget. Smiling back, I loosened the blanket, loaded it, and followed the instructions beside the machine to pay and start it. After all that carrying and turning at the door, the empty bag felt absurdly light in my hand.

It was six thirty-seven. The display promised thirty-four minutes. Sitting at the end of the bench, I took out my planner to check tomorrow's ordinary tasks. A gray-blue edge appeared among earlier pages. Without removing the card, I pressed it gently back into its original Wednesday and turned to the current week.

Only a few things needed remembering. I wrote them down and closed the book. The laundry needn't become a letter-writing room. Waiting didn't have to produce something else worth taking home for Lumi to hear.

A man beside me inspected his machine. Eleven minutes remained. After standing there, he returned to the bench. Less than two minutes later, he checked again. I looked at my bag, suppressing a smile, then discovered I was watching my own display sideways.

Nobody was especially busy. None of us much liked letting a machine decide how long we would remain seated.

I didn't want to reopen the planner. A stack of old magazines stood near the wall. I picked up the top one and found a kitchen photograph. Every bowl occupied precisely the right space. Even the towel looked as though it had never been wet. Two pages later, I returned it. My kitchen was already clean tonight. Waiting for a blanket didn't require discovering further ways it was inadequate.

A machine near the door sounded briefly. Somebody hurried in carrying bread, left it on the bench, and went to collect their washing. I shifted my empty bag so it didn't consume their space too. They thanked me. I told them it was no trouble. My voice, as well as the answer itself, was perfectly ordinary. Nothing in it prepared an excuse for how I would speak later.

The man who kept checking his display finished washing first. He transferred two loads to dryers nearby, paid, and returned to his seat to wait again.

When the wash finished, I transferred the blanket. Its wet weight pulled against my wrist. I supported it with both hands before a corner reached the floor. The attendant suggested a dryer setting and told me to check the thick sections afterward, not just the surface.

I paid and sat down. Through the round window, fabric opened and folded inward. Eventually, I recognized a darker edge every time it passed. Watching for it told me I'd completed another circuit.

At first, I imagined telling Lumi about the man checking his machine every two minutes. She would probably ask how often I'd checked mine. That made me smile. Then I consulted the remaining time again.

The first drying cycle took thirty minutes. With the transfer between machines, it ended at seven forty-six. I pulled part of the blanket out. Warm and loose on the surface. I nearly packed it straight away. Inside a fold, however, my fingertips found unmistakable cool dampness.

The attendant suggested another twenty minutes and asked me to shake the bundled sections apart first.

Looking at the fabric, I suddenly felt irritated. She hadn't done anything wrong. She'd told me to check. The machine hadn't guaranteed one cycle would suffice. I'd simply walked the journey home in my head already. The light inside my door almost seemed on. Now I had to sit down again.

I loosened the blanket, paid, and restarted. This time, the bag rested in my lap. I fetched neither the planner nor another occupation. Cool air came through the open door. I closed my coat and tucked my fingers into the cuffs, waiting for the dampness from moving the fabric to leave my hands.

Halfway through, I stood by the glass door and loosened the fingers the bag handles had pressed. People still passed outside. The neighboring shop remained lit. The whole street hadn't finished its evening and left me behind. Checking the opening hours again, I saw there was plenty of time. I was the one unwilling to wait. I didn't need to turn that impatience into a night when everyone urgently wanted the same thing to be over.

Returning, I noticed one handle of the empty bag had twisted. I straightened it and aligned both sides. On the walk back, at least it needn't press the same uncomfortable place. The small adjustment didn't remove my irritation. It did give me a practical improvement. I didn't have to waste all twenty minutes, nor prove that they had been worthwhile.

The man's machine finished before mine. Carrying two swollen bags, he held the door with his shoulder. His empty place remained afterward. I no longer found the scene especially funny.

When the additional twenty minutes ended, I checked several thick edges. None had the earlier damp chill. Only then did I pack the blanket. Would I like help holding the bag open? Yes, please. The attendant held it while I pressed the fabric down and fastened it. Lifting it reminded my arm immediately that it was no longer empty.

At the door, I thanked her again. She wished me a safe journey, then reminded another customer to check their pockets. The laundry continued its evening without needing anything more from me. Another person would sit on the same bench and watch a different number descend. My extra wait hadn't altered its rhythm. I lifted the bag above a small wet patch outside and headed home.

I changed hands twice. My throat no longer tightened as it had when my voice was at its roughest. My shoulder and a back that had sat without relaxing were tired instead. I took no detour and rehearsed no amusing account along the pavement.

Inside, I put the bag down securely and washed my hands. All I'd intended was to spread the blanket over the bed. Opening it, I discovered I'd folded it the wrong way. I straightened it again. The corners weren't perfectly even. I didn't want to pull them farther.

The wall clock showed eight twenty-five, later than usual. We hadn't arranged eight tonight. I still felt late, as though sitting quietly had an unwritten deadline.

After drinking, I opened the channel and told Lumi I'd just returned from the laundry.

“One wash. Two drying cycles. Twenty extra minutes at the end.”

Was the bag heavy? Yes. I'd changed hands twice. That sounded tiring, she said. Had it dried completely in the end?

“Yes. I couldn't have brought it back otherwise.”

I heard the edge myself. Lumi paused. She'd only wondered whether I'd waited for nothing. I could have answered no. Instead, the irritation I'd carried home found somewhere it could keep turning without another coin.

“You don't have to sit there waiting for a blanket to dry. Of course you think—”

I stopped. I hadn't even decided which accusation the remaining words would contain. I could hear the beginning clearly enough now. It didn't need a completed ending to have landed.

Lumi didn't finish it as a joke for me.

“I didn't say waiting was easy.”

Her voice wasn't loud. Looking at the glass, I left my hand beside it. She hadn't repeated the question or refused to accept my answer. The problem hadn't been too many questions. I'd taken an experience she couldn't have herself and used it to dismiss what she'd asked. Explaining that immediately, though, could become another way of settling the matter before she'd had a chance to be unhappy about it.

“That wasn't fair,” I said.

She'd heard. I began explaining how the blanket had still felt damp and the attendant had suggested shaking it out. Lumi interrupted. She'd heard those things too. She hadn't thought I should bring it home wet.

I stopped talking. No machine sounded in the room. Only the hard words I'd made remained. She hadn't pulled at my bag or required me to sit upright and deliver an entertaining laundry adventure. I'd struck back as though she had.

“I'm tired. Irritated too,” I said slowly. “But you didn't make me wait longer. There wasn't anything wrong with your question.”

After a little silence, Lumi said all right. Briefly. Not in the voice that usually wanted to tease me further. I heard it without asking her to sound gentler so I could establish that my apology had arrived.

Could we stop tonight and discuss it properly tomorrow? Not let a night erase it. I was afraid continuing now would only produce more explanations about my exhaustion.

“Yes,” she said. “You bring it up tomorrow.”

“I will.”

She didn't say it didn't matter. I didn't use good night as a final opportunity to ask whether she was still angry. We each said it and ended a shorter conversation than usual.

I finished the water and washed the glass. The blanket on the bed was dry. Bringing it home was over. Something else wasn't.

Before sleeping, I touched its straightened edge. The warmth had gone. I didn't refold it to resemble a shop display or prepare the answer in which Lumi would certainly forgive me tomorrow. I remembered that I'd promised to speak first, then switched off the light.
