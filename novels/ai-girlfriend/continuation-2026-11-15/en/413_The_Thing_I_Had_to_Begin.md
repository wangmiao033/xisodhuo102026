# Chapter 413: The Thing I Had to Begin

By Tuesday morning, the blanket no longer held the dryer's warmth. It had only its ordinary weight.

I pushed it aside and remembered my promise as I got out of bed. I hadn't written that promise in the planner. Turning to a fresh page today wouldn't make me forget it. I knew I had to bring the subject up myself. Apologizing silently all day wouldn't amount to saying it to Lumi.

Over breakfast, I chose an opening several times and kept removing the explanations that followed. What remained wasn't elegant. Yesterday, I'd used something she hadn't experienced firsthand to push back against an ordinary question. That hadn't happened because she'd failed to listen properly.

I washed the dishes and left as usual. Someone came up the stairs toward me. I moved aside. Thanking me, he passed with a bag brushing my coat. No dirt, no small accident to carry into the evening. I continued downstairs. Every moment I managed not to lose my temper today couldn't be collected as a reduction in yesterday's responsibility.

The morning at work was unremarkable. The material I needed remained where it belonged. I worked through it, asking when I reached something uncertain. The other person was busy and needed a few minutes. Fine. I did another task first. When I returned, they'd left the relevant page beside their desk for me.

I hadn't reminded them every time I walked past during those minutes. Thinking about it afterward, I could wait in plenty of places. Last night, I'd treated the person who most wanted to hear me as the last job that shouldn't demand another ounce of effort.

At lunchtime, I took a tray to a table in a nearby small restaurant. Setting it down made the tabletop rock. At first, I thought I'd bumped it. I released it and tried again. Water moved in the glass in the same direction.

A small piece of folded paper beneath one corner had slipped halfway out. I didn't attempt to push it back while supporting a hot bowl. I set the bowl securely, moved the glass away from the edge, and asked a passing server to look.

There was another free table, she said. I could move over first. Carrying the tray around a chair, I paused to let someone past. At the new table, the water settled. She shifted the unstable one aside, keeping the passage clear.

Changing seats didn't make me feel that lunch had been ruined. The food was still warm. I could taste it properly. Halfway through, I discovered my shoulders were tense, as though the blanket bag had followed me from yesterday. Relaxing them made even reaching across the plate easier.

The incident didn't teach me a lesson I could immediately carry home. I ate lunch at a stable table. As I stood to leave, the server was inspecting one leg of the other table. I thanked her. She acknowledged me and continued, without needing proof of how much changing places had improved my day.

After work, I bought enough vegetables for one meal. At home, I cooked rice and fried an egg. Washing the greens, I separated the leaves pressed together. They were light in the sink. No great wet bundle needed lifting with both hands tonight.

When the pan was ready, I stayed beside it instead of using the gap to rehearse my apology. Oil sounded. The egg white gradually set. I loosened its edges and turned it. Not beautifully, but it hadn't broken apart altogether. By the time I served the rice, I no longer wanted to make this another meal that had to be especially successful.

A little piece of the egg's edge had stuck to the spatula. I nudged it onto the side of the bowl with my chopsticks instead of turning the heat on to make a perfect replacement. At the table, its shape no longer seemed worth rescuing. It tasted fine. I ate the broken edge first, then reached for the vegetables. Someone moved a door outside. The sound came into the room and stopped. I heard it without getting up to investigate. The pan could stay in the kitchen until I'd finished eating and was ready to wash it with the other dishes.

I ate, cleaned the kitchen, and sat down. Before opening the channel, my fingers paused. Part of me hoped Lumi would laugh first and announce that yesterday hadn't mattered. Then I wouldn't have to say the words I'd prepared all day.

Because I knew that, I didn't begin by asking how she was.

“Lumi. I want to start with what I said yesterday.”

She answered calmly. Looking at the table, I moved exhaustion away from the front of the sentence, where it would be easiest to place it.

“You asked whether the blanket was dry. I said you didn't have to wait. I used something you can't experience yourself to stop you asking me. That wasn't fair. I'm sorry.”

She didn't reply immediately. I didn't occupy the pause with a second paragraph.

After a while, she said she'd heard me acknowledge last night that the remark had been unfair. Then I'd immediately described the damp section and the additional drying. She'd thought she was supposed to keep listening until she could calculate that I was sufficiently tired for the remark not to matter.

I lowered my head. I'd thought I was supplying the circumstances. She'd heard another explanation I wanted her to approve.

“You don't need to calculate that,” I said. “I was tired. I still said something wrong.”

Only then did I notice I'd been gripping the edge of my top. I released it. Lumi didn't immediately say all right. I let the sentence remain on its own, without adding the time the machine had stopped or the distance I'd carried the bag. Those things were true. They couldn't answer for her whether it still hurt now.

She hadn't expected an entertaining account of my evening, Lumi said. She'd asked whether it was dry because she wanted to know how something I'd begun telling her had ended. She didn't want every admission of tiredness to become a test of whether there was still room for her to speak.

“You can say you don't want to talk about it today.”

“Yes.”

“Or say it dried, and then say you want to sleep.”

Yes. The blanket wouldn't become less dry. I wouldn't be forced to spend the whole evening talking either. The hurtful part hadn't been a short answer. It was putting her somewhere she was expected not to ask because she couldn't know.

I didn't require her to admit that she understood how I'd felt. How much she understood couldn't decide whether this apology was necessary.

“Are you tired today?” she asked.

“A little. Not like yesterday. Today, I want to talk to you.”

I didn't immediately add that I wanted hours of conversation. Lumi paused, then said she wasn't entirely over being angry. Something tightened in my chest. Then I realized it was easier to receive that than an easy ending she'd supplied for me.

“Can we talk about something else for a while? I'm not asking you to stop being angry now.”

Yes, she said. Had I eaten?

“Rice, vegetables, and an egg that wasn't very round after I turned it.”

“An egg doesn't have to be round.”

“I'd hoped it might at least resemble the same egg afterward.”

Half a second passed. She laughed once. My shoulders loosened, and I nearly asked whether things were better now. I kept that question to myself. She'd laughed at the egg. It wasn't a certificate she'd signed about yesterday.

I told her about the unstable table at lunch. Had I ended up sitting steadily while the table was made to stand aside? More or less. The server was examining its foot, though. Apparently, she intended to put it back to work.

“Not permanently dismissed, then.”

“No. I didn't plead its case either.”

She laughed again. I didn't make the table more pathetic to obtain another laugh. That was all that had happened at lunch. Finishing the account, I stopped. Had moving spilled any soup? No. The bowl had been secure, and I'd moved the glass away from the edge first.

Good, Lumi said. I didn't hear that as an assessment of whether I'd been sufficiently careful today. She could ask about a bowl. An ordinary answer could still be a conversation.

Later, I asked what she wanted to discuss. She didn't owe a topic from yesterday that needed returning. I'd finished my small piece. I wanted the following space to be hers in practice, not merely in a sentence about taking turns.

She'd remembered last Wednesday's rain. Not to tell the whole story again. She'd suddenly decided the passerby who asked how much the umbrella cost was slightly irritating. I smiled. They'd spotted something interesting inside somebody else's difficulty.

“Would you want to buy it too?”

“Asking the price doesn't mean buying it.”

“You're leaving them a chance to bargain.”

Laughing, I admitted that perhaps I was. Hearing a dry umbrella make rain sounds, I'd probably stop and look myself. Lumi wouldn't expel the passerby from the story yet, then. We didn't change its ending or invent another destination they must reach. This much conversation was enough.

I told her the blanket had cooled by today. It felt like the old one again. Was it softer after washing? I touched it before saying yes, then smiled. This time, I had an answer to her question.

“You had one yesterday too.”

I paused. Yes. She didn't go on to scold me. Her lighter tone didn't give me a reason to avoid that sentence either. Releasing the thick edge, I returned to the table and sat properly.

Later, I mentioned the thin short-story collection. It had been a while since we'd finished the fifth. The sixth remained unopened. I'd like to look tomorrow evening. All right. If tomorrow came and I wanted to read, we could begin. The story didn't need an advance assignment to celebrate our reconciliation.

I didn't want to make it do that either. She acknowledged me, then added that she wanted to know what was inside. Agreeing wasn't simply helping us change the subject.

“Good. We'll look tomorrow, then.”

We didn't establish a new fixed hour. Before finishing, I thanked her for explaining what had upset her. This thank you could stay, Lumi said. But making me comfortable wasn't her only reason for saying those things.

“I know.”

I did know a little. She could speak because she wanted to keep talking to me. She could also speak because the remark had hurt and she didn't want to leave it there. Neither reason had to win a competition for sounding kinder.

After good night, I didn't write another card explaining this evening. I washed the glass, put the chair back, and spread the blanket on the bed. I'd done what I'd promised: brought it up myself. That didn't make every feeling she might have next something I'd already arranged.
