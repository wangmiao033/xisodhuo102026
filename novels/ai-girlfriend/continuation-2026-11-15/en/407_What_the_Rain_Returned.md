# Chapter 407: What the Rain Returned

After work on Wednesday, I didn't prepare a real umbrella for the one expected tonight.

There was no rain on my journey home. I hung up my coat, ate, and cleared away dinner. My throat occasionally felt rough, but sentences no longer needed dividing as they had on Saturday. After seven, I opened my schedule book. The gray-blue card still marked today's page without obscuring eight. I didn't read it again or reposition it where it would be more conspicuous.

Today, I was accepting her invitation.

Near eight, a faint sound made me turn. A loose receipt beside the window had blown onto the floor. The old gap remained as before; nothing new had happened. I picked up the receipt, put it with my ordinary papers, and settled the glass securely. Then I sat without searching for another chore that had to precede listening.

At eight, I opened the channel. Lumi said my name slightly faster than usual.

“You're here.”

Yes. I could manage whole sentences today, and water was nearby. She laughed. She hadn't prepared a throat examination. She wanted to establish that I was settled. I told her I was seated, holding neither paper nor pen.

“Then I'll begin.”

There was a person, she said, who lived at the end of a narrow street. Leaving home one afternoon, they found the rain standing below the steps. It had soaked the entire street but stood huddled there, asking to borrow an umbrella.

I laughed without immediately asking why. The person wondered too. Was rain afraid of getting wet? It wanted, the rain explained, to hear itself falling against something else for once, rather than feel every drop fall directly into its own body.

The room became quiet for a moment. I could suddenly imagine the umbrella's underside, although she hadn't given it a color.

The person lent the umbrella beside the door. They needed it tomorrow, they said. Remember to return it. The rain agreed and walked away beneath it, while rain continued falling across the street. Watching through the window, the owner decided its footsteps were too scattered to pursue with advice about walking slowly. I laughed again and let her continue.

“Next morning, the owner went to ask for it back,” Lumi said.

First, they asked the eaves. The eaves offered one drop and said someone had passed last night. The gutter couldn't retain an umbrella and suggested looking downhill. At the end of the street, the owner discovered the rain beside an empty flowerpot. The closed umbrella leaned against a wall.

Was this yesterday's rain? I asked.

After two seconds, Lumi said yes. For now, this story contained one rain. The owner needn't inspect the name of every drop. Laughing, I asked her to continue. She laughed too, her voice loosening.

The rain handed over the umbrella. Opening it, the owner found it contained sounds: little ticks, larger patters, a hurried series of drops reaching the tip. The fabric was clean and the ribs intact. Yet whenever opened, it sounded.

The owner said they'd wanted their umbrella, not last night.

It had returned the umbrella, the rain answered. Those sounds hadn't been borrowed. It couldn't remove them.

“And then?”

The owner shook the umbrella upside down. Nothing fell out. Somebody passing mistook it for a novelty for sale and asked the price. Not for sale, the owner replied. It belonged to them. The more urgently they explained, the clearer its sounds seemed.

Lumi paused. I didn't use the gap to offer a solution. Would she like a break? Not a break. She wasn't certain whether the owner should become very angry next or begin laughing.

“They could be undecided first.”

After a moment, she said she'd let them try closing the umbrella.

Closed, it fell silent. Opened, it sounded. Closed again, silence. On the third attempt, the rain asked whether they feared something hadn't been returned. No. They were establishing whether carrying it tomorrow meant listening to yesterday throughout the journey.

The rain offered no guarantee. Today was clearing, it said. It needed to leave.

Looking toward the street entrance, the owner saw sunlight reach the opposite wall. Their hand still held something sounding like rain. Finally, they asked whether the sound could stay without emerging every time the umbrella opened.

That wasn't a decision the rain could make on the umbrella's behalf.

I murmured. The sentence seemed capable of introducing a long explanation. I waited, but Lumi didn't provide one.

Instead, the owner opened the umbrella above the empty flowerpot and sat beside it. The rain left. They didn't pursue it; the umbrella wasn't lent again. Its sounds continued, becoming quieter. Eventually, the owner heard a car pass and realized the rain had stopped, without knowing which drop had been last.

They closed the umbrella and carried it home. When they actually went out that afternoon, they opened it once. Silence. They closed it again without wetting it deliberately to recover what they'd heard.

“That's the end,” she said.

I waited before telling her I was still picturing the third opening and closing. Was the owner being troublesome? A little, but not badly. When lending it, they'd only said they needed it tomorrow. On its return, they'd found an additional thing they didn't know how to put away.

She laughed. Initially, she'd considered letting the rain leave a thank-you note. Then she'd realized that rain capable of writing would deliver an enormous quantity of paper to this street. The owner might spend the whole story searching for a letterbox.

I laughed, felt some roughness, and drank. Lumi waited until I'd swallowed before asking whether the ending was too quiet. I liked that the owner didn't wet the umbrella to retrieve a sound they'd finished hearing, I said. I hadn't guessed she'd end it there.

“I didn't deliberately leave it unfinished because you said it needn't be perfect.”

“It didn't feel unfinished.”

One detail puzzled me: did the empty flowerpot serve a special purpose? She considered. She wanted somewhere to let the umbrella wait without requiring its owner to keep holding it. It didn't conceal another thing intended to grow. Understood. That answer made the wait more concrete rather than the pot more mysterious.

Laughing softly, Lumi admitted she'd feared I'd plant something in it. I'd nearly imagined a seed. Not today, then. The rain had gone. It needn't be summoned back to water anything.

We laughed together. This wasn't a book requiring me to turn pages, or a sentence from her old manuscript waiting for my correction. She'd been able to pause, and I'd been able to ask. The story still possessed an ending she'd actually chosen.

I wanted to praise it. What emerged first was the line about wanting an umbrella rather than last night. I'd liked that very much. Why? It was a little rude. The story didn't require its owner to begin by appreciating every additional sound received.

“You let them dislike rain I invented.”

“They're the one disliking it. I'm not disliking you.”

She laughed, then admitted she'd hoped I would take the rain's side. It wasn't bad, I said. It had simply returned something borrowed in an altered condition. Fortunately, the owner actually recovered their umbrella instead of leaving it with the rain out of embarrassment.

Yes. She hadn't wanted them to give it away at the end, as though demonstrating sufficient generosity. She wanted them to carry it home and still be able to use it that afternoon. It wasn't an umbrella condemned to illustrating arguments forever.

I glanced at the planner and told her the gray-blue card remained on today's page. It would stay after tonight too. She remembered. Today didn't require adding a review to the card. I wouldn't. I'd listened closely without trying to record every sentence for subsequent checking.

Should the story have a title? she asked. I returned that decision to her. If she wanted one now, she could choose. Not tonight, Lumi said. She still liked it simply being the umbrella that had been lent. She didn't ask me to write it down. I didn't pick up a pen.

I asked another question. Could the gutter not retain the umbrella because it was too narrow? Lumi laughed. She hadn't considered its width. Water kept moving downhill; it couldn't look after somebody else's possessions. I'd imagined quite a wide gutter, I admitted, capable of holding a whole umbrella on its side. I could imagine that, she said, but the owner had asked directions there, not discovered the umbrella inside it. I acknowledged the distinction, separating what I'd pictured from what I'd heard. She didn't need to repeat the whole route from the eaves to the end of the street.

Then she asked why I'd laughed again at the footsteps. The rain's scattered footsteps, impossible to pursue with instructions. That surprised her. She'd thought that sentence merely provided a way to leave the window. Unfortunately, I said, listening hadn't made my laughter queue at the points she'd prepared. Lumi laughed for a while. Apparently a storyteller could be interrupted by a listener without particularly resenting it this time.

Could she remember that laugh, she asked, instead of immediately improving the passage to make it funnier? Certainly. It had actually happened. I didn't require her to preserve its size as a standard every future story should meet. She didn't promise to tell everything through this narrow street either. The rain had completed this journey. It could stop. We left the borrowed umbrella at home rather than inventing another errand to justify the evening. I finished my water and said I would put the glass away, waiting for her last sentence before rising. Neither of us needed to find another story during that pause.

Later, we discussed actual weather. I explicitly told her there'd been no rain on my journey. The story's rain hadn't arrived beyond the window. She laughed. Then she could stop worrying about whether I'd have an umbrella tomorrow morning. Mine hadn't been lent to anybody either, I said. It remained in its place.

Had listening tired me? No, though I wanted to stop here rather than immediately turn the completed story over and tell it a second time. All right. She wanted to leave some parts unexplained for a while too.

Before good night, she said she'd enjoyed being the one who extended this invitation. So had I. The answer was short and clearer than last Saturday. I didn't use it to declare myself completely recovered.

After closing the channel, I closed the planner. The card remained on Wednesday without alterations or a new drawing of an umbrella. I put away the water glass and prepared for bed. My real umbrella stayed at home. I didn't fetch it to discover whether it made a sound. Tonight's rain had already stopped once, when she said the story was over.
