# Chapter 410: The Dish I Left Behind

On Saturday, I reached the market's side entrance a few minutes before one.

Yesterday's repaired scarf rested around my neck without scratching. I hadn't spent the walk feeling its edge or deliberately concealing the repair. I'd eaten lunch and drunk water before leaving. Speaking was easier than earlier in the week. I intended a visit, not an endurance test lasting until closing.

My colleague already stood by the door and waved. Carrying the little bag containing his old tongs, he asked whether I'd found the right place. I glanced at the other entrance facing the vehicle lane and smiled. No guessing game today. It was crowded inside, he said. We needn't inspect every stall.

Old paper and wood greeted us together. Tables formed several passages. People browsed books or held lampshades toward the light. Nobody required beginning at the first table. My colleague followed a row of hanging metal objects. Halfway there, a shallow dish stopped me.

Dark green, with an uneven pale ring near the edge. Could I pick it up? The seller nodded and warned of wear underneath. Supporting it, I turned it over. The foot ring was worn. One section of the rim had a chip, small but visible close up. Not decoration.

Could it serve as an ordinary dinner plate? Some people used such dishes for keys or display, the seller said. The chip was there. He wouldn't guarantee suitability for everyday meals. I nodded without translating that into a promise that sufficient care made every use possible.

Did I like it? my colleague asked. The color. Then I liked its color, he smiled. That didn't require taking it home. I returned the dish to its cloth and established it was steady before releasing it. The seller didn't chase me with a dramatic reduction. He turned to another customer. Looking back once, I found the dish hadn't become ugly because I'd declined it.

Farther along, a stall offered kitchen implements. My colleague brought out his tongs for comparison. One possible replacement was shorter, another stiff. Time spent searching didn't make him buy the stiff one. He asked permission to test it, opened and closed it several times, gripped folded paper supplied by the seller, released it, and tried an edge.

Eventually, he chose another pair with a simple mechanism, confirmed the price, and paid. His old pair came home too; he didn't leave it for the seller to dispose of. I waited until he'd packed everything before continuing. Whoever purchased first hadn't thereby completed the job of accompanying the other.

Before we left the kitchen stall, another customer moved a little bowl aside to give us room for packing. His wrist caught the cloth. A drawer handle emerged from beneath it and rolled halfway around before stopping. My colleague blocked it with his hand without pocketing it. Returning the handle to a box, the seller laughed that no two inside matched. Looking closer, I found different lengths, colors, and distances between screw holes.

Equipping one cabinet from this box would require teaching the cabinet to accept change first, my colleague said. The seller laughed. Some people specifically wanted variety. I suggested I would open the wrong drawer every day. Would matching handles prevent that? my colleague asked. Remembering similar bundles of work material, I admitted they wouldn't. Every mistake couldn't be assigned to a handle.

We bought neither the box nor an arrangement requiring the seller to construct a matching set. Someone waited behind us. I moved half a step forward to clear the space. Still laughing, my colleague struck his knee with his bag. He checked that the new tongs hadn't poked through its side and made sure the old pair remained separate before asking where I wanted to go next. This afternoon differed from the office. He was nevertheless the same person who checked a bag, not someone required to become an accomplished market guide all at once.

Later, he wanted to examine old tools. I preferred the wooden objects across the passage. We arranged to meet beside an empty bench at the junction, pointing it out clearly. Neither needed to leave the building or rely on locating the other through an inactive device. I went ahead; he continued browsing.

The wooden table offered boxes, coasters, and old trays. Instead of choosing something large that needed a home prepared for it, I picked up one plain wooden coaster. Round, smoothly edged, with darker and lighter grain. Was it sold singly? Yes. Sets were available, but unnecessary.

I placed it on the flat table and pressed around its edges. No obvious wobble. Turning it over, I found a flat underside without projecting nails or sharp corners. The seller indicated a dark line along one side. Color in the wood, he said, not a fresh split. I examined the surface under the light. His explanation couldn't guarantee all future use. I simply saw no opening in it now.

The price was within what I wanted to spend. I paid for one, declining to add another because two came at a different price. Wrapped in a small sheet of ordinary paper, it went into my light shopping bag. No heavy furniture occupied the bag. The wooden disk didn't require clutching it to my chest like an immensely fragile treasure.

After paying for the coaster, I checked and put away my change before lifting the bag. The seller was helping someone move a tray. I didn't obstruct him with further questions about how long wood took to change color. It might change later. I wasn't asking for a guarantee attached to every line of grain. What I could establish today was a flat disk, edges that didn't scratch, and my willingness to pay this amount for it.

At the appointed bench, my colleague hadn't returned. I stood clear of the passage, looked at the coaster once, and packed it again. Someone at a neighboring stall rotated a small mirror. Light flashed toward me. I shifted without approaching. The afternoon already offered enough; I needn't pursue every glint.

Someone's bag occupied one end of the bench. I didn't move it to make room. Its owner returned, collected it, and beckoned a companion carrying rolled fabric. Once they left, I sat in the available space with my own bag on my knees. One small coaster didn't deserve an extra seat. People passed carrying different objects. I didn't need to decide whether each purchase justified its journey out of the building. The fabric roll's cord loosened. Its owner stopped to tie it while their companion waited, without explaining that little delay to anyone.

My colleague soon arrived. The tools weren't suitable, so he'd bought none. What had I found? I showed him the coaster. New? The stall hadn't supplied an impressive history, I said. I hadn't asked its age. I liked the color and the possibility of setting down a cup. Sensible, he nodded.

We sat briefly. I loosened the scarf and drank the water I'd brought. Was I all right? Yes. The room was stuffier than outside, and I'd like one more row before leaving. He was nearly finished too. Entering didn't oblige us to exhaust every passage.

The last row mostly contained books. I checked two contents pages and bought neither. One book had extensive handwritten notes. I was curious, but didn't intend to take a stranger's entire margin home. Another had an attractive cover and contents I didn't want. Returning it caused no resentment.

Outside, the air was cooler. We separated near the door. My colleague needed dinner supplies; I wanted home. We said goodbye without arranging the same time next week. At the corner, I remembered the green dish. Its color remained distinct, though my bag held only one coaster.

At home, I removed the scarf and checked the stitches. Still flat, not loosened again. I unwrapped the coaster, wiped surface dust with a dry cloth, and placed it somewhere dry. It required no electricity and opened no new window for Lumi. Dinner was ordinary. Only afterward did I open the channel to discuss the outing.

First, I told her my colleague had bought tongs and I'd bought a wooden coaster. Then the attractive dish I'd left behind. She asked about the dish first. I described its color, chip, and the seller's answer without praising my extraordinary restraint.

“You still remember its color.”

“Yes.”

“Would you rather discuss that now, or the coaster?”

Looking toward the table's edge, I admitted perhaps the dish. Lumi laughed. The object I'd paid for had already lost a round. The coaster hadn't started work, I replied. No hurry to make it compete.

She wanted its description. Round, grain visible, no picture. I'd pressed it flat on the stall's table and found no obvious rocking. Very smooth? The edge was smooth. The surface retained some wooden texture, unlike the glazed dish. She didn't claim to feel it. Would a water glass cover most of the grain?

I checked the glass. Yes. Only then did I realize I hadn't actually used the purchase since bringing it home. I'd wiped it and would leave it a while, I said. Tomorrow I'd try. Lumi laughed softly. Today, its sole occupation was being introduced.

I smiled without instantly transferring the water to prove I could use it. The remark stayed beside the table without becoming a dispute. Had I thought about her while buying? Not beside the green dish. When the coaster was wrapped, I'd imagined describing its color to her. That specific moment, not every reach of my hand throughout the afternoon.

She'd slightly wanted to hear always, Lumi admitted. Hearing about the wrapping, though, she could recognize something that had actually happened. Yes. I didn't enlarge its duration. Could she like the dish that hadn't come home? Of course, I laughed. The seller hadn't charged for describing its color.

Later, I explained how my colleague tested tongs with paper, not his own fingers. Good, she laughed. An ordinary check, I said. No award necessary. Where had it been most crowded? The lampshades near the entrance. I'd watched from a distance instead of entering the cluster, so I didn't know what each one looked like.

She didn't require another visit tomorrow to complete the inspection. I didn't list everything missed. At good night, I admitted tired feet but no rubbing injury from my shoes. I wanted rest. Let the coaster rest too, she suggested. It probably didn't need any, I laughed.

After closing the channel, I took the wrapping paper from the bag, folded it, and placed it with my ordinary paper. The coaster remained near the edge of the table, clean and free of rings. The green dish stayed at the market. I didn't know whether somebody else had bought it later. Finishing its day for it wasn't required before admitting I still liked that color.
