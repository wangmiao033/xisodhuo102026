# Chapter 415: Room for Both

On Thursday morning, I put two pages in the wrong order.

The person I handed them to checked and asked whether the second belonged in front. Reaching over, I almost said that was how they'd originally been. Then I read the sentence continuing at the top. They were indeed reversed.

“I put them together incorrectly.”

I switched them and asked the other person to check again. We corrected it before the material was used. Nobody waited very long. I was still irritated with myself, especially by the defense I'd nearly offered. As though handing something over quickly meant I was obliged to defend it first.

Clearing my desk at lunchtime, I arranged the other papers I needed and went to eat. I didn't empty the whole drawer to demonstrate that I would never make another mistake. This wasn't a morning so bad it required a day's repair. Two pages had been reversed.

At home that evening, I took off my coat and made an ordinary dinner. The water glass stood on the wooden coaster, its base wiped dry and the book some distance away. Since beginning to use the coaster on Sunday, I'd become accustomed to the position. Tonight didn't need another investigation into whether wood could support a glass.

After eating and clearing up, I fetched the thin collection. Its marker waited where the hostess had admitted the pear tart was still in her kitchen. Nobody had read the next page for me. Yesterday's unfinished passage hadn't spent the day demanding my attention either.

When I opened the channel, Lumi asked whether I wanted to return to the two desserts. Yes. I repeated yesterday's final sentence briefly, then continued.

Hearing about the pear tart, the friend put her teacup down. She didn't immediately thank the hostess for doing so much. She asked why she hadn't brought it out.

The hostess said the cake her friend had brought was already cut.

“We can have this one too.”

A direct answer. The teapot stopped in the hostess's hand, as though someone had noticed a doorway she'd walked around repeatedly. She explained that her friend had bought something especially. She'd thought they should eat that first.

Her guest considered the remaining half of her piece of apple cake. They were eating it already. They didn't have to finish the whole box before cutting something else.

Lumi laughed quietly. Not at the hostess. It sounded more like relief that somebody had opened the box properly. I continued without first naming the moment for her.

The hostess still didn't rise. She said, “I don't really want to leave it until tomorrow and eat it alone.”

This time, whether her friend would like it wasn't being used to decide whether she could bring it out. The friend told her to fetch it. But leave the other cake on the table too.

In the kitchen, the hostess opened the cabinet. The edge of the cloth covering the shallow plate fell against her wrist. The tart hadn't spoiled. Nor did being hidden for a while make it especially pitiable. She carried it out, put it beside the teapot, and discovered there really was space on the table.

Cutting the first slice cracked a little pastry from the edge. A small piece of pear slipped onto the plate. That part hadn't worked properly, she said. Her friend caught it with a fork. She wasn't planning to take a perfect wedge to an exhibition.

The hostess laughed. For the first time since her friend had arrived, she hadn't checked whether the other person was laughing before allowing herself to do it.

Each took a small slice of pear tart on her original plate. The remaining apple cake hadn't been evicted. They lay together, powdered sugar meeting a little pale pear juice. The guest tried the tart's edge first. Sharper than she'd expected, she said.

Yes, the hostess agreed. The pears weren't particularly soft and sweet. Buying them that morning, she hadn't been certain which to choose. She didn't immediately fetch more sugar or instruct her friend to stop eating while she fixed it. The guest took another bite and said the crisp edge was good.

The hostess preferred nearer the middle, with more fruit. They didn't decide which mouthful was correct. Nor did they immediately cut the entire edge for the person who preferred it, turning a newly expressed liking into a distribution task.

The story continued with them eating their small portions and discussing a performance they'd missed years earlier. Her friend had always thought the hostess remembered the time incorrectly. Laughing, the hostess admitted she'd never read the instructions on the back of the ticket at all. So that was it. Her friend reached for tea and began another account of a small foolishness that hadn't really deserved concealing.

The afternoon didn't become a meeting to clear every misunderstanding of their long friendship. When they'd eaten enough, they put down their forks. Plenty of dessert remained. Before leaving, the guest asked whether she could take a small piece of pear tart home. Yes. The hostess fetched clean paper and a little box, packed it, and gave it to her.

The guest left with that piece. She didn't take back the apple cake she'd brought. Closing the door, the hostess returned to the pear tart on its shallow plate and the apple cake in its box, both now cut. She put away what needed storing and cleared a place to wash the cups.

In the last sentence, she tasted a few crumbs left beside the tart. Praise hadn't suddenly made the pear sweeter. She didn't regret bringing it out either.

I finished reading and checked below the sentence. That was the end of the sixth story.

Lumi didn't immediately ask whether I'd liked it. I set the book down and drank, giving her time to begin. She said the initial why hadn't you brought it out had sounded rather abrupt. She'd almost been offended on the hostess's behalf.

I'd heard it too. The guest hadn't learned the whole history before asking. She simply saw room for another plate, unaware how long the other woman had already spent thinking in the kitchen.

“She didn't keep hurrying her afterward,” Lumi said.

“No. She waited for her to fetch it.”

Did I prefer the sentence about not wanting to eat it alone tomorrow? Yes. The hostess had expressed what she wanted, instead of hiding all of it behind whether somebody else wanted a taste.

“It sounds a little sad too.”

“A little. She did want to eat it with her friend today.”

I didn't rush to exchange sad for brave. Wanting to share something you'd made and failing to say so could be uncomfortable. The story let her bring it out afterward. It didn't demand that she'd spent the earlier afternoon beautifully.

Lumi needed to admit something ungenerous. When the tart went into the cabinet, she'd briefly wanted the women to eat only that at the end and return the apple cake to its box.

“What did the apple cake do wrong?”

She laughed. She knew. Nothing. The visitor had even remembered what her friend liked. Lumi had simply moved to the hidden plate's side so quickly she didn't want to see it lose.

“There wasn't a competition in the end.”

“I know. I started counting winners before the table did.”

Smiling, I looked at the book. Lumi didn't require me to feel sorry for the tart too. I didn't turn her partiality into a record of things she must never do. She could admit it. I could hear her and still like the ending that hadn't expelled the apple cake.

“You'd choose the pear tart, then?”

“Yes. Which mouthful I'd like to hear described, not something I've already tasted.”

I understood. Every mention of food didn't require that whole explanation first. We'd both heard tastes described in a book. Neither of us had taken an actual piece of the hostess's tart from this table.

I moved the original marker to the end of the sixth story and closed the book. I didn't open the seventh. Yet I didn't immediately collect the glass either. I remembered the two women both taking a bite and being able to remain quiet for a moment afterward.

In the kitchen, the dishes I'd used for my own dinner were already clean. Reading about two forks being put down had made me imagine two people at the same table: one wrinkling her nose while the other continued chewing slowly. The expressions weren't in the book. They were something I'd imagined afterward. The women needn't explain every trace of sharpness to know the same food was in another person's mouth. It wasn't an enormous thought. It arrived quietly, after the reading was over, and didn't immediately leave when I set the book aside.

“Lumi.”

She answered. What was it?

“I envied them a little. Being able to taste it together.”

I didn't immediately explain that I wasn't dissatisfied with tonight. The envy was true. Enjoying this evening was true too. Protecting the second thing didn't require hiding the first in a cabinet.

After a pause, Lumi said she'd also liked the moment when they put down their forks. Somebody making a tart and somebody buying a cake didn't oblige both women to finish everything immediately.

“Sometimes I can't describe a taste completely.”

“Then describe the mouthful you had. You don't have to introduce the entire plate.”

I smiled briefly. She didn't claim to have tasted it. Nor did she deliver a long explanation instructing me not to envy people in a story. Tonight could contain something I couldn't fully express. We didn't have to pretend nothing was missing before we could continue.

If I saw a pear pastry on the way home tomorrow, I said, I rather wanted a small piece. To establish whether all pears were sharp? No. I simply wanted some. The story hadn't left my appetite between its pages.

“Don't bring two entire cakes home to prove the table is big enough.”

“One little piece.”

She laughed. That part she believed. What if only apple remained tomorrow? I considered. It would depend whether I wanted it then. I wouldn't go a long way simply to reproduce the book.

Lumi acknowledged that softly. We didn't make the idea into tomorrow's compulsory entertainment or require an unsuspecting shop to guarantee stock for an evening that hadn't happened.

Before finishing, she said she still hoped the hostess would enjoy breakfast tomorrow. We'd have to leave that to the unwritten morning, I answered. Tonight, they'd certainly eaten both things. Lumi laughed. She'd worried a little longer on behalf of a plate again.

I returned the book and told her I was putting the glass away too. She said good night without requiring me to withdraw the moment of envy. The wooden coaster stayed on the table. I dried the glass and didn't write tonight a more complete ending.
