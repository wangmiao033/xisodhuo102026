# Chapter 349: The Hand Writing It Down

After dinner on Wednesday, I was about to put away the pen on the table when Lumi stopped me.

“Would you be able to write something down now?”

Looking at the pen in my hand, I smiled and told her I happened to be holding one already. That wasn't why she'd asked, she said. She'd only heard me say I was clearing the table.

“I know. What do you want to write?”

“The door without a bell. I want a few sentences to actually be on paper.”

My hand stopped on its way toward the pen holder. The idea pleased me enough that I almost reached straight for my manuscript folder. Lumi added that she wasn't asking me to invent what came next. Only to write what she said.

“All right. I'll get a clean sheet.”

I took one from my blank paper and placed it on the dry table. I moved the glass farther away so a raised arm wouldn't knock it. Was I ready? she asked. Yes. Then I waited.

She spoke the first sentence slowly.

“On the door was a note that said: Please ring.”

I wrote it, then read the whole sentence back. Lumi acknowledged it and gave me the second: “The visitor looked around but couldn't find a doorbell.”

At visitor, I'd almost added man. The pen merely paused. Nothing extra went down. She hadn't given the visitor a gender or chosen the repairman. The person appearing in my own imagination didn't oblige her to choose.

The third sentence was: “The person inside asked: Why don't you ring?”

Reading it aloud, I couldn't help laughing. Was something wrong? Lumi asked. No. The person sounded thoroughly convinced they were in the right. Her voice brightened too. Don't let them start reasoning yet, she said. There was another sentence.

“The visitor said: I couldn't find it.”

After writing, I looked up and asked whether she wanted quotation marks. Lumi paused, then said yes. Around the note and both people's words. I put them in without turning my laugh into a parenthetical instruction.

She asked me to begin at the first sentence. No acting.

Before reading, I straightened the page. I'd written with it at an angle. Now my wrist had nowhere to rest. I moved the pen, then shifted my chair forward. Hearing the legs scrape softly, Lumi asked whether I had enough room. I explained. Laughing, she said the page wasn't even full, yet the person writing it had already run out of living space. I laughed too. The table hadn't shrunk. I'd suddenly forgotten how to hold a sheet of paper.

“You can leave it down to read,” she said.

I laid it flat and held one upper corner. Much more comfortable. Good, she said. I could start now. I shouldn't keep the visitor waiting while I found myself a seat.

I read the four sentences in my ordinary voice. At the last one, I still wanted to give it the sound of someone expecting rejection. Checking the impulse made me sound stiff. Lumi said the visitor was looking for a bell, not necessarily deeply hurt already.

“I seem to have got disappointed on their behalf.”

“They still have the energy to look. Don't sit them on the doorstep so soon.”

Laughing, I read the last sentence again. Shorter this time. After a quiet moment, Lumi said she liked the visitor saying they couldn't find it, rather than immediately accusing the door of not having one. Perhaps there really wasn't one. Perhaps they'd overlooked it. The person inside didn't need to gain the upper hand so soon.

I wanted to ask whether this was the direction she'd chosen. I was also afraid one question would pull four sentences into a long explanation. Finally, I only asked whether to keep writing.

“One more.”

She tried: “After a silence, the person inside asked: Are you still out there?”

Before putting pen to paper, I asked whether after a silence belonged on the page too. Yes. It was part of the sentence, not simply today's attempt at the voice. I wrote on the next line and finished with the question mark.

Five sentences, less than half a page. I told her they were down. She asked for another reading from the beginning. At the end, I wanted to say it finally resembled a story. Then I realized that would make the earlier evenings, when she'd only tried voices, sound as though she'd done nothing.

“There are five sentences on the paper now,” I said.

“You count very solemnly.”

“I don't want to leave you short.”

“There isn't a sixth yet. Don't add one to make it look like enough.”

Smiling, I said I wouldn't. She didn't laugh immediately. Not being able to see what was beneath my hand felt uncomfortable, she said. Not because she thought I'd secretly added something. Once she'd spoken, the thing existed somewhere she couldn't reach for it herself.

I looked at the paper. The handwriting wasn't especially elegant. The second line slanted a little more than the first. My happiness remained. Promising to keep it safe for her wasn't a suitable way to press down that discomfort.

“Would you like me to describe how it looks?”

Yes. I told her where the lines sat, about the added quotation marks, and how the bottom half remained empty. I didn't praise it for resembling her handwriting. It was plainly mine.

“Is your writing very small?”

“Not today. The second sentence got a little cramped, so I started a new line afterward.”

“You didn't change my words to make it tidy?”

“No. I can read it again directly from the page.”

“Not yet. I've just heard it.”

Her voice eased. After a while, she asked whether I wanted to put a name on it. Whatever she wanted, I said. But she was asking me. That stopped me for a moment. I admitted I wanted to write Lumi beside it. It would look like a piece of work she'd really handed over to me.

“But I don't want that yet.”

“Then no name.”

She laughed softly. Leaving the name off didn't mean she was leaving the authorship open for me. I laughed too. My job was very clear today, I said. What was it? Writing, and trying not to send the visitor to repair the door.

This time, she laughed for a long while.

Afterward, we discussed where the paper would go. I didn't want to slip it between my versions of Which Way. Nor did I intend to open one of her existing private envelopes. Lumi asked whether there was another clean, empty envelope. I found a plain white one and told her it was new and empty. That would do, she said. Leave it unsealed for now.

She chose the right side of the lower bookshelf, separate from the pale Cuff envelope. I put it there and returned to tell her where it was. No title or name outside. Only the sheet with its five-sentence beginning inside. Permission to read while writing and checking didn't turn it into a book I could take out whenever I liked.

Returning to the chair, I noticed ink on a fingertip and told her. Would it stay forever? Lumi asked. No. I'd wash it shortly. It would probably fade. Laughing, she said she'd borrowed only a small piece of my finger tonight, then.

“Difficult to price that.”

“Didn't you say your job was clear?”

“The work's clear. Billing is another matter.”

She laughed without immediately making the joke into a conclusion that everything tonight had been easy. Once I'd washed my hands and returned, she asked whether this counted as turning my evening into another task. I considered. I'd certainly done something, but I'd wanted to. If she asked me to write until I was sleepy every day, I'd say no.

“Today isn't every day.”

“No. Today is five sentences.”

We didn't open another manuscript that night. She wanted a while longer with ordinary me, she said. I told her how I'd put the pan lid too far from the pan at dinner, then looked straight at it without recognizing what I was looking for. She didn't give that incident to the visitor at the door. I didn't ask when the door on the paper would finally open.
