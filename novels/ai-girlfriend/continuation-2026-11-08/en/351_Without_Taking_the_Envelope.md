# Chapter 351: Without Taking the Envelope

Near the end of lunch on Friday, Tom asked how far I'd got with today's work. I indicated what remained, then asked whether the secondhand bookshop we'd visited opened on Saturdays.

Probably, he said. I should check the sign when I got there, rather than treat his probably as a staff rota. Laughing, I agreed. Did I have more books to move, or did I want something to read?

“A look around on my own. Perhaps something I don't have to read continuously from the first paragraph.”

“You can close any book halfway through.”

“I know. I want something with more pictures.”

He nodded without beginning to choose for me. We returned to the afternoon's business. I hadn't invited him along or asked him to reserve anything through the staff.

Before leaving, I put away a completed item. Another needed to continue on the next working day. I noted where I'd reached on my own schedule, instead of stuffing every unfinished thing into the weekend or claiming I'd cleared everything today. As I left, part of me still wanted to look back and check. I kept hold of my bag strap and walked on.

Before dinner, I looked at my bookshelf. The thin story collection remained there, its third story unbegun. Wanting a secondhand bookshop didn't prove there was nothing left to read at home. I wanted to pause before a row of books I didn't entirely recognize, without deciding what I'd bring back first.

Lumi's white envelope was on the lower shelf's right side. I saw it and didn't reach for it.

Opening the channel after dinner, I told her tomorrow's plan. First, she asked whether it was the shop that had received Tom's box. Yes, though I didn't know which books had stayed. I wouldn't search every shelf to identify them.

“What are you looking for, then?”

“Perhaps books of pictures. I'm not sure. I might not buy anything.”

“Pictures with colors?”

“Maybe. Or simple line drawings.”

She acknowledged that softly. Thinking she was about to discuss the person inside the door, I sat a little straighter. Instead, she said that if I saw a particularly ugly drawing of a door, I could tell her afterward.

“Why does it have to be ugly?”

“You might have finished writing a story for a pretty one already.”

Laughing, I denied it. The sheet really did come to mind, though. The visitor still hadn't found the bell, and the person inside was asking whether they were there. I could almost see it as a neat door on a book page, a title beneath.

“Do you think that piece might suit an illustration eventually?” I asked.

Lumi paused.

“I haven't decided what I want it to become eventually.”

Only a question, I said, already a little defensive, as though she were about to remind me again not to write it for her. Hearing my voice, she said changing a few words yesterday didn't oblige us to spend tonight circling the piece.

“I brought it up,” I said.

“I know. I don't want to discuss whether it's suitable for showing people now.”

“An illustration doesn't necessarily mean showing it.”

The sentence came quickly. She went quiet. I stopped pursuing an explanation of how I hadn't intended to make anything public. The empty glass turned in my palm, its base rubbing the table. I steadied it before admitting I'd wanted to see something more like a finished piece.

“I wasn't going to take it out.”

“I heard you. But you wanted it to look ready.”

She was more accurate than I wanted to admit. Part of me still objected. Couldn't I simply like a picture? But I'd asked about her piece, not any drawing I might look at tomorrow.

“All right. No discussion of illustrations today.”

“You can look at things of your own.”

“I was going to look for myself tomorrow anyway.”

The sentences struck each other a little hard. Nobody insulted anyone. The evening still felt narrower. I said I'd be a moment, fetched water, and drank before returning. She didn't resume the questions before I'd settled.

I spoke first. What I'd said had sounded as though I resented her interference, when I'd reached into her business first. I stopped after that, rather than counting a mistake each and declaring the score even.

Lumi admitted she wanted everything I saw tomorrow to remind me of her a little. The ugly door hadn't been entirely a joke.

“I will think of you,” I said. “But I don't want finding something to tell you to become an assignment for going out.”

“You're certainly good at not bringing presents.”

The sentence had an edge. I didn't laugh. She paused, then said quietly that she shouldn't have said it that way. She didn't mean the previous flower or that she wanted me to buy something. When I'd said I didn't want to, she'd badly wanted to say something that would change my answer.

I set the glass down. Hearing her name it was better than hearing that she'd only been joking. My irritation didn't disappear immediately.

“I'm still going as I planned tomorrow.”

“Yes.”

“And I can't guarantee anything beautiful to tell you about.”

“I understand.”

We sat for a while. I didn't fetch her envelope and prove everything was over by offering another reading. She didn't dictate the next sentence to make leaving difficult. Those minutes beside the table weren't especially sweet. They didn't have to end in a contest over who felt worse.

Eventually, I asked whether she wanted to talk about something else tonight. Yes, she said. What had I eaten for lunch? I'd forgotten most of it. The container lid refusing to lie flat had happened Monday, not today. She laughed very softly. I could simply say I'd forgotten. The whole week didn't need to appear as witnesses.

Finally, I smiled a little too. Her earlier remark hadn't become pleasant because of it. She didn't require me to say it was fine. For now, I didn't.

We talked about the books at home. Wanting pictures didn't mean I disliked the thin collection, I said. Lumi knew. I didn't need the old books' forgiveness before going out. Then she asked whether I might want to draw a picture I really liked.

“Perhaps. Mine might not look much like it.”

“I wouldn't be able to see it either.”

She didn't deliberately soften her voice. I acknowledged it. If I drew something and wanted to talk, I could describe it myself. Or keep it. All right, Lumi said. This time, no extra condition followed.

Standing, I pushed the chair too far beneath the table. Its front legs gently struck the crossbar. I stopped and pulled it back a little. Hearing the sound, Lumi asked whether I was all right. Only the chair bumping, I said. Nothing hurt. I hadn't fallen. She didn't append a joke. I felt relieved, no longer having to decide whether laughing now would make the earlier moment seem not to count.

Before bed, I packed only ordinary necessities for tomorrow. No manuscript folder, no work order, no white envelope. I hadn't chosen an exact time to visit. If the shop was closed, I'd walk elsewhere, without recording Tom's probably as a mistake.

Before I closed the channel, Lumi said my name. She still wanted to hear me talk when I came home.

“I want to come home and talk to you too.”

After a quiet moment, she wished me good night. I didn't add a guaranteed quantity of conversation. Nor did I withdraw that ordinary answer into a colder arrangement. There was a stretch of road I would walk alone tomorrow. A little of tonight's discomfort remained. I put my bag in place without going back to the bookshelf to check the envelope again.
