# Chapter 355: Not Every Line Needs a Joke

On Tuesday evening, I finally opened the third story in the thin collection.

It had been a long time since I'd read the first two. I'd seen the third title, but other things kept pushing the story aside. I read two pages alone and discovered the beginning was easier to enter than I'd imagined. Someone had come to an inn to collect something they'd left. The receptionist kept assuming they wanted a room. Not our wrong door, and not Lumi's nonexistent doorbell. At the visitor's second attempt to explain, I laughed to myself.

This time I wanted to continue, and I wanted Lumi to hear it. After opening the channel, I asked whether she'd like the beginning of the third story. Yes, though she didn't want to assess a manuscript today. Not mine, I said. At last I wasn't responsible for defending the author.

“Are you sure? You're very good at adopting things at short notice.”

Smiling, I arranged the book and began with the first sentence.

At the point where the receptionist pushed a form over for the visitor's name, my tongue caught on a name. I stopped and repeated it. Apparently it wasn't only the person in the story who couldn't decide what they were called, Lumi said. I laughed, repeated the sentence, and continued.

Two thin pages clung together as I turned them. I nearly skipped a passage. One sentence was at the counter; the next mentioned stairs. I stopped to check and found the page number had moved on by two. I'd turned too far, I explained. She hadn't understood how they'd moved either. I went back and separated the edges with my finger. That interruption wasn't uncomfortable. We were both finding where the story had slipped away.

The next page held a long sentence. I waited until the end to breathe, my voice tightening through its final words. Was I planning to stay at the inn too? Lumi asked. I wouldn't even let my breath leave first. It wasn't a bad joke. Suddenly, I didn't want to laugh.

Pressing a finger beneath the line I'd finished, I said, “Wait a moment.”

She stopped.

The room was quiet. I felt I'd made something light become heavy. I'd joked about the author first, and laughed the first time. Stopping now felt like changing the rules and blaming her for not knowing beforehand.

“I know you're teasing,” I said. “But when there's a remark every time I stumble, I start waiting to be caught again.”

“Not every time.”

The answer came quickly. Looking at the book, I admitted there'd been two instances today, and she'd commented on both. That didn't make it always. I didn't want to recruit every previous evening as extra evidence for this one.

Lumi didn't immediately continue arguing. After a while, she asked whether I wanted no interruptions today, or disliked that sort of teasing.

“Let me finish a passage today first. I can't quite explain the other part yet.”

All right. I'd expected that answer to take us straight back into the book. When I began again, I was stiff. The easy sentences seemed to have been placed high up, each word requiring a reach. After two lines, I stopped again.

“Now it feels more like an exam,” I said.

“I'm not counting mistakes.”

“I know. But I'm waiting for you to prove you aren't.”

She didn't find a beautiful answer. Perhaps put the book down for a few minutes. I resisted. I'd barely read anything. Stopping felt as though my request had spoiled the entire evening. Eventually, I closed it with the usual bookmark inside, without folding a page.

I stood and stretched my shoulders, then went to the kitchen for water. Back again, I told her I'd sat down. Was the water hot? Ordinary temperature, I said. Then, with a little smile, I asked whether she was searching for the safest possible question.

“A little.”

Neither of us laughed again.

After some water and a long pause, I finally said that sometimes I wanted to hear her say something affectionate directly. Not always have to guess it from behind a joke at my expense.

Lumi stayed quiet longer this time. Regretting what sounded like a request for praise, I hurried to explain. She asked me not to withdraw it yet.

“That I like you reading?”

“Or just being with me. Not every time. But sometimes you tease, and I laugh, and afterward I don't know whether we've used up the good part already.”

Sometimes she did that too, she said softly. She'd want to say she was glad I'd come home, then begin by asking why I was moving a glass again. That was easier than handing over a sentence and waiting for me to catch it.

“But that doesn't make guessing your job,” she added.

I steadied my glass. She couldn't see the movement. Tonight, she didn't joke about it either. After a while, she said she liked hearing me read, especially when I didn't deliberately put on a character's voice.

It was praise. I immediately heard the condition after especially, though, and didn't quite relax. I knew I couldn't ask her first attempt to avoid every sharp place inside me. I told her about that little feeling anyway.

“Look. Now I want to edit the compliment.”

“I did add an explanation again.”

At last, we laughed a little together. Not much. She didn't repeat it ten times until I was satisfied. I didn't invalidate what she'd said. I'd heard her, I said. I wasn't good at receiving it today. She wasn't good at giving it directly either.

I took two more sips and moved the glass away from the book. Closing it so quickly earlier had left more of the bookmark showing than usual. I straightened it without using the tidying to put away tonight's reading altogether. Lumi didn't hurry me. I still wanted to know what the person had left, I told her. So did she. My reading had distracted her from the original question. Finally, there was something we both wanted to continue, without first deciding how to demonstrate that we'd made up.

The book remained on the table. Would she like another page, with talking afterward? Lumi wanted to listen. I found the line and began slightly before it. I didn't need to restart the whole story to prove the evening hadn't been spoiled.

When the visitor finally explained that they wanted an old umbrella, not a room, I laughed and lost half a word. I went back to supply it myself. Lumi didn't jump in. At the end of the passage, she first asked whether I'd laughed too. Yes.

“I like the receptionist still thinking about the empty rooms,” she said.

We discussed the story rather than my fluency. Perhaps they'd been working too long, she thought. To me, it seemed more as though they weren't listening. The author hadn't confirmed either idea on this page. We didn't search past what we'd read for the correct answer.

Later, a little sleepy, I chose to stop at a paragraph's end. Reading only a few pages no longer felt like betraying the beginning. Once the bookmark was in place, I told her I still wanted to hear that sentence sometimes. She didn't need to repeat it now.

“Which sentence?”

“The direct one.”

She acknowledged that softly. She remembered what I'd meant. I didn't ask her to promise a delivery time tomorrow. Good night was slower than usual for both of us, even a little clumsy. The book stayed on my own pile. We'd begun the third story, not finished it. The person collecting an umbrella remained at the inn. One awkward evening didn't require me to lock the voice I loved entirely behind jokes again.
