# Chapter 353: The Tree on the Left

On Sunday afternoon, I moved yesterday's drawing to the part of the table nearer the window.

Light didn't improve the pencil lines that had seemed so heavy last night. It merely made them easier to distinguish. Where I'd thought the eaves met the window frame, a little white space remained. I turned the sheet the right way and wrote a small left in the lower corner, so I wouldn't rotate Lumi's entire street halfway through describing it again.

Then I laughed. If the people in the picture could walk, they'd complain that their city had only one direction.

Lunch was over, the kitchen clear. Opening the channel, I told Lumi I wouldn't recite every line today. Just the wall, tree, and empty ground. Was the ground empty because I hadn't finished? Partly, I admitted. I hadn't remembered the rest yesterday. Adding it now would only mean guessing.

“Start with what you remember, then.”

The wall was on the right, with a small overhang. I paused to make sure I hadn't turned the paper. The tree stood on the left, some distance away. Not the tree in the drawing book. I'd seen this one at a corner on the walk home. I made that clear before the pictures gradually became the same place.

How broad were its branches? Lumi asked. Looking at the shape, which resembled an uneven roof, I said it was wider than the door. I'd actually drawn it too short.

“How can you tell?”

“Once I added the trunk, it looked as though the tree had sat down.”

She laughed. Why couldn't it sit? If trees really could, I said, the benches might object. Good. Neither would be in a hurry to stand. We didn't give the tree a career. We returned to its position on the page.

I'd meant to erase the branch reaching out on the left. It crossed too much blank space and crowded the wall. When I explained, Lumi asked me to leave it for a moment.

“You like it?”

“It sounds capable of catching someone's hat.”

“But it draws too much attention.”

“It's the part I want to hear about.”

The eraser stopped at the edge of the sheet. I knew she couldn't see it. She was imagining from my description. I was still slightly annoyed, as though a disorderly branch had defeated the eaves I'd struggled with yesterday. I told her a picture didn't only have to show places where something might happen.

She went quiet.

Looking at the line, I heard myself teaching her something I'd only discovered yesterday. Lumi didn't dispute the theory. Had I wanted to know what she liked, she asked, or wanted agreement about something I'd already decided to erase?

“Probably the second, just then.”

“I didn't know which answer you wanted.”

That made me uncomfortable. Not because she was wrong. I'd only wanted to share an unimpressive drawing. How had I secretly given her an assigned position again?

I put down the eraser. I explained that I wanted more open space beside the wall. The branch crowded it. That made sense now, she said. She still liked the chance of a caught hat. I smiled a little. She really wanted the tiny figure from the book to walk onto my paper.

“Perhaps. You needn't invite them over.”

After looking for a while, I shortened only the tip of the branch. Most of it remained, with a little more space. I described the change without declaring it a compromise that satisfied everybody. Her branch had lost something, Lumi said. Yes.

“You don't have to like it.”

“I know. I'm not angry either.”

Only then did I realize I'd been waiting for her dislike to sound like permission. I took a breath and asked whether she'd hear one last detail. Yes, she said. No trial for the drawing, please.

At that point, I noticed my elbow had shifted the paper. Left remained in the corner, but I was looking at an angle again. I told Lumi I needed to move things, set the water glass on the other side, and straightened the page. Was I preparing to erase again? No. The table was too narrow. My arm kept bumping it. After the change, the branch hadn't shortened further, but my hand had more room. Lumi had wondered whether speaking would earn the tree another punishment. The tree had finished work today, I told her. We laughed without making it prove which of us listened to the other.

The last detail was two uneven steps before the door. While drawing, I'd remembered only that people needed to lift their feet. Now they resembled empty boxes. I described the proportions. After listening carefully, Lumi asked whether taking out a ruler would make those two steps more reliable than the entire house.

At last I laughed aloud. The ruler stayed where it was. I didn't fetch it. Nor did I add crookedness to every line to prove I didn't need one.

The page wasn't full when I finished. I wanted to continue drawing. Lumi asked whether I could put down the pencil and talk about something outside it for a while.

“You've heard enough?”

“Yes. I've imagined a lot of lefts and rights. Now I want to hear you.”

That didn't make me a prize more important than a drawing. It still pleased me. I laid the pencil beside the paper, its point toward me instead of hovering over another correction, and told her I was sitting somewhere brighter than usual. The back of my hand felt warm. Then I moved farther inside rather than keep sitting in the sun for the sake of a pleasant description.

Did I still think she'd chosen wrongly? I almost said no. Then I admitted a little disappointment lingered, though I knew it wasn't her problem.

“Not entirely yours either,” she said. “You wanted me to like what you liked. I wanted you to tell me more about the part I wanted. We were both pulling a little.”

I didn't make that into a settlement requiring us each to give ground. She still preferred the longer branch. I liked the slightly emptier space. Next time we could describe something else without acquiring identical eyes tonight.

Later, we discussed the assistant telling me not to flatten the book's spine yesterday. Had I instantly stood straighter? Almost, I said, laughing. I'd certainly released my thumb with exaggerated care, as though raising a hand to prove my innocence. She played a cautious customer who dared turn pages only with their eyes. Eyes needed registering too, I said. She laughed and stopped being the customer.

Before the afternoon ended, I told her I wanted to keep the drawing. Not as proof it was good. I might forget why I'd shortened that branch.

“You can write my girlfriend disagreed on the back.”

“She just didn't like it as much.”

“She's still here.”

The last words were quiet. Pressing the corner of the sheet, I said I knew. Not every difference needed a declaration of staying. Hearing her say it herself still made me reluctant to cover it with a joke.

On the back, I wrote Sunday street-corner practice, without her comments. The paper was clean and colorless, the branch shortened once. After closing the channel, I laid it flat at the back of my schedule book, separate from the old room sketch. The afternoon hadn't produced a perfect drawing. It had left a branch I'd changed, which she liked less.
