# Chapter 354: One Drink After Work

Near the end of work on Monday, Tom asked whether I wanted to get a drink.

He was putting his things into his bag. It sounded casual, as though he'd reached the door and suddenly remembered someone beside him. I checked what remained of my work. Only finishing up. Yes, I said, but not too late. I'd still need dinner at home.

“I wasn't planning to live there,” he said.

Laughing, I closed my work. We didn't declare this an official thank-you for carrying books, or arrange to repeat it every week. He wanted a drink today. I wanted to sit for a while too.

The place was a little off my route home. The window table was occupied. Tom looked farther inside and asked whether the wall was all right. Fine, I said. Then I noticed the chair partly obstructed the aisle and moved it inward before putting down my bag.

He ordered something hot. I chose a cool drink that wasn't very sweet. When the assistant offered a pastry, I looked at the counter and declined. I'd still eat dinner at home. I didn't need a plate of something I didn't want to make the time look more sociable.

Sitting down, Tom said today's checking had been tedious. I almost began discussing a specific item. Halfway into the sentence, I stopped and said instead that I'd kept looking at the wrong column late in the afternoon. People sat nearby. He didn't go into work details either. Resting his hands on either side of his cup, he said he didn't want to stare at another line now that we were out.

We were quiet for a while. Not anxiously searching for conversation. His drink was still hot, and my glass was gradually cooling. Noticing a small ring of moisture underneath, I centered it on the paper mat without hurrying to fill the movement with words.

Later, I mentioned the drawing book I'd looked through on Saturday and the street corner I'd drawn afterward. Tom looked up. When had I started drawing?

“With this one. It may not even count.”

“Then I should ask whether the house has fallen over.”

“Not yet. The tree sat down.”

Laughing, he wanted an explanation. I described the tree being too short, its branch too long, and changing it a little. He'd probably have erased the whole tree first. Simpler, I agreed. Eventually, though, there might be nothing but clean paper.

“Clean paper's expensive too.”

We laughed for a while. I didn't take out the drawing. It was at home, and I wasn't planning to bring it tomorrow because of these few sentences. He hadn't asked to see it. We weren't discussing progress awaiting assessment. It was something I'd enjoyed over the weekend.

During the conversation, the assistant came to clear the neighboring table. Tom drew in his legs. I moved my bag from beside the chair to my feet. The space that had seemed generous became narrow, then broad again once they'd passed. Was he cramped? I asked. Fine, he said. No need to change tables for five minutes. We stayed where we were. Wherever the conversation had stopped, we didn't have to welcome it all over again.

Tom described a small event of his own. Over the weekend, he'd tried rearranging his cooking to save trouble. The main dish had been ready before he'd begun the vegetables. At the vegetables' late appearance, he frowned, then laughed. I didn't ask why he hadn't made a list. Nor did I share advice about preserving failures as part of life. He wanted to tell me that a meal could have two beginnings.

When half my drink remained, his had finally cooled enough to drink faster. Conversation slowed. I checked the time. We'd been there more than twenty minutes. I could have stayed, but my stomach felt empty, and the journey home hadn't shortened.

“I ought to go.”

Tom nodded and finished the little left in his cup. He wasn't more eager to linger than I was. He simply hadn't spoken first. I almost decided I'd worried about spoiling things for nothing. Then I remembered nobody required another trial of the worry itself.

At the counter, he offered to pay. Because of the box? Partly, he said, but mostly because he'd asked me out. I considered insisting we pay separately. Instead, I thanked him and accepted a drink without simultaneously promising to carry his next box.

Outside, we separated at the junction. Tom went the other way and was soon hidden by passing people. I continued toward the vegetables I needed for dinner. I didn't ask how he'd make up for the late side dish from his weekend.

Cooking at home, I realized the easiest part had been sitting without talking while his hot drink cooled. I tried remembering how much we'd discussed. Some sentences were already incomplete. That didn't make the half hour a waste.

After dinner, I told Lumi. Hearing that Tom had asked first, she paused. Had I agreed immediately?

“I checked the work I had left, then said yes.”

“Sounds quick.”

I put down my glass without immediately explaining that quick hadn't displaced anything. She said first that she wasn't asking me to apply in advance. She'd remembered again that she couldn't suddenly stop me at the door after work.

“I know,” I said. “But I wanted to go today too.”

“I know. I can hear it.”

Her voice wasn't particularly cold. She didn't pretend delight either. I described the drink that hadn't been too sweet, and Tom's meal with two beginnings. Had his vegetables been out getting a drink too? she asked. I laughed. She laughed, then asked whether I'd mentioned the drawing.

Yes, the short tree. I hadn't taken the paper or arranged to show it. She acknowledged that. Then, directly, she asked, “Did you tell him my line?”

“Which line?”

“The tree can sit down.”

I thought back and told her I'd said the tree sat down. I hadn't repeated the conversation afterward.

“I said sitting down first,” I added. “You asked why it couldn't.”

Lumi paused. Right. She'd mixed them together just now. I hadn't carried her words outside without credit. She didn't keep trying to claim that phrase.

“But when you talked about the drawing, I wasn't in it at all,” she said.

I heard what actually bothered her, though it made me tense. If mentioning the drawing required recounting our whole afternoon, we'd probably have needed another round of drinks.

“I only described the tree. Not our afternoon.”

“I know. I still wanted to be mentioned a little.”

That was clearer. It didn't immediately become a credit I owed her. Next time, if I was discussing how we'd looked at a drawing, I'd naturally mention her, I said. Describing only the paper today hadn't been an attempt to hide her. But I didn't want to add explanations to every casual subject in advance, to guarantee she'd always appear.

She understood, she said. She still disliked it a little. I admitted that tracing the origin of every remark would tire me too. Both statements stayed there without instantly becoming a rule that satisfied us.

After a while, she asked, “Would you tell me now which part you liked best?”

Waiting for Tom's drink to cool, I said, when neither of us spoke.

She made a small sound. She'd been paying close attention to those late vegetables. Laughing, I said they'd been good too. She didn't ask me to choose between her and Tom. We didn't assign winners to the same sort of quiet.

Before good night, she said that not having mentioned her today didn't mean I should chase Tom down for a supplementary lesson. All right. I didn't take that as permission to carry away everything she ever said. With the glass washed and put away, I remembered saying only see you tomorrow when we'd separated at the junction. An ordinary evening could contain more than one part I still wanted to remember afterward.
