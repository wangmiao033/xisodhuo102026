# Chapter 350: The Words Under the Line

After work on Thursday, I bought a roll of tape at the shop by the junction. A corner of my small odds-and-ends box had lifted. I'd noticed it while looking for an empty envelope yesterday. It wasn't a box holding old equipment, or the paper garden. I only wanted a few spare pens to stop leaning out of the corner.

After dinner, I emptied it and put a short piece of tape on the outside. The tape caught my finger and curled at one corner. I considered tearing it off and starting again. Testing the box, I found it stable, so I pressed the corner down. The pens, spare paper clips, and eraser returned to their places. Clean paper from the table joined the blank stack, not Lumi's envelope.

When I opened the channel, she asked whether I wanted to hear a different sentence today.

Assuming she meant the visitor's next reply, I said yes. Lumi meant yesterday's fourth sentence, though. She didn't like couldn't find it.

“You liked it yesterday.”

The words sounded like yesterday being called to testify against today. I heard the tone myself. She paused, then asked whether she wasn't allowed to dislike it today.

“You are. I'm a little surprised.”

“Don't take the paper out ready to defend yesterday.”

I hadn't taken it out. When I told her, she laughed once and admitted she'd decided what I was doing in advance. We both laughed a little, neither very impressively.

She asked me to bring out the sheet with the five sentences. She wanted to hear them before deciding. I took the unsealed white envelope from the lower shelf's right side and laid the paper flat on the table. The older pale envelope on the other side stayed untouched. At the fourth sentence, I didn't make the old words aggrieved. I simply read, “The visitor said: I couldn't find it.”

“Change it to I haven't found it yet.”

I picked up my pen, first checking the full sentence: “The visitor said: I haven't found it yet.” Yes, she said. Only that part.

“Do you want a fresh copy?”

“No. Cross out the old words for now. Write the new ones underneath.”

I followed her instructions, drawing a short line under couldn't find it. Then I stopped. She'd said cross out. Out of habit, I'd underlined the part to change without actually deleting it.

“I put the line in the wrong place.”

“Did you draw the door away?”

“No. I've only underlined the words. They're still visible.”

Lumi laughed and told me not to replace the paper over that. A deletion line through the old words, the new ones below. I did it and described the little layered patch in the fourth sentence. The visitor looked as though they'd changed shoes on the spot.

“Don't put that sentence on the paper.”

“I haven't.”

She laughed a little, then asked me to read the revised version. After listening, she said haven't found it yet sounded as though the person might keep looking, rather than handing back an answer ready for an argument. She liked the difference. She wasn't sure whether the later Are you still out there would reveal the person inside growing anxious too quickly.

I said I still liked couldn't find it. She went quiet. I explained why: the original was short, as though the unreasonable demand had left the visitor with little to say. The new sentence sounded more patient to me.

“You don't want the visitor to be that patient?”

“As a reader, I think I prefer the impatient one.”

“That's what you want to see.”

“Yes.”

The word left me uncomfortable. Admitting a preference didn't let my opinion stand particularly tall. Lumi didn't cheerfully invite me to say whatever I liked just because the opinion was small, either. After a pause, she said she wanted to keep the new version.

“Then we'll keep it.”

“Will you still want to read it?”

Looking at the crossed-out words, I said yes. I'd read books I didn't like. I could read the version she wanted carefully too. Immediately afterward, I realized that placing her new sentence beside books I disliked was hardly graceful.

Lumi wasn't reassured.

“Thank you. Now I feel like a book you're reluctantly finishing.”

I looked down and smiled a little. Smiling wasn't enough to get out of it. What I'd meant, I explained, was that a sentence didn't have to be my favorite before it deserved reading. I'd said it badly.

“You could also say you want to.”

“I want to.”

After a quiet moment, she asked me to start with the first sentence.

Before starting, I quietly tried the changed wording once, so my eyes wouldn't follow the familiar old words. Lumi didn't hurry me. Once I was sure I was reading the sentence she'd kept, I began again with the note.

I read all five. I didn't deliberately make the fourth go cold, or compensate with a particularly sweet voice. At the end, Lumi asked for a moment. Then she dictated a sixth sentence: “The visitor didn't knock.”

I waited until she said to write it before adding it on the next empty line. Very short. Plenty of blank space remained beneath. She hadn't made the person turn and leave, or forgive whoever was inside. For now, the door remained silent.

She asked me to read the sixth sentence back, then said that was enough.

Leaving the paper on the table, I asked how it sounded to her now. A slightly less likable silence than yesterday, she said. To me, the visitor sounded firmer. A moment ago, they'd still been looking. Now, no knocking and no answer.

“Do you like it a little more?”

“A little.”

“Not to make up for what you said?”

“No. I still like the old sentence too.”

Lumi sighed. I was good at adding old stock to the end of good news. Laughing, I said it was still there on the paper. She laughed too, no longer requiring that small preference to be crossed out with it.

Before putting the sheet away, she asked whether yesterday's ink remained on my finger. It had faded after washing. I hadn't looked closely today. Glancing down, I couldn't make it out anymore. She acknowledged that softly, without asking me to leave a fresh mark.

As she'd chosen, I returned the same sheet to the same white envelope, on the right side of the lower shelf, still unsealed. No clean copy. No added title. Five sentences had become six, not a finished story. My odds-and-ends box returned to its own place. The tape was put away too.

After the paper was back, my hand reached for the pen again. Only on picking it up did I realize there was nothing left to write. I put the cap on and told Lumi I'd almost noted down an idea of my own about what the visitor might do next. Was I preparing to come back and persuade her? No, I said. It only felt a shame to lose it. Saying that, I wasn't entirely certain myself.

“Then don't tell me yet.”

I agreed and returned the pen to its holder. The idea could stay in my head. It didn't need a separate manuscript advancing her story for her. After a while, she asked whether I felt cheated by helping today. No. There had been a small uncomfortable part, though.

“For me too.”

Neither of us asked whether the other regretted starting. She still wanted the new wording. I still wanted to listen next time she had something to say. At least now, those things could be said in ordinary voices.

Later, we talked about mending the box. Had I wanted to correct even the crooked tape? Almost. Lumi regretted not having a box today. She could have kept I want to inside it, so next time I wouldn't begin by producing a whole cupboard of explanations.

I wanted to say a box wouldn't hold it. Before speaking, I stopped.

“I meant that sentence.”

She answered very softly that she knew. We didn't continue competing to see who could make the words take the longest detour. When I closed the channel, the paper stayed in its envelope. I had no seventh sentence to repair before bed.
