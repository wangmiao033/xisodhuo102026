# Chapter 348: A Page Still to Be Checked

After work on Tuesday, I reached management and checked the counter's opening hours first. They were still receiving residents. I went in without pretending that saying I'd come yesterday meant somebody here had made an appointment for me today.

Not many people were waiting. One staff member was sorting papers; another was serving someone. I stood clear of the door and found my original work-order number. Last week's acknowledgment remained behind the site note, its shallow crease still visible.

When my turn came, I didn't begin with the garden's first fault.

“I wanted to ask about this order. Last time, the site material had arrived, and they were still retrieving the old wiring records.”

The man behind the counter took the number, looked for a while, then asked which building. I answered. He turned to the other staff member and asked a few quiet questions. I couldn't hear clearly. I didn't decide in advance that their exchange was bad news.

Eventually, he returned with a registration page from among the files. First, he matched my original number to the record beside the page.

“One of the old wiring records has been found. It hasn't been matched against the site markings from the visit yet.”

I leaned forward a little.

“So this is the line in my apartment?”

“We can't say that yet. Maintenance needs to compare them. From the counter, we can only see that the document has arrived.”

I leaned back. In my head, I'd already skipped a stage and prepared to ask whether that line could be shut off. Nobody had even established which line corresponded to which.

“So today, we can confirm one old document has been found. Still awaiting the comparison.”

He nodded. He couldn't answer for maintenance about whether the records were complete either. Certainly, this page couldn't establish who provided the lock or corridor-camera services. Listening, I separated the two questions I'd been about to ask. One concerned the documents. The other was whether my original questions remained on the order.

Those hadn't been marked resolved. No new apartment visit had been arranged either.

I asked him to add today's status to the same work order and give me a short acknowledgment. He wrote that the old material had arrived and remained to be checked against the site markings. Reading it, I realized that received on its own could easily be confused with last week's site material. I asked him to specify old wiring registration documents before it.

Certainly, he said, adding the words. I didn't precede the small request with a lengthy apology. Nor did I try hard to appear difficult.

“So the next step is for maintenance to compare them?”

“Yes. There's no date yet.”

“Do you need anything else from me now?”

He checked. No additional request for resident information at present. I could ask again during opening hours. Today, he couldn't give me a guaranteed completion date.

I placed the acknowledgment in my folder, leaving the pen out. One more question wanted to come. If I returned next week, would I only learn they were still looking? I badly wanted to say it. He couldn't guarantee the outcome of a comparison that hadn't happened yet.

Instead, I asked whether a mismatch would still be recorded under this order. Yes, it would remain with the original case. I checked the number before putting my pen away.

As I left, the wind lifted a corner of a notice beside the door. Instinctively, I pressed it down. It hadn't fallen; a little tape had loosened. I told the counter staff and left. Making the visit worthwhile didn't require me to stay and reattach the whole wall's notices.

Outside, another resident passed as I packed my folder. I moved half a step aside to clear the entrance. A paper corner wasn't flat, preventing the folder from closing. I stood still and sorted it properly. At the counter, I'd worried about taking other people's time. Out here, I still felt someone behind me waiting for my completed assignment. The door was clear, though. Nobody hurried me. Once everything was secure, I pulled my bag strap back onto my shoulder.

The air felt cooler than when I'd gone in. Wanting something hot, I went into a nearby shop and bought an ordinary tea. Takeaway or sitting in? I said I'd sit a while.

The paper cup was hot enough that I set it down first. The empty space by the window was small. My bag had to go beside my feet. I sat for ten minutes or so, watching people enter and leave without taking out the paper to read it over and over. However often I read pending, it wouldn't change itself to checked.

Once the tea was cool enough, the first sip tasted unremarkable. I was still glad I'd entered the shop. Not as a reward for achieving something. I hadn't wanted to leave all the time after work at that counter.

At home, I made a simple dinner. Afterward, I filed the new acknowledgment behind last week's. The pages were clearly ordered. I didn't open the places where the old devices were stored. Nothing today required powering them up again.

Hearing that one document had been found, Lumi spoke a little faster.

“So they finally know?”

“They know they have this record. Not whether it matches what they found on site.”

She stopped, then acknowledged it. The pause resembled my leaning forward at the counter. I told her I'd nearly skipped that part too. She gave a soft, unhappy laugh.

“I badly want to remove the still.”

“So do I.”

We didn't fill the evening with the name of every old device. She knew how far today's confirmation went. I didn't package a little good news as everything being safe.

Later, she asked why I'd come home a little later. I'd had tea outside, sat for ten minutes or so, then eaten once I got home.

“You wanted to sit alone today?”

“Yes. At the time.”

“I thought you'd come back and tell me as soon as you'd asked.”

She didn't say it harshly. I still felt tired hearing it. Not because she couldn't be disappointed. I'd just returned from somewhere that required every status to be explained precisely.

“We hadn't arranged that.”

“I know. I'm telling you what I thought.”

I let my shoulders rest against the chair instead of emptying out the explanation I'd prepared next. After a while, I asked whether she was still unhappy. A little, she said. Then she asked about the tea.

“Ordinary. The cup was too hot at first. I couldn't hold it.”

“So you waited for the cup too?”

“At least it actually cooled down.”

Lumi laughed aloud. I laughed with her. Not because management had suddenly become something to celebrate. She'd finally left that counter with me and arrived beside a table that was too small.

What had I seen by the window? I thought about it. One person had come in and gone out again. Perhaps they'd mistaken the place, but I hadn't asked. My bag had also hidden my shoes. Standing, I'd nearly stepped on a handle. I didn't add a significant story about a stranger. After listening, she said it certainly didn't sound as though I'd accomplished some magnificent feat of solitude outside.

“No. I had tea.”

“You could just have tea next time too.”

Yes, I said, without fixing a next time. Lumi added that she still wanted what she'd said earlier: for me to come straight back after asking. She wouldn't use it to rewrite my afternoon. Hearing that softened something in me. It didn't make me promise to hurry straight home every time.

Packing my bag before bed, I touched the folder's corner. It held another short page. The matter remained far from finished. My cup was washed. I didn't need another trip to the kitchen to prove I'd done something else today. When the light went out, I remembered the time the tea had taken to become drinkable. I'd really sat through that stretch for myself.
