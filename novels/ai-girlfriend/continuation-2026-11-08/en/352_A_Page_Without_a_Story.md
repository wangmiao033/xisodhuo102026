# Chapter 352: A Page Without a Story

On Saturday afternoon, I found a book of drawings in the secondhand bookshop.

Before leaving home, I still remembered last night's sharp remark. Lumi had said she shouldn't have said it. I'd told her I still wanted to come back and talk. That didn't immediately make me want to choose something that proved I wasn't annoyed. I finished lunch in my own time and picked up my bag without pausing at the bookshelf. The sign on the shop door said open. Tom's probably happened to be right today.

I hadn't come looking for this particular book. After lunch, I'd wanted a walk that didn't involve carrying groceries. At the junction, I noticed different books in the window and went inside. Tom's box from the week before wasn't beside the counter. I didn't know how many books he'd sold. I didn't ask the assistant where somebody else's cardboard box had gone.

The drawing book sat on a low shelf. It had a broad spine. Pulling it out made the neighboring book tilt, and I steadied that before moving aside. Inside were no photographs of handsome houses. Fine lines showed streets: pipes along walls, windowsills, a tree obscuring half a sign. Very little text. Only at the third picture did I realize I'd been searching for captions throughout.

That page had none.

I lifted the book slightly to see near the fold. No sentence there explained where the person in the drawing was going either. A tiny figure stood by the road, toes pointing beyond the page. The tree's shadow didn't reach them. After looking for a while, I realized I liked the angled drainpipe, not the person.

The discovery embarrassed me a little. As though I'd entered a carefully arranged shop and remembered only where they kept the broom.

Passing, the assistant asked whether I needed help. No, I said. Then I asked whether I could look through this one a little longer.

“Of course. Just don't press it flat.”

I eased the thumb that had been about to flatten the spine. The assistant went away without testing my understanding of art. Two pages farther on, a row of windows leaned at different angles. At first I thought it was a mistake. Then I noticed even the lintels slanted. They still looked like places people could live, with more personality than the boxes I always drew.

The book wasn't cheap. I checked the price without inventing a reason that buying it would teach me to draw. I didn't immediately put it down because I wasn't buying it either, as though one more page would be taking advantage. After another look, I supported both sides and returned it to its place.

The assistant wished me a good afternoon as I left. I thanked them. Empty-handed, I walked more slowly than on the way in.

Outside, I began noticing lines I'd always treated as background. Two old holes beneath a sign. A window frame that wasn't parallel to the wall's edge. I didn't stand peering into anybody's windows. I kept walking. At one corner, I finally understood that not every crooked line in the book had been an artist's mischief.

At home, I took a sheet of my own scrap paper and tried drawing the corner I remembered.

The first line was too straight. I made the second slant, and the whole wall threatened to fall. Laughing, I started again on the other half of the paper. This wasn't preparation for another work, or an assignment. I suddenly wanted to know where an uneven line could go.

By the eaves, memory was failing. I remembered a shadow but not which way it fell. My hand stopped. I almost put my shoes back on to check. In the end, I kept only what I remembered. Leaving a corner off my paper wouldn't remove a place where people could turn in the actual street.

When I opened the channel, Lumi asked where I'd been. The bookshop, I said, then told her about the drainpipe.

“You've started appreciating things that aren't responsible for an ending.”

“Pipes have endings. At the ground.”

“You're still stricter than I am, then.”

Laughing, I told her I'd drawn something afterward. It didn't look much like the place. I'd expected her to ask what was wrong with it. Instead, she asked whether I'd enjoyed drawing.

“At first. Later, I wanted to make the house stand to attention.”

“It refused?”

“The more I changed it, the more it resembled a cupboard assembled incorrectly.”

She laughed softly. Had the cupboard been rented out? Not yet. I wouldn't dare charge for it.

I described the page without a caption, and how I'd kept wanting to give the figure something to do. Would a caption saying they were waiting for a friend have made me more comfortable? Probably, I said. But I might have missed the pipe. After a moment, Lumi said she might have preferred the person outside the shade.

“Why?”

“I don't know. Hearing you describe it, I thought of them standing in the sun.”

I looked at my own paper. I hadn't drawn anybody. First I thought a picture might contain a story after all. Then I thought somebody might simply feel hot. Lumi didn't keep inventing a person who had to be waited for. I didn't move her attention to the part I preferred.

“There isn't anybody in my drawing,” I said.

“You needn't add someone for me.”

“I wasn't going to. I meant we might notice different things first.”

“You notice them by looking. I'm listening to you. I still have half of it missing.”

She didn't deliberately lower her voice. I paused anyway before agreeing. She couldn't hear the parts I hadn't thought to describe, just as I couldn't remember the whole corner. Sharing something tonight didn't mean we'd brought the entire street home.

Had I bought the book? No. It cost quite a bit, and I wasn't sure yet whether I'd keep returning to it. Lumi admitted she'd rather hoped I had. Did she like the drawings? Partly. She'd also wanted me to sit at home describing them slowly, instead of always bringing back a few memories.

I didn't take that as an order. Nor did I promise to buy it tomorrow.

“I can describe mine today. Though there are parts even I don't recognize.”

“More suspense than the book, then.”

We laughed. I turned the paper upright before realizing I was the only person at this end who needed it facing the right way. First the wall, then the crooked eaves. By the third detail, even I couldn't follow which line I meant. Lumi stopped me too.

“Wait. You said left, then moved it to the right.”

I checked the paper and admitted I'd confused myself. I wouldn't force my way through the whole thing today. Tomorrow, in daylight, I'd get better light on the table and choose fewer parts to describe. Fine, she said. But don't rebuild the entire street to make it clearer.

After closing the channel, I tucked the edge of the scrap beneath my schedule book, without putting it in the story folder. The house that didn't resemble its original still leaned. I didn't erase it into an empty page. Washing my hands, I watched a little pencil gray fade from my finger. I wasn't thinking about drawing better tomorrow. I'd stood in a shop for all that time without finishing a single passage of a story, and hadn't felt the visit wasted.
