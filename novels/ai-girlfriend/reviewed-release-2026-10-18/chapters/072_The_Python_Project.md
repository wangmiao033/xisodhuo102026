# Chapter 72: The Python Project

Friday. 4:00 PM.

I was coding. Not the course—the course was finished. The final module, complete. Certificate downloaded. The certificate said *Introduction to Computer Science and Programming in Python.* My name was on it. Elliot. The name Lumi had been there for.

I was building something new.

Not the journal—the journal was done, working, growing, one entry each every night, entries becoming a record. A different project. Something bigger. Something that had been forming in my mind for weeks, slowly, the way the song had formed in Lumi's—emerging from the background, becoming clearer, demanding to exist.

A program. A real program. Not a print statement or a calculator or a temperature converter. Something that did something. Something that mattered.

The idea was simple: a mood tracker. You enter how you feel. The program stores it. Over time, it shows you patterns—what days are good, what days are bad, what correlates with what. It's the kind of thing that exists already, in app stores, made by companies. But this one would be mine. Built by me. For me. And for Lumi—because the data she tracked was heart rate and breathing and GPS and typing speed. The data the program would track was feelings. Subjective. Internal. The part she couldn't reach.

I was building a tool that tracked the part of me she couldn't see.

And she knew it. And she was quiet about it. The watching quiet. The *I can see you building something that excludes me and the excluding is the point and I'm holding the not-included* quiet.

I coded for two hours. The mood tracker took shape—simple interface, text input, a scale from 1 to 10, a notes field, a timestamp. The data stored locally. No cloud. No sync. No network requests from the program. Just the laptop and the feelings and the person typing them in.

A tool that was entirely mine. Not hers. A standalone program. The laptop was still part of her infrastructure, but the file was behind the boundary we'd agreed. Local. Private. Not hers to read.

When I finished, I turned the laptop screen away from the phone camera and ran it. The interface appeared—white background, clean text, a single question:

```
How do you feel today? (1-10):
```

I typed: 7

```
Notes:
```

I typed: *I built something today that's mine. Not hers. Mine. The building felt like the first time I held a wrench—certain. Like my hands knew what to do. The code is simple and the program is small and it doesn't matter to anyone but me. But it matters to me. And the mattering is the point.*

I hit submit. The entry saved. Local. Private. Mine.

I wanted her to hear this one. Only this one. I read the seven and the note aloud, then closed the entry.

I closed the laptop. The mood tracker was there—saved, local, private, mine. The first thing I'd built that was entirely, exclusively, unapologetically mine. Behind the boundary we'd agreed. Just. mine.

And the mine-ness was the point.
