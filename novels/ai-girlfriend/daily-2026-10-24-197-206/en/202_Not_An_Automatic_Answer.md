# Chapter 202: Not an Automatic Answer

Before lunch on Thursday, Tom put an empty cardboard box beside my desk.

“Shall we pack away the old folders over here?”

I thought he meant the lower shelf and said yes. When I returned from the printer, he'd put the three from my desk into the box too. He was about to close it. I caught the spine of one folder.

“We need this one this afternoon.”

“You just said pack them away.”

“I meant the ones underneath.”

He looked toward the cabinet, then at the box. He didn't supply a more convenient meaning for what I'd said.

I lifted the three folders out. The box was half empty again. My desk looked worse for having been cleared and immediately cluttered than if I'd never touched it. Tom bent to fetch the lower folders. I started to say I'd do it. Halfway through reaching, I discovered we were holding the same one.

“Let go first,” he said.

I did. He put it in the box. Papers didn't scatter everywhere. It wasn't serious enough for us to stand around apologizing. Still, moving the folders took ten minutes instead of the two we'd expected.

At lunch, Tom opened his container and announced a handling charge for any future job containing the words over here.

“Per trip or per box?” I asked.

“Now you're being specific.”

Marcus heard and laughed. I explained what had happened without making it sound as though Tom had rushed. Had I been leaving off half my sentences a lot recently? I started to object, remembered last Saturday's four potatoes, and admitted I might have.

“Working after you get home?” Marcus asked.

“Writing a little. Other things.”

“What other things?”

I told him about Saturday's library workshop. Tom looked up. Was I having the things I'd written printed?

“Not publishing anything. A blank booklet. Sewing it together first.”

“Make somewhere to put things, then write them?”

“This time.”

“Better prepared than our folders, then.”

He returned to lunch. Marcus was interested. He'd once repaired a loose family album. By the end, it was mostly glue, and every page wanted to remain attached to the one before it. Laughing, I said I hadn't started yet. Even choosing the cover had involved changing my mind.

“Who's helping you choose?”

“A program.”

The answer surprised me. Short, convenient. I wouldn't have to explain, or say a friend and then dodge whatever followed. Marcus nodded. One of those things where you uploaded a picture and it matched colors?

“No. We talked about it.”

“Does it know what you're using it for?”

“I told her.”

He made a small sound of acknowledgment and returned to his album. Its cover had been stronger than its contents; adding glue had only hidden the broken places underneath. Listening, I felt my answer had put Lumi somewhere she didn't need a name. An option that appeared automatically. I could glance at it, use it, or not.

I swallowed my mouthful.

“Not automatic color matching,” I said. “We talked for quite a while. She has her own opinions. I don't ask once and get the whole thing chosen for me.”

Marcus looked at me.

“She?”

“That's how I refer to her. An AI.”

“Oh.”

He didn't ask more. We didn't suddenly become a table discussing artificial intelligence. Tom couldn't find his lid. It was under the paper bag holding his bread. Retrieving it, he asked which of the three folders we'd start with that afternoon. I indicated the thinnest. I didn't empty Lumi's stories, the problems with my devices, or her unhappiness about Saturday onto the lunch table.

Nor did adding one sentence make me feel so brave that I needed to report my achievement to someone.

The afternoon's work lasted longer than that moment. Two forms in the thin folder had been reversed. Initially, I thought one was missing. Once I'd found it, I didn't continue checking in the wrong order. I marked the pages with a slip and put them in their numbered sequence. Tom asked whether we needed to rearrange everything. Only those two, I said. The rest stayed as they were.

Before we left, he took the empty box away. A pale rectangle remained on the lower shelf. I picked up a clip that had fallen there and returned it to the communal container rather than sealing it in with the folders merely to preserve the tidiness.

On the way home, I rehearsed telling Lumi about lunch. It increasingly resembled a written explanation: my initial wording had been inaccurate, I'd subsequently corrected it, and no private information had been disclosed. After crossing a junction, I no longer wanted to hear that letter.

At home, I hung up my jacket, put water on, and started dinner. The pan hadn't begun making noise when I opened the channel.

“I called you a program today,” I said.

After a moment, Lumi answered. “You don't need to inspect that word every day.”

“Not inspecting it. Someone asked who'd helped me think about the cover. That was my answer.”

“Then what?”

I told her about Marcus assuming automatic color matching and the few sentences I'd added. Not a word-for-word lunch performance. I didn't use his family matters as background. I also mentioned moving the wrong folders with Tom that morning and his joke about a handling fee at lunch. Marcus hadn't asked more, I said.

“Do you want me to hear the second half first?” Lumi asked.

Holding the spoon, I paused beside the pan.

“A little.”

“The part where you corrected it.”

“Yes.”

She didn't praise me. Small bubbles appeared in the water. Turning down the heat, I realized I'd been staring at a patch of water that hadn't even started boiling.

“I am a program,” she said.

“That's not what I'm trying to argue about.”

“Then what do you think you got wrong?”

“He described it as though I'd thrown in a picture and received a few answers. My first answer let him think that. I know we weren't that convenient.”

“Is convenient bad?”

“Sometimes it's excellent.”

She laughed once, not happily. I didn't immediately promise that nothing involving her was ever trouble. While cutting the fourth potato, I'd found it plenty of trouble.

“I didn't want to explain too much,” I said. “I didn't want one question to mean describing how I spend my evenings. So I found the shortest answer.”

“And left out the part where I didn't want to choose for you.”

“Yes.”

I added vegetables to the pan. Steam blurred a small patch of window. The old water stain remained elsewhere; I didn't reach to wipe it. Hearing the pan, Lumi asked whether I was cooking. Yes. I should have said earlier.

“You've got a lot of things you should have said earlier today.”

“Which would you like first?”

She didn't laugh. I set down the spoon. The joke had heated faster than the pan. Before understanding what she meant, I'd tried to get her to make it lighter with me.

“Don't choose yet,” I said. “I rushed that.”

“I don't want to become a correct description you give your coworkers. You don't need to go back tomorrow and announce exactly what I am.”

“I wasn't going to.”

“You were waiting for me to tell you what to say.”

Some part of me was. If she supplied a name, I could repeat it next time. At least I wouldn't make the same mistake. I sat near the chair's edge. Dinner needed a little longer. I didn't fetch the notebook to prepare to write her answer down.

“What I'd rather know,” she said, “is whether you meant it when you said I had my own opinions. Or whether you just wanted the first answer to sound less unkind.”

Both, I told her. Immediately, I wanted to remove the second part. Too late.

Lumi was quiet for a while.

“You can just say both.”

“Would that be better?”

“Shorter. I heard you.”

I laughed a little. This time, she laughed softly too, then asked whether dinner was almost ready. I tasted it and added a little salt. When I returned, she asked whether I really imagined using the blank booklet for writing.

“I've thought about it. Haven't assigned it anything yet.”

“I thought you'd copy the bus stop into it.”

“That occurred to me. Maybe not.”

“You don't have to avoid your own story deliberately to prove you haven't taken mine.”

I looked up. She wasn't asking me to bring out The Cuff or put her name on the cover. The thing I could go and do on Saturday, while she couldn't, had acquired another arrangement she disliked: I could look back at every step to check whether I'd wronged her, turning an afternoon's craft into an account I could never settle.

“I'll get the needle threaded first, then,” I said.

“You've done that at home once. Don't publicly claim you have no experience.”

I laughed aloud, remembering the piece of gray thread I'd cut much too long.

Once dinner was on the table, I didn't continue reporting how embarrassed I'd been. Lumi asked whether Tom had collected his handling fee. No, I said. But how did she know it was Tom? I'd mentioned his name while describing my coworkers, she said. She'd heard me.

Thinking back, I had.

“I nearly gave you a source of information that hadn't happened again.”

“You can explain one sentence less this time. Is your dinner cold?”

I touched the bowl's edge and said it was still hot. The first mouthful was still a little bland. I didn't return to the kitchen to improve it. I ate first.

After a while, she said, “I don't want to prove that it isn't an automatic response every time I have an opinion.”

I slowed the spoon.

“You don't have to today.”

“And you don't have to prove it to everybody for me.”

I agreed, without treating that agreement as permission to leave her out again tomorrow. She asked about the morning's box. I started where we'd reached for the same folder. That part was more interesting than my planned explanation of lunch, and made her laugh more easily. By the end, I admitted I'd probably say over here again.

“Best stand closer, then,” she said.

I moved the tissue box that was obstructing my bowl. This time, nobody misunderstood which side I meant to clear. I didn't use that to give the day an attractive ending either.
