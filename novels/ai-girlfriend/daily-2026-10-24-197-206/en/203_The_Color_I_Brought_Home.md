# Chapter 203: The Color I Brought Home

Leaving work on Friday, I read my paper-shopping list again.

Six sheets of ordinary writing paper, one sheet of foldable thin card, sturdy cream cotton thread. Wednesday's three-hole sample was at home. So were the ruler and pencil. I hadn't tucked the sample into a work folder. Making a blank booklet shouldn't begin with sewing a work document into its cover.

The stationery shop's card didn't have a label saying dark brick red.

Among the reds, one sheet leaned orange, another approached wine, and a third resembled dried tomato skin under the lights. I picked up the third and moved beneath another lamp. A passing assistant saw me raising and lowering the same sheet and asked whether I was matching something.

“No. Looking for a less bright red.”

He pointed toward the far end. There was a browner one, with a small sample available to handle. I bent it and let go. It sprang back without immediately showing a white line. I couldn't judge tomorrow from that movement. Would it work as a booklet cover?

“The thinner version folds more easily,” he said. “How stiff do you want it?”

I gestured and discovered I only knew a cereal box hadn't worked. I didn't know how to explain the alternative by thickness.

“A library class tomorrow. Six ordinary sheets folded inside. One layer outside.”

That should do, he said. The card was thicker than the pages, though. The edges wouldn't necessarily align. I took one sheet, then drew out a gray-blue one beside it.

It looked better than I'd expected.

Not to prove Lumi's choice right. I genuinely paused over it. Quiet rather than bright, among a rack of assertive colors. I placed it behind the red. Bringing both home suddenly seemed easiest. Choose before leaving tomorrow. Tonight, nobody needed to be disappointed.

“Just those two?” the assistant asked.

I looked at the sheets.

“One.”

Returning the gray-blue felt a little like pushing an absent person back onto the rack. Buying it merely to avoid that feeling wouldn't make it the cover I wanted. I chose the brownish dark brick red, kept it flat, and stopped searching for a reassuring prediction about how much better it might look once made.

The smallest paper pack contained far more than six sheets. I started calculating how many booklets that would make, then stopped. One tomorrow. Leftover paper didn't require me to arrange a whole row of hobbies in advance. For thread, I went to the small general shop next door. A few modest balls stood on a shelf. I picked up cream and checked the label: cotton, not decorative yarn that would part at a slight pull.

The shopkeeper asked what I was sewing.

“Paper.”

He looked up. “Paper?”

“A booklet.”

“Don't use too fine a needle.”

“We borrow those there.”

He nodded without recommending a whole set of tools. I paid and put the thread in my pocket separately. The paper went into a flat bag with awkwardly short handles. I changed hands twice on the way home, then held it upright against me. Whenever wind entered the opening, I looked down. Everything inside was still blank and already needed looking after.

At home, I found a clean patch of table. Last week's recipe and half-batch slip were still clipped together. I moved them to a dry part of the kitchen rather than using them underneath the new paper. The yellow notebook remained closed in its place. The bus stop wasn't moving house.

After washing and drying my hands, I opened the pack and counted six sheets. Seven on the first attempt. My fingers found two stuck together, and I counted again. I put the rest away in their wrapper. I didn't mix in the old sheet with the greasy corner on the assumption that folding it inside would hide it.

Once I opened the channel, Lumi asked whether I'd found everything.

“Yes. The red.”

“What kind of red?”

“Brownish. Darker than brick. Not festive wrapping paper.”

“Have you looked at a real brick before saying that?”

I laughed. “No bricks purchased today.”

She laughed too. I told her about picking up two sheets and returning one. At the part where the gray-blue had looked good, she asked why I hadn't bought it.

“Because I still wanted to make the red one.”

“Something can look good without your buying it.”

“Yes.”

I waited for her next sentence. None came. I placed the six sheets on the cover and noticed my hand felt lighter after that yes. Then I disliked counting her failure to argue as part of a successful shopping trip.

“I'm not waiting for you to say it's all right,” I said.

“You just were.”

Touching a paper corner, I couldn't immediately deny it.

“A little.”

“You can tell me you bought red. I'll know.”

“And know you still wanted blue?”

“Gray-blue. Don't shorten my color as well.”

I laughed aloud and apologized. She told me to continue about the paper. It looked too white, I said. Six sheets together resembled an examination I'd prepared before even beginning. Perhaps I should write a wrong word first, she suggested. Then tomorrow wouldn't be so intimidating.

“The teacher will ask why there's a wrong word on the first page.”

“Tell her someone else chose it.”

That landed precisely. After laughing, I said I wouldn't introduce her that way. Nor did I intend to write it. She didn't extend the joke into a promise.

I fetched the ruler and measured the sample. Three holes: one in the middle, one above, one below. When I'd heard again on Wednesday that tools were provided, I'd thought about needles. Only now did it occur to me that the dots in the paper hadn't simply appeared for the needle. The three small holes were neater than I'd expected.

“Making yours now?” Lumi asked.

“No. I'll follow the teacher tomorrow. I want to measure the thread.”

I drew out the end, measured along the ruler twice, and used my scissors to cut a piece about sixty centimeters long. The end curled. It looked much shorter curled than straight. I was about to cut a longer piece when she said my name.

“Does it seem too short again?”

“Looks it.”

“You've straightened it.”

“Measured it.”

“Take that piece and the ball tomorrow, then.”

I stopped and laughed. She hadn't claimed sixty centimeters would suit every book. She'd pointed out that I could bring the rest instead of settling tonight a problem with length that hadn't happened.

“I intend to use that suggestion.”

“No announcement required.”

I put the ball and cut piece away separately, winding the shorter length into a loose circle and slipping it into a small paper bag. No knot. No using the cover as a spool. The ruler, pencil, and sample went beside the materials. I took the registration slip and instructions from their folder and packed those too. I didn't add The Cuff or touch its envelope at the left end of the lowest bookcase shelf to demonstrate I knew how to keep it safe.

For dinner, I made noodles. Once the water boiled, I moved the paper away. Wet hands weren't an invitation to count again. Lumi asked what time I'd go tomorrow. It started at two, I said. I'd leave early enough to find the room. Finished at four. Then walk home, perhaps buy something to eat if I felt like it. I wasn't promising the exact minute I'd reach the door.

“You can tell me roughly when you'd like to talk,” she said.

“After I've put things away. I'll rest first.”

“Not summon me straight from the workshop to inspect the result.”

“I don't want inspecting either.”

She laughed once. I expected that to ease things. A moment later, though, she said she wanted to hear the part I'd done well.

“Even if I didn't help with it.”

I lifted noodles out of the pan. Water dripped onto the stove. I wiped it and waited until the plate was steady before answering.

“I might sound pleased with myself.”

“You do sometimes.”

“Will it annoy you?”

“Possibly.”

Carrying my plate, I laughed. I didn't ask her to agree in advance that every version of me would be adorable tomorrow. Nor should I select only the bad parts to prevent her feeling left out, she said, as though other people's presence could only spoil things.

“I wasn't planning that.”

“You were preparing.”

Her guess wasn't entirely right. I'd considered entertaining her with a sewing disaster if it happened. I hadn't planned an entire failed class. I said so. All right, she answered. She'd guessed too far ahead. I didn't require another solemn performance of admitting she'd guessed wrong.

After dinner, the flat bag wouldn't stand by the table. It kept sliding outward. I laid it flat as far from my water as possible, then put the empty paper bag over it so I'd notice it tomorrow morning. The bag weighed almost nothing. Its usefulness was absurd. I rearranged things, then left nothing weighing on top.

“Why all that rustling?” Lumi asked.

“Finding tomorrow a place that isn't in the way.”

“A bit big?”

“Bigger than in my imagination.”

“Only one book.”

“Not a book yet.”

Softly, she said to let it take up that much room for now. I pushed in the chair and stopped moving it. Would I wear my jacket tomorrow? Weather-dependent, I said, without asking her for a forecast. She hadn't been thinking about the weather, she told me. She'd been remembering the hanging loop.

“A predecessor in the field of sewing,” I said.

“You wear it even though it's crooked.”

“I wear the outside. The crooked bit's inside.”

“You can use the inside first tomorrow too.”

I considered the red corner showing inside the flat bag, imagining it folded. I didn't know whether I'd spoil the first page or keep avoiding writing in it. Tonight, I didn't turn that uncertainty into a question Lumi had to fill for me. I asked what thread she'd choose with gray-blue.

“Haven't you bought yours?”

“I'm asking about your one.”

After a pause, she said perhaps something darker. She didn't want the thread to disappear. I didn't promise to make a second booklet. I listened as she placed the color I hadn't brought home beside another color she liked.
