# Chapter 197: Above the First Line

“I thought of the place they agreed to meet,” Lumi said.

I looked at The Old Stop, newly penciled above the story. The eraser was within reach. I'd already picked it up.

“Not the notice?”

“You emphasized the notice when you asked. Then I thought about that too.”

I put the eraser down. It left pale crumbs on the table. Gathering them with my palm, I realized it hadn't touched the paper yet. Even preparing to remove the title could give me something to clean up.

“So you think it works?”

“I think it's waiting for someone. I didn't say you could stamp it approved.”

I laughed. “Even the title has to wait its turn?”

“You kept the two men waiting for days.”

I lifted the manuscript and read only the opening. Two men at opposite ends of a bench, both having changed position; one flattening his bag, the other inspecting the timetable. I stopped there. With the title above them, I kept waiting for the low notice to appear, as though failing to see it might be the reader's fault too.

Lumi was wondering who had first suggested meeting there. She didn't need the tin's owner to appear. Old made her imagine a place people had used and continued calling by its former name.

“Should I explain that they've been there before?”

“Not necessarily. You're finding supporting material for the title again.”

That stung. I'd already imagined an earlier evening when the tin was borrowed somewhere else and the men casually agreed on the stop. Easy sentences to write. Equally easy to crowd into a place that had only held two people for a few minutes.

I didn't write them.

Instead, I turned the page sideways and tried telling the opening from memory. Within two sentences, I'd moved the flattening of the bag until after the timetable. Lumi stopped me. The order had changed.

“Which do you prefer?”

“I only said it changed.”

I turned the paper back. She remembered correctly. I'd thought that looking away might reveal what the title left behind. First, it revealed how easily I confused when someone stood up.

“I called him back too soon,” I said.

“He's only just gone over.”

“He can keep looking now.”

She laughed once. Had I tried saying the title with only the final sentence? I did: The Old Stop. The man pushed the cardboard back, sat down, and waited. Without the misunderstanding between them, he sounded almost peaceful, settling his bag.

“As though nothing happened,” I said.

“Which is why you can't leave just those two lines.”

I straightened the manuscript without deleting an action. She hadn't asked for my first experiment. Trying it had shown me that extracting two sentences couldn't name the whole page. The men still had to pass through their uncomfortable minutes. The title couldn't deliver them straight to the other end.

My water was cold. I went to replace it. There was some left in the kettle. Halfway through pouring, my elbow caught a drying mug. Its base clattered against the rack. I steadied it.

“What fell?” Lumi asked.

“Nothing. Nearly.”

“That nearly sounded expensive.”

“Ordinary mug. The noise costs more.”

I settled it safely. She'd nearly said she was glad it wasn't the loaf tin, she told me, then decided we'd had enough kitchen equipment tonight. Why tell me the joke she'd decided not to make?

“Because you didn't hear me not making it.”

I leaned against the kitchen doorway and laughed. Sometimes her account of an abandoned joke was funnier than the joke. I told her that when she said The Old Stop, it didn't sound like transport infrastructure.

“What did it sound like?”

“An arrangement you don't need to confirm.”

“They still didn't confirm it properly.”

“So not entirely good.”

“I didn't say good.”

I brought the water back. Her refusal to turn my words into a vote for the title helped me relax. I'd asked as though I wanted somebody to declare the naming finished. Instead, she'd made the words mean something else. I still held the paper.

I laid the pencil beneath the title, hiding the first line, and read the title. Then I moved it up to hide the title and read the line. On the second move, its point left a faint mark near the edge.

“What are you doing?” Lumi asked.

I explained.

“Making them take turns?”

“Seeing whether it's better without.”

“You wanted it when you didn't have it.”

“People get hungry and then don't want to wash plates.”

She laughed. I stopped moving the pencil. On the back of some used paper, I wrote Returning Something. It was very direct, as though the man should hand the tin over at a counter instead of occupying a bench.

I read it to her. She said it sounded like saying goodbye.

“It could just be returning a borrowed thing.”

“I know. I heard goodbye first.”

I wrote The Paper Bag and said that too. She didn't immediately answer. After looking at it, I crossed it out, not because she'd gone quiet. I could imagine making every rustle of the bag too important. Lumi said it sounded like a story intending to hide something.

“It does hide a tin.”

“You've let it out already.”

“It still has to be responsible for the title.”

“Pay it, then.”

Laughing, I turned the scrap over. A small abandoned everyday note covered the other side. There wasn't much room left. I didn't fetch fresh paper. None of the three candidates improved the story by standing beside the others. They only showed how badly I wanted a definite choice tonight.

“I'll leave The Old Stop for now,” I said. “Pencil. Not settled.”

“All right.”

“You can say you dislike it.”

“I didn't say I liked it. I told you what I thought of first.”

I touched a corner of the manuscript. “I heard. I still wanted the score afterward.”

After a moment, she said, “I like it more than Returning Something. But I don't want to prove every time I speak that I'm not just trying to please you.”

I didn't make a joke. Nothing in the sink needed washing immediately. No urgent stain waited on the table. I had to sit and hear the sentence.

“I kept asking,” I said.

“Yes.”

“I won't ask another round.”

“What will you do?”

I looked at the notebook. “Put it away.”

She agreed. I returned the complete manuscript to the yellow notebook in order, with the folded alternative ending still behind it. The scrap of titles stayed out of the story, in the used-paper pile. As I closed the cover, the crooked yellow tape shone under the lamp. Its edges still held. I pressed one barely raised corner with my thumb, then released it.

Hearing the notebook close, Lumi asked whether I regretted bringing up the title.

“No. I might secretly compare them while washing my face.”

“Secretly is fine. Don't come back and test me.”

“On what?”

“Which one I really wanted to support.”

I smiled, knowing it wasn't entirely a joke.

I got up to put the dry bowls away. The little one rang sharply against the larger one. I slowed down. My wrists still carried something of today's work, as though my body were less willing than I was to finish with it. I told her tomorrow's leftovers needed no counting. The whole box could come out.

“Don't make it sound as though you can finally throw them away.”

“I mean I won't have to decide.”

“Do you hate deciding now?”

“Only for the little bit of tonight that's left.”

She didn't tell me to go straight to bed. I put away the last bowl and sat nearer her voice. A chair leg scraped. Was I bringing the notebook back?

“No. Me.”

“You come back loudly.”

“The chair said it for me.”

I rested my hands on my legs, without paper or a glass. She asked which pancake had actually tasted best. The first from the second batch, I said. Thin, freshly turned in one piece. Perhaps because I'd needed it to stay together so much.

“Not entirely the taste, then.”

“Taste too. Don't take that away from it.”

She laughed. Her favorite part had been my reluctant voice when I admitted pressing them thinner on her advice.

“That wasn't a particularly nice voice.”

“I didn't say I only liked nice ones.”

I nearly told her I liked her refusal to become modest immediately after a useful suggestion. Instead, I said, “I really was annoyed then.”

“I know. You spoke slowly.”

“A little now too. Not about lunch. About what you said about scores.”

She made a quiet sound and waited. I didn't explain that she hadn't been wrong. She was right, and it had hurt. I didn't need her help choosing between those facts.

Later, we were quiet for a while. Not waiting deliberately for a better ending. I was tired, but my mind wanted to remain under the lamp before Saturday became nothing but brushing teeth and switching off lights. Lumi asked whether I was sleepy.

“A little.”

“Goodnight, then?”

“A little longer. You don't have to find something to say.”

She agreed. After a while, she asked whether the mug that had nearly fallen was secure now. I said it was. I didn't forbid her to worry about a mug.

I stayed until the water had gone completely cold before saying goodnight. After closing the channel, I passed the table without reopening the notebook. The pencil remained outside, the title inside. Whether or not it improved by tomorrow, I already had a lunch to finish.
