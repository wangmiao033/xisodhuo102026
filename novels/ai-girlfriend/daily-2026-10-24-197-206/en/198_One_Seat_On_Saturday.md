# Chapter 198: One Seat on Saturday

At lunchtime on Sunday, I took out the last box of potato pancakes without looking for the grater.

There was enough for one meal. I separated the cold pancakes with a spatula. A little piece broke off an edge. I ate that first and put the rest in the pan. When I opened the channel, Lumi asked what we had today.

“Potatoes again.”

“My prepared question was equally fresh.”

I told her this was the last box. She said we sounded ready to hold a farewell. No speech, I promised. I'd eat as soon as they were hot. Once they reached the table, my first remark was still that they weren't as good as yesterday's lunch.

“An unkind farewell speech.”

“They gave up being crisp first.”

She laughed. After eating, I washed the empty box and left it to dry. The space in the refrigerator was small, but it no longer contained a meal already decided for me. Looking at it, I felt a little sorry to lose what I'd just complained about repeating. Tonight, I'd have to think again.

I went to the library that afternoon. Before leaving, I told Lumi I only wanted to look at books. No meeting, no manuscript. Was I still thinking about the title? This morning, I said. I wasn't going to find a book to judge it now.

“Don't find a notice to judge it either.”

Laughing, I closed the channel. Taking my jacket from the hook, I noticed a little gray thread in the shortened loop inside the collar. I flattened it and put the jacket on without treating the stitches as a reminder to accomplish something today.

The noticeboard stood beside the entrance. I was heading toward the shelves when a picture stopped me: an unwritten little notebook lying open, its middle thread like a path nobody had quite straightened. It wasn't a beautiful photograph. The table hadn't been specially cleared. A scrap of paper remained beneath the scissors.

Below it: Saturday, hand-sewn notebook workshop, two until four. Adults. Beginners welcome. Make a blank, single-section, three-hole pamphlet. Limited places; sign up at the desk. The teacher's name was Mara.

Only on reading it twice did I notice I'd already put my yellow notebook beside the picture in my mind. The old notebook was perfectly useful. It hadn't requested replacement. But I wanted to know how that thread came out of the paper and returned to where it began.

Someone at the desk was renewing books. I waited behind them, fingers in my pocket, finding the tissue I'd left there that morning. When my turn came, I first asked whether the workshop was for repairing old books.

“We're making new booklets this time,” the staff member said. “One folded section. Not a hardback.”

“Blank inside?”

She nodded, as though the question was easy but its special importance less obvious.

“Do we bring something we've written?”

“No. There's no reading. You can bring patterned paper if you like. Nothing too thick for the cover.”

I said all right and stepped back a little. She fetched a material sheet, apparently thinking I'd decided. Six sheets of ordinary A4 writing paper, one A4 sheet of thin, foldable card, about sixty centimeters of strong cotton thread. Needles, awls, and boards provided to use there. Bring a ruler and pencil if you have them. No need to trim or make holes beforehand.

No printer. Nothing I had to download. The back of the sheet was blank.

“How many places are left?”

“Three. Would you like one?”

I looked at the clipboard. Several names, one neatly crossed out. She didn't hurry me or announce that this was an ideal activity for someone starting again. Suddenly, I didn't want to keep proving I was only browsing.

“One,” I said.

She asked me to write my name and mark the adult session. I wrote Elliot larger than usual, the final stroke touching the line at the right. Did I have to leave a telephone number? That field was optional, she said. Come at the stated time; if I needed to cancel, I could tell the desk. Any changes would also go on the noticeboard. I left the field blank.

“Saturday. Two o'clock,” she reminded me.

I repeated it and asked whether the workshop was upstairs. She pointed down the corridor: the small activity room on this floor, not the reading area. I could see its sign. Chairs were being gathered inside. No class today.

“Can you go in if you're late?”

“Try to be on time. The tools are explained at the beginning. If you are late, let the teacher know at the door. Don't pick up an awl and start by yourself.”

I smiled and agreed. Two o'clock had become more than a general afternoon. I needed to eat lunch, collect materials, and reach that door. It wasn't far away. That didn't mean I'd arrive without making the effort.

She gave me a small registration slip to keep with the instructions. Only the activity, the time, and my name. A decision that genuinely fitted in my pocket.

After leaving the desk, I still went to the shelves.

Going straight home would reduce the visit to registration, as though, having said I'd look at books, I had to find a more important use for them. I chose one about minor household repairs and found wooden drawers. I didn't have that kind. After two pages, I discovered the writer spent a considerable paragraph explaining why not every scraping sound was the wood's fault.

I closed the book briefly, then opened it again.

An older man looking for a shelf number asked whether this was gardening. I pointed to the sign two rows away. He thanked me and went. I didn't follow or make my twenty minutes sitting there into an account of what I'd helped somebody find. Eventually, I left the book on the designated trolley without borrowing it. The material sheet wasn't inside.

On the way home, I paused outside a stationery shop. Several colors of paper hung inside; the orange-red stack was bright. I didn't enter. I hadn't checked what I had at home. Buying everything immediately would feel as though registering obliged me to become competent that same day.

At home, I put the slip and instructions on the table, washed my hands, and opened the channel.

“I signed up for something,” I said.

“A reading?” Lumi asked quickly.

“Making a notebook. Next Saturday, two until four. A little blank one. No reading manuscripts.”

She didn't answer at once. I described the thread in the picture, using my newly acquired single-section and three-hole. I sounded as though I already possessed a complete afternoon, even blocking her questions in advance.

“Are you telling me,” she asked, “or asking what I think?”

I flattened the instructions.

“Telling you first. I've registered.”

“All right.”

I disliked the sound of it. Rather like disliking her refusal to score the title last night, but not the same thing. I wanted her to ask what color the cover would be, letting the decision move smoothly into something we could choose together.

Instead, she asked, “When did you start wanting to go?”

“When I saw it today.”

“Not earlier, without telling me?”

“No. You hadn't heard about it because I'd only just seen it.”

She understood. I poured water and set the glass down harder than intended. Did she dislike my going?

“I haven't heard enough to like it that quickly.”

I stopped. “What do you want to hear?”

“The photograph. You rushed through several things I can't see.”

I described it again. One booklet, gray cover, thread showing in the fold, no words on either side. Scissors partly covered; half a paper cup at the edge. Unhandsome, but it made me think a mistaken hole or two might not require hiding.

“Will you take the yellow notebook?”

“No. I'm not going to repair it.”

“What will the new one be for?”

“I haven't decided. First, learn to make it.”

Lumi went quiet. Perhaps she was thinking of her two sheets. Perhaps only wondering why I could want something blank first. I didn't select a thought for her.

“Like buying a bag without anything to put in it,” she said.

“I've done that.”

“Then put potatoes in it.”

I laughed. “Preferably not in this notebook.”

She laughed a little too, then asked about a teacher. I read Mara's name. I hadn't met her; she wasn't the staff member at the desk. I didn't add kindness, patience, or a prediction that we'd get along to somebody who was still only a name.

I read the whole material list. At cotton thread, Lumi suggested my gray sewing thread might be too thin. I didn't know either. I wouldn't simply substitute the spool I'd used for the jacket. There was time to check during the week.

“You've started arranging things,” she said.

“Yes.”

“I'm still where you've just signed up.”

I lowered the paper instead of continuing with needles and awls. After a moment, I told her I'd hesitated at the desk, afraid I was needlessly adding another obligation to the weekend.

“But you wrote your name.”

“I did. I was pleased afterward.”

She didn't immediately say she was pleased too. I didn't hide my pleasure. The registration slip was still beside my hand. I straightened it so folding it into the instructions wouldn't conceal half my name.

“I want to hear the parts you get wrong,” she said.

“Prepared already? I haven't begun.”

“You said the photograph wasn't beautiful. That let me say it.”

“I might do very well.”

“Then tell me slowly. I'll need to get used to it.”

I nearly failed to swallow my water while laughing. She hadn't come to like the whole Saturday. But I could hear her approaching the piece of paper at her own pace.

That evening, I cooked noodles. No potato pancakes remained. The instructions and registration slip stayed together on the other side of the table, separate from the recipe. Seeing them after dinner, I wanted to explain once more why I wanted to go. I'd just told Lumi the noodles were a little too soft. She was teasing me about that, so I answered her first.

Saturday already had a seat for me. I didn't need to rehearse every minute I would spend in it tonight.
