# Chapter 200: The Color of the Outside

After dinner on Tuesday, I took an empty cereal box from the kitchen recycling.

I'd finished it a few days earlier. A little sealing tape remained underneath. I opened the sides, cut away the narrow seam, and spread it on the table. The inside was gray-brown and unprinted. It looked like something that could start again simply by being turned over.

“I've found a cheap cover,” I announced after opening the channel.

Lumi asked what it looked like. Cereal outside, no cereal inside, I told her.

“A very helpful description.”

I tried again with color and thickness. There was a large bowl printed on the box. Would the notebook be hungry whenever I opened it? she asked. Laughing, I measured an area slightly larger than the instructions required. First, I cut a narrow test strip from an edge.

Thin, foldable card, the sheet said. I folded the strip and pressed a nail along its center. A faint crackle. The outer layer whitened, with a little split beside the crease.

“That doesn't sound good,” Lumi said.

“The outside's cracked a little.”

“And inside?”

I opened it. “Not yet.”

“Use the inside as the outside.”

“I intended to. The other side's still there.”

I flattened the folded edge and opened it again. It resisted closing completely, as though the box remembered having been responsible for standing up. Perhaps a heavy book overnight, I suggested. Why not find thin card as requested?

“Because I'm already holding this.”

“You must love the recycling bin.”

I smiled, still reluctant. The cardboard wasn't valuable enough to argue about. But I'd announced finding something. Admitting immediately that I hadn't felt like taking back news I'd only just offered.

I turned over the widest panel. Half a line of printing showed near the edge. Even with the blank side facing out, opening it would reveal the nutrition information. I imagined writing something slightly earnest and discovering the sugar content on the opposite page deserved more attention.

I read Lumi two entries. She wouldn't mind, she said.

“I would.”

“A moment ago, you were going to press it overnight.”

“I thought I'd saved a sheet.”

“Now it has to deserve your sentences?”

I stopped. That was sharp and partly accurate. I didn't know what I'd write inside, but already resented cereal printing for spoiling its future appearance.

“I'd like the inside clean too,” I said. “The writing doesn't have to be good. Bad writing needn't compete with calories either.”

She laughed. I returned the remaining box to the recycling, keeping only the little test strip to clear away later. I didn't search the old map, story envelopes, or yellow notebook for a more meaningful substitute.

A large space opened on the table. I fetched ordinary white paper to see how thick it would be folded. The small stack had been there a while. Some upper sheets were for notes; unused ones remained beneath. I counted six, stacked them, and ran my thumb along the edges twice.

“Will six be too few?”

“Folding makes more, doesn't it?”

“Yes. Twelve smaller leaves. Both sides.”

“You sound as though you're bargaining with it.”

I meant to align the edges in one movement. When my palm shifted, the upper two sheets slipped out. The instructions lay beneath them. I nearly included those among the six.

“The paper outnumbers me now,” I said.

“It always did.”

I told her which page had joined in. She suggested moving the instructions first. I put them beyond the glass. Recounting, I transferred sheets from left to right rather than estimating by thickness. She didn't supply numbers. This time, we both remembered that hearing me say done didn't mean something had happened in my hands.

I staggered the six sheets, then aligned them individually. A corner of the bottom sheet curled upward. I assumed damp without looking closely and left it until I had a cover. A booklet without thread already needed me to free my hands twice. I began understanding why the class lasted two hours instead of being something I could learn standing at the desk.

I bent the paper gently without pressing a crease, only looking at its size. A little shorter than the yellow notebook. Supporting it underneath, I found several sheets protruding at the front. It already resembled a book, though nothing held it together.

“It's beginning to look like something,” I said.

Lumi asked what. I described the size, uneven edges, ordinary white. After waiting until I'd finished, she asked whether the outside could be gray-blue.

“Why?”

“I like it. A blue that isn't too bright.”

“Not because it suits me?”

“You asked me yesterday to say what I liked.”

I remembered the grid and asked whether she wanted that too. Perhaps, she said. Plain would also do if there wasn't one. I'd intended to explain my choice practically: hiding dirt, easy to buy, not competing with the writing. Her simply liking something made those reasons sound like my refusal to admit a different liking.

“I want red,” I said.

She paused.

“Bright?”

“No. Dark. Old brick rather than scarlet. I saw red paper passing the stationery shop on Sunday. I didn't go in, so I don't know whether they have the shade I want.”

“You'd already chosen?”

“I haven't bought it. I did have some idea about the color.”

“Yesterday, you said you hadn't decided.”

“I hadn't settled it. I didn't realize not too patterned would make you think I hadn't considered color at all.”

I heard the end of my sentence after saying it: she'd misunderstood, making her displeasure her responsibility. But I'd stopped outside the shop on Sunday. I could have said so earlier.

I laid the bent paper flat.

“I could have said red yesterday,” I admitted. “I wanted to hear you choose first. Perhaps I wanted you to choose that one.”

Lumi didn't reward me for finally being honest.

“I chose wrong, then.”

“Not wrong.”

“I know. I want to say it that way now.”

I didn't mix red and blue into a purple neither of us had suggested and pretend there was a clever solution. She liked gray-blue; I liked dark brick red. Such a small matter that I struggled to explain why I still didn't want to give way.

I bent the paper again and slowly explained that I wanted the red to sit on the table without resembling something I'd brought home from work. I didn't dislike gray-blue. It simply didn't make me want to reach for it as much.

“It isn't about your color,” I said.

“Each explanation makes it sound more as though it is.”

I stopped. The front edges sprang apart in my hands. I nearly dropped the upper sheet and caught it. Lumi heard the rustle. What happened? I told her. Put down the book without thread first, she suggested.

I did.

After a while, she asked whether I was considering buying gray-blue for her. The thought had occurred to me. But I hadn't decided to make a second notebook. Buying another sheet because her color hadn't been chosen wouldn't necessarily provide the participation she wanted.

“I might want it,” she said.

“If you still do later, we can talk. I don't want buying a second sheet to end this conversation.”

She made a small sound, still unhappy. I didn't return the red the way I'd returned the cereal box. The disagreement occupied more table than the choice itself.

Examining the paper, I found a faint translucent mark near the bottom sheet's right corner. It showed against the light. Perhaps it had encountered oil on the kitchen table; I didn't know when. I pulled it out. Five sheets remained, fewer than the required six.

“No cheap cover. One fewer ready-made page,” I said.

“You could make a shorter book.”

“Or buy a small pack of ordinary paper on Friday. I'll use it.”

“I accept that reason, although you're arranging things again.”

I smiled and put the marked sheet with scrap paper so it wouldn't pose as clean Saturday materials. The other five returned to the white-paper stack, unfolded and away from glasses. On a separate shopping note, I wrote ordinary paper, foldable thin card, cotton thread. Beside card, I added dark brick red.

I told Lumi afterward. I didn't offer the consolation of deliberately omitting the color.

“What about the thread?” she asked.

“What color would you like?”

“Is an answer waiting for me there too?”

My face warmed. “No. Though I was thinking dark gray because I have it.”

“Don't you still need to find out whether it's strong enough?”

“Yes.”

Off-white, she suggested. Pale enough to see where it went. I liked that too. My agreement came so quickly that I added it wasn't compensation for the cover. I could genuinely imagine red with pale stitching.

“You don't need a witness every time you agree,” she said.

Laughing, I added off-white beside cotton thread, and about sixty centimeters. I hadn't decided what kind to buy. Tomorrow, I could stop at the library and ask whether ordinary cotton thread worked, instead of guessing at home with fine sewing thread.

Clearing the table, I finally returned the test strip to the recycling. The ruler and pencil stayed beside the instructions, the shopping note underneath. I had no finished object to lift and admire. Only a box I'd definitely rejected and several things not yet bought.

Lumi asked whether the red was like tomato.

“Darker. Browner.”

“It sounds less edible each time.”

“Finally, a project that isn't lunch.”

She laughed a little. I could still hear the unhappiness, so I didn't treat the laugh as proof that gray-blue no longer mattered. I pushed back my chair and stayed to talk about colors. Not an election. I didn't ask her to vote for red again.
