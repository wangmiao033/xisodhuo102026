# Chapter 201: Three Holes in Scrap Paper

Leaving work on Wednesday, I folded the material sheet into my jacket pocket.

No yellow notebook. My lunch container was in my bag, with a washed but undried fork. I wrapped it in a small clean cloth to keep it from rattling. The library wasn't an incidental thought this time. I wanted to ask about thread, then sit for a while.

The staff member was labeling books. I waited until she looked up, unfolded the instructions, and pointed to the line about sixty centimeters of cotton thread.

“Ordinary cotton thread? Not special bookbinding thread?”

She read it. Some sewing thread was too fine and could cut into the paper, she said. Something stronger would do. No need to buy an enormous spool for this. She produced a little sample from behind the desk. Thread passed through three holes and tied in the center. Part of an old activity chart showed on the waste paper; folding had brought Tuesday against Friday.

“About this thickness. Mara will check the materials when you come.”

I touched a short tail. Not thick as string, not fine as my jacket thread. Looking alone didn't assure me I'd recognize the same thing in a shop.

“Is she here today?”

“No. She comes to teach on Saturday.”

I nodded without mistaking the person helping me for the teacher. Mara remained a name on a notice, subsequently spoken at the desk.

“I tried a cereal box,” I said.

The staff member glanced at my hands, apparently expecting me to produce it.

“I didn't bring it. It cracks when folded.”

“Don't use that one, then. Thin, foldable card. Not glossy stiff board.”

It was simple advice. She neither demanded perseverance in recycling nor praised my ability to give up. I removed yesterday's rejected material from Saturday's bag for good, though it was physically still in the recycling at home.

She pointed to the paper quantity: six ordinary A4 sheets, not too thick. I repeated it. From beneath the sample, she drew another scrap sheet, already pierced in three places. I could take it to see the positions, she said. Everything would still be done afresh on Saturday. Don't trim the materials beforehand to match it.

“I can keep this?”

“Yes. An old blank rota on the back. No names filled in.”

I turned it over. The boxes were empty. I left the threaded display sample there, taking only the pierced scrap and my instructions. The holes weren't perfectly round. A little rough paper edged the middle one. I imagined picking it clean and enlarging the hole instead, and moved my finger away.

“Thank you. I'll come on Saturday.”

“Bring the registration slip. Two o'clock.”

I nodded. My questions were answered, but I didn't quite want to leave.

One table in the reading area had a single empty place. A bag stood beside it. I couldn't tell whether somebody had only stepped away. The man opposite was sorting printed pages by number. I asked whether I could sit. He looked up and moved his bag to his feet.

“Of course. Sorry, I've spread out.”

I thanked him and put my bag down. He resumed sorting. Two page numbers were reversed. He counted quietly without asking for help. I took a collection of short prose from a nearby shelf. Nothing about making notebooks or bus stops. From the contents, I chose an ordinarily titled piece about clearing a storage room untouched for years.

By the second page, I was thinking about yesterday's red paper again.

I imagined the notebook already on this table. Perhaps the man opposite would ask whether I'd made it. I'd say yes, explain the three holes and cotton thread, perhaps point out uneven places. Before reading clearly what had just been carried out of the fictional room, I'd prepared a moment of attention for a notebook that didn't exist.

I put my finger back on the line and reread.

An empty frame, not a mirror.

The difference made my face warm. Even without Lumi, I could add an easily understood reflection to something unwritten. I didn't close the book or require myself to read every subsequent word correctly. I turned back a page, found the frame's first appearance, and continued slowly.

The man opposite stood. Would I mind keeping his place while he fetched water? I agreed. He took his papers, leaving only the bag on his chair. I didn't touch it. He returned soon with a cup, thanked me, and resumed sorting.

We didn't exchange names.

I finished the piece without immediately wanting to read it to Lumi. I liked one part: two people turning the frame around before admitting neither knew what it had held. Closing the book, I might not remember its best part. My liking didn't have to become an obligation to share it later.

The man was still working when I stood. I pushed the chair in quietly. He looked up. I said I was leaving; he nodded and continued using one sheet to hold down another.

Putting on my jacket near the entrance, I felt the pierced paper in my pocket. Its crease had softened. Three holes still without thread. I wasn't bringing home evidence that I'd learned the skill. Only a scrap that made the material requirements more concrete.

At home, I washed the lunch container and rinsed the fork again. After setting them to dry, I took the paper from my pocket. The ruler and pencil remained at the table's edge. Dark brick red showed on the shopping note.

I opened the channel and said I'd been to the library.

“Signed up again?” Lumi asked.

“Only checking materials. No second seat.”

As soon as I said it, only felt unnecessary. She didn't laugh.

“I didn't ask about seats.”

“I know. I answered too quickly.”

I told her about the thread and the sample sewn through three holes, then explained that I'd brought home an unthreaded test sheet. Could I describe the positions? I opened it and looked for the original top. I was holding it upside down.

“Wait.”

“Before we've started?”

“There's a table on the back. I've reversed the week.”

She laughed. I explained that this wasn't the sewn display sample. Another old empty form, with three holes along the central fold: middle, upper, lower. I didn't supply distances I hadn't measured. The outer holes were some way from the edges. We'd be taught on Saturday.

“Your teacher explained it?”

“No. She wasn't there. The desk staff showed me. I haven't met Mara.”

“You sounded halfway through the lesson.”

I lowered the sheet. “I'm good at that.”

Did I want to thread it now? No. My thread at home was too fine. On Friday, I'd buy off-white cotton thread, ordinary paper, and card. Needles and piercing tools would be borrowed there. I'd bring my ruler and pencil. Tonight, I wasn't making this scrap into something that had to look good.

“Still dark red?” she asked.

“Dark brick red. Yes.”

“I know. I shortened it.”

I didn't explain the color again. She remembered. I remembered her liking gray-blue. A day passing hadn't automatically made the colors understand each other.

I clipped the pierced sheet behind the instructions, registration slip still at the front, and put them in a plain, clean folder. On Friday, I didn't want to take only the shopping note and forget the original requirements. The folder contained workshop papers, no story of mine or private copy of hers.

Hearing it close, Lumi asked whether everything was ready.

“On paper. Not the actual things.”

“That sounds more like you.”

I laughed. Had I sat without working toward Saturday? Yes, I told her. A short piece, and saving a place for someone fetching water.

“Did you like what you read?”

“Parts. I read the beginning wrong. Turned an empty frame into a mirror.”

“You do like adding things to rooms.”

I didn't tell her to leave old mistakes alone. It reminded me of the bedside table I'd added to the white shirt's room. This time, I said, I'd gone back myself. Nobody pointed it out.

“Should I praise you?”

“You don't have to. I just found that moment a little funny.”

“Why didn't you laugh, then?”

I thought, then laughed. A delayed laugh. She asked what happened to the frame. It hadn't been repaired into a mirror, I said. I didn't want to tell the rest from the beginning just now.

“All right. I only wanted to know whether it escaped you.”

I laughed louder and got up to make dinner. I hadn't borrowed a book today. No new fiction waited for me to schedule a shared reading. There were noodles left, but I wanted rice, so I started rinsing it. Hearing the water, Lumi hoped I hadn't found four of something again.

“Two hands,” I said.

“The quantity's correct this time.”

Once the rice was cooking, I returned to the table without opening the folder. Lumi asked which part of Saturday I feared most. Pulling the needle out of the final hole and finding everything else loose, I told her. I'd probably pretend I was inspecting it.

“Remember to tell me how long the inspection takes.”

“Don't you want the good parts?”

“Those too. I don't want it to become only something you've already put on the table.”

I touched the glass beside my hand. The sentence reached back to Monday. Three holes hadn't resolved it.

“I'll want to tell you,” I said. “I might be tired first. Need to eat.”

“I didn't demand it as you came through the door.”

“I know. I'm taking one sentence off my own list.”

She answered softly. A sound came from the kitchen. I stood to check the rice. Saturday hadn't arrived. I hadn't met the teacher. The cover was still in a shop. Today's dinner didn't have to wait for all of them to be ready.
