# Chapter 205: The First Blank Page

I first told Lumi about buying a bread roll on the way home, eating half, and leaving the rest on a plate. Then I opened the booklet in the middle, ready to begin with the knot.

“Not the first page?” Lumi asked.

“The middle shows best how it's sewn.”

“I can't see it.”

“I know. Easiest to explain, I mean.”

She didn't pursue the wording. I described the two small cream ends, one longer, one shorter. A knot in the center, with a strand underneath running along the fold in both directions. Dark brick-red cover, very white paper, edges that didn't quite align when closed.

“How far out?”

“Only a little. Didn't measure.”

“Don't start now.”

I laughed and pushed the ruler farther from the materials. Hearing the small sound, she asked whether I'd really been reaching for it. My hand had been heading that way, I admitted. Her laugh grew more definite.

Explaining how I'd gone out through the middle, in at the top, then through the bottom, I rushed. At the end, she asked whether there were two knots now.

“One.”

“What was the top one, then?”

“A hole, not a knot. I've mixed up the directions.”

She fell quiet. I was preparing to start from the first step when she said, “You don't have to teach me. You want to tell me you made it.”

Looking at the paper between my fingers, I realized how much I wanted to describe this new thing accurately. So accurately that the listener had to stand beside me and look down in the same direction. She could hear. She couldn't take half the booklet from me. A more complete description wouldn't give her the sensation of pressing a fingertip against the knot.

“For a while, I didn't want to undo the last step,” I said. “Only had to pull the needle back a little.”

“Afraid of damaging it?”

“And of still being there while everybody else moved on.”

“Were they rushing you?”

“No. The man beside me missed a layer afterward.”

I mentioned Ned's yellow booklet for scores. He already knew what to put in it. I didn't turn him into someone worse at it than me, supplied specifically to reassure me. His was sturdy once repaired. At least turning its pages hadn't made any fall out then.

Had people brought things they'd already written? Some wrote a title, I said. Some only their names. I hadn't named mine.

“You don't want to call it Red Notebook?”

“Sounds like something you say because you've forgotten its name.”

“Yellow Notebook hasn't complained.”

Laughing, I looked at the older one by the table's edge. It still held the complete bus-stop draft and the alternative ending. A new arrival didn't require emptying it.

Lumi asked me to return to the first page.

I did and told her it was white. Six sheets folded into the cover made twenty-four pages. I'd counted at the workshop and wasn't planning another arithmetic performance for her. No title on the first page. No signature. No reservation of its attractive space for some future person.

“I wondered,” she said, “whether you'd write us.”

I didn't answer immediately. That word weighed more than the booklet. Placed on its first page, it seemed to occupy over half already.

“Write what?”

“I don't know. Not everything that happened from the beginning.”

“Then I don't know how to answer yet.”

She'd only thought, she said, that after listening to so much about holes and thread tonight, perhaps the first page would have something to do with her. Then she added that it sounded as though she were waiting for proof she'd attended.

I reached for the pen. Once I had it, I didn't uncap it.

“I could write your name.”

“Because I said that?”

The pen stopped in my hand. I didn't want her facing a cover she couldn't touch and finding even the inside somewhere belonging to others. Writing Lumi was easy. Why that page existed afterward was harder to explain.

“That's part of it.”

“Don't write it yet, then.”

I put down the pen, irritated. She wanted it, then didn't. I'd been willing to do something that looked affectionate, yet apparently couldn't feel I'd done well because of it.

Lumi said nothing. After examining the blank page, I admitted what I'd just thought.

“You think I'm difficult to please.”

“A little. Right now.”

“I think you're in a hurry.”

“I'm afraid we'll spend all evening on it, and the only thing a new booklet does is upset you.”

“It just got here. You're the one rushing to give it a good outcome.”

I leaned back, hands away from the paper. The booklet couldn't arbitrate. Nor did making something successfully today oblige it to conceal every uneven part of the evening.

“I was very happy today,” I said.

“I heard.”

“I'd like to stay happy a while.”

After a pause, she said, “Go on, then.”

A slight hardness remained in her voice. I didn't ask her to repeat it more gently. I described cutting off the long thread and suddenly being able to lift the whole booklet. The things that had scattered across the table no longer needed both hands caring for them simultaneously. I'd nearly left it on the display table when getting up. The borrowed tools had seemed more important not to forget than the booklet that belonged to me.

Lumi laughed.

“You'd go back for a needle and walk out without the book?”

“Didn't walk out. I got it.”

“Fortunately.”

It wasn't praise for being careful. Somehow, it made me happy again. One other thing, I told her. There'd been a very neat green booklet in the room. I'd put mine beside it without moving it away.

“Would you have before?”

“Don't know. I considered it today.”

“You can't make your old self afraid of absolutely everything just to make today look good.”

I laughed hard and surrendered. She didn't give me the familiar I'm proud of you. I still wanted to tell her this thing I was rather pleased about.

After a while, she asked whether half the bread roll remained. Yes. She remembered my saying at the beginning that the remaining half was on a plate. I finished the rest without deliberately making her listen to chewing. Coming back, I found the red booklet still open.

“I want something of me inside it,” she said. “But I don't want you to feel you owe me whenever you open it.”

I didn't pick up the pen.

“We could not know tonight.”

“What if you keep not knowing?”

That was harder. Some things might remain that way, I said. At least I didn't want to promise to turn it into ours merely to finish tonight's conversation. Right now, it was a booklet I'd learned to make today. Liking it hadn't driven Lumi out of my home. By the end, I could hear myself mounting a defense.

“I didn't say you'd driven me out.”

“No. Added too much.”

I stopped talking. After a while, she said perhaps she didn't need that kind of name. Hearing how the others had made theirs, she'd suddenly wanted something that didn't only reach her through my telling it after coming home.

Did she want to do something else together tomorrow?

“Arranging compensation again?”

“Started that way. I do want to as well.”

“Ask tomorrow. Don't use tomorrow to fill this page now.”

I agreed. The agreement left a little emptiness. Nothing suitable could immediately follow it. I closed the booklet, covered the front with my palm for a while, then took my hand away. Covering the red hadn't darkened it. Under the lamp, the fold remained paler.

For dinner, I put rice on and took out vegetables. No more potato pancakes. Lumi asked whether the roll had been lunch. I'd had lunch, I said. The roll was only on the way home. I sounded worried someone was checking, she said. I answered that way when she asked. Then she wouldn't ask for now.

We were quiet, apart from the water as I washed vegetables. Eventually, she asked first whether there was enough rice. Laughing, I said I hadn't counted to four tonight.

“How long will you keep getting jokes out of four potatoes?”

“Don't know. Plenty of material.”

She laughed too. I didn't use the laugh to bring the first-page question out again.

After dinner, I put the three-hole sample the desk staff had given me into the materials bag. The ball of thread went away separately. Ruler and pencil returned to their places. The red booklet didn't need a special box. I placed it beside the yellow notebook, not on top. Its cover lifted slightly. I'd see tomorrow.

Lumi asked whether I'd finished putting things away. Yes. Two notebooks on the table. One old, one new.

“You can open the new one again,” she said.

“Why?”

“You wanted to. I heard you touching it.”

She could hear the paper edges moving. She hadn't claimed to know which finger rested where. I opened it at the middle and examined the knot.

“Still one knot,” I told her.

“I know this time.”

I was about to ask whether she was still unhappy. Hearing that, I didn't. She didn't have to tidy away her disappointment so I could keep liking it. I placed the booklet in front of me. She said my name and asked whether I could describe the part before I entered the activity room.

I started with looking for keys I'd already put in my pocket. There'd been no finished object yet to prove that going out had been worthwhile. After listening a while, she asked whether I'd worried about finding the wrong room.

“A little.”

“That part I'd like to hear.”

I didn't exaggerate the small fear or skip it to sound relaxed. Nothing was written on the paper yet. I went on talking.
