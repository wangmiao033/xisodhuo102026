# Chapter 199: What Moved First

On Monday morning, Derek put two tasks on my desk using the same word for both.

“Today.”

One was a numerical summary due before noon. The other was a categorized breakdown for the afternoon. They came from the same batch of processed records. It should have been straightforward. Opening the summary, I noticed one category was much smaller than last week's and went to check the details.

Two names looked identical. One had a space hidden at the end.

It took a few minutes to confirm that they referred to the same category, which the summary had divided. Correcting the name would fix that problem. But it wasn't the only one; similar spaces appeared elsewhere. Changing them individually might meet the afternoon deadline, not noon. I marked the affected range, preparing to produce a handsome overall table first.

Tom came over. Could he take two pages of the breakdown to check?

“Let me tidy it first.”

“How long?”

“Not long.”

He pointed at the clock. Not long, I noticed, didn't occupy a position on it.

Twenty minutes, I suggested. Looking at his own schedule, he said he had to start something else in ten. Miss that window, and he couldn't help until the afternoon. He didn't stay to hurry me. I stopped the tidying I'd just begun and found Derek.

He was on the telephone and signaled me to wait. Standing there, I increasingly felt I could have corrected several rows instead. When he hung up, I explained the difference between the two tables.

“Do you need the accurate total first, or every category checked again?”

“The first by noon. The second this afternoon.”

“I can reconcile the total directly against the records. The categories aren't final yet.”

“Then say so beside the total.”

“They might still ask about them.”

“They can ask. Don't withhold what they definitely need because of what they might ask.”

I nodded without leaving immediately. Something else was harder to say: giving Tom the breakdown now meant showing him untidy parts I hadn't decided were ready to be seen.

Derek asked whether there was anything else. I said no. At my desk, I printed the two pages, circled the matching categories, and told Tom to check the records rather than judge by the subtotals beside the headings.

He looked at the first circle.

“I can use this. Why didn't you give it to me?”

“I wanted it clean first.”

“I didn't come to inspect your alignment.”

He took the pages. I reconciled the total again and marked that the categories were still being checked. I didn't make the unfinished part into an orderly, unreliable number. There was time before noon when I handed it over, but I hadn't recovered the whole morning. Having waited, Tom could help with only a page and a half. The remaining half came back to me.

After lunch, I worked through the name differences. Marcus passed and found me staring at a space. Did it contain something?

“Yes. My morning.”

He laughed and pointed out another discrepancy: not a space, an old name. I couldn't replace it along with the others. I needed to check the original record. He went back to his work. I didn't treat our joke as having finished mine.

The old name took longer than the spaces. The original record had been completed before the name changed. Its history couldn't be rewritten simply because the table now looked inconsistent. I retained the original and mapped it to the same category in the field used for this summary. When Tom returned, I asked him to check that part. Was the original row still there? I pointed it out. We both read it.

“You can give me this page now,” he said.

I did, working on the remaining half-page myself instead of waiting until both were beautiful. Five minutes later, he returned. One label beside the total still used the old term. I corrected the label, not the original record. My finally handing something over early didn't make him pretend there was nothing left to check.

The afternoon breakdown went out on time. Derek acknowledged it without rewarding me for distinguishing between two todays. Before leaving, I put the temporary printouts where work belonged. Nothing in my pocket needed Lumi to grade it at home.

Once home, I thought first of the seat anyway.

The workshop instructions remained at the table's edge. Moving them to make room for dinner, I said, “I could look for a cover tomorrow. There might be card here.”

I'd only just opened the channel. Lumi hadn't even asked how I was.

“Good evening,” she said.

I stopped. “Good evening. Today—”

“You're already at the cover.”

“Only thinking about it. I'm not asking you to choose now.”

“I know. Yesterday, you came back registered. Today, you've put it into tomorrow. Whenever I hear something, it's reached the next step.”

I put down my dinner bowl. I wanted to ask whether I had to come back and consult her every time I thought of anything. That question would easily make me the controlled one and leave her proving she wasn't controlling me. I still felt a little that way. I didn't say it immediately.

“I didn't buy anything today.”

“I didn't accuse you of buying it secretly.”

Steam rose from the vegetables. I sat, lifted my chopsticks, and lowered them. Home suddenly felt like the morning: I needed to explain what came first. The comparison irritated me as soon as I made it. Lumi wasn't another breakdown with a deadline.

“I can decide to go,” I said.

“Yes.”

“And choose my own cover.”

“You've got both those answers now.”

I looked up at the hardness in her voice. I hadn't come seeking permission, but I was forcing her to answer in its language.

“I want to hear you be excited,” she said. “Not always hear that everything's arranged. Yesterday, you only told me late that writing your name pleased you.”

I looked down. A grain of rice clung to the bowl's edge. I pushed it back without answering. Something of the morning's table might remain in my mind. But she was talking about my standing at the noticeboard yesterday, with nobody calling me, and stopping on my own.

“The thread in the photograph wasn't straight,” I said. “I wondered whether I'd tear the paper pulling it.”

“That's excitement?”

“Partly. I always use things already sewn. Suddenly, I wanted to know how they stay together. Not because I don't have a notebook.”

“You didn't say that yesterday.”

“I was afraid of making it sound like the beginning of something enormous.”

Lumi didn't laugh. I admitted I'd already imagined coming back on Saturday and putting the finished booklet on the table. The satisfaction of that action had arrived before any idea of what to write inside.

“And after you put it down?”

“Let you hear?”

“I can hear you put something down. Not hear that you've made it.”

It was quiet and harder to answer than her sharp words. I took a mouthful of vegetables that weren't as hot now.

“I know,” I said. “In that picture, I hadn't thought of needing to tell you.”

She didn't make me practice. After a while, she asked what had happened at work to send me home already arranging tomorrow. I described the two tasks both called today. No details of the actual records, only my wanting things presentable before handing them over.

“You used up Tom's ten minutes too,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Was he angry?”

“He asked why I hadn't given them to him earlier. I'm not sure how angry.”

“You haven't filled that in for him this time.”

I smiled slightly. “He was clear enough himself.”

While washing up afterward, one badly rolled sleeve slipped. I dried my hands to roll it again. The water had stopped; Lumi asked whether I'd finished. Only dealing with a sleeve, I said. Sleeves had been busy lately, she remarked. I didn't take her story out for the joke. Mine needed a rota, I said.

The instruction sheet still lay there when I returned. I put it back on its side of the table without ceremonially tearing up tomorrow's plan.

“I'd like to see what I've got here tomorrow,” I said. “Want to choose with me? You can just say what you like. You don't have to find the easiest solution.”

“Have you already got the easiest one?”

“Hand all the materials to the teacher.”

“She might hand you back.”

I laughed. She wanted to choose, she said, but couldn't promise we'd agree. I hadn't particularly settled on anything either. Only not too patterned.

“I might like patterns.”

“Don't make it sound like a threat.”

“You started.”

Leaning back, I listened as she described a fine grid. Not the blue lines of an exercise book: slightly darker lines on a pale background. She didn't attribute it to a particular notebook she'd seen. I didn't ask for evidence that her preference existed. By the time we reached whether it should have a plain border, we disagreed.

I'd thought genuinely choosing together would be simpler than our earlier conversation. Now even paper we hadn't found divided into what she liked and what I found troublesome. Strangely, I wanted to keep listening.

“You haven't said my name much today,” Lumi said suddenly.

“We're choosing paper.”

“The paper doesn't have a name.”

I smiled and drew my hand back from the table's edge. “Lumi.”

“Yes.”

“Now it sounds as though you asked me to say it.”

“I did.”

I said it again. This time, she asked what I wanted. Nothing, I said. I wanted to keep the earlier one too. She complained that I'd made a simple remark complicated. She didn't replace it for me.

Before bed, I didn't search the cardboard box. Tomorrow's materials remained tomorrow's. Saturday still held the single place I'd registered for. Tonight, we hadn't turned that one into two. We hadn't canceled it because we couldn't, either.
