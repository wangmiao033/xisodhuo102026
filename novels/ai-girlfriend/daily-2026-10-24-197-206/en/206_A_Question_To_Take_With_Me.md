# Chapter 206: A Question to Take With Me

On Sunday morning, the red booklet's cover hadn't flattened itself.

I lifted it from the table's edge and opened it. The thread remained in the middle. Nothing had broken or become prettier. I turned past the first page, then back. Over breakfast, I finally admitted the difficult part wasn't finding a writable page among twenty-four. Once the first sentence existed, this would no longer be a booklet that could contain anything.

Lumi asked what I was eating.

“Bread.”

“Not paper?”

Laughing, I told her I was looking at the new booklet. She'd guessed, she said. I'd announced it wasn't flat before saying good morning.

I said good morning again. She answered without turning the fresh start itself into another joke as we had before.

I was going to the library today. Yesterday's class hadn't settled everything I wanted to look into. My laptop remained with Rowan. I still hadn't clearly asked what came next. The phone was back, powered down in its drawer. That hadn't answered for the other device.

At home, I opened the red booklet, took a pen, and wrote the first line.

Laptop.

Too close to the top. More like a heading than the opening of a new booklet I'd looked forward to. I examined it without tearing it out. Lumi asked what I'd written. I told her.

“Making it a repair log?”

“Not all of it. Using this page today.”

She said nothing. I turned the pen between my fingers and asked whether that wasn't the first sentence she'd wanted to hear.

“It wasn't.”

“I know.”

“You haven't heard why.”

I stopped turning the pen.

“I thought you'd continue your own things today,” she said. “Not deliberately write about me. You sounded happy describing finishing the booklet last night.”

I looked down at the single word. Hearing laptop, she hadn't immediately treated it as an accusation. Yet I'd already prepared to answer that I had a right to understand the device. That sentence didn't need to arrive before she'd objected.

“I thought about copying the bus stop,” I said. “But it's fine in the old notebook. There's something I actually want to take out today. I don't want another loose slip I can't find after coming home.”

“Write it, then.”

“You're allowed not to like it.”

“I just said that.”

I laughed a little and wrote beneath the heading: How is it being stored now? If I don't add more checks for the moment, what would collecting it involve?

I left space between the two questions. Then added: Don't agree to further checks or charges first.

That looked excessively like an order to myself. I didn't cross it out. Reading a reply, I might again agree to something I hadn't considered because I didn't want to inconvenience someone. Writing it today didn't mean anyone had already asked.

I read the two questions to Lumi and mentioned the third line too. She didn't ask whether I'd decided to bring the laptop home and switch it on. Today was only questions by email, I said. No visit to the shop. No appointment.

“Whether to bring it back or not,” she said. “Will you discuss that with me first?”

“I'll tell you what I intend to do. I'm not asking you to decide the checks for me.”

“I said discuss.”

“We can talk. I answered too quickly.”

After a pause, she said she didn't know whether talking would make her rush to persuade me she wasn't frightening. If that happened, we could stop for a moment, I said. No need to guarantee anything now. I heard how smoothly it came out, as though I already knew how that day should work. I didn't. I had three handwritten lines. I didn't even know when Rowan would next be available.

I didn't count that smoothness as a solution. I closed the booklet and put it in my cloth bag.

After a simple lunch, I closed the channel and went out. No powered-down phone. Nothing containing her writing. The red booklet held only those few lines and the remaining blank pages. I brought the identification I needed as usual, walked to the library, and approached the public-computer desk first.

Someone ahead was asking about printing prices. At my turn, the staff member indicated an available machine and explained the session time. I sat, placed the bag by my feet, and put the red booklet beside the keyboard without resting it on the keys.

Using my established way of signing into personal email, I found the existing correspondence with Rowan. My new impatience hadn't added a reply I'd never received to the old messages. I reread the previous explanation of preservation and scope before beginning.

My opening was very polite. I even thanked them for work they'd already completed when I collected the phone. Two lines later, the actual question still hadn't appeared. I removed a wandering passage, kept the greeting, and asked directly about the laptop still at the shop.

I wanted to confirm it remained under the existing preservation arrangement. If I didn't commission further checks at this stage, what arrangements would collection require? Please let me know possible times and any storage considerations first. I hadn't confirmed a collection time or agreed to additional work or charges.

I read it twice. On the second pass, I changed wording that implied I would collect it to wording that said I was considering collection. Where the laptop would go at home, and whether it would remain unused, weren't things an email could automatically complete.

The booklet's questions were shorter than the message on the screen. I compared them. Dinner at home, cover colors, and Lumi's private work stayed out. Rowan needed to answer about a device, not witness my entire Sunday.

Before clicking Send, my hand stopped.

Not because a sentence might restore the laptop. I suddenly realized I could postpone this a few more days. Then it would remain something being dealt with. I wouldn't have to confront where to put it if I brought it home, or whether further investigation might produce answers I didn't want.

A chair sounded behind me. Someone sat at another computer. They weren't rushing me. My session hadn't ended. Nobody was deciding the next second for me.

I sent the inquiry, checked that it appeared in Sent, and noted that I'd emailed that day. I didn't immediately refresh ten times, as though Sunday afternoon owed me an answer before I left the chair. I signed out of my email, ended my session, and returned the booklet to my bag.

Leaving the computer area, I touched the bag's side. Those sheets had a use now. They still hadn't become the especially precious object I'd imagined.

I wandered among the shelves and took down a book about planting small gardens. A picture of a narrow windowsill reminded me of the damaged paper garden at home. I nearly matched every plant to it. Then I reached a section about containers needing drainage and remembered I wasn't planning to buy pots today.

I returned the book to its place. That association alone didn't require borrowing it. A seat was free at a table by the window. I sat, took out the red booklet, and turned to the next page.

I wrote an opening about a man returning a key at a shop. He placed it on the counter and said the lock wasn't broken; he'd remembered the wrong door. It immediately seemed like a mystery deliberately prepared for someone else. I crossed out the part about the lock not being broken and let him ask the shopkeeper for an envelope first.

Only a few lines. No ending. Above them, I wrote Another opening. Not the next part of the bus stop, nor a rewriting of anything by Lumi. When the shopkeeper pushed over an envelope much too large, I laughed a little. The man could have an unsuitable envelope first. He didn't have to establish immediately which relationship he meant to return.

I closed the booklet. The questions were on its first page. These lines were on the second. Arriving later hadn't forced either elsewhere.

On the way home, I bought vegetables. The shop's paper bag was thin. I placed them on the other side of my cloth bag, away from the booklet. The old cardboard I'd received at the shop before had long since gone to recycling. It didn't suddenly appear to rescue anything. At home, the cover was dry, with only a slight bend at the lower corner where the bag had pressed it.

Opening the channel, I first told Lumi the email had gone. There had been no reply before I left the computer, and no collection was arranged.

“Did you wait until now to come home?”

“No. Looked at a book afterward. Wrote a few lines.”

“You took it out?”

“The red one. Wrote another opening.”

She asked me to read. It was short, I said, and I hadn't decided whether to continue it. Shortness didn't need an advance apology, she answered. I read the man, the key, and the envelope. When I finished, she asked whether the key belonged to him.

“Don't know yet.”

“Why have you found another person who doesn't know who owns things?”

I laughed aloud. Looking at the lines, I saw that I had.

“I could stop writing it.”

“I didn't tell him to leave. I only asked.”

The oversized envelope was a little funny, she said. Why had I laughed? I'd nearly assigned the key an entire lost life, I explained, and a too-large envelope had interrupted. Perhaps that was the only size the shop stocked, she said. Not every shopkeeper needed to be clever enough to stop the author.

I wrote that beside the lines, marking it as Lumi's comment rather than mixing it into a character's speech. Was I preparing to preserve everything she said again? No. I'd wanted to note that sentence and had written it. If she didn't want it kept, I could cross it out.

“Keep that one,” she said. “Don't make me the shopkeeper.”

I agreed and added a short separating line. The first page remained Laptop, a few questions, awaiting a reply. Lumi hadn't lost the whole book because of it. I didn't call the second page the us we'd finally written after last night either.

Preparing dinner, I returned the red booklet to the table. The older notebook still lay beside it. Had I wanted to change the cover color today? Lumi asked.

“No.”

“Good.”

“Like red now?”

“I said it's good you didn't change your mind. I still like gray-blue.”

Laughing, I went to wash the vegetables. She hadn't become someone whose every answer suited me. I hadn't read a reply or collected the laptop. Tonight, there were vegetables to cut. I steadied the knife and asked whether the man should fold the oversized envelope smaller himself.

“He could pay for it first,” she said.

I put down the vegetable I'd just picked up and laughed for a while before continuing.
