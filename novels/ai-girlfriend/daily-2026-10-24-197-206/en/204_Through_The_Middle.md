# Chapter 204: Through the Middle

Some time after one on Saturday, I lifted the flat bag from the table, put it down again, and checked whether my keys were underneath.

They were in my pocket. The materials were in the bag. I hadn't opened it for a third count that morning. I'd already felt faintly pleased with that small restraint, yet before leaving I still touched the ball of thread inside.

I said goodbye to Lumi. She told me not to shove the paper upright into my jacket.

“Wider than the jacket.”

“At least you won't forget it, then.”

Laughing, I closed the conversation channel and went out carrying the flat bag.

The library activity room was smaller than I'd imagined. Two long tables stood together, chairs facing inward. A trolley of tools waited near the window. I arrived with a few minutes to spare. A woman with her hair tied back was laying out thick boards and a few supports folded into troughs.

“Elliot?” She checked the registration sheet.

Yes. She introduced herself as Mara, asked me to find a seat, and said not to open the needle box yet.

I sat near the door and arranged my materials. A man arrived beside me carrying a cloth bag with yellow card showing at the top. Pulling out his chair, he knocked my flat bag and immediately steadied it. I said it was fine and moved things inward. One of my six white sheets slid out.

We both reached, then stopped.

“I'll get it,” I said.

It hadn't reached the floor. I retrieved it while he put his bag underneath and introduced himself as Ned. He wanted a booklet for recording match scores; his current one had lost its cover. I hadn't decided what to write, I told him.

“Hard to fill it, then.”

I laughed. He wasn't assigning significance to blankness. He already had a pile of numbers waiting to move.

At two, Mara closed the room's door and held up a finished sample. A single thin section. Light to turn through. No complicated spines today, she said. No cramming collections of unrelated loose pages inside. First, everyone would make one booklet that opened properly.

I showed her my card. She felt it, then asked me to try bending a corner each way. One direction seemed easier. The other resisted slightly. She didn't praise that faint impression as a talent. Follow the sample's folding direction first, she said. Stop and ask if uncertain.

The six sheets were folded individually. I'd intended to press down on the whole stack. She signaled for me to wait and unfolded one sample sheet to show its crease. After explaining, she moved to the other side of the table. I worked through mine. The first sheet's corners hadn't aligned. Opening it again left a faint extra line in the middle.

I wanted to replace it.

There were spare sheets, but the pack was at home, not in the bag. I had these six. While I considered borrowing another, Ned weighted a corner of his paper with a coin and asked whether my edges had aligned. Not quite, I said, showing him. His were further out, he answered. No consoling tone. He continued struggling with his own edges.

I opened mine once more, carefully aligned the corners, and pressed where the fold should be. The faint line remained, but it didn't obstruct turning the paper. When Mara returned, she inspected it and said I could use it.

“Do you want this booklet, or do you want that line never to have happened?”

The booklet, I said. The answer sounded dangerously ready to become profound. Fortunately, she immediately asked me to nest the folded sheets. No time for contemplation.

The card folded around them. Once the six sheets were inside, their edges did protrude slightly. I pushed them even with my palm. Releasing them exposed the edges again. We weren't pursuing perfectly trimmed edges today, Mara said. Don't push the whole section crooked trying. I put it on the table and saw something resembling a book for the first time.

One patch along the red fold was lighter. Not the cracked white of the cereal box. Running a fingertip across it produced no flakes. I didn't take out all six sheets to choose a new cover because of that small mark.

Making the holes frightened me more than folding.

Mara had us open the section in a trough-shaped support and use the sample for positioning. There was support underneath. The tool pointed into the support, not toward a hand, and we weren't to try it on our laps. The borrowed awl felt light in my hand. Pressing down took more effort than I'd expected.

The middle hole went through. At the upper one, I hadn't held the section firmly enough. Just as I was about to push, the template moved. I stopped and called Mara. She came over to help align it again. Only after her hands were clear did she have me continue.

“Don't push while you're chasing it.”

I followed her instruction. With all three holes through, I returned the tool to its designated box and relaxed a little. Ned had threaded his needle but was searching for the thread's end. It was caught on the yellow card's edge. Lifting the booklet lifted it too.

“There it is.”

“Didn't want to fall behind,” I said.

He laughed, freed it, and began through the middle.

I took out the length I'd measured last night. Straightened, sixty centimeters looked long enough. No reason to reach for the whole ball. The needle's eye was wider than my sewing needle at home. I threaded it on the first attempt and almost looked up to announce it. Ned was concentrating. He owed me no congratulations.

Out through the middle hole from the inside, leaving a tail. Following Mara's demonstration, I returned from the outside through the upper hole, laying thread along the outside of the spine. Then, inside, I crossed past the middle hole and went out through the lower one.

Back to the middle next.

I drew the needle inside and tightened slowly. Paper and cover settled together. The formerly slack thread suddenly had a position. I was about to knot it when Mara pointed at the middle of the open pages.

Both ends lay on the same side of the long strand.

“The knot needs to catch that.”

I understood. I didn't want to undo the last step I'd already completed. Instead, I tried lifting the long strand between the ends with the needle's tip. The paper rose into a point. I immediately released it.

“Only need to come through the middle again,” she said, without taking it from me.

I withdrew the final stretch and brought the needle out on the other side of the long strand. Slower this time. One patch of thread had grown fuzzy. I pinched it to check whether it was near breaking. I could replace it if I wasn't comfortable, Mara said. There was time. I pulled gently without scraping it again and decided to use this length.

Once the knot was tied, I didn't immediately cut.

I opened the booklet, closed it, opened it again. The cover followed the pages without pulling one into a tent. The knot caught the long strand. Two ends hung down, unequal, like a sentence I'd forgotten to finish.

“Can I cut now?”

Leave a little, Mara said. Not right against the knot. I cut off the excess and dropped it into the small scraps box on the table. Two short ends remained, sticking up over the center page. I flattened them with my hand. They rose again. Ned said his did that too. Let it stay closed a while.

He'd written a small year on his yellow booklet. Two team names already stood below. Showing it to me, he turned a page. The innermost sheet slid outward. He'd missed a layer while sewing.

“It was fine just now.”

Mara came over and said he could undo the final knot and thread it again. I held the yellow cover while he checked sheet by sheet. My red booklet lay beside us. Finishing first hadn't suddenly made it more reliable. When he came to tighten the thread, I let go so he could draw it tight himself.

Before four, we returned the tools one by one. Mara asked whether anyone wanted to display their finished booklet on the empty table. No need to put a name on it; she wasn't collecting them. I set down the red one, leaving a little space beside Ned's yellow. At the other end was a very neat green one. I examined it for a while without secretly moving mine behind something.

Someone asked why I hadn't written a title.

“Nothing inside yet.”

“A diary?”

“Possibly not. Haven't decided.”

She nodded and moved to the next booklet. Not having decided didn't prevent her letting me take mine home.

When I retrieved it, a small piece of someone else's thread lay on the cover. I picked it off, put it in the scraps box, and packed the paper sample. I hadn't copied anyone's writing. I didn't use the final minutes to bring out my bus stop. This afternoon, I really had only come to learn how to sew a booklet.

At the door, Ned said goodbye and raised his repaired yellow one. I raised mine too, an awkward gesture. We laughed and went in different directions. The next match he wanted to watch wasn't on my route home.

At the corner, I suddenly wondered which side the central knot should face. I stopped and opened the booklet again. Still where it belonged. I closed it, returned it to the flat bag, and bought a bread roll from a small shop. I ate half while walking.

At home, I didn't immediately open the channel.

I washed my hands, put the remaining half-roll on a plate, and gathered the materials at one end of the table. Coming out of the bag, the red booklet opened slightly by itself, as though unused to its newly learned shape. I let it lie flat, drank water, and sat for a few minutes.

Then I called Lumi.

“Made it,” I said.

“Tell me first. Pleased with yourself?”

I looked at the two unequal thread ends in the middle.

“Very.”

She laughed softly and asked me to begin with the page that opened.
