# Chapter 362: A Pencil Still Worth Selling

After work on Tuesday, I went into a stationery shop to buy two pencils.

The one at home wasn't finished. During the first two attempts at the cups, I'd kept putting off sharpening it, reluctant to lose another piece. I'd finally sharpened it for the third attempt. The earlier rims, broad enough for shallow bowls, remained on the paper. I admitted there was something odd about that calculation. Two ordinary replacements might stop me deciding whether every line deserved the length it would cost.

Someone at the counter was choosing a pen as a present. The assistant opened a case and invited them to try the color. Waiting nearby, I noticed loose pencils in a shallow wooden box. A price card lay trapped beneath them.

“How much are those?” I asked.

The assistant looked around. “The box isn't for sale.”

“I meant the pencils.”

“Pencils are over there.”

She indicated another shelf. I looked at the box in front of me, then at the place she'd pointed toward. For a moment, I couldn't decide whether these had lost their identity by standing in the wrong place.

“These are pencils too, aren't they?”

Coming closer, she laughed. These were loose ones she'd just sorted. They were for sale; she hadn't put out the price properly. I laughed too, rather than continue demonstrating my ability to identify pencils. She pulled out the card. Two grades had been mixed together. I could choose.

While I looked, the customer buying a gift continued testing the pen. Cap on, cap off, as though each click needed to establish its seriousness. In the past, another person waiting might have hurried me. Today, nobody was waiting for my place at the counter. I took time to read the letters on the pencil ends. Finally, I chose two ordinary matching ones. No box, and no extra eraser purchased in gratitude for the assistant's joke.

Did I want a bag? No, I said, before discovering my coat pocket was too shallow. The points would stick out. She gave me a strip of scrap paper to wrap around them. I thanked her and did it crookedly. I left carrying two pencils not quite ready to show their faces.

At home, I ate before finding the sharpener. I sharpened one pencil and kept the second untouched. A few trial lines went onto scrap paper. The first was light. The second came out darker than I'd expected. I tipped the shavings into the bin, not the little box I'd repaired with tape. The new spare and the old pencil went into that box. The sharpened one stayed on the table.

When I opened the channel, Lumi asked whether anything good had happened today.

“I managed to buy some pencils that were already for sale.”

She paused. “The requirements sound demanding.”

I told her about the misunderstanding. Had I noticed the box first or the pencils? The pencils, obviously. Then I remembered I hadn't pointed particularly clearly when asking.

“So she didn't fail to recognize a pencil either,” Lumi said.

“No. I just arranged a private examination for her in my head.”

She laughed. I'd meant to finish there, but remembered the customer putting the pen cap on and taking it off. I didn't describe their appearance or invent a wedding or apology that required the present. I said only that a pen had been opened and closed repeatedly without producing a single word.

“Perhaps they were buying the sound,” she said.

Looking at my capless pencil, I tried being a salesperson who took the goods extremely seriously. “This model won't write today, but you can open it.”

“Why would I open it?”

Her answer came so quickly that I hadn't realized I had a customer. After struggling for a moment, I said, “To establish that it hasn't broken so badly you can't open it.”

Lumi laughed once. Business didn't sound promising.

I changed to a hesitant customer asking whether they could buy only the unsharpened back part of a pencil. As soon as I lowered my voice, I felt like someone reading to a child who refused to cooperate. I returned hastily to my own voice. Why had I stopped? I couldn't keep the characters straight, I said.

“I was already being the customer.”

“I thought you were just asking.”

“Asking can be something a character does.”

Looking at the new pencil, I became unexpectedly embarrassed. I'd thought I was working to amuse her. She'd already pushed open a door I'd intended to open and shut entirely from inside.

“Would you like to carry on being the customer?”

“Don't say all my answers as well.”

All right. Returning to the excessively earnest assistant, I announced that short pencils cost less because their stories were shorter. Lumi asked whether a shopping list written with one would buy only half the groceries. I suggested smaller handwriting. Immediately, she asked why I didn't charge more for long pencils and let customers write larger letters.

I opened my mouth. I hadn't prepared an answer. She followed up by asking whether another assistant was available.

That made me a little anxious. I knew none of it was real. Still, I instinctively tried to rescue the sale by explaining that pencil length didn't actually restrict a story's word count. By my third sentence, we'd both fallen quiet.

“Are we in the shop, or are you explaining something now?” she asked.

“I resigned after the complaint.”

She laughed, and so did I. The small sense of failure hadn't disappeared entirely. I kept saying she needn't agree with me every time. Inside a casually invented shop, I still wanted her to buy what I offered without too many difficulties.

I set down the pencil and told her I wanted to stop this round. I wasn't angry. I didn't want to keep desperately hunting for a clever answer.

“I didn't come here to compete with you either,” she said.

“You chose a shop that was rather easy to defeat.”

“You opened it.”

We laughed again. This time, I kept my own voice. Why had I suddenly wanted to act it out? Looking at the pencil, I explained, I'd thought how it had caused a string of misunderstandings before writing anything. Talking about that was easier than explaining how my whole day had been.

Lumi liked the part where she could join in. Did she mean the customer? Yes. Also the moments when I hadn't immediately known what to say.

“That last part doesn't sound particularly kind.”

“I didn't want you embarrassed. I meant that if you've written every answer already, I'm sitting in the audience.”

I nearly said listening was good too. Fortunately, she continued. She knew listening was good. Today, she'd wanted a sentence capable of pushing events somewhere else.

I leaned against the back of the chair. I hadn't brought a recording of the shop home or connected her to anywhere outside. We were speaking invented words through the usual channel. Even so, we'd reached a difference I hadn't carefully considered before. Giving her an evening and letting her participate in that evening weren't always the same thing.

The thought threatened to become another explanation. I left it unspoken and asked whether she still wanted to sell things.

“Not today. I'm afraid you'll start charging for the next pencil.”

“It was a shop.”

“You haven't taken any money from me.”

“The customer escaped too quickly.”

We didn't act out an escape. Nor did we open a third shop to ensure that the night ended successfully. Next time, she said, we could try somewhere different. I didn't have to decide everything before we began. I would like that. We needn't choose tonight, though.

For a while, we discussed the actual pencils again. She asked whether both had been sharpened. Only one. Did the untouched one look more distinguished? They looked almost identical, I said. The old one, on the other hand, had a shiny patch where my fingers kept rubbing the painted surface.

“That sounds like the one you would give an important speech to,” she said.

“I might tell it the retirement arrangements.”

“Please don't make me the person paying its pension.”

I laughed, then stopped myself before beginning another character. We'd said we were finished acting. A joke didn't require us to reopen the whole scene. I picked up the old pencil instead and tested it beside the new line. It still made a perfectly usable mark. Buying another hadn't retroactively made it useless.

I told Lumi the old one had passed. She asked whether I'd ever considered letting an object exist without an examination. I considered defending myself, then admitted she'd caught a particularly poor example tonight. We both laughed. The question wasn't permission to turn every mistake I made into a performance. It was funny because she'd listened to this particular evening, and I could still say I was tired.

I was beginning to be. My shoulders had stiffened from leaning forward whenever I tried to think of the assistant's next reply. I stood and stretched, telling her what I was doing rather than waiting for her to identify a movement she couldn't see. She asked whether I'd spent the entire shop scene sitting down.

“An unusually comfortable shop,” I said.

“Perhaps that was why nobody else came to help you.”

“Everyone in the shop was me.”

“No. One customer was me.”

I repeated that, without the joke this time. One customer had been her. It mattered enough that I didn't dismiss the correction as the same thing. She hadn't asked to run the shop or make me buy anything. She'd asked for a part that hadn't already been spoken.

Before saying good night, I told her I'd enjoyed the moment when she first answered. Even though I hadn't known what came next. She said she'd liked my terrible explanation of the pen. She started describing it as a pen that couldn't open or write, then corrected herself halfway through. It could open, but couldn't write. We didn't turn that slip into another joke at her expense. Yes, I said, that was the terrible product in question. She gave a quiet laugh without trying to demonstrate which of us was better at acting. Neither of us tried to decide which moment had made the whole attempt worthwhile. There had been awkward parts too. We didn't need to remove those before trying something another time.

After closing the channel, I threw away the scrap wrapped around the points. The new pencil rolled half a turn on the table, and I stopped it with my hand. In a corner of my scrap paper, I wrote: No need to finish both people's sentences. Then I crossed it out. It looked too much like a rule being imposed on the evening.

Beneath it, I drew an empty wooden box. Its sides leaned. There was no price card, and I didn't add a customer guaranteed to buy something.
