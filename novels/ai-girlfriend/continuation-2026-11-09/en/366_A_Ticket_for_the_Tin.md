# Chapter 366: A Ticket for the Tin

After lunch on Saturday, I washed the dishes and sat at the table.

I hadn't prepared anything special that morning. I'd washed my clothes, cleared part of the tabletop, and returned the borrowed drawing manual to my own stack of books. Lumi's envelopes hadn't been moved to make room for a stage. The four prompts remained at the front of my schedule book, the street drawing at the back. Taking one out wouldn't mix them together.

I checked the time. This seemed suitable, so I opened the channel and called her. Lumi answered, asking whether I was already outside the window.

“I'm arriving seated today.”

“The passenger service has improved.”

Yesterday we'd suggested ten minutes or so. I still wanted a short attempt, I said, not a performance lasting until dark. She laughed. Ticket clerks finished work too. I put my water within reach and asked whether she still wanted the same roles.

We could try them first, she said. Afterward, she'd like to swap.

“You want to buy a ticket?”

“I want to know whether you'd reserve a window seat for yourself behind the counter.”

I laughed without immediately promising perfect fairness. We began with a simple greeting. As the traveler, I asked for a ticket to the next station. She had one. I waited for conditions. She only asked whether I wanted a seat.

“Yes.”

“Here you are, then.”

I stared. Had I forgotten to pay? Laughing, I said there hadn't been enough dialogue. She wasn't charging by the word. At last, we laughed at a transaction containing no difficulty. It barely qualified as a scene. I didn't demand that she take back the ticket and trap me again.

Before swapping, she asked whether she could bring something of her own. Yes. I didn't require dimensions in advance. She cleared her throat without deliberately making her voice more delicate.

“Hello. I'd like to go one stop. This has to come with me.”

“What is it?”

“A cake tin.”

Looking at my empty table, I immediately imagined a round container. How big? Very big. Her arms were tired already. I almost heard that tiredness as hers before remembering the person she was playing. Lumi wasn't holding an object. I didn't need to invent hands for her.

“You can use the luggage rack.”

“I don't want it above my head.”

“Why?”

“Every time I think of it falling, I'll have to look up.”

I laughed once and suggested the floor by her feet. Someone might kick it there. I nearly complained that she wanted too much, then remembered my own questions about windows, standing, and sunshine two days ago. What would she prefer?

“A seat at the side. I can keep it on my lap.”

“You'll have to buy a very wide lap.”

The sentence escaped before I considered it. Lumi laughed, temporarily losing the character. I laughed too, looking at my own knees. The station had become a deeply unlikely furniture shop.

We didn't start over. Once she'd finished laughing, she asked whether there was a seat where she wouldn't be squeezed against other people. I'd check, I said, before realizing I had nothing to check. The prompt sheet couldn't manufacture seats on a fictional train.

“Still there?” she asked in her ordinary voice.

“Yes. I made checking sound too real.”

I could simply think of an answer, she said. After considering, I told the character there was an aisle seat. The person beside her would need to get out. I couldn't guarantee nobody would touch the tin along the way.

“I'll hold it and stand when they need to pass.”

This time, I was surprised. She'd accepted an imperfect place instead of defeating me with an even larger tin. Words came more easily afterward. I reminded her to get off at the next station.

“I'm going there to return the tin.”

“You won't eat the cake?”

“There isn't a cake inside.”

I stopped, then laughed so hard that a complete sentence broke apart. I'd already imagined an entire birthday: someone waiting beyond the station, perhaps something she'd made inside the tin. She'd said cake tin. I'd supplied all the cake.

“Why take such care if it's empty?”

“It's borrowed. If I dent it, the lid might not fit.”

“Single or return?” I asked next.

“I won't have the tin when I come back.”

“You'll still be the same person.”

“So an ordinary ticket will do?”

At last, I had an answer ready. It had always been an ordinary ticket, I said. We hadn't established a separate class for containers. Why had I discussed her lap so seriously, then? That was an additional incorrect service offered by this window. She laughed. Her customer still wanted to know whether she'd have to return to the same counter afterward.

I almost supplied opening hours for the entire imaginary station. Instead, I said she'd need to see which window was open. I couldn't guarantee it would be mine.

“That's fine. I'll ask for myself.”

Hearing that, I restrained the clerk's urge to explain everything in advance. She wasn't someone who required me to escort the tin throughout the journey. She didn't necessarily represent anyone either. A traveler disliked the high luggage rack and didn't want to damage a borrowed object. I let her choose a single ticket without keeping her at the counter because the return journey wasn't arranged.

Would the ticket need a name? Our fictional station hadn't asked for one, I said. She had prepared a name anyway: Person Holding the Tin.

“What about the tin?”

“No introduction necessary. It doesn't give interviews.”

I laughed aloud and nearly offered a comparison involving her private manuscripts. In the end, I didn't. A joke could remain attached to a tin. We didn't have to dismantle every one to uncover some injured part of our actual lives. Lumi waited for me to finish laughing, then prompted me gently. The thing in her arms was still very large. Could she please have the ticket before the conversation required her to put it down? The customer had a practical reason for being there, and I was making her wait while I enjoyed myself behind the counter.

I considered asking who'd lent it, then stopped. A clerk didn't need to register the family connections of every container. I gave her the ticket and reminded her not to block the aisle with the tin when she stood.

“Thank you. Can you eat your lunch now?”

“Yes. Mine doesn't need a ticket.”

Laughing, she asked what I was eating. Soup, I said, then remembered that lunch had belonged to her clerk in the earlier round. In my own voice, I asked whether I could borrow that detail without making it another arrangement imposed on her.

“Yes. It's our station today. You aren't stealing my actual lunch.”

Returning to the clerk, I announced that I was leaving for my soup. Her traveler departed with the empty tin. We stopped there, without a railway accident or a compulsory happy reunion with the person who'd lent it.

I checked the time. More had passed than I'd thought, but we were still within the small stretch we wanted to spend playing. Should we finish there? Yes. She'd laughed enough too. That was how she described the conversation. I didn't infer a body that needed to catch its breath.

Leaning back, I realized I'd been pressing my toes against a table leg. Relaxing made the area below my knees ache. When I told Lumi, she said apparently I'd been the person carrying the tin. Laughing, I admitted I'd been more tense than the character.

What had I liked best? The empty tin. I didn't begin by telling her how accomplished she was. I admitted that cake tin had made me imagine a birthday. I'd almost offered the passenger congratulations. Lumi laughed. Her person wanted to return something borrowed, not attend a celebration I'd arranged at short notice.

“Would he be disappointed?” she asked.

“The clerk?”

“You.”

After thinking, I admitted a little. I'd liked the picture in my head. Hearing that the tin was empty had made me more curious about how this person would sit down with it, though. She liked that I hadn't insisted there must still be a slice inside after she'd said otherwise.

I looked at the four prompts. They no longer described everything we'd performed. We'd exchanged roles; an empty tin had joined the objectives. That didn't make the paper so useless it needed throwing away. Could I write the role swap and empty cake tin on the reverse? Yes, Lumi said. But don't transcribe every sentence from memory and call it a complete script she'd already agreed to.

No full text, I promised. On the back, I wrote: roles swapped; empty tin; return at next stop. Then I added a provisional title, A Ticket for the Tin, and read it to her.

“The tin didn't buy a separate ticket.”

“Not an accurate title?”

“Not entirely. It sounds funny, though. Leave it for now.”

I wrote working title beside it rather than charge the tin an extra fare in the interests of precision. The sheet returned to the front of my schedule book, separate from her doorbell draft. Her six sentences hadn't acquired a seventh through our little scene. Her other private works didn't need to turn up and congratulate us either.

I nearly suggested doing this every week. The words stopped at my mouth. Not because I feared inviting her. We'd happened to want this today. We needn't decide the fate of every Saturday immediately. Could I ask her again if I thought of something else?

“Yes. I might think of something first too.”

“Then ask me.”

She agreed, then said directly that she'd liked hearing me genuinely laugh today. I didn't promise to laugh every time or ask her to explain how she distinguished real from false. She meant this afternoon. A moment earlier, I'd laughed too hard to finish speaking. She had heard that.

“I liked it too,” I said. “Especially when you didn't give me the cake.”

It had sold out, she said, laughing. The small disappointment finally had the ordinary weight of a joke. Nobody needed to compensate me for it.

After closing the channel, I stood for water. Much of the afternoon remained. I could continue the borrowed book or hang the washing first. I chose the washing. Hangers knocked together; I separated them and opened the damp sleeves one by one. One cuff had folded inward. I turned it out with my fingers before hanging it up. Clean clothes still needed to dry; the afternoon still contained ordinary things to do. Being happy a moment earlier was no reason to leave them wet. No empty tin waited at the door. There was no train to catch. Still, I felt I'd visited a small place that hadn't existed a little while ago, then returned in my own time.
