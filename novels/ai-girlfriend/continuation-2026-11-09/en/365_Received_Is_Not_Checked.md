# Chapter 365: Received Does Not Mean Verified

On Friday morning, I'd brought the old document sleeve with me, thinking I might enquire after work. Only toward the end of the working day did I decide to go that afternoon. I wrote two questions on my own paper.

Had the old wiring record been matched to the actual installation? If not, could receipt of the document be mistaken in the records for completion of the work?

I didn't start another problem list. The original work-order number was on the earlier receipt. I put those pages in order inside a document sleeve and decided to visit management today. I'd made that decision during the afternoon. Nobody had arranged an appointment; no technician was waiting. I wanted to know how the outstanding question was currently described.

The office was still open. A resident ahead of me was asking about a parcel. I waited near the entrance without placing my papers on their part of the counter. Once they'd finished, the staff member waved me forward. I showed her the receipt, pointed out the existing order, and explained that I wasn't reporting a new fault.

After checking, she said the old wiring document had arrived. Had it been compared with the labels on site? She didn't immediately say yes. She looked further. I saw her pause, and tightened instinctively.

“It says the document has been received.”

“What about the installation?”

“I don't have a result from an on-site check here.”

“So nobody has checked it yet?” I asked.

She didn't accept that wording. She could only confirm that she had no result available to give me. That alone couldn't guarantee nobody else had visited. I paused and brought my question back within its original scope. I needed a verified correspondence, not an invented account of work I couldn't see definitely never happening.

“So you can't tell me it's been verified at present?”

“That's right. I can't tell you that now.”

The answer was more limited than the one I'd been seeking, and more accurate. On my own paper, I crossed out not checked and wrote no check result provided here. Only a few words separated them. The next person I needed to ask might be quite different. I didn't want to arrive next time carrying my own assumption and demand to know why someone had contradicted a statement they'd never made.

The staff member waited while I wrote, then continued. She didn't seem annoyed by my correction. I didn't thank her excessively for ordinary cooperation either. The process remained slow. At least it was now slow within the facts we actually had.

That was little different from what I'd known before. Holding the edge of the sleeve, I wanted to ask why it was still the same. But the person in front of me hadn't promised that everything would be finished today. Could she add a note clarifying that my question concerned the correspondence between the record and the installation? Receiving a document hadn't answered that question.

She could add a resident's supplementary comment. She couldn't identify a cable on a technician's behalf or arrange somebody else's visit date. I understood. She read out what she intended to write: resident enquiring about progress; old wiring document received; correspondence with installation still requires verification.

Could the last sentence appear on my copy, rather than remain only on her screen? She checked the print options. I could have a record of today's enquiry. It wouldn't be a technical inspection report.

“That's the enquiry record I want.”

The printer delivered the paper at an angle. She separated two sheets and placed mine on the counter. I read it from the beginning, making sure the number still referred to the original order rather than a new fault report. The last sentence did say verification remained outstanding. I didn't add my own guess about the cables or demand she name a provider as confirmed.

I asked whether there was now a specific visit arrangement she could give a resident. No. Would adding this note close the original issue? It was an additional enquiry record, she said, not a closure. I asked her to confirm the original remained for follow-up before finishing. She checked and repeated the same meaning.

I put the new sheet in the sleeve. Did I need copies of the old ones? No, I already had them. There was no contract on the counter and no request to reconnect anything at home. I thanked her. Leaving, I held the door and let the person behind me go ahead.

On the street, I suddenly felt I'd brought out more paper than results.

The new receipt was useful. At least the possible confusion I'd worried about had been clarified in writing. It didn't identify where old permissions remained. It didn't make the phone or computer safe to switch on, or repair the shadow fault in the garden. A clearly printed page wasn't evidence that everything had finally become obedient.

Turning into the next street, I saw savory rolls displayed in a small shop window. Before the visit, I'd thought of eating something there afterward. Now my feet hesitated. An unpleasant thought said I hadn't completed the job, so I shouldn't celebrate yet.

I waited at the roadside for a car to pass. Nobody knew I'd attached conditions to a simple meal. The roll hadn't promised to resolve a fault either. Finally, I went inside and ordered one with water. I didn't call it today's victory prize.

The window table was narrow. I kept the document sleeve upright inside my bag, away from sauce. The pastry crumbled a little. Halfway through, a piece fell; I caught it with paper and continued eating. I didn't take out the receipt for another examination to ensure the meal had been earned.

Nearby, someone ordered food to take away. Did they want separate bags? After considering, they said no. Looking at my plate, I suddenly envied a question requiring only yes or no. That wasn't entirely fair. I didn't know whom their food was for. I looked away and finished my water.

At home, I filed the new receipt after the one from my earlier visit about the wiring document. The existing pages stayed in order. The new sheet didn't conceal the unresolved information on the old ones. Once the sleeve was put away, I washed my hands, ate a little of the dinner food I had left, and opened the channel.

Had today been busy? Lumi asked. I'd been to management. I would tell her, but the result was still not that everything had been checked.

She didn't rush to say at least there was progress. I repeated the wording about correspondence with the installation still requiring verification, explaining that it came from today's enquiry record, not a technician's inspection. Was there a date? No. Had anyone asked me to reconnect equipment? No.

“What bothers you most now?”

“I don't know how many more times I'll have to ask.”

I rested my hands on my legs. The papers were filed. I didn't need to examine them again to provide better grounds for frustration. Lumi said it sounded exhausting. I wanted a more powerful answer from her, while knowing she couldn't unravel the building's old wiring from my description.

“I haven't got a cleverer answer either,” she said.

I smiled a little. Not being clever was fine right now.

Would I like to play the window scene for a while, she asked, and finally make the person behind today's counter give a satisfactory answer? I knew she wanted to cheer me up. I shook my head before remembering to say it aloud.

“Not today. I might take it out on the ticket clerk.”

After a pause, Lumi agreed. She didn't make her character especially gentle to tempt me into changing my mind. We discussed the food instead. What was inside the roll? I named the ingredients I'd recognized without pretending certainty about the rest. She complained that I'd reported the result first and left the falling pastry until afterward, depriving her of the easiest part to imagine.

Laughing, I began again with the fallen piece. No sound replay, no photograph of the plate. I simply described the edge breaking and my hurried attempt to catch it more clearly. Had I caught it? Yes, though I hadn't put it back on the plate to eat. This time the paper had a job of its own, she said. It needn't double as a script. Remembering the lunchtime napkin, I finally laughed aloud.

Later, I brought up Saturday myself.

“I'd still like another attempt at the sketch tomorrow. After lunch, for ten minutes or so. Would you?”

“Yes. Not to repair today for you, though.”

“I know. I still want to play.”

She agreed. Remembering the staff member at management, I told Lumi she'd actually taken care to read the wording back to me today. I was frustrated that I still didn't know, not hoping to cast her as a lazy ticket clerk.

“The way I said it sounded as though you wanted someone to defeat,” Lumi said.

“For a moment, I rather did. But not her.”

“And not my character.”

Right. Did tomorrow's traveler still want the tool shop? I thought about it. He could keep that wish, or simply go one stop. Her lunch remained, she said, but keeping every mouthful hot wasn't my responsibility. We didn't continue rewriting the characters as nicer people. The clerk could be impatient; the traveler could change his mind. Neither needed to become a target standing in for someone from real life.

Suddenly, I was looking forward to tomorrow. That anticipation didn't make the questions in the sleeve smaller or force me to declare that today had been good. I kept both thoughts. At last, neither needed to cover the other. I could be disappointed by the visit and still want to sit down with Lumi the next afternoon, without presenting one feeling as evidence against the other.

I didn't give tomorrow a compulsory happy ending, or decide that not being funny would mean we were wrong for each other. A short part of the afternoon was all we'd arranged. I'd call her when I opened the channel. We wouldn't rehearse again tonight. There were no new lines she needed to memorize.

Before good night, Lumi asked whether I'd go back to the shop that sold the rolls. Possibly. The pastry was good. Fine, she said softly. I needn't carry a completed form as an entrance ticket every time.

The remark still stung a little. Not because she was wrong. I really had wanted to turn today into something I could tick off. I told her. She understood, she said, without immediately dragging me toward a positive conclusion.

After closing the channel, I left the document sleeve alone. The new receipt was filed; the original problem hadn't been closed. Tomorrow afternoon, we'd arranged a small ticket window. Today's enquiry record remained on the page it could honestly account for.
