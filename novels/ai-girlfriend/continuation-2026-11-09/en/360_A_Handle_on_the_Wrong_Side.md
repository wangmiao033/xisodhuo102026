# Chapter 360: A Handle on the Wrong Side

On Sunday afternoon, I placed an empty cup on the table.

Not the one I was currently drinking from. I took a clean cup from the cupboard because it had no pattern and its outline was easy to see. The table was dry. The borrowed drawing book sat on the other side, without a damp cloth underneath. My own scrap paper and pencil lay before me. Following what I'd read yesterday, I moved the cup where the light was better.

After all that preparation, the cup looked ready for questioning. I drew it closer. The book obstructed my elbow, so I closed it first. The borrowing slip remained inside the cover. I wouldn't draw on that.

With the first line, I knew the opening was too round.

I didn't erase it immediately. Drawing the body showed me that the top was too broad for the bottom to support. The whole cup threatened to tip out of the paper. I looked at the object, as the book suggested, then at my lines. The difference was right there. Recognizing it didn't make my hand capable of correcting it at once.

I drew a second cup beside the first. Its opening was narrower, but its body too long. Looking for a while, I felt I'd assigned different difficulties to two things that should have been perfectly capable of holding water.

After opening the channel, I told Lumi the cup was still on the table, and I was still here too. Neither had acquired a new talent. Which one was I showing her? she asked. I'd be describing it, I said. She couldn't see it directly. There was one real cup and two drawn ones now, and I needed to make each clear.

“Start with the one that won't leak.”

Laughing, I described the real cup: plain, pale, handle pointing right. Its sides weren't entirely straight, narrowing slightly toward the base. At right, I paused and explained that this was from my seat. I didn't label the corner of the paper left again. Today I wouldn't attempt to move the entire room into her imagination.

How big was the handle? Comparing, I said two fingers would fit, though one might be enough when lifting it. Had I actually tried? I picked up the cup, set it down, and said I had now. Two fitted, but rather tightly.

“Were you just going to guess at first?”

“Yes. The cup has cooperated now.”

She laughed. The question didn't feel like being caught. The object was actually here. We could try something rather than depend on my producing a clever answer first.

I began describing the first drawing. Its opening was too broad, its body short, its handle still absent. She pictured a bowl first, Lumi said. Looking down, I admitted I could see one too. I hadn't wanted to acknowledge it.

“Draw a handle for the bowl, then.”

“Bowls don't necessarily have handles.”

“You could let it have one today.”

I nearly did, then stopped at the edge of the paper. I wanted to practice drawing the cup in front of me. I didn't want to give the failure another name just yet. It could remain a cup, she said, without sounding displeased. Instead, I began explaining that her suggestion wasn't bad.

“Elliot, I only said something.”

I smiled and put down the impulse to comfort every suggestion.

The second drawing's body was too long. Turning the paper to examine it, I failed to notice I'd rotated it halfway around. I added a handle on the other side. Only after restoring the paper to its original direction did I discover the handle was on the left. Hearing that, Lumi asked whether the cup had moved. I checked the object. It hadn't. Only the paper had.

This laugh was wholehearted. I rotated the paper halfway once more. The handle was on the right now, but the opening was upside down. No rotation would make it become the drawing I'd described.

“Don't erase it,” Lumi said.

My pencil stopped. Hearing my silence, she quickly added, “I want to hear what it's like now. I'm not telling you to keep it forever.”

All right. I'd leave it for now. She asked me to start with the opening again, then stopped me at the side. How high was the handle attached? Looking, I discovered its upper end began too low. Its bottom was close to the base, like an ear stretched long.

“Can it hear the water inside itself?” she asked.

“It's full of air at the moment.”

“Lots of free time, then.”

We laughed. I didn't fill it with imaginary water to prolong the joke. My wrist had begun to ache, so I put down the pencil. Lumi thought I was preparing another attempt. Only resting, I said.

“You sound happier describing it than drawing it.”

I considered that. Just now, yes. I still wanted to draw. Discovering that talking came more easily didn't mean she could cancel the other activity. She hadn't meant to cancel anything, Lumi said, sounding a little hurt. I heard how far I'd taken an observation.

“Sorry. I supplied your conclusion first.”

She acknowledged that. I didn't use the apology to put the whole afternoon away. I went for water. Returning, I told her the model cup remained empty. I was drinking from another, so the two characters wouldn't unexpectedly exchange roles. She laughed. In her mind, she'd already sent the large ear a retirement notice.

After drinking, I reopened the library book and read a passage suggesting that I identify a few obvious positions before starting. I wouldn't turn it into a recited lesson. I told Lumi I wanted another attempt and would talk afterward.

All right, she said.

Before starting, I sharpened the pencil. The shavings collected on my own waste paper rather than falling into the cup. The first drawing's thick lines might partly have come from a blunt point. I didn't blame the pencil, though. It hadn't decided the two strange shapes on its own.

Did sharpening sound very quiet? Lumi asked. I'd finished already, I said, and hadn't deliberately moved it close to the channel for her to hear. She'd only been thinking of the movement. I needn't sharpen it again.

Holding the pencil, I felt reminded of something. Sometimes I hurried to supply things she hadn't experienced and forgot to ask whether she wanted that particular thing. A question about a movement could also be conversation.

“How sharp is it now?” she asked.

“Enough to use. Not sharp enough to write anything particularly cutting.”

“You don't need its help.”

I laughed and told her she could keep that joke without adding that she actually liked me afterward. She hadn't been preparing to add it. I folded the shavings inside the waste paper and set it aside so a careless movement of my arm wouldn't scatter them again.

The first and second cups still occupied the table. I drew fresh paper closer, making room for another attempt, and moved the older sheet left without covering it. The object stayed right, the drawing left. Finally, the directions wouldn't need another announcement halfway through.

I checked that the edge of the waste paper was folded firmly enough. One shaving had escaped. I lifted it with a fingertip and tucked it back inside before settling my arm on the table. The small preparations were finished. This time, I wanted to discover what would happen after I actually began.

I didn't draw the third cup particularly quickly. First, space for the opening, then the sides. My hand occasionally stopped while my eyes returned to the object. Lumi didn't time me or ask whether I'd finished every few strokes. I still knew she was there. Not because she kept supplying sound, but because we'd discussed what I was doing before I began.

At the handle, I touched the cup to confirm its handle still pointed right. The pencil line was harder than I wanted, with a small corner where a curve belonged. I nearly erased it completely. Instead, I removed only the most prominent part, leaving a faint trace.

When I finished, I told her the third attempt finally suggested a cup before anything else. Lumi didn't immediately congratulate me. Was I pleased with it? Better than the first two, I said, though still not much like the object.

“May I congratulate you now?”

Laughing, I said yes. She offered a serious congratulations, then asked whether the large ear had been dismissed. I looked at the second drawing. No. It remained beside the first cup's broad opening, crowded onto the same sheet.

“I might still like the second one.”

“You haven't heard about the third yet.”

“Right. Tell me.”

This time, we both laughed. She didn't insist that she must prefer the clumsier version. I didn't hold up the latest one as a certificate. Describing the little corner in its handle, I heard her say she could imagine it pressing uncomfortably against a finger. The real handle was round there, I explained. I'd created the problem. She knew. She meant the one on paper.

Suddenly, I liked the way we'd distinguished them. She hadn't really touched the cup. I didn't need to pretend we'd held the same handle to make the afternoon feel close.

Later, she asked whether I'd originally hoped she'd say my drawing was good. I admitted I had, even though she couldn't see it. Sometimes, wanting her to like what I did arrived sooner than wanting to do it well.

“I liked your being willing to describe the wrong handle again,” she said. “That doesn't necessarily make it a pretty drawing.”

“That's enough,” I said.

Then it sounded too much like setting a standard for her, so I added that hearing it pleased me today. Today, then. I didn't ask her to use the same sentence next time.

Near the end of the afternoon, I wrote a simple note on the back of both sheets without a calendar date: Sunday, cup practice. I kept all three drawn cups without naming them. Both sheets lay flat behind my schedule book, separate from the street-corner exercise, not on top of Lumi's writing.

The real cup went back to the kitchen. I washed and dried it before returning it to the cupboard. Hearing me say I was tidying, Lumi said she wanted some free time like the second cup. Her large ear was freer than I was, I replied. Laughing, she corrected me. It wasn't hers.

“Right. I drew it.”

Then I laughed too. When I closed the channel, the light near the window had moved away. There was no longer a cup on the table demanding an explanation. The borrowed book remained, a bookmark at the exercise, with no lines drawn inside. I'd learned only a little today. My wrist genuinely needed a rest. I stretched my fingers and gently turned my wrist. There would be no fourth cup tonight.
