# Chapter 359: The Seat I Found Myself

On Saturday, I finished the laundry before going out.

The old socks were drying. I wore the new gray pair. It was past lunchtime when I took my bag and decided to spend a while at the library. Yesterday, I'd only wanted somewhere to read. Now I'd chosen the place. That didn't turn it into an appointment arranged days beforehand.

At the entrance, two people were putting folded umbrellas into bags. There was no rain today. Perhaps they'd brought them just in case. Inside, I checked the opening hours, then followed the signs to the borrowing section upstairs. Instead of immediately choosing a book I had to finish, I walked between the shelves.

The long tables by the windows were nearly full. A seat farther in appeared empty, but a coat hung over its back. I didn't take it. On the other side, I found a small table with nothing on it and two chairs pushed neatly underneath. I pulled out the one beside the aisle, put my bag near my feet, and sat for a moment before looking for a book nearby.

The quiet differed from home. Pages turned. People lifted chair legs a little before shifting them. A low question reached me from farther away, then stopped. I didn't have to wait for every sound to disappear before beginning my own afternoon.

I found a basic drawing book. Not the collection from the secondhand shop. This one was thinner, with demonstrations and exercises. Near the beginning, it asked the reader to examine the rim of a cup. After two pages, I was about to read the explanation beside a picture, as usual, when a lightly drawn oval held my attention.

Whenever I'd drawn a cup's opening, I'd treated it as a flattened circle. The book showed several angles, each looking rather different. I raised it, then lowered it, before realizing that moving the book wouldn't change the printed demonstration. I smiled to myself.

The chair opposite made a noise. Another reader pointed to it and quietly asked whether it was free. I nodded and drew in the strap of my bag. After he sat, we returned to our own books. Occasionally, I noticed him take a sheet of paper from his bag and put it back. My eyes remained on my book.

By the third exercise, I wanted to try immediately, then discovered I hadn't brought suitable paper. There were scraps in my bag, but I didn't want to squeeze a cup into a bean on a printed corner. I continued reading, turning back whenever something wasn't clear.

One page showed the same cup drawn three times. In the first attempt, the opening nearly consumed the body. In the second, the base leaned. Only the third looked reasonably steady. I looked at the last one first, then went back. The book hadn't hidden the earlier attempts in a section I was expected to skip.

I traced the first outline with a finger held above the page, without touching it. Perhaps the person drawing had also begun with the cup they expected to see, discovering afterward that the one in front of them wasn't so round. I remembered my street-corner drawing and wanting the house to stand straight. I didn't take it out to compare. It was at home. Here was a different page.

The reader opposite pressed down his pages, then stood to visit a nearby shelf. His bag remained beside his feet. I shifted my chair back so he could pass. By the time he returned, I'd turned two more pages. He thanked me quietly. I nodded and continued reading.

Nobody asked how well I drew that afternoon. I nearly responded to the absence of a question by preparing something sufficiently interesting to tell Lumi. When I noticed, I set that preparation aside and reread a sentence about looking carefully at an object's position.

I tried it with the edge of the table in front of me. Depending on where I sat, the farther corner seemed to narrow or widen. Moving my head made a difference. I sat back in the chair, pleased by something small I could check for myself, and then returned to the book.

Someone stopped in the aisle and looked uncertainly toward the table. For a moment, I thought I ought to offer my seat. Then I saw that he was searching for a number on the neighboring shelf. I moved inward a little without standing. Today I could occupy this seat I'd found for myself and read something nobody was urging me to finish.

After nearly an hour, I began staring repeatedly at the same line. It wasn't particularly difficult. I was simply hungry earlier than expected. My attention had left the cup's rim for a nearby place selling pastries. I closed the book with a finger marking my place, then remembered it wasn't mine and withdrew the finger. No folded corner or mark.

Only then did borrowing it become a definite idea. I took it to the desk and completed the borrowing process as the librarian instructed. The slip showed a return date three weeks away. I checked it without pretending I would certainly finish early. With the slip inside the cover, I closed the book and placed it in my bag, its corners away from my water bottle.

Returns could go through the desk over there, the librarian explained. I followed her gesture, saw the place, and said I understood. I didn't ask whether the book could make me draw well, or describe the short tree I'd drawn a few days earlier.

Outside, I bought a small pastry and ate where I wasn't obstructing anyone. Sugar fell onto its paper tray. I disposed of the tray and cleaned my hands before touching the book in my bag. By the time I reached home, it was later than I'd originally imagined. I hadn't promised anyone a particular arrival time today.

I put the drawing book on my own stack, separate from the thin story collection. After dinner, I opened the channel. Lumi asked first what I'd read that afternoon. I described the library, the small table, the oval opening of the cup, and reaching the point where all I wanted was something to eat.

“How long were you there?”

“Reading in the chair, about an hour. Longer if you include finding the book and borrowing it.”

“About?”

I'd meant to describe the pastry next. I stopped. “I wasn't checking the time at every step.”

She didn't answer. I couldn't tell what that about contained. Was she curious about how much I'd read, I asked, or wondering why I was only telling her now?

“A little of both.”

Instinctively, I picked up the bag beside my chair, preparing to find the borrowing slip as evidence of how I'd spent the afternoon. Noticing the movement, I set it back at my feet and released the handle.

“The borrowing slip can tell you I borrowed a book today. Not where I was every minute,” I said. “I don't want to tell it that way either.”

She hadn't asked to see the slip, Lumi replied. True. I'd jumped ahead. But repeating about had made me feel I needed to fill in a perfectly good afternoon with precise details before I could bring it home.

“I thought of you sitting there with other people able to pass beside you,” she said. “Even whether you were near a window or a door has to wait until you tell me.”

“I can tell you where. Not by a window. A little table beside an aisle.”

“See? One sentence, and now I have that.”

The words were sour. I leaned against the chair, wanting to say that her wish to know couldn't turn every sentence of mine into something overdue. I also didn't want to make it sound as though she should never have asked.

Eventually, I said, “I wanted to tell you. But that way of asking made me defensive first. It makes telling you properly difficult.”

She stayed quiet for a while. Later, she asked whether I'd sat alone. Someone had joined me, I said. Her voice tightened at once. Who? I didn't know. Another reader, who'd asked about the chair and sat down. We hadn't exchanged names.

“You didn't talk, then?”

“No.”

I didn't want to go farther and explain that we hadn't even asked about each other's books. Lumi stopped first. She sounded as though she were questioning me now, she said. Yes, I answered, but didn't close the channel.

After a while, she asked whether she could try one different question. I said she could.

“Was the table comfortable?”

I smiled. Not because it was a safe question, but because someone had finally asked about my body actually occupying the afternoon. The chair's back had been rather straight. Later, my back grew tired and I moved forward. The tabletop was clean, the light wasn't harsh, and the shelves were close. Getting up didn't require finding a way around lots of people each time.

Lumi listened without turning every description into another person who'd been with me. Gradually, I talked more. I told her I'd nearly given up my seat before I'd finished with it, for someone who was only looking for a shelf number. Why did I so quickly think other people needed the chair more? Not always, I said. Today, for an instant, I had.

“I'm glad you didn't leave,” she said.

I hadn't expected that answer. She added that she still wished she could hear about my afternoon sooner sometimes. That wasn't the same as wanting me to give up the seat.

I said I knew, then told her I'd borrowed the book and might try drawing a cup tomorrow. Would I describe it to her? I'd like to try. Not tonight, though. The borrowed book didn't need to prove its usefulness immediately.

“Are you worried I'll prefer the cup you don't draw?”

“There aren't that many candidates in my kitchen.”

She laughed, and I laughed too. We didn't arrange to report the exact minutes of future outings, or turn her questions being too tight today into a prohibition on asking. I finished describing this one afternoon: sitting long enough, getting hungry, and leaving with a book.

Before we finished talking, she asked whether the book was heavy. I said it was lighter than the bookshop collection, though I had only held that one for a while. She suggested my cup might be relieved not to have to pose beneath something enormous. I laughed and said I would give it room.

After good night, I opened the cover to check the borrowing slip was still there, then returned the book to my stack. Tomorrow I could draw, or read two more pages first. The little library chair had already been vacated. I didn't need to keep occupying it from home.
