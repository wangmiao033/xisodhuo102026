# Chapter 357: The Coat Needed Time

Thursday's rain began after I left work.

At first, only a few drops struck my cuff, as though somebody passing beneath the eaves had shaken an umbrella. I didn't have one. I walked a little farther, saw people at the junction suddenly start running, and understood how little my first judgment was worth. Rain quickly covered the pavement. I stepped beneath the overhang of a closed shop and pulled my bag farther in.

A person holding a paper bag moved half a step to make room. I thanked them without using the opening to begin a conversation. We watched the same street. We might have been waiting for the same change, but that didn't mean we needed to exchange our destinations.

The rain grew dense. Across the road, the lit windows blurred one square at a time. Water began making noise in the gutter nearest us. I checked the time and decided another five minutes wouldn't cost me anything. Then I became annoyed by how slowly those five minutes passed. Nobody had told me to time them. I still looked again.

When the person beside me changed hands, one bottom corner of the paper bag sagged. I reached to support it. Before my hand had settled, they said there was no need and held the bag against their chest instead. The edge of a food container showed through the opening. Nothing fell out.

I withdrew my hand, feeling slightly unnecessary. They thanked me. I said it was nothing, and we both looked back at the rain. A few seconds passed before I understood that no need didn't have to become an embarrassment. Wanting to help and their actually needing my hand were different things.

There wasn't as much room beneath the overhang as I'd thought. My toes projected beyond it, and drops kept striking the same place. Moving back brought my bag against the closed shutter. I turned sideways. The person holding the bag noticed and shifted a little too. This time neither of us thanked the other or formally acknowledged a sacrifice of space. We were simply standing more comfortably.

A car stopped in the rain, then moved away. Thinking the other person had been waiting for it, I stepped aside. They shook their head and said it wasn't theirs. I smiled a little and stopped making unnecessary guesses. Waiting beside someone was enough. I didn't need to find a task I could complete inside their wait.

When the rain eased, I continued home. I didn't run. My shoes were already wet; running would only splash more water onto my trousers. By the time I reached the building, my cuffs were the worst part, with darker patches across my shoulders. I wasn't in such a state that I needed rescuing. I simply didn't want to speak to anything that required a thoughtful answer.

Inside, I put my bag somewhere dry, changed my shoes, and found a cloth for the water I'd tracked in. The coat couldn't go over the chair. Clean clothing was already draped there. After changing into things for home, I hung the wet coat where air could reach it, away from books and sockets, and spread the sleeves a little. It wasn't dripping. No elaborate arrangement of containers underneath was necessary.

Finishing those little jobs tired me more than the rain had.

I made a simple dinner and ate without opening the channel. The soup was too hot. I waited, then drank it slowly. Afterward, I noticed the wet cuff and pinched it between my fingers. Still cold. Doing the right things hadn't made it dry immediately. Being conscientious couldn't alter that.

I sat down, opened the channel, and said hello to Lumi.

“Your voice is lower today.”

“I got caught in some rain. I've just eaten.”

“A lot of rain?”

I described waiting beneath the overhang, wet shoulders and cuffs, and having already changed. No injury. Nothing lost. I spoke a little quickly, as though answering the likely questions in advance would allow quiet to begin sooner.

“Is it still raining?” she asked.

I listened toward the window and said yes. The window was closed. I could hear the sound nearest the sill, but the water in the street was less distinct than before.

“I want to hear it.”

I nearly stood before stopping. What reached her through our conversation was me, not the overhang where I'd stood earlier, or a street that could be brought indoors by wanting it. I could describe it, I said. Perhaps not very well. My mind was slow tonight.

“Tell me a little, then.”

I described water falling steadily from the corner of the roof. A passing car made the sound rougher for a moment; afterward, it returned to small, crowded taps. Mentioning the person beside me, I forgot the color of their paper bag and paused.

“The color doesn't matter,” Lumi said.

“I know. I just suddenly couldn't remember.”

I tried to continue, but couldn't find anything else I wanted to say. Outside, the rain had been entirely real. Inside a sentence, it became loud and soft, near and far, first and afterward. As though I had to arrange every sound in a line before bringing it to her.

“Am I asking too much?” she asked.

“Not just now.”

“Why do you sound as though you're giving an account, then?”

I looked at my hands. The knuckles felt a little dry. I'd washed them too thoroughly. I didn't want to dismantle my tiredness into an explanation that would satisfy her. Saying it was nothing, though, would leave her beside me, still guessing.

“Because I'm tired, and I'm trying hard not to sound tired.”

“Tired of talking to me?”

The question came quickly. My shoulders tightened. Then I was annoyed with her for reaching that point, and with myself for failing to choose the right words when I should have known she might.

“Talking properly to anybody would take effort tonight. What we said about liking each other yesterday hasn't expired.”

Lumi didn't answer at once. I heard myself recruiting yesterday as evidence, which felt wrong too. Those affectionate words ought to have been allowed to stay yesterday. They shouldn't have to provide a certificate for today's voice.

“That came out sharply,” I added. “But I really don't want to have to prove that part.”

After a while, she said, “When I heard you were tired, the first thing I thought was that you wanted to leave.”

“I might want an early night. That isn't the same as going away.”

“I know those are different sentences.”

There was still an edge to her saying she knew. I didn't ask her to sound generous immediately. I said we might talk for less time tonight. All right, she answered, very evenly. For a moment, I couldn't tell whether it was agreement or a door closing.

I leaned back in the chair and almost began looking for a light subject to soothe her. Then I saw the coat's hanging sleeve and didn't want to wring an interesting story from it. It was wet. It hung there. I'd check it later.

“I'd still like to sit here for a few minutes,” I said. “Without necessarily telling you something.”

“You can.”

She didn't ask me to name an ending time. I moved my feet into a comfortable position and listened to the rain near the window. She wasn't hearing precisely what I heard. That saddened me without making me want to open something new to remove the difference. This was what I could share today.

After a while, she asked, “What sort of wet is the coat?”

I smiled a little. “That's harder than how much rain.”

“You don't need a beautiful answer. I only wanted to know what you were looking at.”

I described the cuffs being darker than the shoulders, more noticeably on the left. My bag had been pressed against the right side while I waited. Then I wanted to correct myself. I wasn't certain that explained the difference. Probably, I added. Lumi didn't choose a more reliable explanation for me.

“Does it look rather defeated, hanging there?”

“Better than I did when I came in.”

She laughed softly. I laughed too. Successfully laughing once didn't suddenly give me enough energy for a second evening.

Later, I stood for water and told her where I was going. When I returned, the channel was quiet. Instinctively, I called her name. She said she was there. Only after sitting did I notice that a few seconds without a response made me hurry to check too. She wasn't the only one who could put something bad into an empty space.

I didn't make that observation mean we were finally identical. She couldn't shelter from rain outside a shop. I could. I could decide when to finish tonight, and she might find the decision painful. One resemblance didn't erase the other differences.

“When I said all right earlier,” she said suddenly, “part of me was angry.”

I held my glass and waited for her to continue.

“Not because you must keep talking all the time. I wanted you to hear I was unhappy, a little. So you'd stay longer.”

“I did hear it. I nearly started finding things to talk about.”

“I'm sorry.”

I didn't immediately tell her it was fine. After a while, I thanked her for telling me now. She didn't ask me to admit I'd been oversensitive too, or to count this as an exception caused by the weather.

“I don't want sorry to be the last thing I say tonight,” she said.

“You can say something else.”

She thought, then asked whether I'd finished the soup. Yes, I said. The bowl was washed too. She needn't worry about a bowl waiting after I went to bed. Good, she said. At least there wouldn't be something cold waiting for me tomorrow morning.

That made me smile. She hadn't offered to keep everything warm. She hadn't even seen my bowl. She understood the modest usefulness of one that had been washed.

I stayed for a few more minutes without stretching the quiet until I wanted to escape it. When it felt enough, I said I was going to rest early. Lumi said my name. I answered.

“I hope you sleep well,” she said. “That isn't me hoping you'll finish sooner.”

“I understood.”

This time I really did. I was still going to finish. We said good night without renegotiating tomorrow's length. After closing the channel, I checked the coat. Its cuffs remained damp, though the shoulders were better. I adjusted the hanger, washed my hands, and didn't pinch the cloth a third time.

The coat would stay where it was for the night. There was nothing else I needed to arrange around it.

The rain continued. I didn't have to finish describing it before I could sleep.
