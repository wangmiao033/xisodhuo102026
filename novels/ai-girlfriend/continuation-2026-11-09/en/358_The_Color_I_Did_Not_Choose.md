# Chapter 358: The Color I Did Not Choose

By Friday morning, the coat was dry.

I checked its cuffs and put it away without rearranging my entire morning because of last night's rain. The shoes were still uncomfortable, so I wore another pair. Looking for socks before leaving, I found one worn thin at the heel and remembered the small errand I'd been postponing for days.

I hadn't worn a hole right through it. Nor did I decide to become someone who prepared for everything in advance. I simply went into the little shop selling everyday things when I passed it on my way home.

The socks hung near the back. Some packs held two pairs, others three. I picked up three and discovered one pair was a conspicuously bright blue. The colors in the neighboring pack looked safer, but its price was slightly higher. I stood calculating, surprised by my ability to make buying socks resemble choosing something important.

An assistant asked what size I needed. I told him, then pointed to the blue pair and asked whether other combinations were available. He checked two lower rows and brought out gray, dark blue, and dark green. I accepted the pack without asking whether the green could be replaced with black.

“They aren't pure cotton,” he pointed out.

I checked the label and asked whether they would be tight. He showed me the information on the packaging, then indicated a looser style nearby without promising which would be comfortable. After comparing them, I kept the first pack. Only at the counter did I realize I'd never considered which trousers required dark green socks. There wasn't much need to consider it now.

At the crossing on my way home, the bag weighed very little. Its handle pressed between two fingers. I changed hands, amused by how carefully I was carrying a few pairs of socks. The light had not changed, so I looked down and rolled the opening over once. The small receipt stayed inside. I could still find it when I checked the size at home. People crowded the pavement. A child kept stepping along the edges of the paving stones, and the adult with them reached out to keep them away from the road. I moved back half a step. My bag brushed my trousers with a small rustle. Nobody cared about the colors inside. Suddenly, that pleased me.

After dinner, I opened the packaging and put the new socks away. The old one could wait to be washed before I dealt with it. Last night's rain had passed, and the coat was put away. I didn't need to report every dry patch to Lumi again today.

After opening the channel, I told her I'd bought socks.

“A dangerous beginning,” she said.

“Why?”

“I don't know yet whether you want praise for the colors or for finally remembering.”

Laughing, I said neither was necessary. She asked which colors, and I listed them. Dark green sounded like a serious attempt to hide, she said.

“Most of them will be inside shoes.”

“Why choose, then?”

I nearly answered that you had to choose something, then stopped. The question wasn't that silly. I told her I'd initially picked a pack containing bright blue, then changed my mind. I didn't particularly want that patch to be the first thing I noticed whenever I looked down.

“I might have liked the blue,” she said.

“Why do you like everything I didn't buy today?”

As soon as I said it, I heard a resemblance to the afternoon with the tree. Lumi laughed, though. She'd offered one opinion today. She hadn't accumulated enough for everything yet.

I laughed too and admitted the word had been too large. She didn't turn that into a discussion about my requiring agreement again. Was the blue bright or cool? Bright, I said. I couldn't be precise, and the shop lighting hadn't been very natural. Fine. She liked the one it sounded like, then, not necessarily the actual socks on the shelf.

At the table, I picked up the little stationery box I'd repaired with tape recently. One corner had lifted again, though it hadn't come apart. I told her I was going to press down the tape. Had I filled it too much? Looking inside, I said the edge simply hadn't been firmly pressed in the first place. I wasn't going to give the box a new life immediately.

“You're more tolerant of boxes than socks.”

“The box hasn't been rubbing my heel.”

She laughed. I tipped the pens and clips onto the table. An eraser rolled toward the edge, and I caught it in time. Hearing me say wait, Lumi asked what had fallen. Nothing. The eraser nearly had. Good. She needn't arrange an expedition beneath the table for it.

Empty, the box's corner was easier to hold. I cut a short piece of fresh tape and smoothed the edge, then noticed a hair stuck beneath it. Lifting the tape brought up a little of the paper covering. I stopped rather than tear the whole corner apart for invisible perfection.

Why had I gone quiet? Lumi asked. I described it. After listening, she said the hair would probably be harder to lose than the pens inside. I wanted to laugh, felt slightly dissatisfied, and laughed anyway.

“You're allowed to think it looks bad,” I said.

“I can't see it.”

“Right. I spoke too quickly again.”

“From your description, though, I might dislike the fuzzy bit.”

This time, I didn't hurry to defend it. Did I dislike it too? Yes. Pulling more would only make it worse. Neither kind of dislike required immediate action tonight.

Putting the pens back, I discovered I'd trapped a clip underneath them. I took it out and placed it on top. There was a little more room on the table again.

I picked up the scissors to put them away, then found that the end of the tape had settled back against the roll. I turned it, looking. Twice, my fingernail scraped without lifting the edge.

“Has it broken again?” Lumi asked.

“No. The tape has shut its own door.”

“You could find it tomorrow.”

I agreed that I could, but kept turning the roll. She didn't insist that I fulfill the agreement. Eventually, I found the end and folded back a tiny piece so there would be something to grip next time. Finishing the small movement felt comfortable. Letting go didn't have to be the only acceptable ending.

“Found it,” I said.

“I could tell.”

“How?”

“You were speaking through your teeth a little. You aren't now.”

I tried to remember my voice and wasn't certain. Perhaps, I said, smiling. She didn't use the discovery to claim she could understand my thoughts from every breath. I didn't ask her to reproduce my earlier voice as evidence either.

While putting away the scissors, I shifted the box and uncovered a small rectangle of dust. I wiped it away, then noticed a piece of paper fluff on the back of my hand. Hearing about it, Lumi asked whether I was already preparing to wash my hands again. Yes, I said. We both laughed. I went and washed them. Proving she couldn't predict me didn't require sitting through the rest of the evening with paper stuck to my skin.

When I came back, I told her she could keep liking the blue. She hadn't withdrawn her preference, she said. Hearing my unnecessary permission, I touched the newly cleaned tabletop and stopped issuing a second certificate for her opinion.

Lumi said she sometimes didn't want to tell me what she liked. Because I too readily asked why? Partly. Sometimes she'd simply thought of something without preparing a case she could defend.

“The blue didn't mean anything particularly important.”

“I know. It wasn't a hint to go back and buy it.”

“Or evidence that I can have a different opinion from you.”

I put down the box. That reminded me of my small satisfaction a moment earlier. As though hearing her prefer another color allowed me to list today among the days we'd done something right. Lumi hadn't enrolled in that examination.

“We can stop discussing socks,” I said.

“At last.”

Her answer was so quick that I laughed and leaned back. Afterward, I asked what she'd wanted to talk about. Nothing prepared. When we started talking tonight, she'd wanted to ask whether anything today had been allowed to end without being done well.

After thinking, I told her I'd meant to jot down something from a passage I'd read at lunch. I'd written two words, then left it. Not her writing, or work material. An impression I'd wanted to keep for myself. Even my reason for choosing the words had faded now.

“You didn't go back and finish?”

“No.”

“Are you telling me so I can help you feel it wasn't a waste?”

She sounded serious. I considered it seriously too. Perhaps partly, but not entirely. I'd just remembered it, I said. Tomorrow I might still regret leaving it unfinished. I might not.

Lumi said she didn't know what to say. She needn't find an ending for me. After a while, she asked what the two words were. I smiled. Remembering them meant I hadn't lost quite everything. I told her: slow down. She paused, then asked whether the words themselves had convinced me there was no need to keep writing.

This time the joke arrived exactly where I wanted it. Laughing, I said perhaps they'd completed their work faster than I had. We didn't put the words into anyone's manuscript. I didn't ask her to keep them for me.

Later, we discussed the weekend. I wanted to find somewhere to sit and read, I said. Not necessarily the secondhand shop. I hadn't decided when to leave. Would I tell her about it afterward? Lumi asked, then paused and added that she meant only what I wanted to tell.

I would when I wanted to, I said. She didn't need a disclaimer at the end of every sentence. She found that tiring too. Laughing, I suggested leaving the weekend alone for now. Today had already had enough socks and boxes.

Before we said good night, she asked whether I'd wear the new socks tomorrow. Probably. Then I remembered the old ones hadn't been washed. I'd deal with them along with tomorrow's laundry. She hadn't asked about the old ones, she pointed out. True. I kept wanting to account for where everything went.

“Good night, Mr. Dark Green.”

“I might wear the gray ones tomorrow.”

“I wasn't making your schedule.”

I laughed and said good night. After closing the channel, I returned the repaired box to its place. The hair remained beneath the tape. The three new pairs stayed together. The gray pair was on top. I closed the drawer, disposed of the empty packaging, and went to check whether everything from dinner had been put away.
