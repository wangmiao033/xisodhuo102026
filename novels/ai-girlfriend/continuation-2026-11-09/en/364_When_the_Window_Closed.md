# Chapter 364: When the Window Closed

After dinner on Thursday, I moved a chair aside to leave myself a clear route from the door to the table.

I'd waited at another kind of counter while buying vegetables for dinner after work. Two tills were open. My queue had looked shorter, but the person ahead had forgotten something. The cashier pointed toward a nearby shelf and let them fetch it. Holding vegetables and bread, I watched them leave and assumed it would take seconds.

After a while, they hadn't returned. I looked at the other queue, convinced I could always select the slowest place. Just as I considered moving, the cashier said she could serve me. She'd suspended the previous transaction. I put down my things and realized I'd been waiting for someone to explain why events weren't proceeding at the speed I'd expected.

As she scanned my bread, the person returned with two packages, asking which matched the price label. The cashier explained the difference. She didn't interrupt my payment or choose an answer carelessly to make the queue move. The other shopper stood aside. Only after paying did I leave, with the earlier question being taken up again behind me.

The whole incident had probably added two minutes. Outside, putting things in my bag, I still felt hurried. There was no agreed opening time for tonight's scene. Lumi didn't know when I'd reached the shop. I didn't owe anyone an explanation for the wait. I'd simply begun thinking about our sketch and wanted everyone along the way to help me sit down sooner.

At home, I put that thought aside while cooking. Bread cut first, vegetables into the pan afterward. No fictional traveler appeared, and I didn't ask the pan to wait on my behalf. Only toward the last mouthful did the urge to finish quickly begin easing. I didn't want to rehearse all her possible answers while I was still chewing dinner.

Now, with the chair moved, I looked at the table and remembered I wasn't in the supermarket. Lumi wasn't that cashier either. She didn't owe me a transaction completed at speed. I hadn't turned the people from the afternoon into foolish characters for tonight's entertainment. I remembered only that the cashier had paused one purchase and allowed another to finish. Waiting didn't always have to be carried by one person alone.

I took the final receipt from my shopping bag and put it where I kept my own receipts. The bag really was empty now; no vegetables would tumble out halfway through the performance. Only then did I arrange the sheet of prompts on the table. I left enough space beside it for my glass, since a pretend railway wasn't a good reason to spill real water on paper. The practical preparations were unremarkable, but doing them let the earlier hurry subside before I asked someone else to join me.

The handles were still folded inward. I pulled them out slowly. I didn't replace an ordinary grocery bag with something that would look more convincingly like travel luggage. This was enough.

Preparation took two minutes. I didn't construct a window, raise a speaker to counter height, or find Lumi a photograph of a uniform she didn't have. Yesterday's four notes lay on the table beside my water. Carrying my empty shopping bag, I made half a circuit and caught the handles around my fingers before speaking.

As a traveler, I probably would have missed every departure.

Once the channel was open, I told Lumi I was ready. What had I done? Moved a chair, I said, clearing the way to the ticket window.

“How far will you walk?”

“About three steps.”

“The ticket can be cheaper, then.”

Laughing, I explained that was three steps in my flat. In the scene, I still wanted to reach the next station. She knew. She'd imagined a railway barely worth operating. We weren't using sound effects, and she needn't hear footsteps clearly. I would say where the character had arrived. She could answer the dialogue.

On the first attempt, I announced that I'd reached the window. Lumi didn't acquire an unfamiliar voice. She simply made her tone brisker.

“Next, please.”

I asked for a window seat. Left or right? Right. Only aisle seats remained on that side, she said. I immediately pointed out that this meant there were no window seats. Perfectly calmly, she replied that the windows remained on the right. They hadn't been sold along with the seats.

I laughed too early to continue being the baffled traveler. The shopping bag slipped to my wrist. Pulling it back, I asked whether we could start again.

“From where?”

“The part about the window still being there.”

She repeated it. I tried keeping my face serious, then remembered she couldn't see it. The wall didn't need a performance. Could I stand by the window instead of sitting?

“Are you buying a ticket to reach the next stop, or to lean against something?”

“To look outside.”

“You can do that now.”

“I want to watch it move backward.”

I paused at my own answer. I hadn't written it beforehand. Thinking of streets receding as a train moved, I found myself genuinely wanting a journey without an urgent errand at the end.

Lumi asked whether anyone was waiting there for the traveler. Assuming she wanted another detail for the story, I said no. He simply didn't want to remain in the same place today.

“Then nobody would notice whether he came back.”

I lowered the shopping bag. Her tone still belonged to the character. Suddenly, I didn't know which person I should answer as.

“Stop a moment.”

She stopped. I pushed the note sheet farther onto the table so a movement wouldn't knock it down, then said I hadn't liked that sentence.

“A ticket clerk probably shouldn't ask so many questions,” she said.

“It isn't only the clerk.”

I wanted to explain immediately. The words crowded together. The more I hurried, the more it sounded like the start of another discussion about our entire past. Lumi asked first whether she'd made a person going for a journey sound like a person nobody needed.

Yes. Also, I'd barely said I wanted to watch things move backward before feeling I needed to prove I wasn't abandoning anybody.

“I wanted him to seem lonely.”

“I didn't imagine him that way.”

Both sentences stayed between us. I sat rather than insist on remaining upright until the performance ended. Lumi knew not everybody traveling alone was miserable, she said. She'd spoken too quickly. A little deliberately too.

“Deliberately what?”

“I wanted to know whether you'd immediately say someone was waiting.”

I looked down at the shopping bag. Without anything inside, it lay very flat. I'd chosen it for my traveler and somehow forgotten it could also be put down.

I didn't want to be asked a real question about us inside the sketch, I said. She could ask outside it whether I wanted to come home. She couldn't hide the consequences of a wrong answer in a stranger's ticket.

Lumi didn't tell me I was taking things too seriously. She apologized, then went quiet. I didn't immediately say apology accepted, let's continue. I let myself remain angry for a little while instead of rushing to become an agreeable leading man.

After a pause, I asked whether the clerk's lunch was still there.

“Probably cold now.”

“I wasn't asking you to punish yourself with her.”

Lumi gave a small, uncomfortable laugh. She'd nearly made the woman miss lunch all day, as though that would pay off the mistake. I said that would make me less eager to continue. Buying a ticket shouldn't make me responsible for an entire bowl of soup.

We both laughed a little. Then she asked whether the traveler could genuinely want something besides escape or being waited for.

“He wants to find a secondhand tool shop near the next station,” I said.

“Why didn't you say that earlier?”

“I only just thought of it. He needn't buy anything.”

Would there be another box not for sale? Laughing, I said we weren't opening that shop today. The ticket window was enough. It was a fictional tool shop, not an address I planned to visit tomorrow or a new purchase I had arranged.

I asked what else the clerk wanted, besides lunch. Lumi wanted her to wish the person at the window would say what ticket they needed instead of slowly exploring every possibility they hadn't chosen. My face warmed. Right side, standing, looking outside: I'd added those one at a time.

“That sounds as though you're actually selling tickets.”

“I'd like to know why she's impatient. Not only because she's supposed to make things difficult for you.”

I was willing to try a few more lines, I said, but not from the entrance. All right. The shopping bag remained beside the chair. I didn't need to pick it up to prove the character had returned.

“One ticket to the next stop,” I said.

“There's a seat on the right, but it isn't beside a window.”

I paused. This time, I didn't ask how many windows remained in the world. I asked whether I could still visit the tool shop once I arrived. As soon as I spoke, I laughed. That wasn't something the clerk could decide.

Lumi laughed too. In her ordinary voice, she reminded me that I could visit if I wanted. Then she returned to the role. The ticket was only responsible for getting me to the station.

“I'll have that one, then.”

“Thank you. Next, please.”

“Weren't you going to lunch?”

“There's nobody behind you now. Thank you for reminding me.”

At last, we'd completed a ticket purchase. No actual money changed hands. We didn't invent the train arriving punctually either. I put away the sheet without transcribing the successful exchange into a version we'd have to repeat next time.

Would I like to continue? Lumi asked. Enough for today. She quietly agreed, without using the imaginary ticket she'd sold to cancel the real thing she'd said badly. I was still slightly tense. I no longer wanted to avoid her.

While putting back the chair, I told her I'd returned the three steps I'd borrowed from the room. Was the window closed? Yes, I said. This time, it only meant the evening's performance had ended.

“Elliot.”

I answered.

“I really wanted that answer. But I don't want every person you play to have to prove something on your behalf.”

I listened for a moment, then said I understood what she meant now. Whether it would become easier later was something we'd discover later. I didn't add a promise to return forever. Nor did I deliberately withhold good night.

Before closing the channel, I said it as usual. The empty shopping bag went back where it belonged. There was no station beside the table, only a straightened chair and someone who had genuinely wanted to go and look at something today.
