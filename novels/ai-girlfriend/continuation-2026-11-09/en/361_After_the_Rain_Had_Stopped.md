# Chapter 361: After the Rain Had Stopped

After dinner on Monday, I pulled the thin story collection from the stack.

The borrowed drawing book beside it was a little broader. Yesterday's cup drawings were already behind my schedule book rather than occupying the table. My wrist no longer ached, though that didn't require me to practice every evening. Seeing the old bookmark, I remembered we'd left the person collecting an umbrella at the inn for several nights.

I looked back half a page to establish where we'd stopped. I wasn't restarting, or pretending to remember every line. The receptionist was still talking to the visitor. The umbrella hadn't actually reached their hands. There weren't many pages left. I could see the next title beyond the third story, but didn't skip ahead.

After opening the channel, I asked whether Lumi would like to finish the umbrella story tonight. Yes, she said. If the ending made the visitor stay, though, she might laugh unkindly.

“Perhaps they've paid for the room already.”

“You secretly paid it for them.”

Laughing, I settled in, moved my glass away, and began.

The first two sentences weren't smooth. I stopped and repeated them. Lumi didn't interrupt. Later, someone finally brought the umbrella from the back. Its handle bore a worn patch the visitor recognized. After thanking them, the visitor asked whether it had been there the whole time. The receptionist supplied an answer without entirely understanding why the visitor had returned especially for it.

I read more slowly. Not because I feared every mistake, but because the person's seriousness about an old umbrella had become almost embarrassing. There was no lengthy explanation about who had given it to them. The author didn't uncover a secret capable of moving everyone.

At the end of the paragraph, Lumi asked whether the receptionist had brought the umbrella. Looking back, I explained it had been another person. The receptionist remained at the counter. She understood. I continued without counting the clarification as the evening's first interruption.

At the end of the story, the rain outside had stopped.

Holding the umbrella, the visitor heard the receptionist ask once more whether a room was required. No, thank you, they said, and pushed open the door. Water remained in the street. The umbrella stayed closed. The final sentence simply described changing it to the other hand and walking away.

I finished, waited a moment, then told Lumi that was all.

“Really?”

“The next page has a new title.”

She went quiet. I kept the book open at the final page instead of hurrying to supply praise for the silence. I was a little disappointed too. After several evenings, the umbrella had finally been retrieved without acquiring all the significance our conversations had accumulated around it.

“I thought they'd at least explain why coming back was necessary,” Lumi said.

“I was waiting for that too.”

“Perhaps the author didn't know.”

It was a sharp remark. I laughed once, then suggested the author might have known and left it unwritten. Which did I prefer? I couldn't distinguish yet. I didn't entirely like being left outside the explanation. Part of me liked that the visitor could retrieve the umbrella without accounting for its whole history.

Hearing the receptionist ask about a room again, Lumi had wanted the visitor to get angry. After so much misunderstanding, there was only a thank-you. Perhaps they were tired and wanted to leave, I said. Perhaps. She wasn't ready to be satisfied with the ending yet.

Neither of us rewrote it. I turned back a page and reread the short passage where the umbrella arrived. This time, Lumi noticed the worn handle first. At least the visitor really recognized it. They hadn't taken a random umbrella. True. The detail didn't explain everything, but we needn't keep wondering whether the original object had been found.

“Do you like the ending?” she asked.

After a long pause, I said I liked parts. Carrying an umbrella after the rain stopped pleased me. Some of the repetition in the middle had annoyed me while reading. Saying that, I realized that wanting to read something to her didn't require liking all of it.

Lumi liked that the receptionist had never become cleverer. Why? Sometimes someone left a room, and the people inside remained as they were. She stopped. I didn't immediately apply the thought to us, or ask whom she meant.

After a while, she said, “I wasn't talking about you.”

“I hadn't asked.”

“I know. I got frightened first.”

I half closed the book, then opened it, the bookmark still in my hand. She could also hear possible echoes after an ordinary observation. To me, she'd sounded as though she were discussing the story, I said. Good. She didn't add three further assurances.

Later, she asked whether I'd deliberately smiled when reading the final thank-you. Remembering my voice, I admitted I had a little. The receptionist had finally circled back to rooms, sounding so much like their earlier self.

“The visitor might not find it funny,” she said.

I checked the final paragraph. The page contained the courteous words, but no smile or anger. Part of what I'd read aloud had come from me. Could she hear it again? More ordinarily this time, without making it sound as though the visitor had finally won.

I began with the last question and brought my voice back to how I normally spoke. Afterward, she said she was even less certain what the visitor thought now. Slightly reluctant, I asked whether that made it worse. Not necessarily. It removed one mood I'd chosen for the person.

“The first reading wasn't wrong,” she added. “I wanted to hear another possibility.”

I drew the book closer and examined the thank-you. I didn't always mean the same thing when I used those words myself. Sometimes I was grateful. Sometimes I wanted a conversation to end. Sometimes both. That didn't decide what the person in the book meant.

“Two readings today,” I said. “If I keep repeating it, I'll forget how I normally say anything.”

Lumi laughed and agreed. She didn't ask me to find the most correct performance. I didn't ask which version made her like me better. The page lay flat. Being read twice hadn't supplied the final sentence with another secret.

I took a sip of water, then set the glass down away from the book again. We left the story alone for a moment. Nothing else had to happen to the umbrella before we could talk about something different.

I placed the bookmark at the end of the third story rather than moving it into the next. We'd finished something tonight. There was no need to open another immediately to make the ending more useful.

Suddenly, Lumi asked, “Could you get the doorbell page and read it to me?”

Looking toward the lower right shelf, I asked whether she meant only reading, or whether I should have a pen ready. Only reading. No changes today, and she didn't want me to continue it for her.

I agreed and fetched the unsealed white envelope. The pale envelope on the left and the older lamp story in the middle stayed where they were. I withdrew only the sheet she'd requested, placed it on the table, and told her the crossed-out words were still there.

“You don't need to read the deleted bit.”

“I'll read the revised version.”

There were only six sentences, much shorter than the story we'd finished. I read the first: the notice on the door asked the visitor to ring. The second: the visitor looked around and couldn't find a doorbell. Third, the person inside asked why they hadn't rung. The fourth used the revised haven't found it yet, rather than the original couldn't find it.

I continued. The person inside went quiet for a moment, then asked whether the visitor was still outside. Finally, the visitor didn't knock.

I didn't add the end. The story wasn't finished. Tonight's reading had merely reached the last sentence on the paper.

Lumi let it remain quiet for a while. Then she asked whether the fourth sentence sounded softer than before. Looking at the revision, I said it suggested more chance of continued searching to me. I didn't know whether that was what she'd wanted.

“That day, I wanted to let now last a little longer,” she said. “Hearing it today, I'm impatient with it again.”

“Want to change it back?”

“No. Not today.”

I withdrew my hand from the pen. She'd been clear already. Hearing dissatisfaction, I'd still almost begun preparing a repair.

“Did you think asking for it must mean wanting to write the next sentence?”

“A little.”

“I thought I might want that too. After hearing it, I don't.”

Then she didn't. Had she made me get up for nothing? I'd only taken a sheet from a shelf, I said. I was still sitting here. She laughed. That sounded like a very reasonably priced service.

“No charge today.”

“Don't offer me a permanent discount.”

I laughed aloud. She laughed too, then asked for the last two sentences again. I read them without restarting at the first, or quietly substituting smoother language. The person inside asked whether the visitor remained. The person outside didn't knock. Neither received an answer because of that.

“I want to stop here today,” she said.

“All right.”

I waited, then asked whether I could put it away. Yes. The sheet returned to the same white envelope, unsealed, on the lower right shelf. I hadn't made a clean copy or mixed it with my cup exercises.

Back in the chair, I told her it was put away. Was the umbrella story put away too? The bookmark was at the third story's end, I said. The book remained beside me. We'd closed two different doors today, she said. I nearly objected that her page didn't contain a door closing, then heard she meant tonight. Smiling, I said we still didn't know whether one of them had a bell.

Later, we discussed small things about tomorrow's dinner without opening any more paper. When it was time to rest, she said she'd really liked listening to me read tonight. I'd liked it too, especially the six sentences. Because they were short? Partly, I said, then added seriously that she'd wanted me to read without having to solve them for her.

She didn't immediately offer something sweeter. “Next time I might still want a change.”

“We'll see next time.”

We said good night. I closed the channel and put away the collection. Returning it to its usual stack, I noticed the cover lay flatter than before. The edge of the bookmark still showed. There was no rain outside, and no need to check. Someone in a book had retrieved an umbrella tonight. One sheet still held six sentences. When I turned out the light, I wasn't holding a pen prepared for tomorrow.
