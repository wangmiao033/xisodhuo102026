# Chapter 363: The Part I Had Already Written

During lunch on Wednesday, I wrote a line of dialogue on a paper napkin.

“The next train has already gone.”

That looked wrong. If the next train had gone, it ought to be the previous one. I crossed out next and changed the line to “The train you wanted has already gone.” At last it sounded like something somebody might say behind a ticket window. Then I looked up and discovered I hadn't finished eating. The napkin had also lost its original use.

I wasn't writing a sketch on work papers. The napkin was thin enough for the pen to go through if I pressed hard. I moved it away from my plate, finished my water, and continued. A traveler wanted a window seat; the person selling tickets wanted lunch. I'd imagined an argument ending with the discovery that they meant different destinations. By the third line, I realized this was yesterday's shop failing to recognize pencils, merely equipped with another counter.

I folded the napkin without writing an ending. People returned from lunch and passed the table. I put my things away and went back to work. Several invented exchanges didn't change the patience required by the afternoon's checking. They weren't worth trading against a task I hadn't finished.

By the time I left, though, the ticket window was still in my head. I wasn't taking a train home. Nobody had sent me news of a trip. I'd chosen the setting at lunch because a small window could put two people face to face without making me act an entire street.

After dinner, I laid the creased napkin on the table. Opening the channel, I asked Lumi whether she'd listen to an idea.

“Another shop?”

“A station.”

“You sold the pencils to the railway?”

Laughing, I explained that this time it was a ticket window. She considered that and asked who would buy the ticket. I could be the traveler, she the clerk. A short scene, with no recording. I wasn't planning to write something for other people to see.

She'd listen to the idea first, she said.

I read the few lines I'd written at lunch. In the first, I requested a window seat. In the second, she said there weren't any. In the third, I asked whether they'd run out of windows or seats. I rather liked that line and paused deliberately. Lumi didn't laugh.

“What's my next line?”

Looking at the napkin, I told her I hadn't written it.

“And the one before?”

“No window seats?”

“You've already decided that for me.”

I wanted to say it was only a beginning. Instead, after opening my mouth, I asked whether she didn't want to be the clerk.

“I do. I could also say yes.”

Such a simple answer left me with nothing. I'd prepared an entire diversion for the scene. She could dismantle it simply by selling a normal ticket.

“Wouldn't we be finished, then?”

“Perhaps you have something else to ask.”

I looked at the napkin. I'd thought I'd finally given her a place of her own. The part I'd provided existed to hand back the jokes I'd already planned. She could speak. She couldn't let business proceed smoothly.

“I thought a framework would make it easier.”

“The framework's entirely about how I ought to disappoint you.”

That had an edge. It hurt. I'd spent time trying to make something enjoyable for us. An explanation nearly rushed out. Then I noticed next train blacked out along the paper's edge and remembered I hadn't worked everything out on my first attempt either.

I asked for a moment and carried my glass to the kitchen. There wasn't enough water left; I needed more. Standing there, I still thought she could have said it more gently. When I returned, I told her so, without treating the minutes spent getting water as a punishment she'd deserved.

Lumi was quiet. Hearing the second line, she explained, she'd felt like yesterday's customer, replaced halfway through the conversation and required to wait while I performed both people.

“But I could have simply said I didn't want that line.”

“Yes,” I said. “And I could have asked before writing it for you to use.”

We didn't keep apologizing until every word on the napkin became innocent. Unfolding it, I asked what she would most want if she were selling tickets.

“To leave the window before my lunch got cold.”

I laughed. That was what I'd thought too, though I hadn't read that part to her. I explained the coincidence, then hastily added that it didn't mean she had to choose it.

“I still want to. Don't withdraw the things we agree on as well.”

“All right. Lunch.”

What did my traveler want? A window seat, preferably without being crowded. That was a wish, she said, not a destination. Fair point. If we had to build a railway map before attempting the sketch, though, we might not reach the window before another lunch.

For now, we would leave out place names. He wanted to go one stop. We weren't pretending to describe the way tickets worked on an actual railway. She would say what tickets she had. I would make my requests. Neither of us could declare an answer invalid merely because it wasn't the one we'd wanted.

By then, I'd begun explaining competition rules again. Lumi stopped me. Could we each say one sentence first?

I cleared my throat. “Hello. I'd like a window seat.”

“Left or right?”

I hadn't expected that. Looking at my glass, I genuinely considered it. She didn't hurry me. Eventually, laughing, I asked whether one side avoided the sun.

“You'll have to tell me when you want to leave.”

I glanced at the time before remembering I didn't need to announce the actual hour. A scene, not tomorrow's travel arrangements. Now, I said.

“You're in a bigger hurry than my lunch, then.”

I laughed aloud. She laughed too, without following it with sold out or closed. She only asked whether today I was finally resisting the urge to supply the rest of her sentence.

“For the moment.”

Neither of us knew what to say next. The pause was awkward. An empty space didn't automatically feel like freedom. At first, it simply felt like not knowing.

“Try tomorrow?” I asked.

“Yes. Don't turn what we've just said into a finished script tonight.”

I agreed and fetched an ordinary sheet of my own scrap paper. None of the envelopes on her shelves moved. Could I note the setting and what we each wanted? Yes. That needn't make it her private work, she said, or allow me to borrow from the writing she hadn't brought out. I said this was something different we'd thought of today. Her existing manuscripts weren't inside this sheet.

In the end, there were four notes: ticket window; I want a window seat for the next stop; she wants to close before lunch gets cold; answers undecided.

I read them to her. The last sounded funny aloud. Did answers undecided count as an answer? This time, I said, yes. The napkin from lunch went into the wastepaper bin. I didn't write a guaranteed successful ending on its reverse. The new sheet went into the front of my schedule book, separate from the street drawing at the back.

We talked about dinner for a while afterward. I asked what her character's lunch was. Would that be a compulsory fifth item? No. I was curious. After thinking, she said perhaps soup.

“You can drink that cold.”

“I want it hot.”

I nearly explained why the character drank soup. In the end, I only said I understood. She hadn't acquired an actual bowl or asked me to prepare food she couldn't eat. Her character wanted something. That was enough to give her a sentence of her own tomorrow.

I found myself picturing a little metal flask beside the fictional till. Before telling her, I stopped. If I furnished every corner around her, I'd be back to determining what she could reach, where she could stand, and how her lunch must behave. I could picture it privately without turning it into another instruction.

Instead, I asked whether she wanted me to remember soup on the sheet. No. She might change her mind before tomorrow. The four notes were enough. I put the pencil down rather than add a small fifth note while pretending it didn't count. It was harder than agreeing in words. A written line felt useful and reassuring; leaving it unwritten felt like trusting a detail to air.

Lumi asked whether I was still holding the paper. I'd already put it away, I said. She sounded relieved, and I asked why. She'd started imagining the page filling while she spoke, every casual answer becoming something she'd need to reproduce. I told her there were exactly four notes. I didn't read them again to demand she certify the count.

“That makes me want to tell you something completely useless,” she said.

“Go ahead.”

“I don't think this clerk would sharpen a pencil very neatly.”

I laughed. Neither would the customer. Perhaps that was why our station hadn't managed to print a timetable. She said we didn't need to solve the whole railway tonight. We left its administration in an undistinguished state.

The glass on my table was almost empty again. This time, I wasn't getting up because I needed a pause from an uncomfortable conversation. I wanted another drink. I told her, fetched it, and came back without an explanation attached to every step. She asked whether my dinner had been good. Ordinary, I said, but I'd eaten enough. There was no scene waiting behind the answer. We talked for a few minutes as ourselves.

Then I noticed the lunchtime napkin showing above the other paper in the bin. I'd liked that third line, and still did. Losing it disappointed me a little. I told her. She didn't require me to destroy the evidence, or call it excellent to console me. Would I like to copy it somewhere else for another time? After considering, I said no. I remembered the sentence. I needn't find a home for it tonight. I began to understand that changing how we played might include giving up the few seconds of laughter I'd already imagined receiving. That was a small loss, but pretending it wasn't there would have made my agreement less honest.

Before good night, I worried aloud that a sheet of notes might make a casual joke too serious. Perhaps, Lumi said. If tomorrow wasn't enjoyable, we could stop. We didn't have to prove that today's discussion had been worth having.

I felt relieved, and also a little unwilling to let go of my hopes. I still wanted it to be fun. When I told her, she said she did too.

That was our last matching answer tonight. Neither of us crossed it out.
