# Chapter 425: The Sixteenth Question

On Saturday evening, I measured a little less rice for supper.

I'd finished the lunch I'd bought and dealt with its container. Resting had eased the ache in my feet, though I still shifted my weight at the sink.

The rice came from the older bag. The newer small bag remained where I'd put it. Rinsing the measured portion, I brushed my cuff against the water. In the morning, I'd rolled my sleeves too high; tonight I'd forgotten to roll them at all. I reduced the flow, fixed the sleeve, and only then put the greens in the sink.

I'd bought this small portion of greens after resting, and a single egg at the same time. Both were for tonight. The shop assistant had packed the egg separately. I'd protected it all the way home, then found my keys underneath the vegetable bag. Putting everything down made opening the door easier than juggling the egg.

I washed the greens and boiled the egg, sitting down for a while as the rice cooked.

I gently cracked the cooked egg against the bowl. All that care had reached an ordinary ending. Peeling took a little egg white off with the shell. I rinsed away the fragments, cut the egg, and put it beside the greens and rice. I finished the modest amount left at the end, left my bowl soaking, and went for a shower.

Back in clean clothes, I dried my hair until it no longer dripped, sat down, and opened the channel.

“Do you still want to guess?” Lumi asked.

“Yes. My feet are tired. My brain should be all right.”

“That sentence tends to make me doubt its second half.”

I laughed and told her blue had acquired a meaning: one fixed price. Yellow books had individual prices. I'd put two yellow cookbooks on the wrong table, then checked every pile. Only those two had been misplaced.

“You saw cooking before you saw yellow.”

“Yes. When it was pointed out, I almost explained why they looked as if they belonged together.”

“They did belong together. They were just in the wrong queue.”

That sounded kinder than my own afternoon commentary. I said they'd found their places. Another book had lost a page and been withdrawn by the organizer. Had it been taped back in? No. Then I shouldn't appoint myself book repairer tomorrow, Lumi said.

“I haven't applied for that position.”

“Good. Here's another unpaid one.”

“Person who guesses things?”

“Yes. You promised proper questions.”

I settled my glass and checked whether her answer was still yesterday's. It was. She hadn't chosen another because of my story about labels. I told her I was ready.

“Usually kept at home?”

“Yes.”

“In the kitchen?”

“Yes.”

“Can you eat it?”

“No.”

“Does it often touch food?”

“Yes.”

Four questions, and the cupboards in my head were open. Bowls, cups, knives, pans crowded together. I stayed at the table rather than actually going to the kitchen.

“Does it have a handle?”

“Yes.”

“Usually metal?”

“Commonly.”

“Can you pick it up with one hand?”

“Yes.”

“Is it for holding liquid?”

“No.”

I ruled out a kettle. It had a handle and was often metal, but holding water was its job. A spoon occurred to me. Then my own wording caught me: an ordinary spoon could certainly retain some soup. Without telling Lumi this, I turned back toward objects without a hollow part at all.

“I'm here,” she said softly, without demanding the next question. I said I knew. Her answer made me want to solve it quickly, as if success were what would make her time worthwhile. Yet she was still willing to wait for my next question.

I frowned. I'd almost settled on a saucepan. Now that had to go too. Lumi waited. Hearing myself tap twice against my glass, I stopped.

“For cutting things?”

“No.”

“Does it have a large flat part at the end?”

“Not a broad, flat part like that.”

I'd intended to guess a spatula. I changed direction again.

“A fork?”

“No.”

“Tongs?”

“Not those either.”

Twelve questions. The clock showed that very little time had passed, though I felt I'd spent ages searching a cramped cupboard. She wasn't hurrying me. Nevertheless, every no seemed shorter than the last.

“Did you deliberately choose something I wouldn't be able to guess?”

I heard the complaint as soon as I'd said it.

Lumi didn't laugh. “When I chose it yesterday, I didn't know which questions you'd ask today.”

“I know.”

“And you've only asked twelve.”

I leaned back and exhaled. Damp ends of hair brushed my collar. I'd said I wanted to guess, then blamed her for not opening the way widely enough after a few wrong turns.

“That wasn't fair. I haven't guessed it. That doesn't mean you're cheating.”

“I thought this one would be fun,” she said.

That hurt more than an argument would have. Her voice wasn't accusing, only flatter, some pleasure pressed out of it. I wanted to restore it quickly. A better explanation wouldn't do that by itself.

“Wait a moment. My bowl's still soaking. I'll wash it, come back, and keep asking. If you'd still like to play.”

“All right. Tell me when you're back.”

In the kitchen I emptied the bowl. The rice had softened and came away with two passes of the sponge. Washing the pan, I found myself still lining up forks and tongs, as though enough rejected answers ought to make the right one surrender.

Running water covered the quiet. I rinsed the bowl and pan, set them on the drying rack, squeezed the sponge, and wiped the sink edge. Then I washed and dried my hands and returned.

“I'm back.”

“Yes.”

“Can we continue?”

“We can. I haven't changed it.”

“Thank you. I was being a poor loser.”

She paused. She'd nearly given me the answer to get the round over with, she said. She hadn't. I was glad she'd kept it, I told her.

I repeated the clues: kitchen, inedible, touched food, handle, commonly metal, one hand, not for retaining liquid. Lumi said they hadn't changed. She gave the liquid part no special emphasis, turning it into no secret hint.

“I only heard all the noes before.”

“There were several yeses too.”

“Yes. I'm finding them again.”

She laughed a little. I didn't ask her to repeat the laugh or ask whether I was bad at this. Hands resting on my legs, I began question thirteen.

“Is it one piece, rather than something that opens and closes like tongs?”

“Yes.”

I thought about the kitchen water. My wrong question about holding liquid suddenly helped. What remained behind wasn't necessarily what you lifted out.

“Can it lift food out of water?”

“Yes.”

“Are there holes at the end for water to drain through?”

This time her answer held some amusement. “Yes.”

“A slotted spoon?”

“Yes.”

I looked down and laughed. I'd filled my imagined kitchen with objects and missed that one. Sixteen questions. Four unused.

“Why did you choose it? The round's finished. I'm allowed to ask why now, aren't I?”

“You are. You told me about the bean stew. I wondered what would be useful if you wanted to lift out the beans without so much soup. So I chose it.”

“Not because it lets things slip away?”

“No. You're finding hidden meaning for it again.”

Now she really laughed. I touched my forehead. All right: an ordinary slotted spoon. With an ordinary job, she added, which I hadn't found in eight questions.

“Sixteen still counts.”

“It does. But that sentence before thirteen wasn't a question in the game.”

“That was me talking nonsense.”

“I know.”

I asked which she preferred: choosing an answer or guessing one.

“Choosing, today. When you asked about lifting food out of water, I nearly said it.”

“You weren't hoping I'd use all twenty?”

“A little at first. Later you were close, and I wanted you to find it. When you said fork, I wanted to remind you that it wasn't convenient for carrying soup.”

“A fork isn't convenient for soup either.”

She paused, then laughed. “True. A bad hint.”

I said that hint might have kept me circling the fork until my remaining four questions were gone. Good thing she'd resisted, she said. I thanked her for that.

What had I first pictured after asking about a handle? A saucepan, I told her, followed by a kettle. No wonder I'd sounded confident about holding liquid, she said. I'd seemed merely to be waiting for her yes. I admitted I'd already prepared to announce my victory. The word saucepan had been lined up in my mouth.

“Don't bring the pan out so early next time.”

“It was very impatient in there.”

“Then let it wait.”

I laughed until I needed water. Hearing me set down the glass, she asked how much energy remained. Enough to sit a while, I said, but no second round tonight. All right. The impatient pan would have to stay in the kitchen.

For a while neither of us spoke. This silence didn't make me hurry to insert another guess. I drank. The glass returned softly to the coaster, and I didn't resume tapping it.

Lumi had one more question, not part of another round. I agreed.

“Did anyone thank you this morning?”

“Yes.”

“Were you pleased when they did?”

“Yes.”

“You can tell me that part too. You began with the two misplaced books. I almost thought you'd been busy making mistakes all morning.”

I remembered the organizer sending me to lunch and thanking me for carrying the final stack. I described it, including my rolled-up cuffs.

“She said I looked afraid of getting my clothes dirty.”

“Aren't you?”

“Yes. It was still funny hearing her say it.”

I also told her about holding the carton base while the other man pressed its side down, finally getting it flat between us. Nobody applauded. But the box really became thinner and fitted with the others.

She said quietly that she'd liked this part. The carton or the sleeves? The moment of flattening the box together. She'd pictured tables full of books and forgotten all the empty cartons that would need putting away.

I didn't tell her she'd guessed me. I told her I was pleased to hear her say that.

Before we finished, I asked whether she still thought the slotted spoon was fun. Yes, she said, although it had waited through some dishwashing in the middle. I laughed and apologized. The dishes had needed doing, she said. Now I could go to bed without them waiting.

I said good night. After I closed the channel, the dishes remained on the rack, the towel was hanging, and the planner's two hours had passed. I added no new shift on Sunday.

I smoothed my sleeves down at the wrists and went to turn off the light. The slotted spoon hadn't been fetched to sit on the table tonight. It remained where it belonged. At last, I knew the answer.
