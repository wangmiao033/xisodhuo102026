# Chapter 424: The Wrong Table

I arrived at the library at nine fifty-two on Saturday.

The activity-room door was open. The woman I'd met on Thursday was standing up table signs. Her hair was tied back today, without the headband. I looked at her face before introducing myself as Elliot.

“I remember. You can put your coat over there. We'll start together at ten.”

I chose a chair against the wall, clear of the passage. Another helper was flattening an empty carton. I asked whether he needed a hand. The tape was already cut, he said; only the last side remained. I held the base while he folded the side down. The cardboard finally lay flat.

At ten, the organizer called us into the middle of the room. Besides me and the man with the carton, there was a woman in a green cardigan. She said she needed to leave at eleven thirty, as arranged. The organizer checked the sheet and said she knew. Labeling would be a good place for her to start.

Books in the blue section were one fixed price. Yellow-section books were priced individually. Approved donations were on the trolleys. Unchecked books stayed near the entrance and mustn't be mixed in. Labels came first, then sorting by subject within each price section. Loose papers, photographs, or other things found inside went into the small box beside her desk.

“Cookbooks can be in either section,” she said, holding up two books, one with a blue label and one with a yellow label. “Check the color, then the category. The yellow prices are already written. Don't change them yourselves.”

I looked from one side to the other. The blue cooking sign was near the window. Its yellow equivalent was on a table farther away. At first I checked every pile before taking it.

The woman in green peeled label backing deftly, collecting it in a box. I picked up a small stack and asked where it went. She touched the blue label on the top book. I carried it to the window and set it down with the spines facing the aisle.

“Covers facing up is fine,” the organizer said. “People shouldn't have to lift a whole stack to find out what it is.”

I spread the four books into two little piles. Less tidy now, but easier to inspect. A title that had been hidden beneath the others was visible. Previously, only half an onion had shown.

For twenty minutes I worked slowly: pick up, check label, find sign, return. Every trip seemed to require learning the route again. The man who'd flattened the carton was arranging another table. Passing me, he asked whether I needed a bookend. I said the books were lying flat. He nodded and carried it elsewhere.

As I grew familiar with the work, my eyes began finding the subject first.

The next small stack had a cookbook on top, with similar plates pictured on the covers beneath. When I picked it up, the woman in green was peeling a yellow label. I didn't wait for her to divide the stack. I carried it to the window.

This time I arranged everything quickly, moving the earlier piles to show more of their covers. Looking back, I thought the table was better than when I'd started. I felt pleased. I was already imagining telling Lumi I hadn't been so clumsy after all.

“Why are these two here?”

The organizer stood beside the table, pointing to two yellow labels. I saw the plate on one cover first. Only then did I notice the yellow corner.

“I just brought them over.”

“This is the blue, fixed-price table. Take those out first, or somebody will read the sign and pay the wrong price tomorrow.”

I pulled out the books, dislodging another and catching it with my hand. My ears grew hot. I started to explain that the entire stack was about cooking. Before finishing, I knew that wasn't what she'd asked.

“I only checked the subject.”

“All right. Check the other piles you've moved too. Don't stop with these two.”

She neither lowered her voice for me nor announced my mistake to everyone. The woman in green looked up. I said I'd taken her stack before she'd separated it. She said okay and indicated an empty space beside her chair. In future, I should wait until she put a finished pile there.

I handed the two books to the organizer to confirm where they belonged, then began at the window, checking every label. One blue corner had curled up. For a moment I thought the book was unlabeled. She told me to press it flat; if it remained readable, there was no need for another.

The check took ten minutes. Only those two books were misplaced. I put them beneath the yellow cooking sign, stepped back to read it, then checked their corners again.

Returning to the labeling table, I waited until the woman had set a completed pile beside her chair.

“All blue,” she said.

“I see.”

I spoke more quietly than before. She tried to flick backing paper from her finger into the box. It stuck, requiring her other hand to remove it. We laughed. Those scraps were the troublesome things, she said. The books behaved better.

As I took another stack, a woman with a shoulder bag came in. She went straight to a gardening book, leafed through it, and asked where to pay.

“The sale starts tomorrow.”

She checked the poster, frowned, and said she'd mistaken the date. Tomorrow she was busy all day. Holding my books beside her, I suddenly wanted to solve it. I nearly offered to reserve her choice. Instead, I put down the stack and fetched the organizer.

There was no payment desk today, she explained, and no advance reservations. The visitor returned the gardening book to the table, leaving her hand on its cover. Was there anything similar she could borrow? The organizer directed her to the lending desk, where staff could search the catalog. This particular copy was a sale donation, not a library loan.

The woman nodded and went to the desk. I straightened the book she'd examined. There were no new creases. Later, through the doorway, I saw her looking at a screen with the person behind the counter. I didn't find out whether she borrowed anything. The gardening book stayed in the yellow section for tomorrow.

After eleven, another staff member wheeled over some large-print editions. Thursday's man and his sister hadn't arrived. That was tomorrow too. I recognized the space left near the door and asked whether these belonged there.

“Yes. Keep them low enough to reach easily.”

I laid them on the lower table without adding a second layer. One was back-cover up; I turned it over. The print really was large. I read the cover, not enough to choose the perfect book for a woman I hadn't met.

As I left the lower table, a thin sheet slipped from a book. I bent and caught it against my shoe before it reached the floor. At first I thought it was a note. Turning it over, I found a page number and continuous printed text.

I took both book and page to the organizer. Instead of asking me to use the box for found papers, she opened the book at the neighboring page numbers and checked the binding. It was a detached page, tucked farther forward where nobody had noticed it.

“We won't sell this one yet.” She put it on another pile behind her table.

I asked whether she wanted tape. She shook her head. Not now; other books still needed checking. I returned empty-handed to the gap. Spreading the neighboring books slightly made their covers more visible. I didn't fetch an unchecked book simply to fill the space.

The other helper passed and asked whether this section was finished. I said it was; the organizer could give us something else. He went around behind me, avoiding a table leg. Only then did I realize I was standing in the aisle. I stepped closer to the table.

When the helper in green began gathering her things, the clock said eleven twenty-seven. A small stack remained unlabeled. She pointed it out. The organizer told her to leave it and not rush. She laid the unused labels flat, put on her coat, and asked whether anybody had seen her cloth bag.

It was on the other side of her chair back. I pointed. She retrieved it, checked the time, and said she could catch her bus now. Nobody asked her to finish those last books first.

In the remaining half hour, I arranged two more piles, wiped dust from a table edge, and helped the other man take empty boxes to their designated place. Open cartons occupied a surprising amount of room, he said. Some could be used after the sale. Following the organizer's directions, I kept sound, clean boxes separate from crushed ones instead of throwing everything away to clear the room.

At eleven fifty-five, she asked us to stop and walk the aisles, looking for boxes or chairs obstructing them. I went from the entrance to the window, around a table, and back. The chair holding my coat projected into the passage.

I took my coat and pushed the chair in. I'd adjusted a chair on Thursday. With the layout changed, I'd almost missed the same problem today.

At noon, a few books still awaited staff attention. I offered to carry a small stack to the organizer's table. She thanked me, accepted, then told me to go and have lunch.

“Eleven tomorrow,” she said. “Come and look if you're free. You don't have to dress for work.”

“Do I look as if I have?”

She glanced at my sleeves and laughed. I looked like somebody afraid of dirty cuffs, she said. Mine were still rolled well above my wrists. I'd stopped needing them that high some time ago.

Outside the activity room, I lowered them. Two hours ended more ordinarily than I'd imagined. There was no line of people waiting to grade me. The misplaced books were on the correct table. I hadn't left my coat behind.

I bought a hot lunch nearby and took it home. Only after eating did I notice the ache in the soles of my feet. Not the heat of wanting to chase another ball, but the sensation of finally resting them after standing and walking back and forth. I sat a while, put away the food container, washed my hands, and let the afternoon slow down.

After lying down briefly, I got up to find clothes for tomorrow. It was still light outside. The water beside the wooden coaster was half gone. Seeing the glass reminded me that Lumi had a word she hadn't told me.

I nearly began listing answers for her: umbrella, book, hanger. I stopped at the third. Yesterday she'd asked me to ask properly. She would probably remember tonight.

I poured more water, dried the bottom of the glass, and put it down. Supper was still some time away. For now I stretched my feet out and listened to somebody descending the stairs slowly, without finding a shortcut to the evening's first question.
