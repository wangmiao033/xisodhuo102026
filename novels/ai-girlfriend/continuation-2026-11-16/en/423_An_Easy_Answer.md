# Chapter 423: An Easy Answer

Leaving work on Friday, I intended to go straight home. At the junction, a small restaurant had bean stew and bread written on its blackboard. I read it twice before opening the door.

It was busier than I'd expected. The little window table was occupied. Two tables farther inside were empty, one close to the serving hatch. The waitress asked whether I was alone. I said yes and indicated the window.

“He hasn't asked for his bill yet. There may be a wait. You can sit inside now.”

I looked at where I was standing. Every opening of the door brought in a draft. I chose the table inside rather than wait for the other man to finish.

It was smaller than my table at home. A menu lay under the condiments. I'd barely settled my coat when the waitress came to ask what I wanted. I ordered the stew from the board, bread, and a glass of water.

“Would you like the bread toasted?”

“Yes, please.”

She wrote it down and went toward the kitchen. Sitting alone, I heard plates slide across a counter and somebody request another spoon. I hadn't brought a book to give my hands something to do. I straightened the menu, then discovered its angle made no difference to dinner.

When the waitress passed behind me with a tray, I realized one sleeve of my coat was hanging into the aisle. I pulled it in, but it slid down the smooth chair back again. She paused and told me there were hooks beside the door. The empty one was available. Looking toward the entrance, I found a row of hooks hidden by the open door.

I got up, hung my coat, and returned to the table. There was more room behind me now. Trays no longer had to pass close to my shoulder. She thanked me; I said I hadn't noticed the hooks. By the time I'd picked up my napkin again, I could tell there was usually a pause between the bell ringing and somebody collecting the food. This table had things to watch as well.

At the next table, a man studied a folded sheet while a woman held her finger against one line. Their voices were too low for me to hear. A bell rang at the hatch. The waitress collected a bowl, then returned with my bread.

Two slices, browned at the edges, with a small dish of butter. I tore off a corner. It wasn't giving off much steam, but the inside was soft. When the stew arrived, I lowered my spoon and pushed gently through its surface.

The water didn't come.

After a few mouthfuls I was thirsty. I looked up twice. The waitress was explaining a bill, then somebody in the kitchen called for napkins. She turned to fetch them. The words about my water circled inside my mouth before going down with some bread.

The stew was thicker than I'd expected: beans, carrots, a little tomato sharpness. I couldn't identify all the seasoning. Smelling something pleasant didn't give me its name. Halfway through the bowl, the waitress passed close enough for me to say excuse me.

“I asked for some water as well.”

“Sorry, I forgot. I'll get it now.”

She went without looking annoyed at being interrupted. I put my spoon down. When the glass arrived, it was still cool from washing. I thanked her and took a long drink, aware of how much effort I'd put into waiting for such a small thing.

The woman at the next table called the waitress too, asking for a container. She brought one and wiped a few drops from their table. Nobody had to introduce themselves again because they wanted a second thing.

When she passed later, I asked whether there was a toasted spice in the stew. She glanced at my bowl and said she hadn't eaten it today. She would ask the kitchen.

I almost told her not to bother. Before I'd finished opening my mouth, she'd turned away. Somebody answered through the hatch, too quietly for me to catch. She returned to say there was a little cumin. Had I disliked it?

“No. There was a warm smell I wanted the name of.”

“Now you can ask next time you order.”

I tried another spoonful. Knowing the name didn't immediately make it more obvious, and I couldn't attribute every smell to that one ingredient. She went away with an empty tray. Eating more slowly, I noticed the carrots were firmer than the beans and one side of the bread was darker than the other.

I used the last bread to gather the stew from the bottom. One toasted edge broke into crumbs. I pushed them onto the plate with my fingertips.

The waitress offered the dessert menu. I touched my glass and said I was full, but the stew had been good. She said I could try dessert another time and took the menu elsewhere. I sat back until getting up to walk sounded comfortable, then fetched my coat from the hooks by the door.

I asked for the bill, checked the items and amount, and paid. The window table had emptied meanwhile. Somebody else was sitting down there. I left without trying to claim it for a dinner I'd already finished.

Outside, the light had faded. A headwind made me tuck my scarf farther inside my collar. The repaired edge passed smoothly under my fingers. My stomach was warm; I didn't hurry. At the turning near the library, I kept going instead of checking tomorrow's time again.

At home I hung my coat, washed my hands, and poured water before opening the channel.

“Are you home later today?” Lumi asked.

“I ate out. Bean stew and toast.”

“Was it good?”

“Yes. I also spent a while fighting for a glass of water that hadn't arrived.”

I told her how the waitress had forgotten and how I'd eaten half the bowl before asking again. Lumi wanted to know whether I'd received an enormous glass in the end. I said it was normal-sized.

“A small prize for such a battle.”

“It was very good for thirst.”

“Half a victory, then.”

I laughed. She asked whether I could dip the bread. I said I'd initially tried to preserve the crispness, then used it all to wipe the bowl.

“Did you leave the toast any dignity?”

“I ate nearly all the crumbs.”

“That sounds like very thorough respect.”

I drew my glass nearer and told her I wasn't hungry or rushing to do anything else. She said she'd like some time that wasn't a dinner review.

“What would you like to do?”

“Guess things,” she said promptly. “You choose something in your head. I ask questions. You answer. Don't start by explaining why you chose it.”

“How long do we guess?”

“Twenty questions at most. Then you tell me.”

I suggested ordinary, tangible things only. Not next week, or regret. She said she wasn't that bad. I preferred to settle it first, I said. She made an impatient sound and told me to choose.

My eyes moved from the table toward the door. A word occurred to me that she would certainly recognize.

“Ready.”

“Usually used at home?”

“Mostly outside.”

“Held in your hand?”

“Yes.”

“Something to do with the weather?”

“Yes.”

She paused. “An umbrella?”

“Yes.”

She didn't laugh immediately. Looking at my hand beside the glass, I found those four questions disappointingly quick.

“When you chose, had you already decided I ought to know by the third question?”

“More or less.”

“Because I told that story.”

“Yes.”

“Elliot, I didn't come to collect the umbrella you'd set out for me.”

There was amusement in it, but she put a little more weight on my name. I'd wanted her to win, quickly and beautifully, so the evening could proceed smoothly. She had actually wanted to ask questions.

“Too easy?”

“Too much like you put the answer in front of me and waited for me to be delighted.”

I scratched my chin. I didn't dismiss it as only a game. She hadn't made the game serious. I'd left too little of it to play.

“Again,” I said. “I'll choose properly.”

“My turn first.”

I agreed. She said she needed a moment. I waited, drinking some water. She didn't hurry me to ask; I didn't hurry her to choose. A door closed in the corridor. Keys rattled twice, then everything was quiet.

“I have one. But I won't tell you tonight.”

“Is that cheating?”

“Saving one for tomorrow. You're sorting books, aren't you? Once you're home and you've eaten, we can play a round. If you'd still like to.”

I glanced at the clock, thinking of the two hours tomorrow. She hadn't set an exact time or asked me to sit down the moment I arrived. I agreed. When I opened the channel after supper, I'd tell her how much energy I had left. Right now, I certainly wanted to guess.

“No changing your answer halfway through to win,” I said.

“And you mustn't steer every question toward umbrellas.”

I laughed and agreed. I almost asked whether she'd just heard about her object, then stopped. She heard the beginning of “Is it” and asked where the rest had gone. I said I was saving the first question.

Before we finished, she wanted another turn to make up for mine. I chose a clothes hanger.

First she asked whether it was usually kept at home, then whether one hand was enough to pick it up. I said yes to both, adding that sometimes they were crowded together in rows.

“Does it have teeth?”

“Not usually.”

“For doing your hair?”

“No. Are you thinking of a comb?”

“I'm eliminating things. Does it touch clothes?”

“Yes.”

“Often kept in a wardrobe?”

“Yes.”

“A hanger.”

I said she'd got it in seven. She complained that my clue about rows was too broad: combs could be lined up in shops. I admitted the extra information had led her astray. Next time I should wait for her questions, she said, rather than trying to hand over the entire wardrobe in my head.

Once she'd won, she announced with satisfaction that this was more like it. I said the hanger should thank her for rescuing it from my description of a row of combs.

“My thing tomorrow deserves proper questions too,” she said.

“All right.”

I picked up the closed poetry book and settled it securely again. Looking for a word earlier, I'd nudged the board beneath it, leaving one corner close to the table edge. I pushed the board back. The paper bookmark remained at the same poem. Then I told Lumi I'd just straightened the book.

“Almost chose that?”

“Almost. I was afraid you'd say I was giving you the answer again.”

“You do learn quite quickly.”

Her words landed lightly. I leaned back, smiling for a while. When the amusement settled, I said a proper see you tomorrow. That unstarted round had already given me something to look forward to at home, alongside the real work waiting in the morning.
