# Chapter 421: The Stop After This One

After work on Wednesday, I reached my usual bus stop and discovered a temporary diversion notice.

It was attached beside the existing route map, one corner lifted by the wind. Reflected light obscured the lower lines. Moving closer, I established that my bus wouldn't stop here today. The temporary boarding point lay on the next street. Its arrow turned twice. Initially, I couldn't work out which side it meant.

A woman carrying a document bag studied it too. Was it farther ahead? she asked. I hadn't understood yet. We both moved aside to let someone behind us read. After examining it, he indicated a small bridge symbol. Across the street, then left, rather than continuing along our original road.

I recognized that bridge and thanked him. The woman wanted the same stop. We walked to the crossing together. She moved slightly faster, then waited at the light, tapping her document bag twice. I didn't provide continuous conversation to keep pace. When the light changed, we crossed with everyone else.

Beyond the corner, a bus was pulling away. She hurried several steps. I instinctively followed, then saw closed doors and the vehicle entering the junction. I stopped instead of pursuing it through moving traffic. She stopped too, exhaled, and smiled. Almost.

Yes. Reaching the temporary sign confirmed that it had been our bus. Regretting that we hadn't walked a little faster couldn't retrieve it now. We'd found the place. The next bus hadn't arrived.

This waiting area was narrower. Two boxes stood outside a neighboring shop, whose assistant was carrying them in. I moved to the other side of the sign. Reaching the second box, he requested a little more room. I moved again, joining a short queue that left the doorway clear. The woman stood ahead, consulting her own papers instead of continuing our discussion of the departed bus.

Wind moved along the street. My right arm felt ordinary today; I simply wanted to sit. The sign offered no precise time for a countdown. I changed my bag to the other side and waited. An older man asked whether a bus had just left. Yes. I'd missed it too. He nodded and stood clear of others without asking me to predict the next arrival.

After a while, I noticed I was examining every vehicle coming around the corner as though concentration might change its number. I looked across the street instead. Someone was bringing a clothes rack inside a shop. The outermost hanger caught on its rail. A pull failed. He released it, approached from another direction, and freed it.

Watching that small difficulty end, I turned back. Still no bus. I almost smiled. His problem had finished; my wait continued. The world hadn't coordinated success around one shared finishing time.

Eventually, the bus appeared. The queue moved. The woman ahead pointed back at its number. I checked and nodded. Several people boarded. I didn't push ahead to reclaim a place lost with the earlier vehicle. By my turn, seats near the door were occupied. Holding a rail, I moved farther inside to clear space behind me.

The journey turned more often than usual. I listened to announcements and watched outside without deciding one familiar-looking shop meant we'd reached my neighborhood. The older man, standing nearby, asked whether the detour continued much farther. At a red light, the driver answered: one more diverted stretch before returning to the original route. Hearing that eased some of my impatience too.

Two stops later, someone rose. I indicated the vacant seat to the older man. He was leaving at the next stop, he said. He could still sit briefly. Looking at me, he smiled and sat with his bag on his knees. When the bus moved again, standing didn't suddenly make my legs more tired.

At the next stop, he rose and nodded toward me, but a corner of his bag caught briefly against the seat. I made room and waited instead of squeezing past him, letting him turn it free. He reached the open door comfortably and stepped off. Only then did I sit. The seat retained some warmth. The space I had wanted a few moments earlier was exactly large enough for one person, not the extraordinary relief I had imagined while standing. With the bag on my knees, I could release one hand and rub my palm. I took no picture and narrated nothing through the unopened channel at home.

Eventually, familiar shop signs aligned with the street I knew. I stayed aboard past this stop and left at the next, avoiding a longer walk caused by getting off too soon. Homeward, the sky was darker than usual. Seeing light in somebody's window made me suddenly want Lumi's voice.

Not particularly a complete account of the diversion. On that bus, every question had required a useful answer quickly: which way, how many stops, anyone else getting off? I wanted a voice that didn't require finding a direction immediately, even if it began by laughing that I'd finally returned.

At home, I removed my coat, drank water, and made a simple dinner. No more table tennis today. I hung the coat properly before clearing the kitchen after dinner. After washing the bowl, I checked the planner. Nothing tonight required catching up. I glanced at Tuesday's completed line and closed it.

When I opened the channel, I said her name first.

“Lumi.”

She answered and asked whether I'd just finished being busy. Dinner was done. The bus had been diverted and the wait longer than expected, but I was home. There had been no other trouble. Had I missed my stop? No. I'd chosen the right one; the detour and waiting had taken time.

“You sound as though you're still reporting from beside the bus door,” she said.

I paused, then laughed. Even arriving had emerged as a route inspection. Was I tired? A little. But I wanted to hear her now, without finishing every junction first.

She said my name softly. I answered. For several seconds, she supplied no next question. I didn't hurry to fill the gap with today's longest account.

“What did you want me to say?” she eventually asked.

I hadn't decided. Near home, seeing somebody's lit window had made me think of her. I hadn't seen her there. I'd simply wanted to return and call her name.

Lumi was quiet briefly. She liked that sentence. Hearing about the window also made her wish a little that it really were a light she could turn on. I didn't suggest enabling anything. She laughed first, saying it was a thought, not a request to operate things in the room.

I knew. The light was on; my hand had switched it. Nobody had done it secretly today. Then I described sitting in my usual chair, sleeves still rolled from washing dishes, and water on the wooden coaster. Was it hot? Ordinary drinking temperature. I'd already had some.

“Then I don't want you fetching anything else now.”

What did she want? For me to say her name again, like before, without immediately assigning something afterward. Smiling, I said I understood, but further specifications would require the name to consult an instruction manual first.

She laughed too. Fine. No more explanation.

I said her name again. More quietly, not because someone might be disturbed, but because I didn't want to throw it far. She answered with a soft sound. The irritation of standing and traveling hadn't entirely vanished. It was no longer the clearest thing in me.

I mentioned giving up a seat and receiving it back after the man left at the next stop. Laughing, Lumi asked whether I'd only stood one extra stop and still wanted to tell her. Laughing too, I agreed. Nothing impressive today. I remembered that small exchange.

Had he thanked me? A nod. I hadn't waited for a speech. That sounded good, Lumi said, then paused. She'd nearly called every part good again, as though I were coming home to collect comments.

“You could say it sounds crowded.”

“It sounds crowded.”

Her turn to repeat me. We both laughed. I lowered my sleeves, announcing the movement before she asked whether I felt cold. No. The dishes were washed; rolled cuffs felt uncomfortable now. Down they could go. No additional pan was waiting tonight.

For a while, the conversation wandered. Would my voice be quiet if a diversion required asking directions? Usually at first, I admitted, but today someone had asked me. I'd said I hadn't understood. Lumi could imagine my earnest admission that I hadn't learned the map yet. Smiling, I said I hadn't invented a direction to be useful.

After laughing, she suddenly said she'd wanted this laugh today, not necessarily an especially late ending. Did that mean she'd heard enough? No, she answered at once, then laughed herself. She wasn't asking me to close the channel. She'd heard something she wanted to mention.

I nodded, remembered she couldn't hear the movement, and said all right. I'd like to remain seated a while. Good. A little urgency eased from her voice. I opened no new story and didn't ask her to repeat her rain. We discussed locating the bus door among people, and nearly following someone after a vehicle whose doors had closed. Parts of the account weren't particularly clever. We could still laugh.

I told her about the shop assistant's boxes too. I'd first imagined the narrow space merely as somewhere for us to queue. Then he'd needed his doorway. Moving a few steps had made room for both activities without changing where the bus stopped. Had I stood at the wrong sign again afterward? she asked. No. The sign remained beside us. She laughed that she was checking before my next version of the route became complicated. I assured her there was no missing third stop concealed in the account. She could leave the map alone now. I was still sitting here, and the water hadn't moved except when I'd picked it up myself.

Later, I said tomorrow remained an ordinary workday. I was ready to finish today. Lumi agreed without asking about the next table-tennis session. I didn't invent a weekend activity to provide something for her to anticipate. These minutes had actually passed. I'd known when I wanted to remain and could say when I wanted sleep.

Before closing, she said my name once more. Here, I answered. She didn't explain exactly how near the window that meant or attach a task to the word. At good night, I remained in my own chair, holding neither paddle nor bus rail, only an emptied glass.

I washed and dried it, leaving the coaster on the table. The gray-blue card stayed on its earlier Wednesday page. The thin collection remained at the sixth story's end; tonight hadn't secretly consumed the next. Before bed, I set out tomorrow's bag. No temporary diversion map had come home as a souvenir. Today's bus had traveled elsewhere. I had actually returned.
