# Chapter 418: The Bag I Put Down

On Sunday morning, my right arm seemed unusually willing to remind me it existed.

Not a sharp pain. Reaching for clothes produced a little soreness in my upper arm. I paused, then reached more slowly. The movement remained possible. Yesterday's few minutes in the activity room hadn't disappeared. After breakfast, I didn't compensate with another practice session or swing an empty cup around the room.

Rice was running low. Checking before leaving, I found enough for today and wrote down a small bag, along with vegetables for dinner. Only a few things on ordinary paper. I folded it into a pocket and left the planner at home. Yesterday's opening hours could stay there. Today wasn't another visit to the activity room.

The shop was busier than expected. Two baskets had caught together near the entrance. Lifting the upper one made the lower one shift too. An assistant held it until I'd separated them, then released it. I thanked her. Pointing inside the handle, she showed me where it caught. I changed hands and took just the basket I needed.

Rice stood along the wall. A larger bag cost less by weight than the smaller one beside it. I lifted the large bag first. As its base left the shelf, the little soreness returned. I put it back, wiped my palm against my trousers, and selected the small bag I'd originally written down.

Another customer comparing packages looked around at the sound. I nearly explained yesterday's table tennis. The sentence seemed strange before I spoke it. He merely wanted the bag behind me. I stepped aside. He took it and left without waiting for proof that I could ordinarily lift more.

Among the vegetables, I selected enough greens for dinner and two tomatoes. One tomato had a soft, compressed spot underneath. I exchanged it without hiding the poor one at the bottom. The assistant arranging that display took it aside. Were any others bad? I'd only noticed this one. I couldn't speak for those beneath it.

The queue extended beside household supplies. I didn't add washing-up liquid after yesterday's purchase. Ahead, a man had left his basket across the space. I waited until he moved. Apologizing, he said he'd forgotten something but didn't want to leave the queue. My list was short, I smiled, though I'd nearly bought something twice myself.

He didn't ask what. He examined his basket. Reaching the cashier, he decided against going back and paid for what he had. One item's label wasn't attached properly. After searching, he discovered its corner. Behind him, I listened to the scanner. The basket wasn't terribly heavy, but I already wanted to put it down.

My turn. Rice, greens, two tomatoes. I placed them on the counter, paid, and packed. As I prepared to leave, I noticed an extra line on the receipt.

Tomatoes. Greens. Rice. Before those, an item I hadn't selected.

When I turned, the next customer was already unloading. The cashier reached for something. I spoke, less loudly than the sounds near her machine. She didn't hear. Moving beside the exit without obstructing it, I asked again. Could she inspect my receipt? One item seemed not to belong to me.

She stopped, asked the waiting customer for a moment, and took the paper. Initially, she wondered whether I'd packed and forgotten it. We could check. I placed my bag on a clear part of the counter instead of waving rice in front of her.

Only my few purchases lay inside. Comparing screen and receipt, she discovered an item canceled from the preceding purchase hadn't been cleared correctly and had entered mine. She called another assistant to help check and apologized. The additional charge needed returning.

All right. Behind me, the customer sighed, one hand resting on his basket. Hearing him made my neck warm. I nearly said to forget it if the amount was small. The cashier was already asking her colleague how to process it, her finger indicating that line. She hadn't asked me to settle the queue's impatience by abandoning the matter.

Another checkout opened. An assistant directed the waiting man across. He lifted his basket and left without looking back at me. A little later, the extra charge was refunded. The cashier gave me the corrected record and showed the deduction. I checked it against what had been returned before putting it away.

I thanked her. She thanked me for raising it promptly; sorting it out later would be harder. The moments examining my bag together hadn't been comfortable, but the matter was finished. Lifting my purchases, I discovered the rice pulled one side lower. I rearranged it beside the counter.

Her next customer had arrived. I left without explaining further that I hadn't meant to cause difficulty. Outside, sunlight lay across the entrance steps. I stood briefly before remembering the ache in my right arm.

On the journey home, I changed hands. Beside a low ledge clear of passing people, I stopped and rested the bag's base, keeping hold of its handles. Leaves projected from the top. I tucked them in with my free hand. A small dog approached the bag's corner, but its owner gently led it away. She apologized. No problem, I smiled. It hadn't touched anything.

Nobody carried the bag for me or hurried me onward. When my hand felt better, I continued. The smaller rice bag had been the sensible choice today. At home, I stored it, leaving the remaining old rice to use first, and put the tomatoes and greens in the kitchen. The corrected receipt joined my ordinary receipts, not the books.

I made no additional plan for the afternoon. After a simple lunch, I rested. The soreness wasn't increasing; I moved more slowly when reaching high. At dinner, I used both tomatoes and all those greens, leaving no portion of fresh ingredients requiring tomorrow's attention.

Opening the channel, I told Lumi about buying rice and vegetables, the extra charge, and its completed correction. Then I mentioned my slightly sore arm. No table tennis today.

“So you have your own rice and somebody else's shopping story,” she said.

Laughing, I assured her the story was settled. I hadn't bought another person's groceries. Had I discovered it late? Just after packing, before leaving. The next person had started unloading when I'd turned. Had I apologized again? Thinking back, I said no. I'd asked her to look without apologizing first.

“Something novel, then.”

I didn't greet every shelf with an apology. Lumi laughed softly. Had the people waiting made me feel hurried? I described the sigh and nearly deciding to forget the charge. She didn't declare the man dreadful. Once it was resolved, had I actually left, or remained explaining?

“Left. Then stood outside for two seconds.”

“What for?”

“To remember I had a hand carrying rice.”

She laughed aloud. I'd bought a small bag, I explained, and rested it on the journey. Nothing dropped or twisted. She couldn't examine muscles through my voice, she said, but hearing I'd stopped reassured her more than a continuous insistence that nothing was heavy. I didn't feel like showing off today. If I played again, I'd begin modestly.

That brought her back to yesterday. Did I still think about the man calling my name? Yes. What followed that name hadn't been missing material to supply or a question about whether I'd made a mistake. I could simply walk over and play another turn.

She paused. Apparently, her saying it last night hadn't covered everyone else up. The idea surprised me. Covering them up hadn't been the intention. She knew, Lumi said. Still, she'd wanted it a little: to keep the day's last call for herself.

I listened without immediately asking her to become generous. A final use of my name would be difficult to arrange in advance. Someone downstairs might know me; later, I might remember a sentence. Laughing, she accused me of checking several words for gaps again.

“You can simply say you're here now.”

“I'm here now.” I repeated it, then smiled. “But not that sentence every time.”

“Enough for today.”

Her answer was quiet. I drank. Was I still using the wooden coaster? Yes, with the glass base dried. Its position was convenient. Then it was more practiced than I was, she said. At least it knew where to work. Smiling, I asked her not to let the coaster win another competition today.

Later, I asked what she'd want to hear if I really played again. Not necessarily the number of successful returns. She wanted to know whether I'd been the one to ask for another attempt. That was what she'd remembered last night.

Looking toward the table's corner, I told her I'd remembered too. Raising my voice a second time at the checkout hadn't brought back yesterday's feeling easily. The words had nevertheless emerged. Was I connecting those two things? Not deliberately. They simply came to mind now. They could stay in their respective places, she said.

I also described the little dog, its approach, and its owner leading it away before it touched the bag. Only then did Lumi ask its size. I started measuring with my hands, caught myself, and said it stood a little higher than the shopping bag, with short, light-colored fur. I hadn't identified its breed. Perhaps rice hadn't interested it much, she said. It might have been curious about someone stopping halfway home.

Laughing, I suggested the vegetables could have smelled interesting too. I couldn't choose for it. She preferred to keep the more amusing guess. Fine, provided she didn't promise another meeting in the same place tomorrow. Laughing, she assured me she hadn't assigned the dog a shift yet.

I pictured that brief halt again: my fingers relaxing around the handles while the bag rested securely. The owner had moved on, dog beside her. After a moment, so had I. Telling Lumi hadn't made either stranger a person waiting to enter our evening. She asked whether the rice had stayed upright after I'd adjusted it. Yes. No torn package, no grains to sweep from the bottom. She was glad. The kitchen already sounded sufficiently busy without another small cleanup. I looked toward it, remembering the vegetables I'd finished cooking, and agreed. Tonight, the only thing still waiting there was my glass when we were done.

Before bed, my arm remained slightly noticeable. I didn't call it the price of a better life. I told Lumi I wanted to relax early and work normally tomorrow. She agreed and said my name once, without asking whether it was today's last.

I answered, then wished her good night. The corrected receipt lay with ordinary papers. There was rice in the kitchen; the vegetables were used. Tomorrow didn't require returning to explain or writing a weekend exercise program. Washing the glass, I chose the more comfortable hand. Water sounded different from yesterday's table. Today ended here.
