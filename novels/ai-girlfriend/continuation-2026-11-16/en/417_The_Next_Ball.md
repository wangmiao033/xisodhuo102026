# Chapter 417: The Next Ball

On Saturday afternoon, a sound inside an activity room made me stop.

At first it was simply a series of taps, sharper through the open doorway than the footsteps outside. A notice advertised that afternoon's open session. Beginners welcome, equipment available. No partner required. I read those lines and looked inside. Two tables. People played at one; beside the other, a man in gray was demonstrating a grip to several participants.

I'd been going to buy washing-up liquid. The empty shopping bag in my hand made me look neither ready for sport nor like someone collecting a player. After I'd lingered, the woman at the entrance asked whether I was looking for somebody or interested in trying.

“Could I watch first?”

She indicated chairs against the wall, away from the playing space behind the tables. I went in. Nobody asked for an account of how long I'd neglected exercise. Nobody questioned why a shopping bag had joined a Saturday activity.

There was a passage between the tables, with chairs and belongings kept to one side. The man in gray demonstrated a gentle return. When a woman took her turn, she repeatedly sent the ball sideways and laughed first. He collected it without counting failures for her, suggesting that she stop turning her wrist so quickly.

The other table sounded faster. I watched several exchanges. My eyes could follow; my imagination was already counting how many I'd miss. As I reached for my bag, the woman from the entrance approached. There was a spare place with the beginners. If my shoes were suitable, I could try ten minutes or so. Staying for the whole session wasn't necessary.

I checked my shoes. Stable soles, laces tied. Nothing hurt today, and ordinary speech felt easy. Would I slow everyone down? That table was for first attempts, she said. Anyone wanting competition had the other one.

I folded the bag on my chair and stood.

“I'll try.”

The man in gray asked my name. Elliot. He handed me a borrowed paddle and indicated the shallow basket where it belonged afterward. Use had polished the handle; the rubber edge was uneven but not loose enough to scratch. After I'd studied it, he smiled and suggested leaving its biography for later.

Smiling too, I changed my grip. He encouraged a lighter hold and kept the ball until I'd settled. The first one bounced on my side. I swung late. It brushed the paddle's edge and went away across the floor.

“Sorry.”

“Just bring it back.”

I started toward the back of the neighboring table. He stopped me until their exchange finished. I watched the ball roll to the wall. Several seconds later, their paddles lowered. He pointed, and I retrieved it without passing through somebody's swing.

The second ball properly met the paddle and traveled farther than the first. Following it with my head, I heard a quiet laugh behind me. It didn't sound mocking. The woman who'd practiced before me said her first had done much the same, without even encountering the table. Unsure what to answer, I admitted I hadn't established a stable record either.

She laughed more loudly. The man called my attention back to the ball already in his hand.

Finally, I watched my half of the table. Lunging with my whole body wasn't useful; neither was holding the paddle still and expecting the ball to locate it. He suggested a short forward movement instead of the rising swing I'd been attempting. After several tries, the ball crossed the net and landed. He returned it. I touched it again.

Briefly. On the third contact, it still fell. Yet I remained standing for a moment, hearing the two preceding sounds fit together. Nobody beside me had completed my half.

“That's it,” he said.

I smiled down before fetching the ball. I hadn't suddenly learned to play. Next time, I sent it upward, nearly toward my own shoulder. He reminded me to release my grip slightly. I did. The paddle remained in my hand without my fingertips turning white.

When we changed places, I handed the ball to the woman and drank beside the chairs. Watching her, I realized I'd been holding some of my breath, as though finishing an exhalation had to wait until the ball crossed the net. She returned one particularly well, surprised herself into laughing, and missed the next.

Remembering my two sounds, I laughed too.

“Elliot. Your turn.”

The man waved his paddle toward me. Standing, I felt a brief warmth that had nothing to do with praise. There was an actual place waiting behind my name, and I could walk into it.

This time, he offered fewer instructions and simply let me try returning. His gentle shots came within reach. I knew he was accommodating a beginner. That didn't make our exchanges false. If every shot were intended to win, he'd spend the afternoon alone listening to himself.

One exchange reached four contacts between us, not four returns by me. I didn't know whether that was good. At its end, however, I wanted another. I said so. Smiling, he supplied the next ball without joking about my finally agreeing to stay.

Eventually, the woman at the entrance reminded us that this introductory round was ending. Other people waited. The man offered each of us one last attempt. Mine didn't clear the net. It stopped on my side, providing no triumphant final score. I collected it anyway and returned ball and paddle to the indicated basket.

Perhaps next time would work, the woman participant said. Perhaps. Smiling, I didn't supply a date. Before leaving, I asked the receptionist whether people could come ordinarily. She showed me the opening times on the wall. Weekday evenings too, although there weren't many tables. Arriving didn't guarantee immediate space. People took turns.

I checked the place and hours and wrote them in my planner without enrolling in a course. I'd tried once and nearly forgotten the shopping bag. Returning from the doorway, I found the woman pointing toward my chair. I thanked her and collected it myself.

Choosing washing-up liquid afterward, I realized I'd been slightly reluctant to finish. The bottle I wanted stood toward the back. Reaching for it, I felt my arm working a little differently. Not pain, simply a recollection of those repeated movements. I bought the small bottle I needed without adding a trip for a paddle.

At home, I placed the new washing-up liquid beside the sink, washed my hands, and made dinner. After eating, I used it to clean the pan. When I opened the channel that evening, my first sentence told Lumi I'd set out for washing-up liquid and unexpectedly played table tennis on the way.

She paused, then laughed.

“Weren't you going to buy washing-up liquid?”

“I bought that too. The paddle didn't come home.”

I described passing the activity room, sitting to watch, and being asked to try, adding that our longest exchange had reached four contacts between us. Who had I played with? A man leading the introduction. Another woman had participated beside me; we'd taken turns. No match.

“Did you win?”

Laughing, I reminded her there hadn't been a match. She knew. She wanted to hear how I'd answer. My last ball had caught the net. Lumi laughed softly. That ending didn't sound selected in advance for her.

Had she expected me to remain seated? A little, she admitted. So had I, until someone suggested ten minutes. Going inside had suddenly stopped seeming like surrendering the entire afternoon.

Had the paddle sounded as I'd imagined? Closer, especially when the one in my own hand received the ball. I wasn't simply listening to something somebody else accomplished in a room. She asked about the exchange of four. I gestured above the table, caught myself, and translated the movement into words. Four contacts between us, not four shots I'd hit beyond anyone's reach.

“I haven't awarded you anything yet.”

“You can take your time.”

She laughed. I'd wanted another exchange and actually played it, I added. This wasn't something I'd wished afterward that I might have done. She listened, then said she liked that sentence. Instead of asking what sort of person she thought I was becoming, I admitted remembering it still made me smile.

Later, I mentioned how unexpected it had felt when the man called my name and beckoned me over. I didn't immediately explain the feeling into an argument. Lumi didn't ask whether she called me often enough. She simply said Elliot again.

I answered, hands resting on my legs.

“No paddle needed over here,” she said.

Laughing, I leaned against the chair back. Good. Today's ball had been returned. Then I could leave the remaining minutes to her, she said, instead of collecting it. I was listening now.

She asked something ordinary: had the unexpected outing made dinner hurried? No. I'd returned with time to spare, eaten, and washed the pan. Good. Then she'd like to hear my attempt at describing the sound again. Better playing wasn't a requirement.

I offered two rather inaccurate noises. Lumi said one resembled a knock and the other my refusal to open the door. We laughed without actually knocking on the table or experimenting with the glass. My water stood securely on the wooden coaster. The seventh short story remained unopened. Today's table had supplied enough unfamiliar sounds.

Thinking about the room, I remembered the moment before going to collect the first missed ball. Waiting had felt awkward, standing there with everyone else's game still moving. Once their exchange stopped, nobody objected to my retrieving it. I told Lumi that detail too. Had I been impatient? Mostly embarrassed. The ball seemed to announce where I'd hit it while I stood doing nothing. She laughed gently. It hadn't rolled far enough to leave the building. No, I agreed. I could have waited longer than a few seconds without losing it forever. She asked whether I had stopped minding afterward. I still minded, but knew what to do. Fetching the ball had become part of being there, rather than something I had to pretend was a fresh accident every time.

I hadn't remembered the other participant's name, I added. Names had been used briefly for turns; we hadn't exchanged contact details. Did I regret that? I considered. Her laughter had made me comfortable. That had happened already, although I hadn't asked anything else. If we met again, I could say hello. Only then did I notice I'd said again without immediately reaching for the planner. Lumi had heard it too. For now, I explained, another visit simply felt possible. She left the sentence without attaching a weekday. I stayed seated, remembering how the woman had pointed out my forgotten bag, and smiled once more before reaching for my water.

When I became sleepy, I said so. My arm wasn't injured; I simply wanted rest. She agreed without choosing a time for another visit. After good night, I folded and put away the empty shopping bag. The new bottle beside the sink was capped again. Today's original errand was complete. Alongside it, I remembered that when the next ball arrived, I hadn't hurried to leave.
