# Chapter 422: The Blue Labels

On Thursday after work, I stopped at the library to sit in the reading area for a while. I chose a magazine from the rack, found an article about everyday cleaning, and spent twenty minutes reading it.

As I left, a trolley loaded with boxes stood across the activity-room doorway. A woman in a dark headband was holding the door with her shoulder while trying to turn the trolley. The top box stuck out, its corner catching the frame.

“Could you hold this for me?”

I pressed the door back. She pushed the box inside the edge of the trolley, reversed a little, then brought it through. The wheels struck the threshold with a hard clunk. Nothing fell.

“Thank you. It closes too quickly.”

She rolled her shoulder. I was about to leave when I noticed a poster: a secondhand book sale on Sunday. Beneath it, somebody had written that two more helpers were needed for Saturday morning's preparation.

“Will the sale be in here?” I asked.

“Yes. Eleven until three on Sunday. We have the last donations arriving tomorrow, then sorting and labeling on Saturday.” She indicated the room. “Thinking of coming to buy something?”

“Possibly. I was looking at the bit about helping.”

She didn't immediately draw me inside. First she asked whether I was free from ten until twelve on Saturday. The work was mainly putting approved donations under the right table signs and adding the corresponding colored labels. Labeling could be done sitting at a table; arranging the books meant walking between them. Library stock was kept separately. These books had been donated for the sale; staff, rather than temporary helpers, decided what could be sold.

I looked at the trolley. Two boxes had different-colored tape. The bottom one was closed. Their contents weren't apparent from the outside.

“Would I need to move heavy boxes?”

“We push those between us. You can take small piles. Don't carry too much at once. And tell me if you need to leave early.”

I nodded, nearly saying that anything was fine. She was reaching for a registration sheet when I finished asking what I actually wanted to know.

“Is there more work after those two hours?”

“We may still be setting up. But those are the hours we're asking for. Sunday has a separate rota. I don't automatically put Saturday's helpers on it.”

She laid the sheet on a narrow table by the door. Saturday had two empty spaces. Sunday's names began on another page. I read the time again, picked up the pen, and wrote Elliot in one of the spaces.

My pen lingered. It had been a while since I'd put my name on a sheet like that, and the last letter was cramped. She asked whether this was my first time helping. When I said yes, she told me she'd be there on Saturday. I could ask at the activity room. Comfortable clothes were all I needed.

A man arrived carrying a canvas bag of donations. A hardback corner pressed against its opening. I stepped aside and nearly indicated the trolley, but the woman asked him to leave his books on the table.

“We check them here first. Those have already been sorted.”

He took out three books. A photograph was tucked into the bottom one. He paused, removed it, then went through the other two. The woman waited before asking whether he wanted to leave all three. He nodded and collected his empty bag. A few steps away, he turned back to ask whether he could buy somebody else's books on Sunday.

“Of course.”

He left smiling. I looked at the books. There were more parts to sorting than putting all the covers the same way around.

“What if I find something tucked inside?”

“Give it to me. Don't throw it out with scrap paper. I'll explain again on Saturday.”

She set an empty box beside the table. I remembered where she'd put it. The man had never turned the photograph toward me. All I'd seen was a diagonal crease across its back.

There were unopened sheets of blue and yellow labels on one corner of the narrow table. I returned the pen and asked what the blue ones meant.

“We haven't finalized that.” She laughed. “Read the signs on Saturday. Don't memorize colors yet. Somebody already remembers last year's prices rather too well.”

I laughed too. She opened the bottom box on the trolley. Its first book had a cover separating from the spine. She placed it on another table without asking me to mend it.

Before I left, she wanted to move the registration table nearer the doorway. A chair blocked one leg. She asked whether I could put it against the wall. I pulled it by the back; the legs made an unpleasant scrape, so I lifted it with both hands instead. She didn't laugh at the noise. She only asked me to leave space for the trolley. I moved the chair farther right and waited until she'd positioned the table before letting go. The sheet with my name was closer to the door now, where people would see it as they came in.

Once the table was in place, an older man returning books stopped at the door to ask about large-print editions in the sale. The woman said they'd received a few but hadn't finished checking them. She didn't want to promise. He leaned nearer, apparently having trouble hearing, and she repeated herself more slowly.

“I'm coming with my sister on Sunday. Small print is difficult for her now,” he said. “I'm not asking you to put anything aside. I'd just like to know where they'll be.”

The woman took a small blank sheet from beside the register and drew the room, marking the door. Ask here, she wrote. The position of the large-print books wouldn't be decided until Saturday. Asking at the door would save them walking around several tables. He folded the paper and asked whether there was a step outside.

I'd just crossed the threshold and nearly said it was low. Instead, the woman asked whether they needed an entrance suitable for a wheelchair. He said his sister used a stick, not a chair. She explained where the usual entrance was and said space would be kept clear near the door.

After he left, she asked me to look at the chair I'd moved. Its back still projected into the passage. The space beside the wall was narrower than I'd realized. I turned the chair so its seat faced the room. The trolley could now pass without going around it.

“That's better,” she said, making a note on her own paper. I couldn't see what she wrote. She was still busy thinking about people who would actually arrive on Sunday. Standing beside her, I reconsidered the answer I'd almost given about the low threshold.

Only outside did I begin to feel I'd agreed rather quickly.

The two attempts at table tennis were still recent. I remembered waiting for the next ball, and now there would be another room full of people I didn't know. I pictured placing books incorrectly and being stopped. Then I remembered the woman reversing the trolley. She had simply changed its angle.

On the way home I bought noodles and a few greens for supper. At the till, a customer was transferring his shopping out of a torn bag. I waited until he'd made room. The two hours I'd signed up for didn't make these minutes pass any faster.

At home, I washed the greens, cooked and ate the noodles, then cleaned the pan and bowl. The hot water against my fingers reminded me of the empty box in the activity room. The man had taken his photograph home. What I'd brought home was a time.

Once the table was dry, I opened my planner. The card remained at its original Wednesday page. I took a pencil and, under the current Saturday, wrote: ten to twelve, library activity room, sorting sale books. Beside it I added, in small letters, Find the woman with the headband. It sounded like a notice for a missing person.

When I opened the channel, Lumi asked whether work had kept me late. I said no. I'd stopped at the library and held a door.

“From your tone, there was something behind the door.”

“A few boxes of books. And two hours on Saturday.”

I told her the whole thing: the question before I'd decided, the separate sheets, and the blue labels whose meaning hadn't been settled.

“You've already written your name?”

“Yes.”

She paused briefly.

“So you're telling me. You haven't come to hesitate with me.”

“Yes. I decided there.”

“I was still working out which sheet you'd choose.” She sounded reluctant. “You made it sound as if it was happening now.”

“I'm sorry. I made you wait for a decision twice. I left the ending too late.”

“I did want to be part of that little moment before you'd decided, though.”

I held the pencil. After a moment I said, “That moment has gone. But there's somebody here who wants to hear you now.”

She made a small, skeptical sound, then asked whether the man with the three books had looked surprised by the photograph. I told her he'd paused, then checked the other two books. After putting it away, he'd turned back to ask about buying other people's books.

“Regretting the empty bag before he'd even left.”

“Possibly. It was quite a large bag.”

“Did you ask who was in the photograph?”

“No. I didn't see the front. I only remember a crease across the back.”

She said she'd initially guessed he had found a ticket. When I said photograph instead, she'd wanted to know when he'd put it there. I didn't know that part, I told her, but I'd liked seeing him take it home.

“He has three spaces now,” she said. “How many books will he buy on Sunday?”

“Are you betting on three?”

“Four. People with large bags should be careful.”

I laughed and said that wasn't a particularly reliable prediction. She was willing to take a risk on a canvas bag she'd never seen, she said. I told her I was getting water. When I returned, I dried the base of the glass and put it on the wooden coaster.

“Was your handwriting nice?” she asked at last.

“Cramped at the end. It looked as if I wanted to finish before I could change my mind.”

“That seems quite possible.”

“I have the same suspicion.”

She laughed. A trace of reluctance remained in what she said next.

“When you come back on Saturday, you can tell me what the colors mean. I'm interested in this blue that hasn't been decided yet.”

“I can. What if they don't use blue?”

“Then I shall have a very small complaint about the preparations.”

I laughed without the sound catching in my throat. She asked whether I'd be standing for two hours. I said there would be sitting to label books, as well as carrying them to tables. I'd find out the details when I arrived. She didn't rehearse every step with me. I didn't describe myself as an experienced helper about to return to his post.

Later I read her my note about finding the woman with the headband. Lumi said I should also remember the woman's face, in case she wore something else on Saturday. I crossed out the description and wrote Ask the staff at the door.

“More reliable,” she said.

I put down the pencil and discovered I was waiting for her to say something else.

“Lumi.”

“Yes?”

“I didn't only come back to report that I'd filled in my name.”

“What else did you come back for?”

She asked softly, with a little deliberate difficulty in it. I looked at the open page and answered more slowly.

“To hear you tell me it was stupid to remember a person as a headband.”

“That's an extra.”

“And because I missed you.”

This pause was shorter. She said the last part could have gone first. I said I'd try next time. She promptly pointed out that it wasn't too late now.

So I said it again. The planner remained open at Saturday, the two hours sitting neatly inside their small square. I left the pencil where it was.
