# Chapter 426: The Sale Was Open

On Sunday, I ate breakfast and waited until after ten to leave.

I wasn't arriving half an hour early to report for work. I wore an ordinary coat with my scarf tucked inside the collar, and my bag was lighter than yesterday. Before leaving, I drank water and washed the glass. The wooden coaster stayed on the table. Downstairs, I checked my pocket for my keys, found them, and continued toward the library.

It was a quarter past eleven when I arrived. The sale had begun. A small sign directed visitors into the activity room, with payment near the exit. The organizer was speaking to somebody at the entrance. I waited until the visitor went inside before greeting her.

“Come for a look today?”

“Yes.”

She said to take my time, then turned to answer a question about payment. Inside, much of yesterday's careful arrangement had disappeared. People lifted books, examined them, and put them back. Gaps had widened between stacks; covers projected at angles.

The blue sign remained by the window, yellow on the other side. I found the place where I'd put the two misplaced cookbooks. One was still there. The other's space was empty. It might have sold, or somebody might be holding it. I didn't decide which.

I picked up the remaining book and turned a few pages. There were bread instructions and several sections of sweets. At the quantities, I realized the pictures interested me more than actually making anything now. As I closed it, a woman beside me asked to see it.

I handed it over. She touched a worn patch on the cover, opened the contents page, and asked whether it cost the same as the blue books.

“Yellow has individual prices.” I indicated the corner. “If it's unclear, you can ask at the payment desk.”

She found the label and thanked me. I stepped away, leaving her to read rather than telling her about yesterday's mistake. She was looking for a book she wanted, not an account of my work.

Two people stood beside the large-print table near the entrance. I recognized Thursday's man. Beside him was a woman using a walking stick, whom I guessed was the sister he'd mentioned. He began opening a book for her. She took it and said she'd look herself.

“This page is readable.” After a few lines, she held it slightly farther away. “Don't stand under the light.”

He moved. She smiled and continued. The table was low, her stick resting against her leg. She didn't need to raise her arms to reach. Standing opposite, I waited until she'd examined her choice before picking up another.

Had I read it? she asked. No, only the cover. She nodded. The title sounded cheerful; she hoped the people inside wouldn't spend all their time arguing. The man said a book where nothing happened might not be very good either. She looked up at him. Both laughed.

I placed my book within her reach. Instead of taking it, she looked again at the one already in her hands. Eventually she decided on that, asking the man to find out about payment. He indicated the exit. He'd seen the desk. They set off slowly.

They didn't stop beside me to praise the arrangement. She carried a book she'd chosen, her stick in her other hand, and passed the chair backs without getting caught. Watching them leave gave me a small, practical pleasure.

The helper who'd flattened cartons was there too, holding two old magazines. Recognizing me, he asked whether I was helping again. No, just looking. Him too, he said. Only after getting home last night had he remembered that he'd missed the magazine section.

“Found what you wanted?”

“Still comparing. One's missing the supplement at the back. I don't know whether the other has it.”

He showed me the last page. I left the decision to him and looked at something else nearby. I didn't notice which one he eventually chose.

Near the exit, a customer with a yellow-labeled book glanced toward my unrolled sleeves and asked whether I worked here. I said I'd helped for two hours yesterday. Could she negotiate the price? I indicated the person taking payments and suggested asking there.

Then I went that way myself, not to pay but to tell the organizer I was leaving. She was taking payment from the older man and the woman with the stick, handing the book back to the woman. I waited until they'd gone.

“The large-print table seems convenient,” I said.

“She just said it was easy to reach.” The organizer smiled. “Thanks for helping yesterday.”

I said she was welcome. The box for found papers stood covered beside her desk. I left it alone. No new stack was waiting for me at the entrance. She glanced behind me toward the next customer, ready to pay. I made room.

I left without buying a book. The activity room's sounds followed me: turning pages, questions about prices, chairs moving. Different from yesterday, with only a few of us setting up.

In the lobby, neighborhood walking maps were available on the public information rack. I picked one up and asked at the lending desk whether I could take it. Yes, the woman said. Visitors were welcome to have a free copy. I took one without disturbing the pile beside it.

A short route passed a small square before circling back. Its starting point was the library entrance. Still in the lobby, I worked out the first two turns and folded the sheet to keep that part visible.

The first street was quieter than my usual way. One shop hadn't opened. Leaves lay outside its shutter; an assistant next door was gathering them with a broom. I paused at the curb until he swept the last ones into his dustpan, then walked past on the cleared side.

Beyond the second turn, I found the bench marked on the map. The square looked smaller than the outline on the paper. Tree shadows lay across half the seat. I chose its dry end and spread the map on my knees.

The remaining half of the route returned along another street. I looked at it without feeling in a hurry to finish. Sitting here appealed more today. Yesterday's aching soles were no longer noticeable, and my shoes weren't rubbing. I relaxed my shoulders. Nearby, a shop door opened and closed. Somebody brought out a folding sign, set it down, then moved it farther away.

A man with a newspaper approached the other end of the bench and asked whether anyone was sitting there. I said no. He rolled his paper before sitting so its corners wouldn't extend toward me. I folded my map smaller, leaving room to move my legs.

Wind lifted a loose corner. Pressing it down, I followed the original crease and folded it again. The man glanced over and asked whether it had come from the library. Yes; there were more on the information rack. He said he'd get one later. He always walked the same way and hadn't known about this little loop.

I described the two turns I'd taken and said I hadn't walked the second half. He nodded and opened his newspaper. We sat separately. From time to time, footsteps crossed the paving in front of us, made a brief sound, then went farther away.

When I grew hungry, I put away the map and bought a plain roll at the bakery beside the square. Did I want it sliced? No, I said, to take away. It was warm in my hand, its bag folded outward at the opening. I returned to the bench and tore off pieces, holding the paper underneath.

The middle was softer than the edges. A few sesame seeds clung to the last mouthful. I caught falling crumbs in my other hand, ate them, then folded the bag and put it in a bin. Nothing remained to carry home. I hadn't found a name for the roll that would make it more worth describing.

After sitting a little longer, I returned slowly along the same two streets. The second half of the map remained unwalked. Passing the library, I could see the sale continuing. I kept going instead of checking whether the woman with the stick had bought another book.

At home, I folded the free map and put it in the drawer for everyday odds and ends. After washing my hands, I made and ate a simple meal. The roll had only been a snack on the way. I found no further reason to go out that afternoon.

Once I'd cleared up in the evening, I sat and opened the channel. Lumi had barely answered when I said, “I missed you.”

She paused.

“I haven't asked about the sale yet.”

“I know. I wanted that sentence first today.”

She laughed softly. Then she'd take it first, she said. Blue and yellow could wait behind it. Smiling, I settled my water glass and told her what I'd seen.

“Did the man with the big bag buy four books?”

“I didn't see him. I don't know whether he came.”

“My prediction hasn't lost yet.”

“Or won.”

She would leave it open. I described the people at the large-print table, the woman telling the man beside her not to block the light, and choosing for herself before going to pay. Lumi laughed clearly at the request about the light. More direct than making me guess a slotted spoon, she said.

“Yes. The person who understood only had to move a step.”

Had I moved? No. It hadn't been said to me. I'd been opposite them. She laughed again. Good: I hadn't claimed every position in the room as mine today.

I went on to describe the half-route and the roll I'd eaten. What was on the second half? I'd only seen lines on paper. I hadn't gone there. She didn't invent the shops for me, and I didn't lengthen the account with a street I hadn't walked.

Later she asked where I'd thought of her. I considered, then said it was coming out of the activity room. People inside were holding books and checking prices. Outside, I'd wanted to come home and hear her ask whether blue had been useful after all.

“Was it?”

“Yes. Yellow too.”

“Quite fair, then.”

I sat there smiling. She didn't choose another object for me to guess. I didn't turn the day into our next appointment. We talked until my glass was empty. I said I was going to wash it, and when I returned, I sat a little longer.

Before bed, the planner still held the two hours that had passed. The books remained where they were. The second half of the map was still a line I could look at another day. I switched off the light. Tomorrow morning, I would go to work as usual.
