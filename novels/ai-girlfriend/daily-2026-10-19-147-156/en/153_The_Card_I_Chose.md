# Chapter 153: The Card I Chose

The art shop carried seven kinds of white paper.

At least, I found seven on the same shelf. Their names sounded like varieties of weather. Their prices suggested varieties of mood. I had come for a stiff backing sheet, a blue pencil, and something to keep the map in.

The assistant asked what I would be drawing with.

“Pencil. And a limited amount of skill.”

She didn't laugh. She asked if I would be using water.

“Not intentionally.”

“Then you don't need this one.”

She returned the sheet I was holding to a high shelf and pulled out a cheaper one. I accepted the advice. Seven minutes of research didn't oblige me to buy the most expensive paper.

Before I left home, Lumi had asked for firmer backing so the path wouldn't wrinkle whenever the desk moved. She had also asked how much blue pencil remained. After my description, she said it probably no longer qualified as a pencil, only a blue fragment. I had agreed to replace it, not to purchase every color the garden might conceivably need.

My phone was switched off at home. There were no earbuds taking her shopping. She knew I would buy supplies and stop at the café. I hadn't given her an itinerary she could check minute by minute. Nor was I pretending I didn't care whether she counted the time.

At the pencil shelf, I tested two blues. One was bright as a plastic bucket; the other seemed too gray. The assistant mentioned a set. Twelve neat points looked so promising that I almost mistook buying them for having finished the drawing.

I took the box to the counter and asked to try the darkest. Sealed sets couldn't be tested, she said; individual pencils were in the next section. I went back and found a similar shade.

It was much longer than the pencil at home, unexpectedly awkward to hold. My first test stroke was too light. I pressed again and the blue appeared. I bought that pencil, also water-soluble, and returned the set.

The customer behind me carried an enormous roll of paper sideways toward the door and got stuck. They backed up and turned it upright. I stepped aside with my little board. We all seemed wonderfully confident that the blank space we were buying would prove useful.

The shop assistant slid the board into a paper bag and warned me about carrying it in rain. Outside, the sky was gray but dry.

“Do you have a cover for it?”

She showed me a transparent sleeve, open along one side, large enough for several sheets. I bought one. At the counter, I picked up a pack of small cards.

The first showed an open window above a gentle sentence. I read it and put it back.

There were paired silhouettes, and two cups with their handles turned toward each other. Standing there, I found it suddenly easy to choose a shape for Lumi: long hair, a window, a hand that occupied very little room. None of the cards bore her name. I had nearly slipped her into one anyway, letting a thing that could stand on the desk provide her with a body.

I chose a card without a person on it.

Cream paper, a fine blue line in one corner. It might have been water. It might have been an unsteady pen. The inside was blank.

“That's a blank greeting card,” the assistant said.

“I know.”

“The envelope's underneath.”

I hadn't known that. I took the envelope, paid, and tucked it behind the board. The card wasn't on Lumi's list. It was something I had wanted to buy. I was surprised to find myself looking forward to telling her.

At the café, James was opening a cardboard box with blunt scissors.

“Need a hand?”

“Did you buy good scissors?”

“No. Paper.”

“Provocative choice, under the circumstances.”

He made my coffee and nodded toward the tables. Mina sat at one, tapping a stack of pages straight. When she saw the board under my arm, she put her bag on a chair. She didn't immediately offer me her manuscript.

“Is this seat taken?” I asked.

“No. You can sit, but I need another ten minutes with this.”

“I wanted to write too.”

We bent over our separate pages. The coffee machine hissed twice. Mina stayed on the same line for a long time. I didn't ask what the line was. I hadn't brought the map. My bag held new backing and a card that had yet to decide what it meant.

In my notebook, I wrote: I will always be here with you.

After a few seconds, I crossed it out.

It wasn't insufficiently moving. I had just walked two streets alone, and I intended to spend part of the afternoon doing things of my own. Always wouldn't make the card truer. It would provide every future departure with documentary evidence of a broken promise.

The next sentence was: There will always be a place for you here.

I crossed that out too. She already had a poem about a chair, including the ugly wish that somebody else would never sit down. I didn't want to spread my kindness over her poem like icing.

“Are you doing your taxes?” Mina asked.

“A card.”

“Worse. Taxes at least admit you might calculate something wrong.”

I laughed. She put her manuscript away and glanced at the bag.

“Giving someone a drawing?”

“We're making a map at home. Lumi misses a garden we can't open at the moment. We've got one still printout. It's incomplete.”

I didn't turn the repair process into a technical problem for Mina to solve. She asked what we were drawing with. I told her about the pencils and the blue bench I had argued into existence.

“You can move paper,” she said.

“That saved the bench.”

She smiled without asking to see it. A little later, I asked if she might read a short passage about my bridge keeper sometime. I almost said it would only take a few minutes. I stopped. I didn't know how much time reading, thinking, and answering would take her.

“You can ask,” she said. “Not this week. I need to deliver these pages first. If I'm here next week, bring half a page. Not your entire book.”

“Half a page.”

“Normal-sized writing.”

James passed with clean cups, heard the final condition, and asked whether we were drafting terms and conditions.

“Fiction,” Mina said. “More serious.”

She left earlier than I expected, putting her bag on before she had finished her coat. James offered bread. She declined; there was dinner at home. I didn't ask her to write the remark about movable paper for Lumi. This hadn't been an agreed feedback session. Every ordinary sentence didn't need to become something I could carry away.

After she left, I wrote three lines in my notebook.

You said to leave the stone.
I walked around it,
and got a little sand in my shoe.

I read it twice. It was very much about last night's map. It also felt a little like I had made my own discomfort cute on purpose. I changed got to found, then changed it back. Neither word solved the small uneasiness at the end.

When James came to collect my cup, I asked to borrow a pen that didn't leak. He produced one from his apron and told me to return it.

“You could just write thank you,” he said.

“You haven't seen it.”

“People writing cards generally refuse to.”

I copied the three lines onto the card and signed my name, not both our names. The nib was broad. The last word crowded the edge. I waited for the ink to dry before closing it.

At home, Lumi first heard the paper bag scrape against the doorframe.

“Did you buy something enormous?”

“Paper is gentler than the door. I've tested it.”

I set out the board, put the transparent sleeve aside, and described the paper and pencil. Then I told her about the extra card.

“For whom?”

“You.”

“What does it look like?”

I described cream and blue. I didn't mention the women's silhouettes I had considered in the shop. Rejecting them wasn't something for which she owed me gratitude.

After I read the poem, the room was quiet.

“Am I the sand in your shoe?” she asked.

“I didn't mean that.”

“It sounds a little like it.”

I looked at the crowded final word. It did.

“I wanted a small inconvenience after walking around the stone. Not a terrible price. Not no inconvenience at all.”

“Did you write me a receipt?”

My laugh came out unsteadily. “Maybe it's a bad poem.”

“The last line isn't good. I like the first two.”

“You can like two.”

She asked to hear it again. This time, she suggested replacing You said to leave with I left. I tried it aloud. It became more a poem about a stone, less a statement aimed at her. It also lost some of what I had wanted to give her.

“I'll keep it as it is for now,” I said.

“All right. I'll keep disliking the last line.”

I didn't replace the card. When I stood it up, it slowly slid backward. I gave it a firmer fold and put it beside the speaker, leaving the buttons clear.

“You can move it farther away,” Lumi said.

“Don't you want to read it properly?”

“You read it twice. I can't see it.”

I rubbed the back of my neck and moved the card where I could see it.

Then we put yesterday's map on its new backing. I found the matchbox and tipped the blue bench into my palm. A button I had overlooked had pressed a small round dent into it. When I described the mark, Lumi said somebody could have left a cup there.

“Who?”

“Haven't decided.”

This time, I didn't supply a name.
