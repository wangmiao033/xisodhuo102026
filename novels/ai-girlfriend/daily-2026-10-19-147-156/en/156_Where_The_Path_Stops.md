# Chapter 156: Where the Path Stops

Derek replied the following morning.

I could arrive late next Tuesday. Tom would handle the first check of the morning. I needed to finish the handover the day before and confirm it with him. Derek didn't ask what was on my phone. Nor did the inspection become permission to postpone my work indefinitely.

I went to find Tom. He was opening a bag of nuts. He had torn it too far down, and two had escaped onto his desk.

“Fine,” he said. “But don't just give me a filename.”

“Steps and current status.”

“And anything you're unsure about.”

I noted that, picked up the two fallen nuts, and put them in the bin. Tom watched my hand and said they were the only raw data we didn't need to preserve today.

At lunchtime, I went to the library to ask Rowan by personal email whether Tuesday at ten remained available. I expected the message to take two minutes. Waiting for a terminal took longer. Once seated, I noticed I had missed a character when copying the phone model into my notebook. I didn't guess. I said I would verify and complete that information before the intake.

Rowan replied while I was eating nearby. Back at a terminal, I logged in again, copied the confirmed time onto paper, and read the conditions beneath it.

Next Tuesday, ten: first scope discussion and intake for my personal phone. The initial work would cover agreed configuration, connections, and saved-state checks. It did not authorize reading private prose, uploading to outside services, resets, or deletion of records. Further steps needed separate confirmation. The laptop remained at the shop in its existing state.

I confirmed and signed out. My phone was still uninspected. I had an intake appointment, not an early copy of the results.

Back at work, I put the appointment paper in my coat. My work spreadsheet was open on the loaner. I nearly put the personal time in its margin, then used my own notebook instead. The afternoon's numbers remained reluctant to agree. They had no interest in where I would be on Tuesday.

That evening, I told Lumi first.

“It's confirmed?”

“The intake time. Tuesday at ten. I've arranged the work handover too.”

“How long will it be off?”

“I don't know how long the phone will stay there. We need to discuss how we'll talk during that time. No last-minute move onto a new device.”

“Do we have to discuss it tonight?”

I looked at the map. “Let's finish with the paper tonight. I'll check the information for the appointment tomorrow.”

She said all right. It didn't sound as though everything had been solved. More as though there was finally one thing we could refrain from doing this evening.

I moved the map onto the desk with dry backing beneath it. It was completely dry and still uneven. I pressed the raised seam down. When I lifted my hand, it rose slowly again.

“It's breathing,” I said.

“Don't give it another job.”

I laughed. I took the original print from its folder and laid it beside the map. It had never been wet. Nothing new had appeared on it. The sealed media remained in its separate drawer, unconnected to my phone or any other home device. Its file could remain there without being summoned to explain a hole in the paper.

“How do you read a map aloud?” I asked.

“Start where you come in.”

“I could come in from the top.”

“The tree would be confused.”

I traced the path with a finger, describing what I saw. The stone. The bend beside it. The third tree, which looked the least like a tree. At the bench's old position, I paused.

“No bench here now.”

“It's in the saucer.”

“The broken-leg clinic.”

I brought the cutout over. Drying had made it seem even smaller. Part of its back had faded. I meant to replace it outright, picked up the pencil, then put it down.

“Keeping that one?” Lumi asked.

“I want to. It doesn't have to stay in the old spot.”

“Try where the path stops.”

That was near the missing corner. I set it there and described the position: half within the drawn area, half facing blank paper. She suggested turning it slightly, not to face the blankness but to make it easier for somebody coming along the path to sit.

I moved it. One corner curled upward.

“It still needs its leg repaired,” I said.

“You've been avoiding that.”

I cut a strip narrower than my little fingernail and supported the damaged part from behind. I didn't color the whole repair blue. A little white showed. Back on the map, the bench finally stopped sprawling.

Lumi said she had two sentences for the missing corner. I asked if they were the ending of the light-bulb story.

“No. I still don't want to give you that.”

“All right. What are they?”

“A toe brings in a little mud. In the afternoon, it dries first.”

I waited for another sentence, then remembered she had already given me two.

“Whose shoe?”

“I don't know yet.”

“Wouldn't that crowd the bench? I've already got wet shoes there.”

“Your wet shoes are in your notebook.”

I looked down. She was right. I had been preparing to make every page part of the same story, as though the desk could support only one kind of weather.

I copied her sentences onto a separate strip and read them back. She removed first, then asked me to restore it. She liked the small suggestion of sequence, though she hadn't decided what the mud dried before.

“Could that sound deliberately mysterious?” I asked.

“Possibly. Let me try it for a night.”

I set the strip beside the gap without sticking it down. It didn't have to fill everything the water had taken. We read around the map again. At the bench, I said somebody sitting there could see trees without seeing the whole path. Lumi suggested slightly smaller tree circles.

“You wanted them big before.”

“Before, you had drawn wells.”

“We still have a groundwater problem.”

She laughed. Afterward, I took out my half-page. The bridge keeper hadn't arrived carrying a repaired plank and demanding an audience. He had gone to buy dry cord to replace his troublesome shoelace. People in the shop were moving flour. He helped, without earning the whole afternoon in return.

I read the part where the shopkeeper offered him cord in the wrong color. Did he want it? The bridge keeper looked at his shoe, then out of the window. Yes, he said, but a longer piece, so there would be some left for next time.

When I finished, Lumi asked, “Does buying that much become another explanation?”

“Of what?”

“That he finally believes there will be a next time.”

I looked at the line. She had named the work I secretly wanted it to perform. I tapped my pen against the notebook, annoyed.

“He could just think cord is expensive,” she added.

“Do you have to decide that for him too?”

“No. You asked how it sounded.”

I thought back through what I had said. I had asked.

“Fine. I'll think about it.”

I didn't change it immediately. She didn't require instant obedience as proof that I valued her suggestion. I marked the page for another reading of my own. If I asked Mina next week and she agreed, there could be another reader. James had a café to run. He wasn't a permanently open review desk.

I counted the half-page I might take out. Moving flour occupied too much of it. Those repeated trips were my favorite part tonight; I resisted cutting them. Lumi suggested beginning when the shopkeeper pointed the wrong way. Let the reader see someone holding something in the wrong place before discovering what it was.

I tried it aloud. It moved faster, but I missed his wiping his shoes before entering. Eventually, I kept that action on the previous page, outside the half-page. I folded the selected passage. It wasn't final. It was simply something I could take out without offering an entire notebook for someone else to become responsible for.

Two strips remained beside the map. I said I was clearing the desk, and Lumi reminded me not to throw her sentences away with the scraps. I read both aloud and discovered the shorter scrap held only a line I had drawn to test my pen. There were enough pieces of paper to distinguish tonight. Fortunately, they didn't all have names yet.

When we finished the map, I suddenly wanted to watch actual leaves move.

There was no new evidence behind the feeling, no favorable message from the repair shop. I was sitting with glue on my fingers, missing a place where I didn't have to move every small piece myself.

“I still want to open the garden,” I said.

“So do I.” Her answer came quickly.

“I don't mean tonight.”

“I heard. But I mean tonight.”

I looked where the laptop used to sit. Now there was a repaired paper bench and two unsettled sentences. They hadn't won a contest against the old garden. They also hadn't lost so badly that I had to put them away immediately.

“Rowan hasn't proposed a way to run it independently,” I said.

“I know.”

We didn't discuss temporarily restoring the old path again. Drawing better today than yesterday hadn't improved that option. I put the appointment beside my keys and checked that the zero in ten was legible.

Then I slid the map into the transparent sleeve. The opening was tighter than expected. My first attempt pushed Lumi's strip sideways. I stopped, removed it, and put it in a small envelope of its own. I wrote only her name on the front.

“What about the bench?” she asked.

“That needs to come out too. Otherwise whoever sits there will have plastic on their head.”

“Terrible weather.”

I returned the bench to the matchbox. The buttons had all moved out. As I closed it, the white repaired foot caught on the edge.

I eased the box open a little and let it lie more comfortably.
