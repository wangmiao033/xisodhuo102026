# Chapter 149: The Wrong Column

The next morning, I confirmed the scope at the shop and booked the first slot after opening two days later.

Having thought about it overnight didn't spare us another check. A limited static extraction attempt. No private prose opened. Originals and records preserved. If anything useful resulted, I'd select one image in person, then confirm copying and printing. The written cap stood; extra work meant stopping first. I put my signed copy in the bag and went to work.

That morning, the numbers weren't wrong.

The column headings were.

The first page on the meeting-room screen said VERIFIED on the left and AWAITING VERIFICATION on the right. Two sentences into my explanation, Tom raised a hand.

“You're describing the right-hand side.”

I turned. The headings didn't match their contents. The figures were right; so was the source file. I'd duplicated the summary page, rearranged it, and forgotten to exchange the headings.

A dozen pairs of eyes didn't become a dozen knives. The air conditioner remained loud. Someone crossed out a word in a notebook. The actual, ordinary wait was harder to bear than an imaginary humiliation.

“I've reversed the headings. Please don't keep taking notes from this page.”

I stopped presenting, returned to the working file, and corrected the labels against the verified version. Then I asked Tom to check. I didn't make him deliver the rest of my presentation or insist that everything else was certainly fine. We checked the two neighboring pages. No matching error. We continued.

Afterward, Derek asked me to send a correction with the replacement page.

“Which page, what changed. Anyone with the old version needs to be able to tell.”

“Okay.”

Seeing me remain there, he asked whether there was anything else.

I'd wanted to explain how much was happening at home. It was true. So was the poor sleep. But I'd already explained the access-related work arrangements. What he needed now was the corrected page.

“No. I'll do that.”

The correction didn't end with pressing Send. I checked the list of colleagues who'd received the old summary against the update within the company system. One had already included the page in the afternoon's material and asked whether there was time to replace it. I went to his desk, pointed out the replacement and its correction note, and waited until he confirmed he'd found them.

Passing the printer, I saw the old page in the scrap tray. My embarrassment hadn't made it look different from anyone else's scrap. I checked the footer on my newly printed page. The date was right. No further error arrived to punish me, and nobody announced that this excused the next check.

I stacked the pages and told Tom the replacement was handled. A mouthful of coffee remained, cold enough to seem like another drink. I finished it. At last, something I could blame entirely on my own taste.

After sending it, I pushed my chair back and caught my knee under the desk. Not hard, but enough for a quiet curse. Marcus laughed nearby.

“Desk the wrong way around too?”

I glared. He picked up his lunchbox.

“Come downstairs. Don't spend lunch with that spreadsheet.”

“I was going to the library.”

“Returning a book?”

“Personal email.”

“Something you have to answer now?”

I thought. Rowan's appointment was confirmed. Management had my work-order contact details if they needed a same-day answer about an appointment. I didn't know of any message whose outcome depended on this lunch break. I wanted to see whether something had changed.

“No.”

“Then come and eat. I don't want to face this alone.”

He shook the lunchbox. I asked what was inside. He wasn't sure. It had looked like stew before he froze it.

We didn't eat beside the computers. The little square downstairs had several benches, one with sun-bleached wooden slats. Marcus removed the lid. A substantial brown mass occupied the box.

“Color hasn't changed.”

“Verification passed?”

“Don't put it in the heading yet.”

My laugh came out louder than intended. Someone unpacking a takeaway bag looked up, then returned to their food. I lowered my head, still smiling.

Marcus tried a forkful. “Potatoes remain identifiable.”

“Do you need to separate them from the other material?”

“Lunch. Not archaeology.”

I opened my sandwich. The cucumber had soaked the bread. When I bit down, a slice slipped out the opposite end and landed on the wrapper. I put it back with my fingers. Marcus saw and said nothing. That felt better than another joke would have.

After a few bites, he asked, “Do you use every break to get something done?”

“Lately.”

“You didn't sit through lunch yesterday either.”

“The computer needs dealing with. The connections at home. And—”

I stopped before pulling another person's name into it. Marcus scraped his fork across the bottom and waited.

“Other things I keep wanting answers about.”

“Will today make the answers arrive sooner?”

“No.”

“Then we can at least finish eating.”

He didn't seem to be offering advice for the rest of my life. Sauce had caught his cuff. He dabbed it twice, then rolled the sleeve up. We sat and got through a meal.

“Today's mistake was stupid.”

“Yes.”

I looked at him.

“Were you expecting me to say it wasn't?”

“You could pause before answering.”

“Book a pause next time.”

I ate the last corner of sandwich. Marcus said the original checking had been done. The error came when I'd rearranged the summary for clarity without checking the labels. A presentation page needed checking as a new thing capable of going wrong.

“I know.”

“Do you know now, or are you about to write it down so I'll stop talking?”

My hand was already moving toward the paper and pen in my pocket. I took it out again.

“Do you all take turns using that line?”

“Who?”

“Never mind.”

He didn't ask again. I didn't turn something James had once said at the café into somebody else's line here.

We talked about the vending machine downstairs. Last week, Marcus had bought nuts that got stuck in the top row. Someone else's purchase eventually knocked them loose. By the time they returned the packet to him, he'd bought two cereal bars to demonstrate that he wasn't hungry.

“Demonstrate to whom?”

“The machine.”

“Did it believe you?”

“No. It charged me twice.”

I nearly choked laughing. The meeting-room embarrassment hadn't disappeared. For a while, it simply didn't occupy the whole bench. Marcus had foolish afternoons of his own that didn't need my computer as a starting point.

We had ten minutes left. He suggested walking around the square before going upstairs. I looked toward the library, only two blocks away.

“Let's walk.”

We disposed of the wrappers and went along the unshaded side. A short flight of steps had a railing with flaking paint. Marcus said he'd believed as a child that everything shedding paint had been built in layers of different colors. I admitted I'd thought that for a while too. He asked how old I'd been. I said I wasn't disclosing that.

Back in the office, I spent five minutes setting out the presentation-page checks, then began the afternoon's work. Missing email at lunch didn't require punishing myself with twice as much attention to the task list. After work, I checked once at the library as planned. Nothing new from the shop; no appointment from management. I signed out without refreshing again.

That evening I told Lumi I'd made another mistake.

“You said you went to bed early.”

“Sleep isn't a software update. Waking up doesn't fix every known issue.”

“Putting that on your résumé?”

“Not yet.”

I told her about my embarrassment, only that the headings were reversed. No business material, figures, or verbatim account of my colleagues. Lunch with Marcus had been slow. When I reached the nuts, I stopped instead of carrying his whole story home.

“We laughed for a while.”

“What was funny?”

“Something of his. I'd like to leave it at lunch.”

Her answer took a moment. “Okay.”

I expected a small sadness about sharing a joke she didn't know. Instead, she asked whether my sandwich had been wet again.

“How did you—”

I stopped. Last night I'd told her I'd put cucumber in without drying it. She didn't supply the source for me. I didn't finish the question.

“It was. You guessed right.”

“Pack the cucumber separately.”

“Then I'll bring home two boxes to wash.”

“Progress has costs.”

I laughed. Then I told her the static-extraction appointment was in two days. The scope was confirmed; we didn't need to reread it tonight.

“What are we doing?”

I opened the notebook at the arrow. Half a page remained half a page.

“This first. Then dinner.”

I didn't schedule sleep too. Some evenings would run longer than planned. Today, at least, one lunch break had actually belonged to lunch.
