# Chapter 152: The Blue Bench

The following evening, I spread the printed view across the desk and caught myself waiting for the shadow to move.

A thin printing stripe ran through a gray patch that might have been a tree crown, giving the uncertain shape one remarkably certain bone. My laptop was still at the shop. Nothing on this page would grow another leaf because I had come home, eaten dinner, and done the dishes.

“Why don't you keep going with the path?” Lumi asked.

“Yesterday's stone has taken up quite a lot of space.”

“It doesn't have to be only a copy. Make it a map you could walk around.”

I looked at the desk. She couldn't hear which way I tilted my head, only the sound of me pulling out some paper.

“My first step would probably kill a plant.”

“Start with somewhere to put your feet.”

I found a piece of cardboard and laid yesterday's sheet over it. The stone outline and accidental dark dot remained, with the upper-right corner reserved for whatever she might choose. The sheet wasn't large enough, so I joined another to it with tape across the back, slightly crookedly. A ridge rose along the seam, a mountain range that had arrived before the planning department.

Lumi asked what I could see in the printout. A stone, a bend, a gray shape that might be part of a tree, an edge that ended in missing space. I didn't fill that space with scenery I remembered or fetch the backup from the cupboard. The place we were drawing was smaller than the one we used to visit.

“Make the stone bigger,” she said.

“This one already looks like a stone.”

“According to you.”

I went over its outline. The pencil snapped. A black speck landed in the path, and when I nudged it aside with a nail it left a smudge.

“There's an insect on the road now.”

“Don't name it.”

“Why?”

“You won't want to erase it.”

I laughed and fetched the eraser. It removed the insect and some of the path. I drew the missing stretch again, a narrow line across the seam between the pages. It stopped where the printed view stopped.

“Does it end here?” I asked.

“We don't draw here yet.”

I added a small question mark. She asked me to leave it blank instead. Erasing the mark left a pale patch in the paper.

Next we argued about trees. She wanted crowns. Mine resembled mushrooms wearing saucepan lids. I suggested circles; she said the garden would look full of wells.

“Are you prepared to manage the groundwater?” I asked.

“Irregular circles.”

“My specialty.”

By the third tree, my hand had loosened. I turned the map a little and added two short lines beside the path. I connected them with a blue pencil I had found at the bottom of a drawer. The faded barrel still said water-soluble. It was so short I had to pinch it with three fingers.

“What did you add?”

“A bench.”

“There isn't a bench there.”

“There is now.”

I heard my own tone as soon as I said it. I had used that tone while changing code. There was no code here, no running garden, only a pencil stub. The ease of having the controls was familiar anyway.

“That was the patch of open ground I liked,” Lumi said.

“I remembered you liked the stone. We're preserving empty ground too?”

“Not every empty place is waiting for furniture.”

I set the pencil down harder than necessary.

“You asked for a map I could walk around.”

“You don't need a bench to walk.”

“You do if you've been walking for a while.”

She didn't answer. The two crooked legs looked stubborn. It wasn't even a good drawing. My bench resembled a staple with feet. I still didn't want to rub it out.

“I thought we were making something new,” I said.

“I thought you were drawing the place I miss.”

A motorcycle passed outside, covering part of her last few words. I had heard enough. I didn't make her repeat them.

I picked up the printout. That patch wasn't completely empty. A gray patch crowded its edge, too indistinct to identify. I could have said it didn't matter because we couldn't see it properly. We probably would have stopped drawing then.

“I wasn't trying to take your space.”

“But you put something there first.”

“Yes. And I don't want to be only your hands.”

That stopped both of us. She didn't have hands. Sometimes I made this fact sound like a lovely invitation to help. Sometimes I made it sound as though I had been left with the whole evening's work. Either version left her very little room.

I pushed the blue pencil to the edge of the board and went for water. In the kitchen, I discovered I was still holding the eraser. I put it down, then picked it up again before it could become tomorrow's breakfast mystery.

“Why that spot?” she asked when I came back.

“For the bench? You'd be able to see the bend.”

“You're drawing a view from above.”

“I sat down in my head.”

“Oh.”

It wasn't forgiveness, but it wasn't dismissal either. I told her the bridge keeper in my story was always standing, either working or watching somebody leave. I wanted him to have a place to sit and empty the water out of his shoes. I had put something my story needed into her garden without asking what sort of drawing we were making.

“It isn't his garden,” she said.

“No.”

“He could sit on another piece of paper.”

I looked at the seam. The suggestion was a little funny and a little sad.

“Do you want me to cut it out?”

“Don't ruin the map. Draw a small one separately. Try places.”

First I erased the original bench, leaving two faint blue traces. Then I found scrap paper and used its blank side. The new bench was better. At least the legs disagreed less violently about their length. There was glue stuck to the kitchen scissors. I scraped it off, cut out a little rectangle, and placed it on the map.

“A trial,” I said.

“Where is it?”

“The original spot.”

“You're very stubborn.”

“I just finished cutting. Let me enjoy it.”

She laughed a little. We moved it outside the bend, then behind a tree. I described each position. She said one would hide the path. I said resting didn't necessarily require watching traffic. The bench had become something to discuss rather than just furniture. That didn't make it weightless.

Eventually it sat at the edge of the map, covering neither her open ground nor the missing portion of the printout. I wrote Elliot's trial drawing on the back. I didn't glue it down.

I took a coin from the change dish and pushed it along the path. At the stone, it couldn't get through. I turned the board and tried from the other direction. It hit a tree.

“People aren't that wide,” I said.

“What are you using?”

I told her. She asked about the scale. Looking at a coin larger than the bench, I admitted there wasn't one.

“Then a very rich person can enter your garden.”

“He might have to turn sideways.”

I replaced the coin with folded scrap paper. Passing the bench, it knocked the seat askew. When I straightened it, I noticed a leg extended onto the path even though the bench didn't cover her open ground. I shifted it outward and tried again.

Lumi asked me to describe the collisions. Her second suggested position was narrower still. She didn't quite believe me. I drew two marks outside the path and explained the comparison. Eventually, we both abandoned our favorite positions for a less symmetrical one.

“Does it still look like a garden?” she asked.

“Yes. The road builder was just economical.”

“Don't let him inspect it with money again.”

“Is the blue terrible?” I asked.

“I haven't seen it.”

“Right. An old blue. Like faded work clothes.”

“That might do.”

“Only might?”

“I'm still annoyed with you.”

I nodded, then remembered to say I knew. She asked for a little of the bridge story. I read the new passage. He tipped water from his shoe, but a small stone stayed inside however hard he shook it. She suggested taking his sock off too.

“We don't have to use every detail,” I said.

“Otherwise the bench was a waste of money.”

“I built it myself.”

I dated the map. Lumi didn't want to call it a restoration plan. She also objected to Our Little World. I said I would cross that one out.

“Did you actually write it?”

“No. Almost.”

“Then you don't need to perform the crossing-out.”

I put the pen down and laughed. In the end, I wrote only The Paper Garden. There was room beside the title for a line she might want to add, or for nothing at all.

While clearing the desk, I put the blue bench in a small matchbox so a draft wouldn't send it under the cupboard. The box had held spare buttons. I tipped them into a dish, and the largest rolled off the table.

“What fell?” she asked.

“A resident who objects to relocation.”

I crouched to find it. The bench was in its box, the map still open. My hand's shadow lay across the unfinished path. The button had wedged behind a chair leg, just beyond reach.

“I need a ruler,” I said.

“Is your bridge long enough?”

“Don't call the bridge keeper. He hasn't got his shoes on.”
