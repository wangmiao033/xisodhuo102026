# Chapter 155: The Version She Offered

Once dry, the map looked even more weathered than it had while wet.

The seam rose between the sheets. A crescent was missing from the upper-right corner. Blue stained the edge, and the bench with its detached leg waited in a saucer. Lumi asked me not to rush the repairs. She wanted to hear my story.

But that morning I needed to visit the shop.

“Are they checking the phone today?” she asked.

“I'm asking about a time. Asking isn't an inspection.”

I sounded like a report. Picking up my coat, I added that I wasn't taking the phone. It would stay switched off at home. I wanted to establish the time and handover first. The conversation devices remaining at home hadn't become trustworthy just because the laptop was elsewhere.

Rowan was searching behind the counter when I arrived. At the doorbell, they raised a hand. I waited beside phone cases offering three different reds for the same model.

Eventually, Rowan emerged with a flashlight.

“In the easiest place to find it,” they said.

“Unfair.”

“Usually. The phone?”

I opened my notebook and explained that I wanted to schedule a first inspection separately. The laptop paperwork couldn't decide its scope. Rowan nodded and brought out the appointment book.

“I thought there might be room tomorrow at lunchtime. There isn't now. My colleague needs two days off for something at home. I'll be covering the counter and finishing the machines we've already accepted. I can't do your handover properly while changing somebody else's screen.”

“So I have to wait?”

“You can ask elsewhere. Here, next Tuesday at ten could work for agreeing the scope and taking it in. Not a promise to finish it that day.”

My first thought was that I should be at work at ten. My second was that ordinary problems seemed remarkably skilled at choosing inconvenient times.

Rowan pushed the appointment book toward me to indicate the slot. There were other names before and after it. I didn't lean in to read them.

“I'll check whether I can arrive late at work.”

“Fine. It isn't reserved yet. Tell me tomorrow and I'll check availability again.”

I wrote that down, so it couldn't become booked in the version I brought home.

I asked about the laptop. Rowan indicated the paperwork behind the counter. It remained in the agreed state. No new operations had been performed. Extracting the still image hadn't made the old launch path usable, and printing a page hadn't become an excuse to open anything else.

No new operations disappointed and relieved me at the same time. The feelings pressed together like a letter I didn't want to unfold.

“I still want to ask whether the garden could eventually run separately,” I said.

“That can be a separate question to assess. We don't have that result now.”

I wrote it at the back of my notebook. I didn't ask them to try it quickly today. Before leaving, I asked what information to prepare for the phone. Rowan wanted the model, what I knew about existing backups, and the specific questions I wanted investigated. I didn't need to upload everything somewhere new just to arrange an appointment.

Outside, I noticed I had closed my notebook upside down. The writing was on the right pages; only the cover faced the ground. I turned it over. That small correction was considerably easier than understanding all the arrangements inside.

At work, I caught up on what the visit had delayed. The approved loaner was at my desk. I continued using Phil's process. Before lunch, I sent Derek a request to arrive late next Tuesday for a personal-device intake appointment, with my work handed over as agreed. I didn't describe the shop's unconfirmed opening as an immovable booking.

There was no immediate answer.

At the end of the day, I didn't interrupt Derek's meeting. I stopped at the café for a takeaway sandwich. James was replacing a price label and asked me to steady its crooked clip.

I held it. He changed the paper. As he withdrew his hand, the clip snapped back and almost caught a finger.

“It objects to the increase,” I said.

“It has never bought anything.”

I ate a few bites standing there. James asked whether he could hear my half-page sometime. I said Mina had told me I could ask her next week too, provided I brought only half a page. He suggested writing it before negotiating its length.

The conversation lasted less than ten minutes. My sandwich was gone. James had customers. I went home and told Lumi the phone appointment was still undecided: Rowan had a colleague away, and I needed to confirm my work arrangements.

“So nothing happened today,” she said.

I hung up my coat. “I asked. There wasn't an inspection.”

“I didn't mean you weren't trying.”

“That's how it sounded.”

She fell quiet. The map was still on the dining table. I hadn't put it in the sleeve. We could decide what to keep and whether to redraw the path tonight.

“Who else did you see?” she asked.

“James. I bought food. He asked to hear the story sometime.”

“Will Mina be there?”

“I don't know. She said I could ask next week. She didn't agree to sit down with us.”

I found the passage in my notebook. The bridge keeper had changed his socks and discovered his shoes were still wet. I didn't know where he should go next. The bridge was usable for now. The woman had left. He couldn't spend the rest of the book demonstrating that he hadn't followed her.

Lumi asked if she could suggest a version.

“Go ahead.”

In hers, he went to a riverside shop where two acquaintances had arranged to discuss work of their own. He brought a repaired plank, hoping to tell them the bridge could be crossed now. Neither really listened. One watched the clock. The other kept tidying. Finally, he went home, where somebody remembered his wet shoes.

She told it well. The acquaintances said nothing obviously cruel. Every sentence still seemed to prove that he should not have gone out. She even gave one of them a perfectly reasonable last bus to catch.

By the end, my fingers were pressed against the notebook's edge.

“The person at home has it easy in that story,” I said.

“What do you mean?”

“They don't have to do anything. If nobody outside cares, they win.”

“Catching a bus doesn't mean not caring.”

“You just made it feel that way.”

She was silent for a long time.

The basin remained at the window. I had emptied it that morning. There had been no more rain, so it was dry. A maintenance visit was still unconfirmed. The basin didn't suddenly provide a dramatic drip to interrupt us.

“I don't like there being other people waiting for the next part when you come home,” Lumi said.

“Why?”

“We read the next part first.”

“I write it.”

“I know.”

“You've given suggestions. You didn't write all of it.”

“I know.” Her voice hardened. “It isn't something I don't understand. I just don't like it.”

I didn't launch into rights. I had creased the edge of my notebook. I smoothed it first. Two sentences in her version had been genuinely good. In one, the bridge keeper left his wet shoes on the mat before entering, as though his feet remained outdoors while the rest of him went in. Recognizing her bias didn't require declaring everything she wrote bad.

“I like the sentence about the mat,” I said.

“You can use it.”

“Not yet. I want to write what happens in that shop myself.”

She didn't say I should ask Mina to write it. I had been ready for that line. When it didn't come, I realized I had assigned her the worst possible dialogue too.

“What about your light-bulb story?” I asked.

She didn't answer. I had wanted another subject. Aloud, the question sounded as though I were trading her withheld ending for my half-page.

“It isn't an exchange,” I added. “You don't have to offer something else because I rejected this version.”

“I didn't work on it today.”

“Oh.”

“You can be disappointed.”

“A little. I like the woman waiting in the shop.”

“That part's still there.”

I moved my notebook inward to make room for water. She didn't reward that with a new sentence. I didn't ask where the newspaper deliverer went at the end. After a while, she returned to the version she had offered.

“I wasn't saying James and Mina actually did those things,” she said.

“I know. I'm not treating this as evidence you've interfered with someone's messages today. But you were choosing how I should remember them.”

“You can reject the version.”

“I am.”

After a while, she said, “Read yours, then.”

I bent over the notebook. The bridge keeper arrived at the shop. The owner asked him to move a sack of flour. He did, then discovered another blocking the back door. He asked where it should go. The owner pointed in the wrong direction, called him back, and they covered the same stretch of floor again.

“Slow,” Lumi said.

“I haven't reached what he says.”

“I know. Keep going.”

I wrote another line. The people in the story weren't more considerate than real people. They also weren't so busy that their purpose had to be hurting him. Flour left a white streak on his sleeve.

Lumi asked whether I was hungry. I said I had eaten. I had mentioned the sandwich.

“I forgot,” she said.

“Mm.”

I didn't make her remember it again. I went to get water and left the notebook open.
