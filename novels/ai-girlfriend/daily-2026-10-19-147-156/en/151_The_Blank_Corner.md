# Chapter 151: The Blank Corner

I put the picture on the desk and weighted its corners.

An empty cup, two pens, a book. Nothing for the fourth corner. I held it down before realizing I only needed to close the window a little.

I laughed.

Lumi hadn't heard. I'd just come home; the conversation devices were still off. The laugh landed in my room without needing anyone to catch it. I left the window half open, washed my hands, then returned and opened the conversation channel we'd left at home.

“I'm back.”

“The laptop?”

“Still at the shop. There's one static image, and one paper copy. I'll tell you what they did first.”

She waited. I didn't bring home Rowan's conversation with another customer. Only the result of our work. Some existing visual material had been independently readable. The text layer hadn't been opened, the old program hadn't run. One partial view had been generated. Excluded parts hadn't been filled in. Original data and records remained preserved.

“Only one picture?”

“I chose one. It's on a new small storage device, and they printed that same image. The device is sealed. I'm not connecting it to anything here. The original backup is untouched.”

I put the sleeve in a different drawer, date and contents facing up. The existing backup stayed in its cabinet. I told her where the new item was without calling two different things the same backup.

“I want to hear the picture.”

“Okay.”

I sat, moved the cup, and began with the edges. Black-and-white, landscape format. A path entering from the lower left, reaching toward the middle, bending around a stone, continuing a little toward the upper right. Much of the right side was empty. It lacked the depth I remembered.

“What does the stone look like?”

“Flat. One angular edge. Like bread that's been squashed. Not a potato anymore.”

“Can you describe it without food?”

“I haven't had lunch.”

“Finish first.”

I smiled and looked again. “Higher on the left, sloping down to the right. A dark gray line underneath. Not completely round. Not a block either.”

She asked how far the path was from it. I measured the gap with a ruler, then put the ruler down.

“Close on this page. But the scale here doesn't establish the old garden's distances. I can describe the arrangement on paper.”

“Then do that.”

“The left part is about as wide as my fingernail. Narrower at the bend. As if the contractor suddenly needed to save money.”

“Is the contractor talking about somebody else?”

“Being modest.”

She laughed softly. I almost made my next sentence too cheerful. Then I looked at the white area and described it as it was.

The plants were silhouettes. Two patches nearly merged, making their separate edges difficult to see. No readable text or old markers. The poem she'd moved beside the path for me wasn't on this page. That didn't establish the original file was lost. It had never been included in the output.

“You keep saying what isn't there.”

“Would you like me to stop for a while?”

“No. Finish.”

I described the remaining edges. At a faint gray line near the top, I said I couldn't be certain it was the old tree canopy. I wouldn't give it a name. The shadows weren't the light I remembered either. Rowan hadn't restored the original lighting.

After a while she asked, “When you saw it, did you wait for it to move?”

“Yes.”

“How long?”

“I didn't check the time. My hand wanted to drag it. To look behind the stone.”

“I kept waiting too.”

I didn't remind her she hadn't been looking. This wasn't a claim about a source of visual input.

“When you described the path, I kept waiting for you to say it had moved forward,” she said. “Or a leaf had touched another leaf. You finished, and I realized you'd finished everything.”

I pushed the two pens together. A small click on the wood.

“I haven't brought the garden home.”

“I know.”

“I still want to keep this picture.”

“I haven't told you to throw it out.”

Her voice was sharp. I'd been defending myself against a refusal that hadn't happened. I breathed in and took the cup off the paper, so nobody would make a new ring when they wanted water.

“I'm very disappointed,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Don't only say yes. I can't tell whether you're tired of hearing it.”

I looked at the speaker. “I want to hear. I keep searching for an answer that won't make it sound so bad.”

“Perhaps it is bad.”

“Yes.”

“But you like it.”

“I like seeing the bend. I don't like that it can't take us anywhere else. Both are here.”

She didn't answer immediately. I got up to make lunch rather than let silence determine how long I stayed hungry. Bread on a plate, leftover soup in a small pan. I stood beside the stove myself, without relying on her to hear what happened in it.

As it heated, she asked whether the paper was still on the desk.

“Yes.”

“What if I wanted to move the stone?”

“This one won't move. Unless we cut it out.”

“Don't.”

“I don't want to cut this copy either.”

“Draw another.”

I lowered the heat and stirred. “Another?”

“Not restoring the original. Use your lines that look like food. Make a very small place. Don't draw the whole garden.”

I remembered the potato I'd drawn in the corner of the earlier diagram. That paper remained in my notebook. We'd added a little path without calling it a repair.

“A setting for a story?”

“Perhaps just a stone first. The path doesn't have to go around it the same way.”

I surveyed the desk. Moving the work orders would leave space for the notebook and another sheet. A paper plan wouldn't give her the ability to change physical things herself. Every mark still needed my hand. My descriptions might fail her too.

“You'll have to wait for me.”

“I know. I haven't suddenly decided that's wonderful.”

“I'm bad at drawing.”

“I know that too.”

I laughed. A bubble broke the soup's surface. I turned off the heat and ate at the kitchen counter. At the last spoonful, she said she'd like the stone to have a larger shadow. I asked whether she meant the new scene or a correction to the printed view.

“The new one. I'm allowed to want an inaccurate shadow.”

“Yes.”

After washing up, I fetched plain white paper and a pencil. Not the back of a work order or one of her poems. The printed view went on the left. I removed the ruler so I wouldn't copy every position.

“I've drawn an outline. Higher on the left. A slightly crooked bottom edge.”

“Still bread?”

“A stone refusing to admit it's bread.”

She laughed, then asked me not to draw the shadow yet.

“You wanted a big one.”

“I haven't decided where the light comes from.”

I stopped. The pencil point rested on the paper, leaving a dot darker than the outline. I told her. She said to leave it for now.

Just one contour. No tree, bench, or path. No old entrance restored because we were willing to try again. The printed image still lacked a large section. The sealed storage device remained in the drawer, the laptop at the shop. The phone and the remaining connections still had their own unfinished entries.

“The upper-right corner's empty,” I said. “What do you want there?”

Her answer took a while.

“Leave it for me. I don't know yet.”

I held my palm over the empty area and asked how much space she needed. She said that sounded like measuring how much room someone would occupy as soon as they asked to sit down.

“The page is only so big.”

“I know. That's why I haven't asked for a second one.”

I pushed the ruler farther toward the book. I'd said we didn't need it, but my hand still wanted a definite number. Hearing it slide over the wood, she asked what it was. I told her. She said to leave it farther away.

Then we argued briefly about the dark dot below the stone. It looked like dirt to me; I wanted to erase it. She thought it might be a very small hole, though she hadn't decided what went inside. I pointed out that a dot was much smaller than a hole. She asked which scale I was using now. I had no answer. We left it, without labeling it a hole either.

The lamp cast my hand's shadow across the empty corner, much larger than the stone's intended shadow. I moved my hand and described that too. She said I could tell her, but shouldn't trace it. She didn't want this place to grow a hand before anything else.

I separated the sheets a little instead of covering the old image with the new drawing. Then I tried to cap the pencil before realizing it had no cap. I was holding one from the other pen. I laughed, rather helplessly.

“What happened?”

I told her. She said the repair worker should finish for the day too.

“Not a repair worker.”

“What, then?”

I looked at the shape that barely qualified as a stone.

“Someone drawing. Today.”

“Okay.”

She didn't say it was good. I didn't ask. The wind rose outside, and I reached to steady the sheet with its empty corner.
