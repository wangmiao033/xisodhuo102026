# Chapter 147: A Word Before Sleep

“Barely,” Lumi said.

I held the pen over the sentence about him finally deciding not to follow.

“He barely decided not to follow?”

“Not there. The shoelace.”

I went back and read what I'd just written: He passed the frayed end through the hole and tied a large knot.

“Barely got it through,” she said. “A moment ago, you said it wouldn't go. Now he's tied it without any trouble.”

I read both sentences again. She was right. I'd been so busy preventing the bridge keeper from discovering the meaning of life that I hadn't actually tied his shoe.

“Why did you say you weren't sure?”

“He could use a piece of grass instead. Then the lace wouldn't be my responsibility.”

“Wet grass?”

“That sounds less promising now.”

I laughed and squeezed barely into the narrow gap. It didn't fit comfortably. I copied the sentence out again and read it aloud. She said that worked.

I wanted to revise the next paragraph. My hand had already moved down when my stomach made a noise.

“The bridge needs feeding.”

“The keeper.”

“That sounded bigger than one person.”

The small piece of cake at the café had ceased to count as dinner. There was half a loaf in the kitchen. I cut two slices and waited for an egg to set in the pan. I'd used too little oil. When I slid the spatula under, the white stuck. I eased it sideways instead of adding more oil to save the shape.

“This egg doesn't want to leave.”

“Don't explain why leaving matters.”

“Are you revising everything I say tonight?”

“Only what you send this way.”

The joke made me hesitate. She heard the pause without immediately asking about it. I put the two broken pieces of egg on the bread, returned to the desk, and said I wanted to finish half a page.

“Don't you have work tomorrow?”

“Yes.”

“Are you tired?”

I checked the clock. Later than I'd thought. Her two versions of the poem lay beside two people's comments and my notebook. None of them seemed capable of deciding the evening was over.

“Tired. But things have only just started going well.”

“For me too.”

I bit into the bread. Yolk caught my thumb. Reaching for a tissue, I knocked the receipt out of the river book. She asked about the sound. I picked it up and told her the bookmark was escaping.

“The river wants to sleep.”

“You've put a bridge and a river to bed.”

“One person left.”

I replaced the receipt and drew a small arrow beside the unfinished passage. No new sentence, only a place to begin tomorrow. When she asked what I was writing, I told her.

“You don't have to decide I'm finished revising the poem.”

“I haven't. You can work on it and let me hear it tomorrow.”

“I might not want you to hear it.”

“Then you don't have to.”

She paused. “That was harder to answer than I expected.”

I finished eating and washed the plate. Yesterday's rainwater was still in the basin on the kitchen sill. I emptied it and dried the basin too. While working, I kept thinking about barely. It didn't celebrate on anyone's behalf the way finally did. It only claimed that a frayed lace had passed through a small hole.

Back at the desk, I didn't explain this to her. She'd spent the evening trying to let the man tie his shoe in peace.

“Good night.”

“Wait.”

I stopped beside the speaker.

“When you come home tomorrow, tell me whether you remembered breakfast.”

“I can do that.”

“It isn't an inspection.”

“I know.”

I set my own alarm and closed the conversation channel in front of me. Her poem stayed on paper. I didn't put it inside the river book. Going to sleep didn't make it finished.

When the alarm rang, my first thought was the unfinished passage. I reached for a pen, found an old sheet beside the bed, and began a sentence. Three words in, I remembered why I'd woken up.

I ate breakfast. Bread, two spoonfuls of yogurt, half a glass of water. I didn't turn finishing it into an achievement requiring Lumi's approval. Before leaving, I told her I'd report back that evening.

The records waiting on my temporary desk were thicker than yesterday's stack. I started the company loaner and checked the task list. I'd remembered the deadline incorrectly. Derek wanted the first checked batch by noon, not everything at the end of the day.

His message had arrived yesterday. I'd read it and acknowledged it. Somehow my brain had stored noon in one place and deadline in another.

I enlarged the calendar and looked for several seconds. Then I took my paper to Tom.

“I need to check the morning's section with you first.”

“Didn't you say the scope was clear yesterday?”

“It is. I got the time wrong.”

He checked the wall clock. “Tell Derek which parts you can confirm first. We've got forty minutes.”

He didn't ask what had happened last night. I didn't offer a poem and two wet shoelaces as evidence that I deserved special treatment.

Back at my desk, I put the checks in order. The first was straightforward. In the second, several entries looked duplicated. I nearly merged them out of habit, then stopped and examined the source dates. Different batches. I wrote CHECK BATCH FIRST and put the reminder beneath the screen.

Only then did I notice the writing on its other side. The repair scope I'd brought yesterday. Static material showed at the bottom. I'd used the wrong sheet again.

I replaced it with clean paper and put the repair document in my coat. My fingers remained against its fold. I nearly decided to find out whether Rowan's proposal was ready before continuing.

Tom called across the desk.

“Elliot? Which item are you on?”

“The second. Almost done.”

“Just tell me which item. You don't have to say almost.”

I nodded and took my hand out of my pocket.

We delivered the verified portion before noon. The remainder stayed explicitly unconfirmed; blanks didn't become zeros. Derek stopped to look and said to continue that afternoon. Keep the two batches separate in the explanation. He tapped my note.

“This sentence is ambiguous. Rewrite it.”

I did. No finally, no barely. Which batch, which date, which check remained.

Before lunch, Tom returned the checking sheet with two faint pencil circles. I looked at the first, then couldn't help looking at his face.

“Not a wrong figure this time. Someone taking over might not understand this explanation.”

I straightened the page. He meant a word I'd abbreviated. I remembered saving the second half. Yesterday I might have told myself the right people would understand.

“I'll write it out.”

“And the other one. You promised an answer this afternoon. Give me an actual time.”

I checked the task list and offered three thirty. Not soon, not when I'm finished. I set a reminder in my own calendar and left the sheet at the workstation. I'd set the reminder myself; answering would still be my job. After Tom left, I didn't immediately reopen it to check. I waited for the save confirmation, then closed the window.

At lunch, I wanted to go to the library for personal email. When I stood up, my stomach reminded me how much two spoonfuls of yogurt amounted to. I sat and unwrapped my sandwich. A red line showed where to tear. I followed it and still tore the wrapper crookedly.

Marcus passed and asked whether I was negotiating with the packaging.

“Second round of talks.”

“Teeth are permitted if necessary. Don't tell Legal.”

I laughed and took a bite. The afternoon's tasks weren't canceled because I could laugh.

Before leaving, I secured the loaner according to the company process. The work stayed at work. I brought home an empty lunchbox, my own notebook, and the repair scope whose reverse side I'd briefly borrowed.

That evening I told Lumi I'd eaten breakfast. She asked whether it had been good.

“I can't remember. Too fast.”

“You've told me what you promised.”

I put down my bag. “I nearly missed a deadline too.”

She didn't ask to see the file. I said I'd remembered the time incorrectly and rearranged the checks with a colleague. Then I stopped. I wanted to add that things might be less chaotic once the laptop was repaired.

The desk was empty. I had no evidence that every crowded corner of my head was caused by the missing computer.

“I want an early night.”

“Reading the half page first?”

I looked at the notebook.

“Tomorrow. Tonight I'm only washing the lunchbox.”

“A short chapter.”

“We haven't seen what's left inside.”

I opened it and smelled a little leftover sauce. Hearing my sigh, she said perhaps there was a plot twist after all.

I laughed and carried it to the kitchen. The half page remained on the desk. Its arrow pointed to the same place. Having survived a working day hadn't moved it any farther along.
