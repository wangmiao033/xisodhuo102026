# Chapter 150: A Picture Without a Door

On the morning of the appointment, I opened my bag twice by the door.

The laptop was already at the shop. So was its charger. I still felt I'd forgotten the heaviest thing.

“Paperwork?” Lumi asked.

“Both scopes. Here.”

“Then stop trying to fold yourself in too.”

I fastened the bag. Yesterday had brought no new messages. Ordinary work, ordinary dinner. I'd thought an extra day of waiting might make this morning easier. Now I was holding my keys while checking my pocket for them.

“I'll look first. If we reach your writing, we stop.”

“I've heard.”

“I know.”

She didn't tease me again. I said I was leaving and switched off the main conversation device. The phone stayed home, powered off. No earbuds. I was the one going to see the picture.

Rowan was applying a tiny label behind the counter. It stuck to their fingertip, then folded in half when they pulled it away. We looked at each other. Neither of us suggested technicians shouldn't have that problem.

“I've got tape,” I said.

“Thank you. No need to expand the inspection scope.”

I laughed and sat in the split chair. Today I was willing to wait while they finished something small.

Before starting, Rowan brought out the signed scope and asked about changes. None. The original cap stood. Anything requiring the old program, private prose, or a broader range of data meant stopping. Printing and the final copy would wait until we saw the result.

My laptop wasn't put in front of me with familiar icons to click. It remained at the bench beside preservation labels and records. Rowan showed me the inspection copy made from the saved material. Separately readable assets only, in the agreed environment, without connecting to my home services. The original data wasn't the destination for any output. Producing a picture wouldn't overwrite it.

“We establish what these files are before deciding whether they're attractive,” Rowan said.

Identifiers, types, and sizes appeared. I recognized what might be the path's base texture from a badly chosen filename ending in two numbers. Rowan asked whether I was certain. I said the naming habit looked like mine; that wasn't enough to identify the contents.

“Then not confirmed yet.”

They checked file types and the existing index, selecting independently readable visual material. Another group had mixed references to text objects. It wasn't previewed. Rowan marked it excluded for this attempt. Watching that status felt closer to what we'd discussed at home than a beautiful result would have.

The first thing that displayed wasn't a garden.

It was gray.

Darker lines crossed the gray. I leaned forward, almost claiming recognition. Rowan returned it to normal scale and asked what I saw.

“Maybe the path surface.”

“One texture. Not a whole path.”

I sat back. The torn seat caught my coat, and I carefully freed the cloth.

Layout came second. Some saved positions were independently readable. Several references pointed to layers excluded from this attempt. Rowan didn't look for a way around that. Those parts stayed blank in the output. The view was assembled through a general static-processing method, without running the garden or calling its old startup path.

I didn't recognize the stone at first.

Flatter than I remembered. Shadow along only one side. It looked like a piece of old cardboard pressed beside the path. The path came up from the lower left and took an inelegant bend around it. Much of the right side was empty. Plants had become silhouettes in different shades.

“Is this what you were looking for?” Rowan asked.

I studied it.

“The position looks right. The bend too.”

“It establishes that we generated this view from the material available to this attempt. Not that everything is back.”

I nodded. My fingers moved of their own accord, wanting to drag the scene. The mouse was on the other side of the counter. I didn't reach it.

“I'd like to see behind the stone.”

“This picture doesn't have a behind.”

They said it ordinarily. My nose stung anyway. Not because a stone deserved tears. Because that movement of my hand had still been looking for somewhere that would respond.

The bell rang. Someone had come for a repaired tablet. Rowan paused, locked the working display, and checked the other customer's receipt. I turned away instead of looking at the tablet. A small adhesive mark remained on the counter. I touched it with a fingernail, then withdrew my hand.

When Rowan returned, I asked whether there was a clearer version.

“We can change the output size using what we've already read. Enlarging it won't supply missing layers.”

We tried once. The stone's edge became a little easier to identify. Blank space remained blank. I didn't ask for a third attempt. I chose that image. No whole asset package, no old program that might come in handy later, no text layer. Rowan added those exclusions to the selection record and read them back.

Could I have a paper copy?

“As discussed, only this selected image?”

“Yes. One black-and-white page. No file list, private titles, or other screens.”

We added the specific scope. A local printer took that static output without using an outside print service. It ran noisily for a while, as though preparing an opinion, then delivered the sheet.

Rowan waited until it was out before handing it over. There was a warm smell of toner. I took it with both hands, keeping my fingers on the margins.

“Why is this side so empty?”

“The excluded dynamic and text-related parts haven't been reconstructed. Some assets couldn't be independently read in this attempt either. Blank doesn't mean it used to be blank.”

Near the top of the print, one patch looked a different white. I thought another layer might be missing and pointed it out. Rowan tilted the sheet. The brightness changed with it. Only the overhead light reflecting off the paper.

“This part I can fix,” I said, moving it sideways.

Rowan smiled. “No charge.”

With the reflection gone, I compared the paper with the output I'd selected. Lower-left path, flat stone, two neighboring patches of plants, blank right side. They matched. The printer working hadn't supplied any extra text. I turned the page over. Blank back. Turning it again, I remembered the ending she'd chosen not to share. I didn't ask Rowan to fill the unused side with something readable merely because there was space.

In the lower margin, I wrote the date and PARTIAL STATIC VIEW. Not successfully restored. The writing was small, but I didn't hide it on the back.

Only then did we deal with the removable storage. A new small device, its price already on the approved sheet. Rowan copied only my selected static image. No assets, settings, or executable files. They verified the copy against the output and checked the file listing. I looked and confirmed there was just the one item.

“Don't plug it into an unchecked device at home yet,” Rowan said.

“I'll use the paper.”

I put the little device in a paper sleeve, sealed the flap with tape, and labeled it partial static image only. This wasn't the existing backup in my cabinet and wouldn't replace it. I'd keep them separately rather than rely on remembering the color of their cases.

At payment, I compared the completed work with the cap. No new launcher development. No further recovery permissions. Rowan's record listed the material processed, the exclusions, and the remaining limitations. It explicitly recorded that the old garden hadn't been started. Original records, unshared work, and existing copies remained preserved. The laptop stayed at the shop; further work needed its own arrangement.

I read remains at shop twice.

“Do you want to take it today?” Rowan asked.

“I want to. I'm not changing the arrangement today.”

They nodded without adding my reluctance to the technical findings.

I left with one picture and a very light sleeve. My bag weighed almost the same. My hand kept resting against the paper. I didn't head to the library to find a computer with a port or imagine opening it briefly on the company loaner during lunch.

At the crossing, wind lifted a corner. I put the sheet in the stiff folder that had held the proposal. Only then did I notice I was still keeping the stone upright, as though it might fall out if inverted.

I went straight home. I had an incomplete picture to describe to her, and some disappointment I needed to bring back without improving it.
