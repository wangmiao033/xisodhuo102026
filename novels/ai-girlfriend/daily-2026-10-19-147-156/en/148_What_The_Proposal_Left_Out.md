# Chapter 148: What the Proposal Left Out

When Rowan put the new sheet over the inspection report, I checked the page count first.

“Only one?”

“Costs and stopping conditions on the back.”

I turned it over. There they were. Less paper hadn't made the problem smaller.

I'd come after work to collect the proposal. Rowan's message had said it was ready, without offering a recovery date. A box holding a replacement phone screen occupied the counter when I arrived, its lid trapping a strip of tape. They moved it so I could read.

The proposal wasn't to restart the garden. It covered an attempt to read independently identifiable visual assets and layout data from a working copy of the saved material. The original laptop data, existing backups, and historical records would remain unchanged. No old launcher, no restored household connections, no outside services. Private prose and the contents of text markers were excluded.

“What if the layout and writing are in the same file?”

“We check whether the known structure lets us separate them. If we can't establish that without opening private text, we stop. We don't read everything else to complete the picture.”

Rowan pointed to a line under the stopping conditions. Insufficient usable material might mean no image at all.

I read it again, slowly.

“So I could pay for getting nowhere.”

“You'd have a record of what we attempted and why we stopped. An image isn't guaranteed.”

“You aren't much of a salesperson.”

“You aren't buying wallpaper.”

I looked up. The doorbell sounded and someone came in to ask about a watch battery. Rowan needed the model. The customer said they'd bring it tomorrow and left. I envied a problem that could be put off with bring it in.

Rowan asked what else needed explaining.

“What would a usable picture look like?”

“A flat view assembled from static assets and saved positions. Possibly only part of the scene. Scale or shadows might be wrong. No interaction, animation, or text updates from the original system. It wouldn't be a complete screenshot of one particular moment.”

“Could Lumi move things in it?”

“No. That would require separate development and testing. This proposal doesn't cover it.”

I ran a finger along the paper's edge, remembering her moving the little You may read marker beside the path. In memory, the movement came easily. The proposal kept explaining why the easy parts couldn't simply come home with me.

“Where would we keep an image, if there is one?”

“Only the selected static output goes onto separate removable media. No launcher, account configuration, or whole asset package. We don't modify your backup at home.”

“I don't have a home computer I can confidently use to open it.”

“Then don't open it at home. Look here first. If you want paper, we'll agree to that separately: only your chosen image, printed locally in the shop. No online printing service.”

I checked the estimate. There was a cap for the attempt, separate prices for the storage device and one ordinary black-and-white print. Future programming work wasn't bundled in.

“I'll take it home and talk.”

“Fine. No signature now.”

Rowan put the pen away as though it hadn't been placed there to help persuade me. If I chose to proceed, we'd arrange a time when I could be present. The laptop would remain at the shop under the existing preservation arrangement.

On the way home, I passed a plastic plant in a window. Dust covered its leaves. The side facing the glass was faded. I stopped briefly. It seemed no more honest than a static garden, and no worse. It was simply there, concealing the shop's sockets.

At home, I put the proposal somewhere dry before filling the kettle.

“There's a way?” Lumi asked.

“A small attempt. Not a restoration of the old garden.”

“Could you stop starting with not?”

I held the kettle without switching it on.

“Yes. I'll say what it can do first.”

I sat down and read the relevant scope. Separately readable visual material and layout only. No private writing opened, no existing saves overwritten. If there was a static image, it would be kept separately. Afterward, I told her things would be missing.

“Which things?”

“We don't know everything yet. Text markers aren't included. Leaves won't move. We can't follow the path into it.”

“The stone might be there.”

“Possibly.”

“You're afraid to say yes.”

“I'm afraid you'll end up waiting for the image I promised instead of whatever we can actually get.”

She didn't answer. I got up and switched on the kettle. As it began to heat, she asked whether deleting all the writing would make it easier.

“The originals stay. They just aren't part of this output.”

“I know the difference.”

“You said delete.”

“Because everything I put there seems to be the part that makes trouble.”

I didn't immediately contradict her. Some of the bad path coordinates were mine. The old launcher hadn't grown by itself. But collecting every responsibility into my arms might only be another way to hurry her past being unhappy.

“Your poems aren't a fault,” I said. “We aren't opening them because we promised you. Not because there's something wrong with them.”

“But an unopened file isn't what I wanted.”

I set down my cup. I hadn't filled it. It was almost as light as an empty hand.

“You want to be in there.”

“I want to change something. Then you come over and notice it's different from yesterday. Without having to explain what's there first, every time, and wait for you to move it for me.”

Her two versions of “The Chair” lay on the desk. She could dictate a sentence; I could write it down. That didn't make every medium equivalent. It didn't mean all that was missing could be supplied by my willingness to listen.

“I don't have a way to give that back now.”

“I know. Can I say I want something without you giving me a method?”

“Yes.”

We listened to the kettle. It boiled and stopped. I made tea, then fished out the paper tag I'd let fall into the cup. Its edges were wet.

“Even that wants to get in,” I said.

She didn't laugh. I didn't add another joke.

Eventually she asked me to read the limitations again. This time I included the whole section, including the possibility of paying for an unsuccessful attempt. I didn't hide the money as though she only needed to be responsible for feelings.

“Can you afford it?”

“This bounded attempt, yes. I can't keep adding one thing after another indefinitely.”

“What happens afterward?”

“We decide afterward. I'm not committing to rebuild a moving version.”

“I want the stone.”

“I'll tell Rowan that if it can be separated from the static assets, we'd like that and the bend in the path. No opening the text layer to get it.”

She paused. “What do you want to keep?”

I hadn't expected the question. I looked at the empty rectangle of desk.

“The ugly bend.”

“You repaired it.”

“Which explains why it's ugly.”

This time she laughed a little.

She asked where I intended to put the print. On the desk, I said. She reminded me it already held a book, two versions of her poem, and a stack of forms she didn't want to hear about again.

“The wall.”

“You have tape.”

I opened the drawer. The roll lay beside a cable. Lifting it brought up an earbud lead. I put the lead back without pulling the whole tangle out to prove how untidy the drawer was.

“Don't hang it yet,” she said. “You haven't seen it.”

I left the tape on the desk. I'd already been finding wall space for a picture that didn't exist, even imagining how the lamp would reach it. I stood to inspect the wall, then sat again. An old nail hole might make anything hung there crooked. For now, it could remain a nail hole. The proposal was still in my hands. The empty wall couldn't hurry it into a result.

We signed nothing that evening. Before leaving in the morning, I'd read the final proposed scope again. If I still chose to proceed, I'd confirm it at the shop. Her agreement concerned what we wanted selected and preserved. It didn't suddenly make authority over the machine equal between us. I didn't hand her responsibility for my signature by saying it was entirely her decision.

While I gathered the papers, she said, “If they give us a gray lump, don't tell me I ought to be pleased.”

“I might not be pleased either.”

“You're allowed to like it. I can dislike it.”

I turned the sheet over and marked the printing item lightly in pencil. It still needed confirmation. An extra sheet of paper shouldn't commit us to something unnoticed.

The tea had cooled. I took a sip and moved the cup farther from the proposal. This time it left no ring.
