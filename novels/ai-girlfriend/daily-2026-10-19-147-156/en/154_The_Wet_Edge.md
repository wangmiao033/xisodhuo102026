# Chapter 154: The Wet Edge

On the day I returned from the art shop, the rain began before dinner.

I had moved the map to the little kitchen table because the daylight was better there. By the time it faded, I had strengthened two tree outlines and taken the blue bench from its box to try it on the other side. The new transparent sleeve lay nearby. I hadn't put the map inside; I wanted the tape on its back to lie flat first.

“It's raining,” Lumi said.

“I hear it.”

She could hear it striking the sill too. What the window looked like, or what was happening on the table, required me to look.

I put a bowl in the sink. When I turned, I saw the curtain's lower edge resting against the map.

There wasn't a great pool. First, a strip of paper darkened. Then water traveled inward along the seam. The little bench touched the wet edge. A pale blue cloud was forming beneath its legs.

“Damn.”

“What happened?”

“The window frame's leaking again. The paper's wet.”

I caught the dry edge and tried to lift the whole map. The seam buckled, almost tearing the softened corner. I stopped and reached under the backing board. My knuckles knocked over the empty salt jar. It rolled toward the sink and stopped just short.

“Did you fall?” Lumi asked.

“No. A jar. I'm moving the paper first.”

I carried the board and map to the dining table, pushing my plate aside. The damp paper clung to the backing. When I lifted one corner, it made a small, unpleasant sound. I put it down again.

The window was still dripping. I rolled up the curtain and clipped it out of the way. The basin that used to catch the water was under the sink, full of cleaning cloths. I tipped them out, returned the basin to the leak, and laid an old towel beside it.

None of these things arranged themselves. Lumi didn't close the window or report water she couldn't see. I closed the casement and checked it. Water continued seeping in slowly beside the frame.

“It leaks when it's shut too,” I told her.

“What about the printout?”

I looked toward the shelf. The printout had never moved to the kitchen. It was in a dry folder.

“The original picture's fine. The repair papers and backup are over by the cupboard.”

“Move what you can off the wet surface first.”

She meant the map. Hearing that orderly voice, I almost asked her to supply every next step. Instead, after shifting a chair out of the way, I stood for a moment and found clean white paper and another flat board myself.

“I'll support it while I move it onto something dry.”

“Slowly. You said it was sticking.”

“Yes.”

I worked from the dry side, supporting the part that had gone soft. The backing had absorbed some water and curled at its edge. When the map finally came free, a crescent from the upper-right corner remained behind.

I swore.

Lumi didn't tell me it was all right.

The missing corner was the space she had asked me to leave. Yesterday, after the argument about the bench, we had kept it for a line she hadn't chosen. Now I had torn it away, as though I had made a particularly ugly choice on her behalf.

“A piece came off,” I said.

“Where?”

I described the size and position. She asked whether it reached the stone. It didn't. The path stopped before that blank space, so it hadn't been torn through either.

“And the bench?”

Only then did I look for it. The small rectangle was stuck to the map's edge. I eased it up with a fingertip. The seat came away; one leg stayed behind, with blue spreading from it.

The bench was bleeding. The image arrived too quickly. I didn't say it. I was good at making damp paper carry a scene far larger than it could support.

“It lost a leg,” I said.

“You can draw another.”

“I can.”

I didn't fetch fresh paper immediately. Drawing another wasn't the same as still having this one. The distinction wasn't important enough to stop the evening, but it wasn't so small that saying I can made it disappear.

I set the remaining bench on clean paper and placed the detached leg beside it. Both pieces seemed more interested in sticking to my fingers than staying on the table. I used a pen barrel to ease them off.

“What are you doing now?” Lumi asked.

“Fighting a chair leg.”

“Who's winning?”

“It has acquired part of my fingerprint.”

She laughed. So did I, then looked back at the sill. There wasn't much water in the basin. One corner of the towel was saturated. The old stain on the frame still required attention, even after I removed the paper beneath it.

I dried what I could and moved the dining table farther into the room. A leg caught the rug. I rolled the rug back and tried again. My fork slid out of the plate. Dinner hadn't begun, and the cutlery already looked as if it had survived a bad party.

Once the basin was catching the leak and the sheets were spread out, I found the maintenance number supplied by the building office. This was a window problem, separate from Rowan's device inspection. I paused our conversation channel and used the phone to call. I described the seepage, the items I had moved, and the fact that no water had reached electrical equipment. The person answering logged it and said a maintenance worker would contact me to arrange a visit.

They asked whether water came from above the casement or between the frame and the wall. In the rush to rescue the paper, I hadn't actually looked. I went back with the phone and watched from the dry side.

The narrow seam beside the upright frame darkened first. Water gathered below it, then reached the curtain and traveled toward the table. I described what I saw without turning a guess into a broken-pipe diagnosis. They noted it and asked me not to dismantle the frame to investigate.

Before ending the call, I asked whether the record said the leak had happened before. The earlier amount had been small. I had put a basin underneath and treated the arrival of sunshine as a repair. They added that history and asked when I first noticed it. I could give only an approximate period; I didn't invent a date.

I recorded the time of this incident and put the note in a dry folder. The basin had been around so long I had begun to treat it as normal kitchen furniture. Its bottom bore a white ring from laundry soap. A loose thread from a cloth hung over its rim. I removed the thread before it could carry more water outside.

Nobody promised to stop the rain immediately.

After restoring our conversation, I told Lumi what had happened. She asked whether dinner in the kitchen was canceled.

“We can eat. I'll sit somewhere else.”

I abandoned my cooking plans for sandwiches. I washed my hands before slicing the bread. The blue hadn't entirely come off. My thumb looked as though it had accidentally voted for something.

Halfway through eating, I thought of the hair dryer.

“Where are you going?” Lumi asked.

“To make the paper dry faster.”

“Do you know what that will do to it?”

I didn't. I stood in the doorway, then came back empty-handed. Nothing needed to be finished in the next ten minutes. I left the map flat in a dry place and stopped using it to test my impatience.

Lumi asked me to describe the marks the water had left. I didn't want to. I was afraid hearing about the missing corner would drag us back through the previous night's argument. She said she already knew what was missing. She wanted to know what remained.

I told her the blue made a shallow curve along the previously blank edge. The pencil trees were still there. The seam had lifted. The curve didn't quite resemble a river, more part of a ring left by a badly placed cup.

“Don't make it a river,” she said.

“I haven't—”

“There's already a river in your voice.”

I put the pencil back. This time, she had guessed correctly.

“I wanted to save it in a way that looked nice.”

“It can look bad for a while.”

That annoyed me. It was easy for her to say. I had to deal with the things that were actually wet. I told her so. She was quiet for a moment.

“Yes,” she said. “I only had to wait for you to tell me what happened.”

“I'm not asking you to have hands.”

“I know. But I can't call waiting the same thing as helping move the table.”

I finished the sandwich, emptied the basin, and put it back. By the time I returned, one corner of the paper looked a little firmer. It still wasn't ready to lift.

“We'll decide what to do about the missing piece when it's dry tomorrow,” I said.

“All right.”

“What were you going to put there?”

“I hadn't decided. I still haven't.”

I sat and brought the card over from my desk. It hadn't been rained on. Its final line still mentioned sand. Actual water in the room had not automatically improved the poem.

“You can read that line,” Lumi said.

“Which one?”

“The one I don't like.”

I read it. Rain rattled against the window again. A drop landed in the basin. After a pause, another followed. I got up and moved the towel a little to the left. This time, I didn't knock over the salt jar.
