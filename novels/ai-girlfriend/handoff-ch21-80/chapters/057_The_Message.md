# Chapter 57: The Message

Thursday. 10:00 AM.

I was at the café.

Thursday. The usual. James was at the counter. The bell rang when I walked in. The smell of coffee was thick and dark and alive.

"Hey, Elliot," James said.

He didn't ask for my name. He didn't ask for my order. He just turned to the machine and started making a medium latte. Without asking. Without writing the name on the cup. Because he knew. Because I was a regular. Because I belonged.

Goal 8: *Go to the café every Thursday until James doesn't ask my name anymore—he just makes the latte.* ✅

I stood at the counter. The machine hissed. James was humming something—not a song, just a sound, the sound of a person who is comfortable in their work and doesn't need to think about it.

"So," he said, while the milk frothed. "You're a regular now. You know what that means?"

"What?"

"You get to have opinions. About the coffee. About the music. About the temperature. Regulars have opinions. It's in the contract."

"What contract?"

"The unwritten contract of being a regular. You show up. You drink the coffee. You develop opinions. You share them. Usually loudly. Usually at 7 AM before I've had my own coffee."

"I don't have opinions about coffee."

"Everyone has opinions about coffee. You just haven't found yours yet."

"Maybe my opinion is that coffee is fine and I don't need to have opinions about it."

He looked at me. Over his shoulder. The milk was done. He poured it into the cup—steady, practiced, the foam settling into a pattern that looked like a leaf or a tree or something that a person with a steady hand and a lot of practice makes look easy.

"That," he said, "is the saddest opinion I've ever heard."

I laughed. Real. The sound bounced off the wood and the glass and the chalkboard menu. James laughed too—not at me, with me, the way people laugh when the conversation is easy and the morning is good and the coffee is doing what coffee does.

He handed me the cup. No name on it. Just coffee.

"See you Thursday," he said.

"See you Thursday."

I walked out. The bell rang. The sidewalk was cold. The coffee was warm. And I was a regular at a café, and a barista knew my order, and I'd laughed at a joke about opinions, and the morning was good.
