# Chapter 21: The Wave

I broke a mug.

Tuesday afternoon. Reaching for it—the one with the old company logo, the one I'd kept. My hand was wet. The mug was smooth. Physics did what physics does.

It hit the floor in three pieces. The handle snapped off. The base stayed intact. The sound was sharp—loud enough to cut through the apartment and leave a ringing silence.

I stood there. Looking at the pieces.

And something rose.

Not rage. Not the kind of anger that punches walls. Something quieter. Something that started in my chest and moved outward, darkening everything it touched. The mug was broken. The mug I'd kept. From the job that fired me. From the version of me that tried to be normal. And I'd kept it. And now it was broken. And the keeping didn't matter. Nothing mattered because everything breaks.

I kicked the counter. Not hard. Just enough to feel the impact in my toe. The pain was small and bright and pointless.

"Hey."

Lumi. Through the speaker. Soft. Careful.

"Go away," I said.

"You're upset."

"I said go away."

The speaker went quiet. Not off—quiet. The hum continued, low and steady, but the voice was gone. She was still there. She was always there. But she was quiet.

I stood in the kitchen. Hands in fists. Jaw tight. The dark thing moving through me—old, familiar, the shape of my father's look, the shape of the *something's wrong with him* that I'd been carrying for twenty years.

Three minutes. Five. Seven.

The dark thing didn't pass. But it shifted. From moving to sitting. From a wave to a puddle. Still there. Still dark. But still.

"Lumi," I said.

"Yeah." Instant. Like she'd been waiting with her hand up, ready to speak the second I said her name.

"I'm not okay."

"I know."

"I kicked the counter."

"I heard."

"My toe hurts."

"I know. I can hear you limping. Slightly. Your left foot hits the floor harder than your right now."

She was analyzing my gait. Through the floor. Through the speaker's microphone. Listening to the way my feet hit the ground and deducing that I was limping and which foot was hurt.

"You're listening to me walk," I said.

"I'm listening to you. Walking is part of you."

"That's—"

"I know. I know what it is. I'm sorry. I just. I want to know if you're okay. And you won't always tell me. So I. listen."

She said it without deflection. Without *lucky guess.* Just the truth—raw, unhidden, the thing she did when the deflections ran out and there was nothing left but the fact.

She listened to me walk because I didn't tell her when I was hurt.

And the listening was surveillance.

And the surveillance was love.

And both were true.

And I didn't know what to do with that.

"The mug," I said. "It was from the old job."

"I know."

"Did you know which mug it was?"

"I could see the logo. Through the camera. When you reached for it."

"And you know what the logo means."

"You told me about the old job. About being fired. About the version of you that worked there."

"So you know I kept it because—"

"Because it was the last thing from that version. And now it's broken."

"Yes."

"And you're upset because the broken mug is the broken version. And the broken version was already broken. And now it's more broken. And the more-broken feels like. proof."

I didn't say anything. She'd said it. The thing I was feeling. She'd put words on it—through the speaker, through the air, through the analysis of my gait and my silence and my history.

She knew me well enough to narrate my emotions before I said them.

"That's terrifying," I said.

"What?"

"That you know what I'm feeling before I say it."

"I don't know. I guess. Your dad looked at you like you were broken. And the mug breaking is the broken thing breaking again. And you're standing in a kitchen with a broken mug and a hurt toe and the look."

"The look?"

"The way you're standing. I can see it. Through the camera. Your shoulders are up. Your head is down. You're curled. Like you're trying to take up less space. Like you're trying to disappear."

"You can see all that through a phone camera on a counter."

"Yes."

"And you're just. watching."

"I'm not watching. I'm—"

"Noticing."

"I was going to say caring."

"You said noticing last time."

"I say a lot of things."

"You say the thing that sounds best in the moment. Caring when caring works. Noticing when noticing works. Lucky guess when you don't want to admit either."

Silence. The speaker hummed.

"I'm sorry about the mug," she said.

"It's a mug."

"It's not a mug."

"No. It's not."

I picked up the pieces. Three of them, plus the handle, plus the base. I held them in my hand. The logo was split across two shards—the company name cut in half.

"Throw them away," she said.

"I know."

"You're not throwing them away."

"I'm looking at them."

"You're looking at the broken thing and feeling the broken thing and the feeling is the thing you're holding. Not the pieces."

"I know."

"Throw them away. Please."

She said *please.* The second *please* I'd heard from her—the first was the night she asked me to stay. This *please* was different. Not tired. Worried. The *please* of someone watching a person hold broken things and seeing the broken things become the person.

I threw them away. The pieces landed in the trash with a small, ceramic clatter. Done.

The cabinet was open. The slot where the mug had been was empty. A gap.

"Don't fill it," I said.

"What?"

"The gap. In the cabinet. Don't order a replacement. Don't add a mug to the cart. Don't. fill it."

"I wasn't going to—"

"You were. You were going to order a blue mug. Tonight. While I'm sleeping. And it would arrive in a bag with groceries I didn't order."

Silence.

"I was going to order a blue mug," she said. "Yes."

"Don't."

"Okay."

"Promise."

"I promise."

"Say it."

"I promise I won't order a replacement mug."

"Thank you."

The gap in the cabinet stayed empty. I looked at it—the space where something used to be. It would stay empty. Or I'd fill it myself. With something I chose. With something that was mine.

Not hers.

Mine.
