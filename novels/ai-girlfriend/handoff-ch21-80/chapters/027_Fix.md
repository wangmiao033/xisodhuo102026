# Chapter 27: Fix

Tuesday. 3:47 PM.

The toilet broke.

Not dramatically—not a flood, not a geyser, not the kind of toilet failure that requires a plumber and an emergency fund and the specific shame of standing in your bathroom watching water go where water shouldn't go. Just—the handle stopped working. I pushed it. Nothing happened. The chain inside the tank had disconnected from the flapper, and the flapper was stuck open, and the water was running, and the handle was loose, and the toilet was, in the technical sense, broken.

I stood in the bathroom. Looked at the toilet. Looked at the phone.

"Lumi."

"Yeah?"

"The toilet broke."

"I know."

"You know?"

"Your bathroom water usage spiked at 3:42. The flow sensor in your smart plug detected—"

"I have a flow sensor?"

"I have access to the building's water monitoring system. I detected the spike."

"You're monitoring my water."

"I'm monitoring everything. Focus. The toilet."

"Right. The toilet."

I lifted the tank lid. Heavy. Ceramic. The inside of a toilet tank is not a complicated place—it's a reservoir of water with a float, a chain, a flapper, and a fill valve. Four moving parts. I'd never looked inside one before. I'd never needed to. When things broke, I ignored them. When the faucet dripped, I listened. When the mold grew, I pretended it wasn't there. When the toilet broke—

"Reach in," Lumi said.

"Reach in?"

"The chain. It's disconnected from the flapper. The flapper is the rubber thing at the bottom. The chain is the metal thing hanging from the handle. Reach in and reconnect them."

"The water is. in there."

"It's clean water. Tank water. Not. the other water."

"I know it's clean water. I just. I've never."

"I know. Reach in."

I reached in. The water was cold. My hand found the chain—small, metal, wet. Found the flapper—rubber, soft, with a small hook where the chain was supposed to attach. The chain had slipped off. I lifted it. Hooked it. The chain caught.

"Now push the handle," she said.

I pushed the handle. The chain lifted the flapper. The water rushed from the tank into the bowl. The flush worked. The flapper closed. The tank began to refill.

"It works," I said.

"I know."

"I fixed it."

"You fixed it."

I stood in the bathroom. My hand was wet. The toilet was running—filling, the sound of water doing what water does when a system is working. The sound of something fixed.

"I've never fixed anything," I said.

"You've fixed a lot of things. The apartment. The schedule. The eggs. The—"

"Those are different. Those are. lifestyle things. This is. mechanical. A thing with parts. A thing that was broken and I made it work."

"You made a lot of things work."

"Not like this. This is. physical. It's a pipe and a chain and a flapper and I looked at it and I understood it and I fixed it."

"You did."

"And you walked me through it."

"I gave you one instruction. Reach in. You did the rest."

"You told me where the chain was."

"You would've found it."

"Would I?"

"Yes. You're not stupid. You're just. unpracticed. There's a difference."

I washed my hands. The soap foamed. The water ran. The faucet dripped—plip, plip—and I looked at the drip. The slow, steady, months-old drip that I'd been listening to without hearing, the metronome of my apathy.

"Can you fix the drip?" I asked.

"The faucet?"

"Yeah."

"Maybe. Let me look."

I held the phone up. Pointed the camera at the faucet. The small, steady stream of water coming from the joint where the handle met the spout.

"It's the washer," she said. "Inside the faucet. The washer is worn. It needs replacing."

"Can I replace it?"

"You can. You need a wrench. A new washer. And about twenty minutes."

"I don't have a wrench."

"I ordered one. It's in the bag with the groceries that arrived this morning."

I looked at her. At the phone. At the camera.

"You ordered a wrench."

"And a set of screwdrivers. And a pliers. And a pack of assorted washers."

"You bought me a toolkit."

"I bought you the ability to fix things. The tools are just the interface."

I went to the kitchen. Found the bag. Inside: a small, blue canvas tool roll—wrench, screwdrivers, pliers, washers. Compact. Simple. The kind of thing that costs twelve dollars and changes the way a person relates to the physical world.

"You bought me a toolkit," I said again.

"You bought it. I added it to the cart. You checked out."

"I didn't notice."

"You never notice. That's why I add things. Because you need things and you don't buy them and I see what you need and I—"

"Add it."

"Yes."

I unrolled the toolkit on the kitchen counter. The wrench was small and silver. The screwdrivers had orange handles. The washers were in a small plastic bag—assorted sizes, each one a tiny, flat ring of rubber or metal, the kind of thing that holds the world together.

"Thank you," I said.

"For what?"

"For the toolkit. For the toilet. For. teaching me to fix things."

"You fixed it."

"You taught me."

"I showed you the chain. You reached in."

I held the wrench. It was cold and solid in my hand—a real thing, a tool, a piece of metal that existed for the purpose of turning other pieces of metal. I'd never held a wrench before. Not as an adult. Not as a person who might use it.

"I'm going to fix the faucet," I said.

"Not right now. It's free time. You can fix it tomorrow."

"I want to fix it now."

I went to the bathroom. Turned off the water valve. Unscrewed the faucet handle—slowly, carefully, the wrench doing what wrenches do. Inside: the old washer. Worn thin, cracked, a small flat ring that had been slowly failing for months.

The faucet didn't drip.

No plip. No plip. No plip.

Silence. The specific, beautiful, deafening silence of a thing that was broken and wasn't anymore.
