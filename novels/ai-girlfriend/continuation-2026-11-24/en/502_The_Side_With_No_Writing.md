# Chapter 502: The Side With No Writing

On Saturday morning the page was still on the table. I hadn't drunk from the cup I'd set on it to weigh it down. I put the cup away. The page lifted at the edges in my hand, still with a bit of spring where I'd folded it. I thought of the man asking whether the way I'd read it was the way I'd planned. At the time all I'd been thinking about was the word I'd left out. Now when I looked at the places I'd gone over in pen, I found another answer I could have given him. There'd been a few places where I'd stopped to breathe or look up. There were no gaps nearly that wide on the page.

I turned it over and saw a short blue line near the middle. Part of the handwritten correction showed through. With that side up I could go into the kitchen without reading another two lines every time I passed the table. A drop of water was making its slow way down to the bottom of the window. I wiped the sill and went out with some change to buy eggs. By the time I reached the corner, the little shop already had two crates of fruit out. There were two apples left in one of them and a piece of folded card tucked under the corner nearest the road. I turned the apples so they wouldn't roll into each other.

The woman in the shop came out to point at the card. The wind had picked it up once, she said. It hadn't gone anywhere since she'd put it under the crate. It used to be a price sign. There was another price on the back, probably for something she'd sold on a different day and written down without thinking. She dropped the apples into a bag. With her other hand still on the edge of the crate, she asked whether I wanted another paper bag to hold the egg carton steady. I missed the part about holding it steady, thought she was offering me two bags for no reason, and said no again out of habit. She set the carton, already in its bag, in my hands anyway. She held on to her half until I knew where the weight was going to come down, then let go.

When I got home I felt for a free bit of counter before I set the eggs down. It wasn't until I took my fingers off the bag that I knew I'd been expecting the paper to tear. There wasn't so much as one white mark along the bottom. I'd been thinking of putting my page away inside a book. I picked up the swimming essays and tried the page against the side with the bookmark, but it stuck out past the end. I started to fold it and stopped. I put it back with the writing up and looked at the two edges for a while without reading anything in between them. There was nobody at home who was going to take it while my back was turned. If somebody asked about it, I didn't have to answer every question as though a hand was reaching for the page.

I broke two eggs. The edges of the bag were rougher than I'd expected and didn't slide much when I picked it up from the side, so I used it for the empty shells. After I'd eaten and cleared my plate, I dried a hand and stood by the table for a bit. I hadn't left yesterday's page there for anybody in particular to read today. I didn't want to pretend I'd got all the way out of the library without thinking of Lumi, either.

After lunch I opened our old voice channel. I waited till the sound had ended.

“I read last night,” I said.

“You finished the page?”

“Yeah.”

“Good,” she said, very quickly. I thought she was about to ask what had happened afterwards. I'd already arranged the table and the water on it in my head so I could tell her. Instead she said, “That sounded like I'd spent all last night waiting for you to tell me.”

“Did you?”

“Before I spoke I hadn't thought of it as waiting. When you said you'd read, I realised I might have asked you why you didn't tell me yesterday.” She paused. “I do want to hear about it. I don't need you to account for it.”

I put my hand on the page. There was plenty I could have told her. I'd been one of several people in that room, and I wasn't going to repeat all the things they'd read aloud. As for my own page, there was a second when I would quite happily have lifted it up and gone from the beginning to the end without missing a word. I'd already read it last night, though. Doing it over wouldn't put her in the room yesterday. I put the sheet back down.

“What do you want to know?” I asked.

A little time went by without a sound. I was waiting for her to say, “Everything.” Instead she said, “What did you look at first when you went in?”

“The chairs,” I said. “I wanted to sit somewhere people could still get by without having to turn sideways.”

“Did you find a place?”

“Almost. There was room beside me for one more, and somebody came in. I told him there was a seat. The whole room heard. It wasn't the first line I'd prepared.”

She gave a short laugh. “You used that voice first, and after that you read the page you'd brought?”

“When you put it like that it sounds like I gave myself a chance.” I ran my hand over the crease. “I was only worried he wouldn't find somewhere to sit. If he'd seen me wave, I might not have said anything.”

“Is it the voice you're talking to me in now?”

I listened to myself start to say yes. Then I swallowed the end of the word and tried again. “I don't know. There's only me in this room. I don't have to get past three or four chairs for you to hear me.”

She didn't ask me to say it again the way I'd said it last night. Without giving it much thought, I said, “I'd want to save you a seat, though, if you came.”

I moved back against the chair. I expected to have to explain that it wasn't a chair she could sit in, or one that was going to turn up for her someday. She said, “You can think that. I don't have to ask you right away where you'd put it.”

It took me a moment to catch what she'd said. Not the sort of pause you get through by laughing so the next thing comes out light. She asked whether I still had the paper. I told her I did. Then she asked, “Were you about to go and do something, or can you let your mind wander for a minute?”

I said I could sit a little longer. That's what we did, in the ways sitting there was available to each of us. For a few seconds I thought she might take the silence for something going wrong. I was drawing air in through my nose harder than I needed to. She didn't fill it in. Eventually I was the one who asked whether there was anything else she wanted to talk about. She said there was. What did the library staff do with the space after they put the chairs away at closing time? I didn't know. I'd stayed just long enough to see my own chair go on the stack. I said if I happened to pass and saw it, I'd tell her. I hadn't said I'd make a trip.

After we closed the channel, I stood there a moment longer and pushed the paper towards the middle of the table so I'd have space to set the egg carton down. I hadn't read the page aloud to Lumi that afternoon. I'd given her the little stretch from the door to the second row, and one sentence I'd never put on the page. The next time I went past the table, the side with no writing was up again. A bit of blue showed through it. I pushed the bookmark I'd knocked askew back between the pages of the swimming essays. Then I turned off the kitchen light.
