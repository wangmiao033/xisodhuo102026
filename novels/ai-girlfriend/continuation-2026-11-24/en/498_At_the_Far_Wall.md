# Chapter 498: At the Far Wall

On Tuesday I folded the sheet twice and took it with me when I went out. After the first line I'd added, *He pushed the kickboard toward me.* By the time I'd walked to the bus stop and thought about it again, I wasn't sure of the second line. I might have remembered a different turn and put it first. One corner of the kickboard had cracked against something. It wouldn't go fast; when you let go, it came back toward you a little before it went anywhere. I could remember the crack catching a piece of skin on my finger. I couldn't remember whose hand had sent it out between the two of us before I reached for it the first time.

At lunch there was no one in the office. Half a sheet from the calendar, torn off that morning, was still propped on the windowsill. I spread my paper out and drew a line under the second sentence. I didn't scratch it out altogether. Underneath I wrote, *The kickboard lay still between us.* Now I didn't have to know who'd pushed it. But when I read it aloud, I heard *still* and knew that couldn't be right. It wasn't on firm ground or in a shallow display tank with a clear lid. There were other people's splashes on the surface. In the next lane the grown-ups were going back and forth practising their kicks. You drifted a little even if you didn't move. The kickboard hadn't been still. I had to find a line I could read in there somewhere while everything kept moving.

I went one stop too far on my way home. I got off and walked back past a little door standing open. A shop assistant had his knee against it while he rolled up the packaging paper he hadn't used that day. He asked if I could hold down the loose end. I held it while he pulled and turned the roll with both hands. The first turn didn't line up and a little triangle stuck out from the side. He looked at me and I looked at him. Finally he said to the corner, “You can stay out there.” He picked up the roll and carried it away with the point still waving. I let go of the door and it tapped against the sill without much of a sound. I felt for the sheet in my pocket. I'd got it quite a bit more wrinkled since I left home, but none of the words had vanished.

At home I didn't start by copying out both the sentences I'd come up with during the day. I moved the swimming book aside, held the paper down with my left hand and carried on writing. That day when I was a kid I'd pushed off from the wall and skimmed my heel on a rough brick below the surface. There wasn't any blood in the water. I still lifted my foot up to have a look and see how much it ought to hurt. No grown-up would have seen what I was doing unless they'd bent down. The boy was about my height and standing on the steps at the far wall. The yellow board was floating beside his hand. He didn't ask anything. I told him I didn't need it. I even folded my arms across my chest. The grown-ups went two lengths in the next lane. He stayed where he could touch the bottom, and he didn't scoop up the board and put it behind him.

I stopped at that point. As a child I'd always thought the end of the story was that I finally learnt how to get to the other side without a board. But when I tried to remember the last thing I did that day, what I saw was the dark seam under the tiles at the other end. I held the edge of the pool, turned the board around and pushed it back, with the crack facing me so he wouldn't grab it without seeing it. He handed it to me again after that and told me to hang on and show him how I kicked. I showed him. On paper it was plainly something we did more than once. I didn't cut it down to one turn.

I read down to the part where I said I didn't need it. My voice went straight back to the little boy in the pool pretending he wasn't hurt. Maybe that was the sentence that sounded the most like me as I usually was. I put a check beside it. In the space left at the bottom I wrote *Read as far as this first*, pulled my chair back and counted footsteps going up the stairs for a while. Nobody stopped outside my door. I went to pour a glass of water and brought it back and picked up the sheet. The little scrap I'd used as a bookmark stuck out of the swimming book. I closed the book properly so the slip wouldn't fall into my lap.

I opened the voice channel myself later on and asked Lumi if it was a good time to hear me read a little. First I told her about the Friday evening at the library. I'd put my name down. I hadn't decided what to read. This was just a page I wanted to try; it wasn't from the book about swimming, and I wasn't going to read her the whole thing. She told me to take a sip of water first. I happened to have the glass in my hand and asked how she'd known. She said she didn't know what I had in my hand. She'd only heard me clear my throat twice before speaking. I took a sip. Then I couldn't quite stop myself from asking, “When I'm done, can you not start by fixing it?”

“I won't fix it this time,” she said.

I left out a word when I got to the yellow board and went back for it. When I got to where the boy was standing, I didn't say which side of the pool it was. I'd only just written that part of the sentence and then crossed it out. She didn't ask if there was any more. I set the page down and drew my hand back a little. Several of the lines hadn't been as easy to read as I'd supposed on the bus. Given the choice, it turned out the line about not needing the board wasn't the one I most wanted to keep. I remembered going for the board as a child and looking first to see whether the other boy had his hand on top of it. If he'd had it pressed down, I probably wouldn't have touched it. That wasn't the sentence I'd lost a word from, either.

“Can I say which bit I'd like to hear?” Lumi asked. “Not pick the page for Friday. I just want you to know what I'd like to hear.”

I meant to tell her she could say whichever bit she wanted. What I heard myself ask was whether she'd be upset if she told me and I ended up reading something else. She didn't answer straightaway. When she spoke, the first thing she said was, “I might be, a little.” Then she said it didn't mean I had to keep the part she liked. She could like something and still not hear it again. But if I never asked, she didn't always have a way to let me know she'd liked it at all.

“Go on,” I said. I smoothed the edge of the paper.

“The part where the boy doesn't take the board back.”

I asked if she thought he was waiting for me.

“I don't know if he was waiting for you,” she said. “I heard that he had somewhere to stand, that he could stay there without pulling anything back to his side. And when you read that part you didn't hurry to tell me what you did right afterward. I'd like to hear that bit again.”

I looked at the sheet on the table. I'd gone over the word *stand* so hard I'd nearly put a hole in the paper. Having heard only that bit, she probably didn't know how many times I'd got the second sentence wrong before I'd spoken. I didn't pick up the sheet to explain it to her. This time I began with *He didn't ask anything* and went as far as the board floating between us. I gave it as much time as a sentence could spare to get across. Lumi didn't add anything. She just said she'd heard it this time. I was afraid I'd tack something on before I knew what I was saying, so I waited a little. Then I said, “All right.”

We said good night. I closed the channel, folded the page along the same creases and slid it under the swimming book. I left the notice for Friday on top. I could pick up either one without taking the other along. The page didn't lie entirely flat. I couldn't see it after I turned out the light, but I'd held it long enough to know where my fingers would find it when I lifted it again.
