# Chapter 503: No Name on the Back

On Sunday I took the bicycle out of the hall, and the moment the front wheel crossed the brick at the door, the handlebars tipped right. I squeezed the tire. Softer than yesterday. Two streets from home there was a neighborhood repair station that brought its stands out into the yard on Sunday mornings. Someone had told me you could do the work yourself and didn't have to buy tools. I walked the bike halfway there before remembering I could have brought my own pump. That would have meant another trip up the stairs for nothing but air, so I kept walking.

The yard wasn't the sort of shop where you'd find a case full of new parts. There was a basin on the ground, a tin, some clamps, and two stools light enough to move. If someone needed to lift a bike, whoever stood across from them had to step away from the tool table. A woman with very short hair asked if I'd taken out an inner tube before. I told her I had, and that I'd once pinched a hole in one putting it back.

She reached between the frame and the tire and brushed out a wet leaf. “Then you know where not to force it. Do you want me to hold it, or do you want to hold it while I take off the wheel?”

“I'll hold it. I'd like to watch you do this one first.”

She said all right, took off the front wheel, and handed it to me. Find somewhere to sit that wasn't on anybody's feet, she said. I'd imagined dropping off the bike and coming back when it was fixed. It wasn't until I had the wheel in my lap that I noticed there were hardly any bikes on the stands waiting to be collected. Most of them still had their owners' hands on them. At the far end of the basin, a boy in glasses was crouching to screw on a valve cap. His mother told him to put away his gloves. He said he was nearly done. A little later the cap dropped into the basin. His mother was already reaching when he rolled up his own sleeve. She took her hand away.

I levered one bead halfway off with a plastic tire lever. There were two old scratches on the rim where the lever could catch and stay put. The woman told me to give the loose tube enough air to plump it up before I put it in the water. I did as she said, feeding a section at a time through the basin. At first there were bubbles everywhere, especially where there'd been mud underneath. I didn't want to mark the wrong place, so I brought that part round again. There were flecks floating on the water; the whole basin looked as though it had a leak. By then the boy had his cap on and came over to watch. I asked if he would mind looking for a string of bubbles all coming from the same spot. He put one glove in his pocket and bent forward. When I brought the last quarter round, a little chain of bubbles rose beside my thumb.

“There,” he said.

My hand jerked, and the bubbles stopped. He didn't point again. He turned his head a little and waited. When I eased my grip, two more floated up. His mother handed him his other glove. He took it without looking up, and only went to get his coat once he'd seen me draw a circle. After they left, I dried the tube in the yard and saw that my circle would have held three or four of the actual hole. I held a fingernail up to the patch on its backing and made a smaller mark in the middle. The woman found blunt tweezers for me to pull out whatever was still in the tire. It was a bit of pale thorn, shorter than the tips she'd handed me. I felt all the way round the inside of the tire without finding another. The woman handed me a square of sandpaper, and I rubbed it over the rubber inside the circle.

Once I put the cement on, I’d have to wait until it lost its tack before putting down the patch. A bare vine reached out from the yard wall. The last few inches stuck into the wind and went back and forth like a tiny saw. There was a queue by then. I didn't want to keep one of the stools, so I stood up with the wheel. The woman raised a hand when she saw me moving. There was no rush, she told me. She had a clear spot in front of her on the table where it would fit. I put the wheel there and went out to get water. The bike stayed. The wheel stayed. Nobody had asked my name. By the time I reached the shop, it turned out to be harder to find someone willing to change a small note than to fix a bicycle. Eventually I bought a room-temperature bottle from a little store. The shopkeeper laid my change out in a row, and it took both hands to sweep it all into my palm.

The way back took me along the side of the library. A long fingerprint streaked the glass at the entrance. I hadn't noticed it going in on Monday. The lights were on, and the table inside the doors was empty. I stood there a moment and tapped the bottle against my palm. Reading on Friday had been the whole thing. I'd left them no copy. Going in today wasn't a way of making up something I'd left undone. I found I wanted to know whether I could come and sit next time, though I might not want to read. I went up to the desk. The person behind it was fitting a jacket round a thick book. She cleared half the counter for me.

I asked whether you had to sign up if you only wanted to listen at one of those read-a-page evenings. She said no. You just had to find a chair when you arrived. If you worked late and didn't get a seat at the front, that didn't stop you listening. She drew a little card from a stack. It had the library hours on it and the website at the bottom. There wasn't a date for another night yet, she said. If one was arranged, there'd be a notice at the door and on the website. “If you want somebody to let you know, you can put a name on the back. I'll leave it here. I can't promise somebody will remember to look at it.”

I turned the card over. The back was blank. The pencil on the counter was shorter than the one I'd signed up with. It would do for a mark, but to write out anything you'd have to hold it with the tips of your fingers. I asked whether I could just take the card. She got the jacket onto the book, held both corners down with one hand, and looked up at me. Of course. She'd given it to me to take. I left the pencil where it was and put the card in my pocket.

Before I went out, I took a look at the chairs. The part I'd wanted Lumi to hear yesterday was still the part I wanted to tell her today. The rest of what I'd written hadn't acquired a use just because I could imagine one more person here asking about it. I supposed I could sit by the door next time, hear a page, and go out to buy some water. No need to leave the shape of myself in the back of a chair.

When I got to the yard, the vine was still moving. The woman had changed one glove. I squeezed a little cement onto the spot I’d scuffed, spread it out, and waited until it was dry enough not to stick to a finger. I pressed the patch down myself. Then I fitted the tube back without trying to stuff the last stretch of tire over the rim all at once. She told me to work it toward the middle with both thumbs instead of jabbing at it with the lever. It snapped as it went over, and I bent down to see what had come loose. Nothing had, she said. That was the bead going into place. Through the tire I couldn't see the hole the boy had found. I put in a little more air, checked that the stem was straight, and put the wheel back on. Before I left I rode half a slow circle by the entrance, braked, and walked the bike the rest of the way out so the next person could get through.

At home in the afternoon, I slipped the hours card beside the bookmark in the swimming essays. I left them a little offset so I wouldn't pull them both out at once. I didn't look up the site or put anything in my calendar. There was a grain of grit caught in the bottom of my pocket. I picked it up with a damp finger. The page I'd brought home on Friday was still here. I picked up the book thinking I'd move it somewhere else, and wound up reading half a page instead. I closed it when the sun went off the window frame. From the sofa I couldn't see the bicycle downstairs, but I'd squeezed the front tire myself before coming up. The next time I rode, squeezing it again would be enough to start with.
