# Chapter 497: A Line in Pencil

I went to the library after work on Monday. I hadn't come to return the swimming book. Before bed the night before I'd found a few words somebody had penciled inside the back cover, so close to the binding that I had to open the book flat to read them. *Go back a page or so.* Two pages back there really was a little mark in the margin. Nothing had been written beside it. I liked that. It was still my book; if I hadn't gone looking, I wouldn't have missed anything I was expected to know. Today I'd left it at home. What I wanted to ask was whether there'd be another book swap like the one on Saturday.

There was a bench where the table had stood. A man sat sideways on it with both feet in what were presumably his own shoes. A much smaller pair lay by his feet. He was looking toward the toilets and when he saw me coming he tried to kick the little shoes under the bench. I told him I wasn't in a hurry to get through. He hooked one shoe back with the toe of his own and set it facing the other, as if he really did have some business to attend to with the pair of them there. After a while a child came out barefoot. He stepped one foot into each shoe, took them both out, and did it again. This time they were on the right feet. The man smiled at me as he stood up. They went out. I didn't go any farther down the passage either. I turned back to the desk.

The woman at the desk said they'd borrowed the table. It had been a one-off on Saturday. She found a bit of paper, wrote down the address of a bookshop that held regular swaps, and slid the paper across. On the other side it said READ A PAGE ON FRIDAY. A streak of ink ran under the words as if somebody printing it had touched the paper on the way to lifting it clear. I turned it back over and realized it had this library's name on it and a date that was still to come. This Friday.

“Is this finished too?” I asked.

“That one hasn't started.” She pulled a separate sheet out from under a pile of slips people had signed for their books. “Seven in the evening. Three minutes at most. You can just come and listen. If you think you might read, put your name down.”

There were two names there already. Whoever had written the first one had left it well over to the left; the second began in the middle of the line and went on for quite a way. I considered turning the sheet over, to give myself another moment to look. She picked up the flyer she'd used for the wrong thing, shook it open, and caught the edge lightly against the counter.

“The address for the swap is still on this side,” she said. “You can take both. I mean, both sides of the paper are good for something.”

I asked whether the page had to be from something somebody else had published. There was a little basket beneath the counter with a handful of pencils standing up in it, all nearly used up. She gave me the longest of them and said the last time they'd run it, a woman had read an old letter from home. Her family hadn't minded her reading it without the names, and when she finished nobody knew who'd written it. A boy after her had opened a book and got halfway down his page before telling them he'd brought the wrong one. He'd asked if he could turn over. Someone said whatever he liked was fine. He thought about it and went on reading the wrong page. She remembered there were two tractors on it. She didn't know what was on the other side.

“So I don't have to put a title down now?”

She turned the sheet toward me. There wasn't even a place for a title; there were just the faint blue lines. If you got up there and found you didn't want to read, she said, you could pass. It wasn't being recorded. Nobody had to hand anything in, either; if you wanted to take your page away with you afterward, it was yours to take. One or two people who'd come to listen had got up and read last time. If everyone wanted to have a go, she'd use a timer. She brought it out to show me. It was smaller than I'd imagined, a little white square of a kitchen timer with no brand on the front. Someone had stuck a strip of tape to it that said PLEASE TURN THE SOUND OFF BEFORE RETURNING. She spotted it before I said anything, put her thumb over the words, and smiled.

“The timer isn't there to punish anybody. Three minutes just gives the people after you a turn.”

I'd put the flyer in my bag. Before I had an answer for her, I was holding the pencil over the sheet. The line was so pale that I had to move my elbow and straighten up to see where I was putting the point. It slid when I drew the first stroke of the E and left two lines that didn't quite match. I thought I might go over them. The light above me threw a very thin shadow off the pencil point; when I moved it, that shadow went right across what I'd written. She didn't hurry me. Somebody came to return a very thick book. She reached out through the opening with both hands and waited for him to let go before she put it down. While she did that I finished writing *Elliot*. I didn't go over the first letter and I didn't put down my last name.

On the way out I paused by the bench. Where the little shoes had stood there were two trails in the dust. The bench was close enough to the wall to make it awkward to sweep underneath. The old swap table hadn't covered the floor this far over. It couldn't have come back every weekend to clear a space for it, either. I was still standing there when a girl who looked too small to be carrying that thick book came out holding it in both hands. It hadn't been about to go back on the shelf after all. She'd been waiting for someone to bring it back. As soon as she was outside, she lifted the book vertically and leaned it against her to get her right hand free to wrap her scarf once around her neck. She didn't try to read the title. She didn't look down to see if it had anybody's name in it. She'd just come to take it away.

On the way home I wondered if I ought to choose the part in the swimming book about letting someone pass. The first time I'd seen anyone actually do that in a slow lane was at night, at a different pool from the one in the book. There was a yellow kickboard with a broken edge. I could remember it more clearly than I could remember anything I'd said. With a whole book of sentences ready for me, though, there were any number of ways to avoid finding out in front of other people if I had it right. Upstairs, I put the notice on top of the book, covering the upper half of the lane rope on the cover. The paper stuck out a little on one side. I'd touch it first next time I picked the book up.

I didn't open the voice channel that night. I picked up the swimming book more than once and thought I might try a line or two to see if the voice already written down on the page could come naturally out of my own mouth. I read the first sentence and knew I hadn't come home to choose the safest thing I could say. I put the book back and found a blank sheet with an address that hadn't printed properly in one corner. I folded that corner in. Then I wrote, *The kickboard was yellow.* It was a short first line. I couldn't easily get it wrong, and if I missed it altogether, nobody had written a second one for me. I'd just finished when I heard somebody outside collecting the metal stands along the street. They made a racket up half the street. None of it reached as far as the pavement below my window.
