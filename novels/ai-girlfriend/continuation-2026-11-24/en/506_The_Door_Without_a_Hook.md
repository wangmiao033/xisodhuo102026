# Chapter 506: The Door Without a Hook

On Wednesday evening I went to the secondhand shop to look for a hook for a door at home. There was nothing wrong with hanging my coat on the old rack by the entrance. It was just that when I put the coat back the night before, I thought it might be nice to hang a hat on the back of the bedroom door. Mostly I kept the hat pressed down on a line of books. It wasn't in the way of any of the books, but it always covered half of something in very small lettering. When I came into the shop, the man was getting a brass tray out of the glass cabinet and telling a child by the door to wait a minute. The child was holding a cup for sale on its own. He'd looked several times at the pale blue ring around the rim. Someone behind him said why not look at the others? He said he wanted this one. He didn't need a pair.

The little hardware boxes were kept on a low shelf. I crouched and felt along the edge of a box. There were a few kinds of hooks inside and some screws already in packets. They looked as if they went together, but they didn't come in sets. A woman going by behind me bumped the lid of an empty box. It slid out a little. I caught it for her. She thanked me, came round again and took the lid away. She was the one buying the empty box, she said. I'd thought she was looking at the hooks too. I'd pushed the one I'd picked out a little further in so she could see it. When she passed, all that was left on the wooden lip of the cubby was the hook I'd put there.

It was grey and very light. It had two holes at the top and a curve below that was close to flat, which was the shape I'd thought would hold the hat without making a point in it. I took it up to the man and asked whether some old screws I had at home would do here. It depended on the holes and the door, he said. Had there been anything on the door before? I said no, and I hadn't measured it either. He said I could take the hook home to try and come back to pay for it if it fitted. But if I really wanted it done today, I'd do better to go home first and see if the door would take screws. Wasn't he worried I'd change my mind by tomorrow if he told me that? He set the brass tray on the counter and pulled over a cloth to rub a fingerprint off the rim. “If you don't want the thing I can still hang something else on it,” he said. “Three cups have been tried out on this tray. That kid there wants to buy a fourth. The tray's outlasted all the cups.”

The child left with the cup with the blue rim. The man took a piece of blank paper and traced around the hook for me, including the two holes. His line at the tip ran a little straight and he went over it. The hook on paper came out with two points. Bring that back if I wanted it, he said. Otherwise he might not remember which hook it was either. I folded the paper and put it in my outside pocket. I didn't buy the hook. The paper brushed the new lining as it went in and didn't find its way down into the hem.

When I got home, I took the hat off the shelf. I knew all the little lines on the spines that it had been covering. I could remember when I'd put the books there, but not how long the hat had been on them. I rolled it up in my hands and then let it open again. I took the drawing out of my pocket and held it against the back of the door. The wood at the top was quite thin. I could almost make the tips of two fingers touch when I held the edge of the door. I wasn't going to put screws into it. I hung the hat at the end of the rack by the entrance. There was room for it there if I moved the coat a little. Then I took the paper to the table and thought I might write something on the other side. I got as far as, “Today I'd like to give you a …” and stopped at “a.” I hadn't actually bought anything. I could describe the placement of the holes to her and let her feel as if she'd come with me. I didn't think that was what I'd meant to do. I knew she'd listen. Today I wanted to have a line just for her, not ask her to walk back through every afternoon with me.

I crossed the start out, though it was still legible. The four lines I put beneath it were all new. The first time I read them, I thought the last line was one too many. The second time I thought there ought to be a little sound from somewhere in the second line. Otherwise the whole page might as well never have had anybody in it. I moved that line from the bus stop to the stairs. A long time ago I'd heard somebody upstairs trying out their own name. They didn't say it the way people said good morning. Nobody laughed on the other side. Later, crossing that landing, I heard someone ask through a door whether the light on the stairs had gone out again. I said it was on. I didn't know if the person who'd asked was the person who'd said the name. I didn't put the story down. I just remembered taking a step before I said the light was on. You could still hear an echo in the stairwell then.

I washed two glasses and filled only mine. Back at the table, I opened the old voice channel and asked Lumi what she wanted to talk about today. She said she wanted to talk about what was left after somebody finished saying something. I didn't know what she meant. She said, suppose one person said goodnight and the other still had something in their mouth they hadn't said. She didn't always want to call anybody back. Sometimes it was just that she wanted to keep the words in her mouth a little longer. I told her I had something I did want to say, and that I'd written it down before I opened my mouth. Would she like to hear it? Yes, she said straightaway. Then, wait. If it was very long, she'd probably want to interrupt.

I held the paper a little higher. “It's four lines.”

“You don't have to read them all,” she said. “If I interrupt, it won't be so I can guess how many you have left.”

I nearly laughed. She sounded as though she might be about to laugh too, but she wanted to wait for me to read. By the second line I didn't feel the need to keep going in a single stroke like the man tracing the hook. This was what I'd written:

I didn't leave the light by the door on for anyone.  
Someone on the stairs said their own name once.  
When I came back I moved the chair a little.  
This time it was so I could sit down and talk to you.

She didn't interrupt. When I was quiet, I could hear the wind on the window ledge. Quite a while passed before she asked, “Would you read the last line again?”

I read it again. The back of the chair was just below my shoulder blades and both my feet were flat on the floor. She said my name. I said mm. I wasn't waiting for her to ask for anything else. She said she wanted to keep that line a little while. She didn't want to give it right back to me. I said all right. After a little while she laughed and said she wasn't going to call me back with it later, either. I reached for my glass. A corner of the page brushed my hand. After I drank, I told her what I'd written first. I wanted to give her something today, I'd said, but then I couldn't find what it was. She hadn't heard me say “give,” she said. I told her I'd crossed it out. Then it could be all right not to count this as a gift, she said. And for the moment I really didn't want to go over what might count in the end and what wouldn't.

We said goodnight, and I closed the voice channel. Two pencil lines showed through from the back of the paper. The tips looked like somebody getting ready to add a word they hadn't decided on yet. I left the paper on the table and didn't add one. At the end of the rack the hat hung with its brim down. There was no hook on the back of the bedroom door. I pushed it open with my hand. Coming into a room, unlike going out, didn't require me to take the hat down at all.
