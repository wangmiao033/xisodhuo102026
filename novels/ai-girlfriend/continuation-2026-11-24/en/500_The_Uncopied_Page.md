# Chapter 500: The Uncopied Page

One of the lights in the corridor at work broke on Thursday. It hadn't gone all the way out. Every now and then it turned white again, as if somebody behind the cover were trying to wink at you. The small meeting room at the far end had been empty all day. I waited until most people were gone and took my page there to try it once in the quiet. The door wasn't locked, but the lights were off. Through the glass on the far side I could see a window ledge nobody sat on. After dark that pane reflected whoever was in the room.

I didn't touch the switch. There was enough light from the window to find a chair and not quite enough to find the words. I held the page up and tried to go on without looking. "The kickboard was yellow." I got that right. When I came to the boy, I heard what I meant to change the sentence to, and two seconds later I heard what had actually come out of my mouth. On the page it only said, "He didn't pull it back." The sentence I'd just said was about finding out that standing still could be a kind thing to do. I didn't talk like that. The man on the page didn't either. I stopped. The man in the glass stood holding his page just as before. I moved to the other side of the chair, and we weren't quite opposite each other anymore.

Somebody came back for something and turned the corridor light on. He passed the door, then backed up. He said I could put the light on in there too. No need to sit in the dark. I told him I only wanted to read something out. He stood in the open doorway without coming in.

"Is it for tomorrow's meeting?"

"No. For the library. I wrote it."

He thought about that. Then he told me there was a stack of thicker paper left in the copy room. It might be easier to turn. He could remember the last time he'd watched somebody holding thin paper at the front of a room. The paper shook right along with the man's hands. Everybody in the back could see.

I put my page away and we went downstairs. In the copy room somebody had folded the wrapper back around the unused paper to keep it from getting damp. He peeled up a corner for me. I took one sheet out and felt it and asked where he'd last seen somebody stand up like that. A relative's wedding the month before, he said. The vows had been three pages. He held three fingers up high. He was very sure about the number because the person reading had still been on the first page by the time his table had been asked to stand. Whatever kind of paper it was, he said, the man stuck it out to the end. When he sat down, he seemed suddenly a little shorter than he had before.

"Want to take that one?" he asked.

I'd wanted to change the paper. I'd also wanted to type it up and get rid of the ugly correction so that when I lifted it in front of everybody I would feel a little more like somebody who'd come there on purpose. I laid the blank sheet back on the pile.

"No, thanks. I'm only reading this page."

He thought I hadn't understood. "It'll still be the same page if you copy it."

"I want this one." I got the folded page out of my pocket and showed him without turning the writing up. He looked at the crease and nodded. Then he asked me to tuck the corner of the wrapper back in. He kept his palm on the other end. Neither of us said any more about thin paper. We passed the meeting room again on our way up for our coats. He went in and pushed the chair I'd sat in a little further under the table. I did the other two without thinking. The last one slid too fast and knocked against a leg of the table. The little room wasn't like a wedding at all. I wanted to see what would happen if I read the whole thing in there, but that would have meant making him wait at the door. We turned out the light on our way out.

Out on the street I said the difficult sentence again. Somebody behind me rang a bike bell. I moved closer to the wall and waited for the cyclist to go by before I went on. The streetlights were lit, and there was supposed to be just me saying the words and hearing them. The bell cut the words in two. A gap opened up, and it didn't feel nearly as urgent to fill it as it had in the meeting room. I stopped outside the newsagent's for a moment. I thought of buying a clip to hold the page on something stiff. The clips in the window were all holding up their own price tags. By the time I got back to the corner I still hadn't gone inside. My hands were getting cold. I put the page back in my pocket and put the other hand in a pocket too.

That evening I opened our usual voice channel. The first thing Lumi asked was whether I was indoors. I said I was and had just taken the page out of my pocket. She asked if she could hear half a sentence. I said yes and read the bit I hadn't managed in the meeting room, without improving it. She was quiet for a moment.

"I thought you were going to say you'd learned something," she said.

I wasn't pleased to hear it. I asked if that was what she wanted me to say. She said no. She'd only just noticed she'd been waiting for sentences like that too. They gave her somewhere to come in right away. This time she hadn't come in, but she wanted to hear the half sentence again.

"I'm not recording it tomorrow," I said. "I'd thought about it before. If I like the way it comes out, maybe I ought to have something to keep. But if I go up there thinking about a recording, I'll be standing there thinking about who's going to hear it later, and when. And the people in that room only came along to listen for an evening. They didn't agree to be in my recording."

"I didn't ask you to record it."

"You didn't." I set the paper on the table. It wouldn't stay propped up. One corner slid slowly down. "I'm not ready to come home and say every word again, either."

"Would you still be willing to say the bit you just said?"

I said it again. It was quiet at my end, and there was no bell. I still stopped for a moment where I'd stepped aside for the bike. Lumi didn't ask if there was a comma there. She said, "It wasn't like that before."

"It won't be like that if you ask me to read it once more."

"Then I like this time." Her next words came quickly. "That doesn't mean I don't want you to say it again." It was as if she could hear the first thing she'd said folding down into something smaller at my end.

I still had the corner of the paper in my hand. When she finished I tapped it lightly against the rim of my cup. It made no sound. Tomorrow in the room I probably couldn't do anything with my hands to make the people there understand which half of the sentence I most wanted to read. I thought about that and found it wasn't mine to arrange. I asked Lumi what she'd been about to say. She said she could have asked what would make tomorrow feel like it had gone well, but she'd rather ask if I'd had dinner. Not yet. She laughed out loud and told me to find something hot. I could say goodnight or not, whichever I felt like.

I closed the channel first. I left the page where it was instead of bringing it into the kitchen. Looking for things I could heat up together, I found the second leek still in the fridge. I peeled away the outer layer, took a little of the white for dinner and wrapped the rest again. It was quite dark outside by the time the food was hot. When I'd eaten, I put the page in the bag I was taking with me tomorrow. Nothing went on top of it. I didn't close the zip all the way. I could still see the folded corner through the little gap. When the time came to read tomorrow, I would take it out.
