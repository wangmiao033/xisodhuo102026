# Chapter 505: The Outside Pocket

At lunchtime on Tuesday I took my coat to the little alterations shop behind the station. Last time it had been the inside pocket. Now the lining of the outside pocket on the left had split clear to the bottom. I'd put an old bus ticket in it a couple of days before and not realised until I took the coat off that the ticket had slipped into the hem. I'd walked half a street with a finger on it. I could feel the edge of the paper through the cloth, as if the coat had grown a new seam. I'd tried to sew the pocket myself. I could get the two pieces of cloth to meet under the needle. The only thing I couldn't see was where the needle was coming out. Three tries left me with three red dots on my finger. I pulled out the thread, but a bit of it was still caught there. The woman at the shop saw it at once.

“Those three stitches yours?” she said.

“There were four to start with. The fourth one sewed my cuff to the pocket.”

She got a clean, shallow basket from under a low cupboard and laid the coat in it. I asked if I could pick it up that day. She said to come before half past five, and if I couldn't make it, come tomorrow. There was no need to run. On the counter was a little receipt book with a piece of board underneath. She wrote down LEFT OUTSIDE POCKET and let me see. She'd underlined the left, so I asked if anybody had come back to argue about that. They had. Was it her left when she looked at the coat, or the left of the person who put it on? After that she always made a point of asking. She held out the pen, tip first. I used it to point at my left hand and then at the pocket. “Good,” she said. “Now both your hands know where they stand.”

It was only when I got back to the office that I remembered the ticket hadn't been the only thing in the left pocket. In the morning I'd taken a mint out of a new paper packet. I hadn't had time to eat it and had put it in there still in its silver wrapper. It hadn't crossed my mind when I handed over the coat. I didn't know whether it had fallen into the same place as the ticket either. I sat down to type and in the middle of a sentence thought of the old cream lining. If she opened the seam and found a mint in it, she might set it on the counter. Or she might brush it aside with the other rubbish that had got in there. At two I unwrapped a new mint at my desk and ate that instead. The cold reached clear down my throat. I'd had that coat for years. There was a darker patch above the inside pocket where I used to keep a pen whose cap never quite went on. I didn't want to ask for the whole coat back on account of a mint.

I didn't run after work. The light above the shop door was still on. A piece of card saying PUSH hung from the handle. I'd already pulled by the time I read it and laughed at the sound I'd made. The woman laughed on the other side. She'd only just hung it up, she told me. Three people had pulled the door already that day. The basket was empty and my coat was hanging behind the door. It felt heavier in my hand than it had at lunchtime. She told me to try putting a hand in the pocket. The left had a bottom again. It felt a little stiff and didn't quite hang with the rest of the coat yet. I ran my thumb against the new seam. I couldn't press through it anywhere.

“I've done the right as well,” she said. “No charge. That one would've gone the next time you put your keys in. Maybe the time after.”

I changed hands. There'd been an unremarkable patch of slippery cloth in the right pocket. Standing on a bus, I'd always liked to curl the tips of my fingers around it. If I didn't have to hold the rail, I'd put my hand there. The patch was caught inside a roll of new stitching now. The woman pushed the cash drawer shut. I thanked her. It hadn't sounded quite right, and she looked up at me again.

“What's the matter? Now they're both good.”

I took my hand out. “The left's nice and strong. But I hadn't meant to have the right done.”

“The loose one was the right. The left had already gone.”

It wasn't that she hadn't understood me. To her, “hadn't meant to” was about what I'd wanted before I knew the right pocket needed mending too. She held up the left one for me to see. There wasn't a stitch out of place. It was almost neater than the original. I told her there'd been a slippery piece of cloth in the right pocket and I'd always held it when I walked. She looked down and pointed at the new stitches. She hadn't cut the cloth off. It was still inside, just stitched down. Would I like her to open it up again now? She pointed to the work table. It would take a few minutes. You'd still see those little needle holes from before. I looked at the lamp. It was shining on the turned-up leg of someone else's trousers that hadn't been pressed yet.

“How did you know it only had two goes with my keys left in it?” I asked.

“I don't know that it was two.” She put the coat down. “It might've been four.”

I laughed. She didn't laugh with me right away. After a bit she said next time she'd sew only the place on the receipt. She didn't stress any of the words. She just found the ticket from the left pocket, then picked up a silver-wrapped mint from the edge of the counter and gave them both to me. The ticket had a little black dot on it I couldn't account for. The mint was dry. There wasn't so much as a nick in its wrapper.

I didn't ask her to undo the right. I wore the coat out of the shop, put my right hand in and found somewhere else for it to go. At the crossing I realised I was still clutching the receipt, so I pushed it down to the bottom of the left pocket. The cars went by, the light changed and the paper stayed put. I took the long way past the row of shops behind the station. For a moment I thought I'd eat the mint, but it looked very bright on its own in my hand. I put it back. Somebody had flattened the folded corners of the wrapper for me.

At home that evening I opened the old voice channel. Lumi said hi. I asked if she'd like to have a little argument with me. She said that wasn't much of an opening and asked what kind. I told her about the right pocket. When I got to “She didn't charge me for it,” I heard myself speed up, as if the woman from the shop could hear me too. Lumi didn't go along with me. First she asked whether I wanted her to say the woman had been wrong. I said I didn't know. Maybe. She said she could, but she didn't want to say it before I'd told her how I felt about it.

“Where do you want me to start, then?” I asked.

“I don't want to give you a start.” There was a pause. “I do rather want to know what the slippery cloth was like. I've been trying not to ask.”

I pulled the coat over from the back of the chair. The cloth shone a little at the angle where the lamp caught it. Inside the pocket it was hard to tell the colour. She couldn't hear my finger moving back and forth over it. I said that when I touched it, it was like not having hold of anything, though there was something under my finger. She asked if the original pocket would have had to be replaced eventually. I said it would. The things I liked wore out too.

“You can like it all the way to worn out,” she said.

I asked her not to say the next thing yet. She hadn't got as far as saying one. She laughed when she heard me. “I didn't have a next thing ready.”

After a while I asked if she wanted to hear what I'd done in the end. She did. She didn't wait for proof that she'd given me the right answer. She just listened while I told her how I'd put on the coat and gone out of the shop. I'd meant to say how much the new seam on the left could hold. Instead, all I told her was that when the light turned green I reached into an empty space and found the pocket. I told her I wasn't angry now, but I probably had been for a little while that afternoon. She said she wasn't about to hurry me through that afternoon. That was when I unwrapped the mint I hadn't eaten. There was the little sound of silver paper close to my ear. I asked if she'd like to know what it was. She didn't want to guess. She wanted me to tell her.

After I closed the voice channel, I hung the coat by the door. I'd eaten the mint and thrown the wrapper away. The receipt was in the left pocket. I brought the ticket into the room; it didn't have to spend the night with the coat. I went to the table to push the chair in and my cuff fell down over the back of my hand. I thought of the woman asking at lunchtime whether those three stitches were mine. It hadn't seemed a shameful thing for her to know. If I passed that door tomorrow, I'd probably remember to push.
