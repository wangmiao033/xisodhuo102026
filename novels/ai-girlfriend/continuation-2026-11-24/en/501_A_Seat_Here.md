# Chapter 501: There's a Seat Here

On Friday after work, I stood outside the library for quite a while getting the page out of my bag. One of the two old folds ran straight through the second paragraph. The handwritten correction was somewhere else, and out on the steps there was still enough daylight to make sure I could read it. One sheet was all I needed. I'd said so, and on the way out that morning I hadn't slipped a spare into the bag. In the lobby someone was pushing a cart of books that hadn't been shelved yet. I turned sideways to let it through and the page drew in with me until it lay against my chest. The cart went by. I noticed my hand was still in the same position, as though there might be another cart coming.

There were two rows of chairs inside, or there had been before somebody took one away from the row nearest the door. A woman holding a book of poems nudged the others aside to leave more room in the aisle. I sat at the wall end of the second row. My knees cleared the back of the chair in front by about as much as they needed to. The clock was coming up to seven. The library employee put a little white timer on the table and came around with her list. Someone ahead of me hadn't arrived. Would I mind moving up? I hadn't read at one of these before, and couldn't tell whether she was asking me to move up one place or go straight to the front, so I asked her exactly what she meant. There'd be three pieces before mine, she said. I'd be fourth. I said that was fine. Only afterwards did it occur to me that I hadn't counted how long three pieces might take.

The first reader was an older man who hadn't brought his glasses. He held the book out about as far as his arms would take it. People in the front row could make out words when his lips moved; by the time they reached me, I could only hear the breaths he took between them. The woman from the library got up, went past the end of the table and shut the door a little more firmly. That took care of the returns cart out in the hall. The man noticed her shut the door. He waited till she'd sat down again. “It's quieter here than at my house,” he told her. “When I read at home, she always says I'm too loud.”

Everybody heard that last part. We laughed. It was only after I'd stopped laughing that I realised he hadn't got that line out of his book.

The third person was reading when the bottom of the door scraped the floor. A man with an old laptop bag came in with a few drops of rain on his shoulder. The first thing he did was feel around behind the door, as if he expected a chair to be there. There was a gap in the front row, but that was where the missing chair had been. To get to an actual seat he'd have to go around half the room along the wall. There was one free beside me. My shoe was up against its leg. I drew my foot back, caught the paper between my knees and waved him over. He didn't see me, so I had to say something.

“There's a seat here.”

I'd meant to keep my voice down. There was a pause up front just as I said it, and the whole room heard me. He came over, sat down and put his bag upright between his ankles. He said thank you. The third reader went on. I looked down at the page. The fold through the second paragraph was starting to lift on the inside, a ridge of white across the writing. It still wasn't my turn, but I'd already used my voice in this room. What I was waiting for wasn't quite the first time anymore.

The woman called my name. She didn't ask me to introduce myself. There was a jug of water on the table and a stack of paper cups still in the wrapper. I put my page down and the second paragraph lifted again. I'd thought I could hold the corners flat, but that would've left me without a hand to help my eyes find their place. I lifted my thumb and uncovered a blue stroke. The paper was thin enough to see it from the other side, too. I didn't have to say which draft this was, or tell anybody what I might have read instead.

“I wrote this,” I said. I hadn't sounded nearly so nice when I told the man where to sit. This time every word came out so round and clear I almost wanted to turn around to see whose mouth it had come from. I did look up. The man had turned his bag narrow end out and made a little more room for his feet.

“The kickboard was yellow,” I began. That wasn't a sentence that needed explaining. It wasn't even the only thing in the pool that day that was yellow. At the part about pushing off from the wall, my mouth went drier than it had at home. My heel remembered the brush of the brick all the same. It had been so slight I'd looked down to check before I went any farther. When I reached the bit where I gave the board back, I took one breath and came out with “towards me” without saying what I'd turned towards me.

“What was that just now?” the man beside my chair asked. He said it quietly. Then he put a hand up in the small, apologetic way you do when you've bumped somebody in a queue. The woman with the poems turned her head. “Which part?” He raised his hand a little higher, unable to point out a place on a page that neither of them had.

I found the line and looked up. “I pushed the kickboard back with the split facing me.”

There wasn't anything that had to be hurried past. I even had time to put my tongue back where it normally went when I talked. Nobody supplied the next sentence for me. I gathered up the page when I'd finished. I was still frowning. It took me a couple of seconds to remember that I'd known how to read those last few lines after all.

There were two or three claps, not quite together. I got the fold wrong when I put my page away and put a short new crease across an old one. Somebody was waiting for me to clear the space at the front. I unwrapped the cups and poured myself water at last and carried it back to my seat, squeezing my shoulders between the chair backs. When I'd sat down, the man leant over and asked, “Was the way you read it the way you'd planned?”

I swallowed before I answered. “I dropped a word. You picked it up for me.”

He laughed a little and reached towards his bag. I thought he was going to pull out a notebook and ask me to write it down, and got ready to say no. All he did was free the strap of his umbrella from the zipper. The next reader happened to have a piece about buying an umbrella at a roadside stall where the bargaining got fierce. Once he'd sorted out the strap, he put his hand back on his leg. I listened to the end before I noticed the patch of floor I'd been leaving clear for him would let me straighten my right knee a little more.

When we finished, some people stayed to stack chairs and some went on talking outside the door. I turned my own chair around to help. The legs didn't line up. On the other side of the stack, the library woman tilted her hand to show me which way, and waited until I'd got it square before she pushed it on. The man in the rain-spotted coat paused on his way past me. “See you next time, if you come.”

“You too,” I said. He took the steps faster than I did. I stopped at the top to fasten my coat and discovered I'd already put the bottom button through the wrong hole.

The rain had let up, not long enough to leave the pavement all one colour. There was a white square, then a dark one, then another white one. I opened the page against my palm to see whether I could find the missing word out here. There wasn't light enough. I turned it and saw only where the paper had gone furry at one edge. At the corner I put it back in my pocket. At home I didn't switch the voice on. I put the page on the table and weighed one corner down. I left the other corner sticking up, took off my coat and did up the buttons again.
