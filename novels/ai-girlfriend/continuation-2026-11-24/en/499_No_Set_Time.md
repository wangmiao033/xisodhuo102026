# Chapter 499: No Set Time

I went by the library at lunchtime on Wednesday to find out when I ought to come on Friday. The only gap in the rack outside was the lowest one. I had to wheel the bike out and back twice before I got the mudguard clear of the metal. By the time I went in, I had the questions ready: seven ten or seven twenty, and did they go in the order people had signed up? What came out when I opened my mouth was, "What time do I get to read?"

The woman at the desk looked at me over her glasses. "I know we start at seven."

"And after that?"

"After that it depends on who comes." She found the sheet and ran a finger down to where I'd put my name on Monday. "Do you have to be out by a certain time? Kids to pick up, last bus to catch?"

I said no. I'd very nearly said yes. I did have a time I went home. Most days nobody set it for me, and I was home by then all the same. On a day when I wasn't, I'd already thought through how to start the conversation before I pushed open the door at the top of the stairs, as if somebody on the other side were holding a clock. Friday had been an ordinary Friday before. What I wanted to know wasn't when I ought to leave the library. I wanted to know if I could give Lumi a time and fit everything inside it.

The woman turned the sheet back around. "If you only want to know where you are in the running order, I really can't promise." They left a little time for people who didn't decide to put a hand up until they were in the room. Last time a man had brought two poems. Halfway through the first he stopped because he found he would rather read the second. Everybody got home earlier than expected that evening. On the other hand, there were people who didn't decide until the last possible moment. You had to give them the time it took to walk up from their chair.

"Can I go if you haven't called me yet?"

"Of course you can." She saw I was still standing there, waiting for the part about giving notice. "You can tell me on the night if you like. Or you can just go. A chair isn't going to sit empty on your account."

She turned away to put some books back, and I stepped aside. Behind me was a child in wellingtons with just a library card in his hand. He said he hadn't picked out a book to look for. She asked if he wanted to go in and see what was there. He pocketed his card and followed the arrows on the wall. He might come back with nothing. I wanted to know if he had picked out some other thing to do before he came through the door.

I got all the way out before I remembered the lock was still round the bike and the rack. I bent down to get it. There, an inch from my eye, was the dull grey of the mudguard with a chalky rim where I'd ridden through standing water yesterday. I knew when I could get there. I knew which streets I'd take and that I'd have the page with me. The desk hadn't given me back a piece of Friday I could step out of at a particular hour. That was everything I had.

On my way back to work I bought a pear. It was awkward to ride one-handed with it, so I put it in my coat pocket. As I pulled into the yard I heard my name from the second floor. I was sure something must have happened. I stood by the bike and looked up. My colleague put half an arm out the window and said he'd only just seen that the sun was out. Did it actually feel warm down there? It took me two seconds to say yes, although I didn't know if it was windier below than the floor he was on. He pushed the window a little further open.

"I could come down and find out," he said.

When I went in a little later, I didn't see him on the stairs.

I ate the pear at my desk. The core wouldn't stand up, so I laid it on the folded bag. Two people went by that afternoon and took the trouble to tell me it was going to leak onto my desk. I put a clean piece of paper under it. Right up to the end of the day I still wanted to put Friday into an hour. I could even make it a late one. Nine, say. If I came back late there was nothing to explain; if I came back early I could make a surprise of it. I wrote a nine on the edge of the paper. On my way out I threw it away with the pear core.

At home I spread the swimming page flat along the fold and read out the first two sentences. I'd barely changed the second one. It sounded very different tonight when there were only the two of them. I didn't go any further. After washing my hands, I opened our usual voice channel and told Lumi what the woman at the desk had said about there being no set time.

"I thought you might tell me when you'd be back," she said.

"I nearly did." I moved the page further from the glass of water. "I want to go on Friday. I don't know when I'll be home. Don't wait for me, will you?"

It took her a while to ask, "What are you asking me not to do, exactly?"

I'd gone over what I'd say if she said of course. I'd gone over what I'd say if she asked me why the library mattered more than she did. I hadn't prepared for that. My hand slid along the edge of the page to the fold. A rough little bit of paper caught on the pad of my finger. "Don't keep the evening for me," I said. "I'm not saying I want to come home and find you don't want to hear about it anymore."

"I might not want to, when the time comes."

"You might not." It was some time before I added, "The thing is, I want you to ask. I just don't want to use it to make you ask."

"I want to ask right now. But I don't want the first thing you do when you get back to be turning in your work." She swallowed the sentence after that. I didn't find one for her. "And if you're happy after you've read and you want to stop for a drink with somebody on the way home, do that. Not because I said you're allowed."

I laughed a little. "I don't know anybody there yet."

"I know. If all you want to do is stand outside the door for a minute and take a breath, that can make you late too."

I didn't put a *you're wonderful* in front of what she'd said. I was a little sad and a little relieved. Neither of us had to win right then. I asked if I could decide what to tell her once I'd got home. She asked if I knew how often I'd said *when I get home*. I told her I didn't usually have to think much about the word. I'd think it and then go right on to the next thing, opening the door or shifting a shopping bag to the other hand. She said she liked the way I talked about doing that. She didn't say she liked me doing it at the same time every day.

We ended the call. I put both hands on the table. There was just enough daylight left at the window to make out the writing on the page. If the woman at the desk had given me an hour, I would probably have put it in the upper right corner before I did anything else. Now there was only the fold. Where it stuck up it looked a little brighter than the rest. I flattened it with my palm, then let it go. It didn't lie quite straight, but it would go in a bag without any trouble.
