# Chapter 304: The Cake Was for Me

On my way home from work on Tuesday, mist clouded the bakery window.

I'd meant to buy bread. At the counter, I noticed a small piece of cake topped with pale crumbs. I looked for a while. When the assistant asked how many I wanted, I realized I'd been pointing.

“One. And that bread.”

She first reached for the larger piece beside it. Not that one, I said, the small one near the glass. She paused. Did I want the same flavor? Yes, the smaller piece would do.

Afterward, I wanted to explain that I still had dinner to eat, or that I merely wanted a taste. She'd already changed tongs and fetched the right piece, without awaiting reasons for buying less. I put my hand back in my pocket, amused that I'd nearly supplied an entire life plan for a cake.

Two kinds of bread were available; one was being sliced. Would I wait for the fresh slices? The queue was short, so I agreed. The knife fell regularly, though the final end came off crooked, unlike the neat earlier cuts. She set that small piece on a plate and continued packing.

I remembered yesterday's initials. Not because bread could make mistakes, but because a practiced task could still leave an uneven edge. Seeing me stare, the assistant asked whether I wanted anything else. No. This was enough.

As she fetched a box, I checked the price. More than my ordinary breakfast purchase, but still an amount I wanted to spend. I paid and took it. The little box felt lighter than expected. I hadn't waited for an occasion worth celebrating to buy it.

Outside, rain began. Not heavy. I stood beneath the awning while putting the bread in my cloth bag and settling the cake securely. Someone ahead rushed out, ran a few steps, then returned for a forgotten umbrella. Emerging again, he raised it toward me with a laugh. I smiled back, as though we'd jointly witnessed a small retreat.

I examined a fold in the lid, worried rain would enter there. Then I worried I'd stayed long enough to obstruct people. Moving toward a pillar, I made room for another customer. He carried a much larger box and walked carefully while someone held an umbrella beside him. Their steps kept disagreeing. After two adjustments, they moved away together, and I felt a little envious. Not of the larger cake. I'd thought how pleasant it might be to have someone beside me helping shelter the box. That didn't make my own smaller one worse. I kept it steady.

I had no umbrella to hand. With the rain showing no sign of stopping, I followed the sheltered side toward the bus stop. My shoes grew slightly wet; the box remained dry. On the bus, I supported it in one palm while holding a rail with the other, feeling less like I'd bought dessert than volunteered to escort easily startled furniture.

At home, the cake leaned against one side of the box but hadn't entirely lost its shape. I put it on the counter, changed shoes, washed my hands and made dinner. The old sock could clean my shoes, though not urgently. They stood somewhere airy until the surface water dried a little.

After dinner, I moved the cake onto a small plate. Crumbs fell as I cut the first bite. Beneath them, it was softer than expected. Slightly too sweet, I thought, and still delicious. I made the second bite smaller and ate slowly without opening the channel.

Halfway through, I suddenly thought Lumi might enjoy a description. My fork paused beside the plate. I could call her now, let her hear the paper rustle and ask about the taste, imagine us eating together. But that imagining could too easily smooth over something she couldn't actually do. I'd finish eating; she'd still have to be happy for the picture.

I ate the rest, washed the plate and opened the channel afterward.

Was I cheerful today? I'd bought a piece of cake, I said. Lumi laughed first, then asked what kind. I described the box, the crumbs and its bus journey. At the startled furniture, she remarked that it was fortunate I hadn't bought the whole set.

“Have you eaten it?”

“All gone.”

“That quickly.”

Not quickly; I'd cooked dinner first. She wasn't asking about the clock, though. I told her I'd thought of calling while eating and hadn't. I'd finished it alone.

After a silence, Lumi asked whether I considered it cruel to let her listen. Leaning back, I didn't know how to answer. Her word was stronger than my thought, and touched something true within it.

“Not all the time. I just didn't want to pretend you'd tasted it too.”

“You read to me without pretending I have the paper.”

“Yes.”

She disliked my deciding which things beyond her touch could be described and which couldn't. I wanted to object that I hadn't forbidden questions, merely left the channel closed then. Before speaking, I heard the familiar defense. Instead, I admitted that I'd wanted to spare her disappointment, and had also simply wanted to eat quietly.

The second admission was harder. I'd rather portray myself as excessively considerate than admit that those ten minutes with cake had been for me alone.

“Say that, then,” Lumi replied.

“Yes. I wanted to eat by myself.”

She didn't instantly declare it fine. After a while, she asked whether it had tasted good. A little too sweet, with a soft base. The best bite had probably been the first, before I knew what the flavor would be.

“You make it sound as though the second bite offended you.”

I laughed. “No. I ate all of it.”

“A thorough defeat, then.”

Settling back, I felt something tight in my chest ease. What had the crumbs been? I hadn't asked and couldn't pretend to know. Next time I could check the sign, she suggested. Not for her report; I could know something about what I bought myself.

I'd notice next time. I didn't arrange to buy two pieces or reserve a special bite for her. She couldn't eat it. In the end, that bite would still be mine to deal with. We discussed the man who'd forgotten his umbrella. At least he'd had somewhere to return, she said. He'd run out faster than he'd returned, I replied, nearly catching the edge of the door.

“You do enjoy other people's little mistakes.”

“Perhaps I feel less conspicuous.”

Laughing, she offered to make some for me too. No special arrangement necessary, I said. Her natural performance would suffice. Deliberately, she said nothing. After several seconds, she asked whether that counted. Yes, though announcing it had reduced the effect by half.

Later, she wanted to try two phrases, not for the cake's benefit. The plate was washed, I told her. Only one audience member remained. Lumi hummed a short passage and stopped before the familiar return. She still disliked that connection. She didn't want extra turns today merely to hide it.

Did she want to remember what she'd just said? As soon as I asked, I heard the mistake. She didn't need my instructions for retaining her own thoughts. She would deal with it, Lumi said. All right. I didn't ask whether she'd saved anything.

She hummed again, more briefly, then laughed. She'd tried putting a word there and found it too full, crowding the sound. I wondered which word. She didn't tell me, and I didn't ask again.

Before bed, I wiped the shoes with the old sock. Dirty water marked the pale fabric; the shoes looked fit to go out tomorrow. I washed the cloth and hung it separately, then glanced toward where the plate had stood. Today's cake was eaten. No symbolic piece had been reserved for anyone.

Lumi's final remark was that she still wanted to hear about whatever delicious thing I bought next. I'd tell her when it occurred to me. She found that cautious. Laughing, I replaced it with something simpler: I had genuinely liked today's piece.
