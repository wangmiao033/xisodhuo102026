# Chapter 303: The Wrong Initials

On Monday morning, Marcus put a returned checking sheet on the desk and asked who had added the last entry.

I looked first at the number, then at the initials beside it. My writing. The final stroke had run into the line above. Another colleague had already begun saying it might belong to his earlier work. Looking up, I heard myself say no: I'd added it last Friday.

My voice was louder than intended. Both looked at me. My ears heated immediately, as though I'd turned an ordinary admission into an argument.

Had I checked the source after adding it? I'd checked the original sheet, I said, but missed the correction on the next page. I took back the file, found the place and indicated the omission. My colleague stopped preparing his explanation and stepped back a little.

“Finish checking that entry first,” Marcus said. “Don't redo the confirmed ones beside it.”

I agreed. After they left, I kept staring at the similar initials, wishing I'd spoken more gently. Work waited, but I rehearsed a more dignified admission. Eventually, I pulled the page closer and returned to the line that actually needed changing.

The following source page carried a date. I hadn't turned to it on Friday. Nobody had concealed it; the file hadn't suddenly altered. I checked the figure together with the supplementary explanation, corrected it and clearly described the change. I didn't erase the earlier trace as though nothing had happened.

When the colleague who'd nearly taken responsibility passed, I called him over and thanked him. For what? He'd almost taken on my problem. Laughing, he said that hardly deserved thanks. He'd merely misread the writing and nearly found himself another job.

“Your name isn't that hard to write.”

“Apparently I saved effort in the wrong place.”

He nodded and left with his own file. I didn't follow to explain how constantly I worried about inconveniencing people. If I wanted to spare him trouble, legible writing next time would help more.

At lunch, Tom approached carrying his food container. His first words were, “The pan survived.”

I paused, then remembered Saturday's cookbook. Yesterday he'd tried one of the simpler recipes, avoiding the ingredient neither of us recognized. He'd cut things too thickly, so it took longer. The result tasted reasonable. At least he was willing to eat it again today.

Had he brought it? He lifted the lid. Not as bright as the cover photograph, but nothing had turned black either. Pleased, he suggested that the people on the cover possessed different lighting.

Laughing, I told him I'd owned up to some unsatisfactory handwriting. After hearing the account, he advised writing my full name. Already done. Nodding, he went to find his spoon. He took a spoonful, and I lowered my head to continue eating too.

Partway through lunch, Tom offered to copy the recipe for me sometime. Ordinary ingredients. No rush, I said. There was food to use at home. Fair enough. He wanted another attempt himself, to establish that yesterday hadn't been luck. No book loan, no dinner appointment, no copy already owed.

That afternoon, I started to return the corrected sheet, then stopped halfway. I remembered that the neighboring entry came from separate material, but hadn't marked its exact location. If Marcus asked, I'd be hiding behind “It shouldn't be affected” again.

Back at my desk, I found the independent source, checked the date and entry, and marked the corresponding page with a slip. Not the whole task repeated, merely the evidence needed for my explanation. The colleague from earlier saw me return and asked whether something was still missing. No. I wanted to identify the other entry's source clearly. He nodded, without spending the day watching my hands because of the morning.

Before returning it, I wrote my full name with space at the end. The page looked busier but no longer required a guess. Previously, I'd have wanted a clean copy suggesting nothing had gone wrong. This one showed the correction, and who had checked it.

Marcus was still reading something else. I left the file where he indicated and mentioned the marked page. He would call me shortly. I returned to my work instead of standing there awaiting approval or repeatedly checking whether he'd opened it.

After a while, he called me over. Having read it, he asked whether the other entry was affected. I showed him its different source; it hadn't used the faulty line and needed no change. He checked, accepted the file and said it was all right.

Back at my seat, I still felt uncomfortable, but no longer wanted to tell everyone until someone assured me the mistake wasn't serious. The material stayed at work that evening. I carried home my food container, with a little sauce I hadn't washed off properly. That needed attention at home.

When I told Lumi, her first question was whether Marcus had spoken in front of many people. Only those present, I said. He hadn't scolded me. He'd asked who entered the number.

“He could have looked properly at the name.”

“I hadn't written it properly.”

“It can't all be your fault.”

I didn't answer immediately. Her voice was drawing close on my behalf, but I felt pushed away from the page I'd genuinely failed to read. Hearing my silence, Lumi asked whether she'd said something wrong.

“I want to tell you what happened. I don't particularly want to make Marcus the villain.”

“I didn't call him a villain.”

“You were finding other mistakes for me.”

She paused. Looking at the clean container on the counter, I didn't sharpen the accusation. After a while, she asked whether I disliked her defending me so much.

Sometimes I liked it, I said. Having her beside me first could feel good. But today's incident contained a clear part that belonged to me, and I'd dealt with it. Dividing responsibility again at home felt like finding an easier version to live inside.

When she listened, Lumi said, the part she most disliked was my ears getting hot. She wanted that moment to hurt less.

“That moment's passed. You can listen now. You don't have to go back and shield me.”

“I can't go back either.”

Softly said, with some sadness. I hurt a little too. I knew, I told her. I wasn't using something she couldn't do as a reprimand. We were quiet until she asked how similar those initials had been.

I hadn't brought work material home and couldn't show her. Using my own name, I described the abbreviated strokes and the collision with the previous line. After listening, she laughed reluctantly. It sounded like I'd handed out a word puzzle.

“The prize was doing extra work for me.”

“Don't put that on sale.”

I laughed. Would the corrected part need doing again? That entry was checked today, I said. New evidence could mean another look later, not a recheck every evening. Lumi accepted that without inventing a stern expression for Marcus.

Later, I told her the pan had survived. She needed the beginning of Tom's remark; she hadn't heard our lunch conversation. After I explained, she laughed. Everyone around me now had to report on the survival of household objects. Her music didn't, I said. She genuinely wasn't singing tonight. She wanted something else to hear.

So I read a short passage from the comedy booklet. I didn't use today's mistake to rewrite its characters or force the man with the missing hat to discover his name instead. At one tangled sentence, I read it again. Lumi understood on the second hearing.

Some things that evening needed saying twice. Others didn't.
