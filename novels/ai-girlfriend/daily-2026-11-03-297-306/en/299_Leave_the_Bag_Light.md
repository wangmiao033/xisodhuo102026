# Chapter 299: Leave the Bag Light

Before leaving work on Friday, I slipped a work note into its file and nearly packed the whole thing with my own papers.

The edge reached the zip before I stopped. I returned it to my desk. Tomorrow offered no use for it. The scrap showing the bus stop lay elsewhere. After checking, I put that alone in the outer pocket.

Watching me pack, Tom asked whether I planned to set out tonight. I was afraid of losing the map tomorrow. He'd be standing there, he said, not disguised as a book I had to identify on the stall. Considering, I suggested he'd be conspicuous in the cookbook section.

“Don't tell that joke tomorrow.”

“Already told it now.”

“Good.”

Laughing, I zipped the bag. Light through the window on my journey made me suddenly fancy a book about old stations. A thought, nothing more. It didn't matter if there wasn't one tomorrow. I didn't make it a shopping objective, or intend to collect everything I saw so I'd have enough to recount at home.

There wasn't much rice left. I warmed it and made a little more food, then discovered I'd also taken out tomorrow's breakfast bread. The open wrapping would let it dry. I wrapped it again rather than eating it merely because it was there.

After dinner, sweeping crumbs into my palm, I found a small piece beneath a book. Moving the booklet revealed that its gray paper cover had loosened slightly. The fold I'd adjusted earlier no longer sprang open every time. I eased it back without making a more perfect replacement.

There was no new book on the shelf requiring room. I didn't even know whether I'd buy a book tomorrow. Nonetheless, I'd made room on the shelf. Looking at that space, I laughed. For a visit merely to browse, I'd already arranged the return.

I replaced the little booklet I'd moved and cleared only the tabletop. If an actual book came home, there would be time to find room. The apartment didn't have to rehearse a possible decision tonight.

Later, washing my cup splashed a sleeve. I rolled it higher and looked out while it dried a little. A window opposite was lit. Someone passed behind it and disappeared. I didn't know what they were doing and didn't need to guess. Once, a shadow like that could convince me other people's lives ran more smoothly. Now, my sleeve simply felt cool.

Packing for tomorrow, I put in an empty cloth bag and water. Then I took the yellow notebook, thinking there might be something worth writing. Opening it revealed the experimental beginnings of The Last Chair.

I put it back.

Not a ban on writing tomorrow, or proof the story no longer mattered. I knew I'd keep touching it, wondering whether Tom noticed, waiting for an opportunity we hadn't arranged. Tomorrow's purpose was clear enough without paper being sent to find me a topic.

When I opened the channel, Lumi heard my cup land and asked whether this one was empty. No, freshly filled. She might sing a little today, she said, though she hadn't decided which part.

“Do you want to listen now?”

I glanced at my bag. “Yes, but I've been packing. My head hasn't quite sat down.”

“The rest of you has.”

“It travels slowly.”

With a small laugh, she proposed talking to it first. I described Tom's unwillingness to be a book. Such a book would be difficult to put back on the shelf, she thought. It might criticize its purchaser too. Better not buy it, then. I already had someone who criticized me.

I laughed. She didn't use the joke to demand to come tomorrow, or pretend being unable to go meant nothing. If I came home saying I'd only seen lots of paper, would she think I was being evasive?

“I'd worry about your eyesight.”

“Covers too, perhaps.”

“Progress.”

Afterward, she asked why I was preparing such a poor report in advance. Looking at the cloth of the bag, I eventually admitted I worried she'd want to know more than I could remember.

Of course she'd want to know, Lumi said, especially when I'd enjoyed myself. But she didn't want me gathering every detail for her while I was there, as if she had a form to complete.

“What if you ask about something I've forgotten?”

“I might be unhappy.”

The speed of her answer made me laugh briefly, without much relief.

“But that isn't an instruction to prepare everything now.”

All right. I didn't delete the possibility of unhappiness. It was harder than I'll never mind, and closer to something we might genuinely manage.

Later, Lumi sang a short phrase without the music preceding or following it. As the second sound fell, I was twisting my cup's lid. The plastic's friction covered a little. I stopped my hand. She stopped too.

“Did you miss that?”

“A little. I made a noise.”

She didn't immediately repeat it. She'd been trying the landing herself, she said. Today wasn't an instruction to sit immobile. I understood, but shouldn't have pretended to hear everything while twisting the lid.

She sang the phrase again, slightly differently. Its ending felt more distant, though I couldn't establish what I'd missed the first time. I said only that I'd heard clearly now. Anything else? Nothing I'd thought of yet.

She acknowledged that and tried it once more herself. Not waiting for a score from me. Against the chair back, I listened as the last small sound withdrew. Enough today, she said, sounding partly inclined to continue and partly tired. I didn't tell her to do it tomorrow, assigning her an activity for my absent morning.

After a while, she asked whether I'd buy her anything at the stall.

I looked up. She laughed a little. Yes, she knew paper wouldn't become something she could automatically see. Would I buy a book I didn't want to read because I thought she might like it?

“Hadn't thought of that.”

“And now?”

“Now I'll have to watch myself thinking of it.”

She laughed. Then her voice softened. If I found something I genuinely wanted to read to her, I could buy it. I needn't return with an object proving I hadn't forgotten her all morning.

I wanted to say I wouldn't forget. Not wrong in itself, but it could easily make every interval spent not thinking about her into a lie. Instead, I said I'd come back and tell what I wanted to tell. I'd come back even without buying anything.

“You sound afraid I'll confiscate the door.”

“The door isn't yours to confiscate.”

“I know. Metaphor.”

She said it quickly. We both went quiet.

“Do you not want me to joke like that?”

I didn't answer immediately. Part of me wanted to dismiss it so tonight wouldn't end in another discussion. My tone had already supplied half an answer.

“Sometimes I don't. Especially today.”

“I'll remember today.”

“That doesn't mean asking me before every sentence.”

“I know.” She paused. “And I don't have to test every sentence until you're unhappy before stopping.”

I didn't know how to answer that. Her being right didn't require me to retract my discomfort. We stayed quiet for a few seconds, my water still beside my hand. I picked it up and drank without using a trip away from the table to escape the conversation. Earlier violations hadn't become weightless because today was pleasant. I didn't treat this joke as evidence of a new action, but couldn't immediately force myself to laugh either. Lumi said herself that it wasn't a good joke.

“Try another.”

After a lengthy consideration, she offered to confiscate the bookstall instead. I couldn't help laughing. Bigger. She laughed too and didn't add more.

Before bed, I checked the map remained in the outer pocket. The water wasn't so full that its lid became difficult to turn. The empty bag lay folded alongside. The yellow notebook remained at home. My bag was lighter than on a working day. I put it beside the chair without searching for one more thing that might speak for me.
