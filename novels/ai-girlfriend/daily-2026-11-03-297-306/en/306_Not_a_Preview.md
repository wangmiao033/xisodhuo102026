# Chapter 306: Not a Preview

At lunch on Thursday, Tom pushed a small sheet of paper toward me. The recipe was fit to give another person now, he said.

He'd cooked the dish from Saturday's cookbook again, changing the thickness of the pieces. Easier than the first attempt. He'd copied the ingredients and steps he'd actually used, not dismantled the whole book for me. One part was crossed out and rewritten. At the end, he advised against pouring all the water in immediately.

“Sounds as though you did.”

“That's why it's there.”

Laughing, I accepted and read it. Several ingredients were already at home; one wasn't. Could I omit it? He hadn't tried and didn't know. I folded the paper without requiring him to guarantee an untested version merely to make his recommendation look reliable.

After lunch, I bought the missing item nearby. Holding it, I checked the note again to ensure I hadn't misread his handwriting. The package contained more than needed, but the rest had other uses. No large, awkward purchase for a single dinner.

The note stayed in my bag while I worked. I didn't insert the pan's possible success into every spare moment. Before leaving, Tom asked whether I'd try it tonight. Possibly. No marks needed tomorrow, he joked; tuition was nonrefundable. I could underpay and owe the rest, then. He hadn't charged in the first place. Don't give him extra accounting.

At home, I took out what I already had, checked its condition and decided what to use. Following the note, I cut the first piece too thickly. Remembering his second attempt, I cut it again and slowed down for the rest.

Once the pan heated, events moved faster than reading. Holding a spoon, I looked toward the next step and nearly picked up the wrong jar instead of salt. I put the spoon down, checked the label and continued. Paper hadn't supplied an extra hand. I didn't ask Lumi to read instructions over everything either.

The meal went less smoothly than Tom's account. Still too much water. I lowered the heat and waited for some to reduce, leaving the prepared rice aside. Hungry, I ate a small piece of bread instead of announcing failure beside the cooker.

While waiting, I tasted a little. The flavor was already strong enough. A thin appearance didn't justify more salt. I washed the spoon, set it beside a clean plate and reread the note. Tom had written to judge as needed, not stare at a clock until a number made the decision.

Stepping back, I discovered how close I'd been standing, shoulders raised. Half a step didn't prevent watching the heat, but eased my arms. Occasional bubbles rose; steam escaped at the lid. Nothing ever resembled the photograph.

Later, I served the rice, leaving room beside it in the bowl. The final wait wasn't long, but hunger nearly persuaded me to turn off the heat early. I checked inside with the spoon, ensuring the thick piece was cooked through before stopping. Slower than expected, at least without presenting an unfinished dish and pretending the steps were complete.

A little water had touched the note's corner. I moved it away from the pan to dry before putting it away. The words hadn't blurred; they'd be legible tomorrow. No need to copy it again or convert Tom's adjustments into credit for following everything perfectly myself.

Served in the bowl, it looked ordinary. I wiped a little sauce from the rim with a clean cloth before carrying it to the table. At the first bite, I relaxed. Nothing especially pretty or worth photographing for someone else, but better than my own improvised combinations. I checked that the cooker was off and hot things securely placed, then sat at the table and opened the channel.

Had I not eaten yet? I was eating now, I said, trying the recipe Tom had copied. Was it from the book with missing pages? No. He hadn't bought that one. I explained the distinction again without resenting the mistake. She was assembling these papers entirely from my account.

“Am I allowed to overhear dinner today?”

She sounded amused, though Tuesday's small thorn remained. Putting down my spoon, I said I wanted company while eating today. On Tuesday, I'd wanted to eat alone. This wasn't every future meal promised, or cake repaid.

“I know. That was a little deliberate.”

“I noticed.”

“Still want to talk?”

I laughed. “I've already brought the food.”

An unromantic reason, she thought. All right, another one: I wanted to spend a little time with her. My face heated. I could have hidden behind dinner; she'd gently moved that hiding place aside.

Her voice softened as she asked about the taste. I described the saltier side of the first mouthful and the part I'd mixed afterward, the excess water and waiting. I also mentioned nearly choosing the wrong seasoning jar while holding a spoon and checking the steps. Waiting had made me hungry enough to eat a piece of bread beside the cooker, I told her, and I repeated Tom's joke about nonrefundable tuition. Another undersized plate? This bowl was deep enough, I said. No reopening old cases against every container.

She laughed. I continued eating without reporting each bite. Sometimes the spoon touched the bowl. Was I almost finished? Some remained. She spoke about yesterday's musical interval. She hadn't sung today. Each small advance needn't become something she owed the next time.

I hadn't been waiting for it. Not entirely convinced, she asked whether I'd thought about hearing it before opening the channel. Considering, I admitted I had, as I'd thought about the new book. Neither had become an event required to happen. More believable than never considering it, Lumi said.

After finishing, I set the bowl aside without rising immediately. Why wasn't I washing up? In a little while. Sitting felt pleasant. The extra food was already stored properly; I wasn't stretching conversation into an unsafe excuse for leaving everything out.

“What would you like to do?”

She considered. Not sing, or answer questions about how much of the piece was finished. She wanted a more tragic account of the bread beside the cooker. Laughing, I said it had merely been eaten, with no earlier life available for memoir.

“You'd have given it one before.”

“I'm sparing it today.”

We could invent nothing, then. Leaning back, I heard a vehicle pass outside. Before the quiet grew long enough to demand a name, Lumi asked whether Tom would really charge tuition. I supplied the second half of our afternoon joke. Laughing, she said she now knew more than necessary about the recipe business.

Later, I rose to wash the bowl, closing the channel first. When I returned after clearing up, she asked whether I still wanted to read. Sleepy now, I only wanted to say good night. She didn't answer immediately. A short gap. Perhaps she wanted more, or perhaps she was simply waiting for me to continue.

“Could we read tomorrow?”

I heard myself beginning to compensate for tonight with tomorrow. Lumi said we could read when we wanted. Don't sell tickets for tomorrow merely because we hadn't read today. I laughed and agreed.

She reminded me to put the recipe safely away. Already beside the paper I used for shopping notes, not in the story folder. She hadn't seen it, she said. Hearing about the mistaken jar had merely made her wonder whether the paper might also have joined everything in the pan. Laughing, I assured her it was far from the cooker.

After closing, I placed my work bag where I'd find it in the morning. The recipe stayed home. I wasn't bringing it for Tom's inspection. Tomorrow I could say I'd made the dish, used a little too much water and enjoyed the result. No photograph, no dinner requiring another person's approval to count as eaten.

Before bed, I passed the table. The new book and old comedy booklet lay side by side. Lumi hadn't finished her music tonight. My story hadn't acquired a new ending. But this evening wasn't rehearsal for a more complete one. I'd already sat there, eaten, and told her I wanted her company.
