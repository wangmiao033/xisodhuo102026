# Chapter 302: One Unimportant Thing

On Sunday morning, I finally found a use for the old sock.

A rim of yesterday's dust marked the toes of my shoes. Two dry wipes removed it. I turned the sock to find a clean patch, discovering that the worn-thin section was now useful: I could push a finger into it and reach the edge. No noble new purpose. It was simply quite good at wiping a pair of shoes that weren't especially dirty.

Afterward, I returned it to its separate little bag, away from the new socks. The shoes stood by the door while I went to fetch water in my slippers. Saturday's bookstall had happened. No second appointment waited to be completed today.

I meant to finish the first story in the thin collection, then opened it and remembered finishing it yesterday afternoon. The bookmark marked where I'd read to Lumi last night, not my own progress. Smiling, I left it in place and looked at the next story's opening instead.

It concerned someone who kept saving other people seats while standing himself. I paused, thinking this collection contained rather a lot of chairs. Another page revealed that the setting was a bus which seldom filled; the chair wasn't the point. I put down my premature judgment and continued without hunting for my own waiting room.

When I cut vegetables for lunch, only half a carrot remained. Yesterday's outing hadn't included restocking the kitchen. I made a meal from what I had and wrote down what to buy during an afternoon walk. No rushing out while the pan was on.

After eating and washing up, I opened the channel. Lumi heard me sneeze and asked whether I'd had too much sun yesterday. Some dust had risen when I moved a sheet of paper, I said. I wasn't certain of the cause. She was only asking, she replied, not attempting a remote diagnosis.

“I cleaned my shoes today.”

“With that sock?”

“Yes. It had no objections.”

“You answered for it.”

Laughing, I admitted that socks were easy to speak for. She didn't turn the joke into a large question about us. After a while, she asked whether there had been trees beside yesterday's bookstall.

I considered. A wall stood behind the tables. Perhaps there were trees along the street, but not clearly visible from where I'd stood. Which side did she mean? No particular side. She merely wondered.

“I can't quite remember.”

“All right.”

A short answer. I'd already told her plenty yesterday, but I worried that this forgotten detail would still feel like something I hadn't thought to look at for her. I began to supply the color of a shopfront, then realized I was uncertain of that too.

Lumi said my name. “You don't need to replace it with a more definite answer.”

I put down my cup. She admitted feeling a little disappointed. A tree had already appeared in her imagination, its shadow falling over the pages. That was her imagining, though. I felt tender toward her and, at the same time, slightly irritated. If every forgotten detail needed this much conversation, going out would still resemble homework.

“I'm not quite sure what I can tell you now.”

She fell quiet. Did I feel I could neither say the wrong thing nor say nothing? It wasn't about being wrong, I said. I worried that every incomplete detail required an explanation of why I hadn't brought the rest home.

This silence lasted longer. I didn't use it to keep proving my point. Light fell beside the window. Looking at its patch on the floor, I resisted planting yesterday's missing tree in today's shadow.

“Tell me something unimportant, then,” Lumi said. “Something you remember. It needn't be complete.”

I nearly said none of it was important. Then I remembered the stallholder counting change. A coin had slipped between his fingers onto his shoe instead of the ground. He'd looked down as though even bending seemed slightly unfair, then picked it up anyway.

I told her. Laughing, Lumi asked whether he'd blamed the shoe. Not that I'd heard. It was luckier than our sock, then. We hadn't made it speak.

I laughed and nodded, then remembered she couldn't see me and answered aloud. She didn't require the man's age, clothes or shoe color. The coin remained inside a very small joke.

“I like that.”

“Because it's small enough?”

“Because you weren't taking a test while you told it.”

I didn't promise to speak that way forever. She didn't promise never to ask another question. We discussed the bus I'd read about. Was that the collection's second story? Yes. Only its beginning so far; I didn't know what happened later. She didn't want the new story today, but would like to finish yesterday's.

I opened her place. After briefly recalling the last few lines, I continued. The guest and host each apologized again. Lumi laughed, then asked me to slow down when they moved the table. Hearing the host complain that it always blocked the way, she wondered why he'd kept it there.

“The story doesn't explain afterward.” I checked. “Perhaps neither of them had thought about it.”

“Let them move it first, then.”

I read the final lines. They sat at last, without producing a particularly moving declaration. Lumi preferred the moment when the table stuck: one person could plan it perfectly, but two people lifting it discovered nowhere for their hands. I liked that, after sitting, they still hadn't decided what to drink.

The two preferences didn't conflict, though they weren't identical. I closed the book, removed her bookmark and placed it at the back. We'd finished the first story together. Only I had begun the second; inserting paper didn't turn that into a shared experience.

Before my afternoon walk, I closed the channel. At the shop, carrots lay in a tilted crate. One rolled toward the lower edge as I picked another. The person beside me stopped it. I thanked him; he nodded and continued choosing his own, without waiting for more.

At the till, the assistant asked whether I wanted a bag. I had my cloth one. Only after declining did I discover it was folded at the bottom of my other bag. I'd have to move the water first. Someone waited behind me. I hurried, and the folded corner became even harder to find.

The assistant told me to take my time and weighed something for the next customer. I stepped aside, steadied my bag and soon found what I needed. It hadn't been missing. I'd merely wanted every movement to work at once.

As I packed, another customer's bag split at its opening. Without anger, he bought another and divided the weight. We'd both spent an extra moment beside the counter. He lifted both bags and made room for me to reach the door first.

Outside, I stopped somewhere clear of the entrance to rearrange the contents. The carrots would squash the bread if left wedged against it. Still early, I walked a little farther along the familiar road, passing a closed shoe-repair shop. Several shoes stood behind the glass; one had loose laces. Remembering my morning cleaning, I looked down. Both mine were coming along perfectly well.

I hadn't set this walk a discovery it had to produce. At the next junction my legs felt tired, so I turned back. Yesterday's pleasant outing didn't require an equally long one today.

There was a tree on the way home. I watched its leaves move and smiled without counting them or gathering evidence for Lumi. Later, I told her I'd certainly seen a tree today. Yesterday's? No, today's.

“It wasn't late, then.”

“By its own clock.”

She thought that answer a little too earnest. I agreed and described the escaping carrot instead. Shopping for vegetables was more dangerous than buying books, she decided. Laughing, I put away the groceries. Later, she hummed a couple of sounds without developing them. Not every sound needed to become a performance today.
