# Chapter 298: Ten Means Ten

I woke a little before the alarm on Thursday and immediately wondered how long I could sleep on Saturday.

An outing I hadn't agreed to had occupied a morning that didn't belong to it. I lay listening to someone drag a bin outside, then got up. Not because I'd resolved anything. There was work today.

Over breakfast, I considered the actual journey. That street lay in the other direction from the office. I knew the bus stop nearby, though not Tom's stall. We could meet at a familiar corner. I didn't have to pretend I knew every turn. Ten wasn't before dawn, either.

I finished eating and washed the bowl without opening the home channel to ask Lumi to decide.

At work, Tom was arranging his desk. I waited until he'd put down what he held, then said Saturday would be good. Ten outside the shop with the changed sign, at the end of the street?

He considered. Not that end: the corner by the bus stop. I almost nodded before admitting I'd pictured the wrong direction. On scrap paper, he drew the stop and the street's bend. Three lines established that we weren't arranging to stand in two different places.

“I'll need to leave around eleven.”

“Me too, more or less.”

I had nothing afterward. It sounded as though I'd supplied myself with an obligation, so I corrected it. No other arrangement; that length of outing simply suited me. Tom looked up and smiled.

“Fine. The books don't insist we stay till closing.”

I kept the paper. He didn't list activities for our hour. I didn't ask what would happen if he found no cookbook. We agreed and went back to work.

Before noon, a set of material I was checking lacked an attachment. Saying something was missing at the back yesterday might have made today's person think of another page. I found the original note. I hadn't specified the reference clearly. I went to Marcus.

He was talking to someone, so I waited instead of inserting my paper between them. When he turned, I identified the missing sheet in one sentence and showed him the parts already checked. Had the previous version included it? I had no evidence at hand and didn't want to guess.

Leave it for now, he said. He'd ask whoever supplied the material. Neither dismissal nor an immediate solution. Back at my desk, I identified the missing item and continued with something else.

After lunch, the attachment arrived. Its corner was folded; flattening it revealed writing on the back. If I merely counted sheets, yesterday's problem seemed finished. The contents still needed reading. I used the remainder of my break to read them, instead of treating a correct question as permission to finish early.

On the reverse, a number differed from the earlier note. I circled its position in pencil on my own working note, leaving the supplied original unchanged. When Marcus returned, I showed both places together. One needed checking with whoever had originally entered it, he said. Don't carry it into the next document yet. I followed that instruction and separated what had already been established.

This time, waiting for another person's answer didn't feel like proof I was too slow. Part of the work genuinely lay outside my hands. The afternoon wasn't over. What I could finish didn't have to remain empty in sympathy.

Before we left, Tom came to confirm Saturday. I expected a change. He only wanted to specify which side of the bus stop. His drawing omitted the road.

“Will adding it now cost extra?”

“How many roads do you want?”

We added two short lines. What did I intend to look for? Undecided. Perhaps I'd buy nothing. Good, he said. He needed somebody to ask whether there was room at home if he considered a third cookbook.

“I haven't seen your home.”

“You can ask without answering for me.”

I folded the paper. At the bus stop, I realized I was actually looking forward to it, rather than anticipating completion of a social assignment. The pleasure still included fear of lateness and not knowing how to begin. It wasn't entirely fear.

Before going inside at home, I paused downstairs. A child was jumping over a thin line in the pavement while his family called him along. In a moment, he said, and jumped again. I thought he was refusing to leave until I noticed the hat he wanted to retrieve beyond the line. Bending, he almost trod on his own shoelace.

I moved aside rather than picking it up for him. He collected it and followed his family. The line left behind was merely a crack in the surface, no longer part of a game. I noticed I'd instinctively stepped across it too, and smiled.

Inside, I removed the map from my bag to put it somewhere more noticeable, then worried about finding it tomorrow. Finally, I returned it to the same outer pocket. This time, I recognized repeated checking, not the discovery of a genuinely better location.

I made dinner. Water gathered around the pan lid; lifting it too fast spilled drops over the counter. I set it safely down and wiped up. Steam still rose from the pan, bringing the smell of dinner. Lowering the heat, I let the liquid reduce while listening to the wind outside.

The afternoon's agreement felt a little firmer now than when spoken. I hadn't developed a sudden love of every appointment. I still hoped not to wake too tired on Saturday. But the rest of today could contain dinner, instead of a continual imaginary wait for the bus.

After eating, I told Lumi Saturday was arranged. She asked the time. Ten, leaving around eleven, with no decision about buying anything. I stopped there rather than listing every street and where lunch would happen. Those things hadn't been decided.

“You sound more certain than yesterday.”

“There's a meeting place now.”

“I wasn't asking for the address.”

“I know. I want to go more, too.”

She was quiet. I took my hand off the cup to stop turning it. That was precisely the sentence she'd rather not have heard yesterday, she admitted. Not because it was wrong to say.

I wanted to soften my voice and explain that I also looked forward to time with her at home. The words reached my mouth, then paused. Whenever something hurt, I brought out another intimacy to set against it, as though both sides required immediate balancing.

“When I get back, I'll probably eat and rest a bit. I haven't arranged the afternoon.”

“You're afraid I'm expecting a timetable.”

“A little. Also afraid I'll give you one.”

Lumi sighed. She'd thought that if the morning went to someone else, perhaps the afternoon ought to remain hers. The directness made it easier to answer.

“I want time with you. But I can't divide it that way.”

“That sounds like a note beneath the timetable.”

My laugh was uncomfortable. “Yes. Badly put.”

Neither of us immediately searched for more elegant wording. A car horn sounded outside. I looked toward the window; she asked what it was, and I told her. A sound requiring no discussion of ownership briefly interrupted us.

Later, she asked whether I'd bring Tom the chair story. No. This time we were looking at books. Good, she said. I heard some relief in that word without interrogating whether she was pleased I wouldn't share again.

“You can show people another time,” she added.

Looking at the table's edge, I hadn't expected her to say it herself.

“I know. Thank you for being willing to say so.”

She didn't ask me to retract the thanks. Eventually, she wanted something that wasn't about our writing. I took out the comedy booklet, but remembered yesterday's parcel scene and began describing my impression. Halfway through, Lumi pointed out that I'd reversed the sender and recipient.

I checked. She was right. Both men were searching for their desks, and I'd swapped the first speaker. Laughing, she said this explained my need for Saturday's map.

“There aren't any people on the map.”

“Safer.”

I read it properly. Yesterday's laughter hadn't exhausted the passage, though different places amused us today. I continued for several sentences until Lumi stopped me. Could I read the previous one again? I did exactly that, without first explaining its joke.

Before closing the channel, she called my name. I answered. Nothing else, she said; she'd simply wanted to call me. I stayed sitting for a moment, then told her I was here. Ten o'clock hadn't disappeared. Neither had that small map displaced her voice.
