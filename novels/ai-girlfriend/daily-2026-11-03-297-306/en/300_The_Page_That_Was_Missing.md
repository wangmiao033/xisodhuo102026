# Chapter 300: The Page That Was Missing

I reached the corner a few minutes before ten on Saturday.

The bus stop was indeed on the side Tom had drawn. His map omitted an advertising board, so I changed position in front of and behind it once before seeing him approach. He carried no book and wasn't impersonating one. We said good morning and walked down the street.

The secondhand stall was narrower than I'd imagined: two folding tables, one behind the other, with boxes against a wall. Someone leafed through a large illustrated book while the seller warned him about its binding. He held it flatter. Its pages yellowed in the morning light.

Tom found the cookbooks immediately. I began following, stopped half a pace later, and went to the other table. We didn't need to look at the same row of words to prove we'd arrived together.

A small book with old buildings drawn on its cover first caught my eye. Inside were mostly explanatory paragraphs, only a few pictures. It smelled faintly of damp although the paper was dry. The price wasn't high, but not so small I could disregard it. I replaced it and tried a collection of short stories nearby. I asked its price too. The seller named the amount and pointed to a small label on the back. I nodded, then opened the contents.

Several ordinary titles appeared in its contents, including The Late Guest. I turned to the first story and read half a page. Before deciding, I was interrupted by a man asking whether I worked at the stall.

No. Holding a large-print book, he couldn't find its price. I pointed out the seller, then saw him taking another customer's money and helped look for a sticker on the back.

There was none. I didn't invent a price. Once the seller finished, I passed on the question. Thanking me, the man resumed browsing with his glasses low on his nose. He wanted bigger print; small type tired him in the evening.

“This one looks good,” I offered.

Only after speaking did I realize I hadn't read any of it. Turning a page, he said he didn't particularly like that sort of story. I immediately wanted to find another. My hand had moved before he set the book down and said he'd have a look around. He hadn't asked me to assemble his reading.

I withdrew my hand and picked up the collection. The same half-page remained, but I had to find my sentence again. Tom called from the next table. He held a cookbook whose cover showed soup more enthusiastic in color than anything I'd made.

“It uses more pans than I've got altogether.”

“You could buy pans.”

He looked at me. Laughing, I admitted this wasn't the assistance he'd invited me to provide.

We examined several pages. Some dishes required ingredients he rarely bought. Others were simple enough. He put it beside him and tried another. He wasn't looking to cook one spectacular dinner, he explained, but to avoid thinking of the same dish after work every night.

Perhaps we should start with contents instead of covers. Turning back, he read several entries and nodded. We discussed something apparently requiring an ordinary pan. Halfway through, we discovered a jump in the pages.

A narrow paper stub remained in the binding.

Tom turned backward and forward. No two sheets stuck together. The missing leaf had taken the second half of our recipe. He didn't put it down at once, as if dinner had already begun and he no longer knew how to finish it.

Seeing the stub, the seller offered a further reduction. I wanted to point out that the other pages remained. Before speaking, I looked at Tom. He was shaking his head.

“I wanted to do less guessing.”

The seller nodded, setting it aside to mark the missing page. No argument. Tom chose the first book for the dishes that suited him, to try gradually at home. There would be no third purchase.

As he paid, someone reached between us for a large book at the back. I moved aside, catching my empty bag on the table corner. A small tug freed it. I still felt awkward, as if browsing required prior instruction in standing.

Tom saw without laughing and made room beside him. Once I'd settled, he remarked on the narrow passage. The man with the illustrated volume was protecting his elbows in the same position. Nobody was particularly accomplished at this.

The seller's change lacked a coin. Tom looked at his palm and calmly mentioned it. The seller counted again and supplied it. I'd always imagined worrying about seeming petty in such a moment. No quarrel arrived. The money went away; the incident ended.

“Aren't you afraid you'll count it wrong?”

“That's why I count.”

Then he laughed. Last time, he'd counted wrongly in a shop and made somebody repeat it with him. Not every occasion looked like today. He held his book and examined a shallow crease. Twice he pressed it with his thumb; the cover still lifted a little.

Which dish would he try first? He showed a page we'd seen. I admitted not recognizing one ingredient. He wasn't certain either. He'd look properly at home, and choose another recipe if he couldn't get it. This time, two people guessing together didn't make something definitely present in his kitchen.

I still held the story collection. Was I waiting for it to become cheaper? Laughing, I said I hadn't finished its first opening. Tom checked the time. There was a while before eleven. He went to find water nearby and left me to read.

Alone, I actually continued. A guest thought himself terribly late, only to find his host unprepared. They apologized to one another beside chairs that hadn't been arranged. It wasn't my Last Chair. Two stories containing chairs didn't require an anxious demonstration that I hadn't borrowed this one.

I checked the contents, some page numbers farther in, and gently inspected the binding. Finding no missing page wasn't a guarantee I'd checked every leaf. I returned to the first opening, wanting to establish that my pleasure wasn't merely relief at having found somewhere comfortable to stand. One sentence had seemed awkward on the first reading. It still did. I didn't replace the book because of it, or intend to buy it to teach the author revisions.

Did I need a bag? I opened my own, finding its folds too firmly pressed. I needed both hands to shake it out. The seller held the book for me without hurrying. Thanking him as I took it back, I noticed a previous owner's name inside. Faded ink left only the beginning clear.

I didn't ask why that person had sold the book. The answer wasn't something the seller owed me. Moving my finger away from the writing, I checked that the price remained the one he'd quoted.

I told the seller I'd take it, paid, and placed it in my cloth bag. Its cover was thin. Nothing hard lay inside to press against it.

When Tom returned, I was trying to fold the bag's opening neatly. What had I bought? I showed the cover. He didn't request a complete account of the story, merely observing that its size suited a bus journey.

Near eleven, we walked toward the corner. The sun was brighter now and my back felt warm, but my purchase was light in my hand. Tom said if next week's attempt at the dish didn't destroy his pan, he'd tell me how it went. No need to endanger himself on my behalf. He was feeding himself, he said, not writing a report.

At our parting place, he took another street. I waited for my bus without immediately retrieving the book. After an hour standing, my eyes had browsed enough. Drinking water, I saw the man seeking large print emerge from the street carrying a book. Too far away to identify it. He hadn't remained empty-handed waiting for me to finish his errand.

The bus grew crowded for a while. I protected the cloth bag in front of me and moved clear of the doors. I didn't rehearse the whole morning to retain it. If Lumi asked later, I might forget what the seller wore, or the name of the dish on the cookbook's cover.

But I remembered the missing leaf's paper stub, and Tom wanting less guessing. At my stop, I stepped down with the thin book, glad my bag didn't contain a yellow notebook whose job was to establish that I was a writer.
