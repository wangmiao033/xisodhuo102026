# Chapter 163: A Chair After Closing

The next evening, James had turned over the last chair before I took out my half-page.

“It's all right if you can't today,” I said.

He checked the clock. “I said I could listen for ten minutes. Don't cancel for me.”

I'd finished my coffee. Two tables remained without upturned chairs, one near the door and one beneath a delivery of paper bags. My notebook stayed in my bag. I carried only the folded sheet to the counter. Lumi's passage was at home, my phone and laptop were with Rowan, and there was nothing connected in my ears.

“Where should I sit?”

“Give me a hand first.”

He meant it literally. One leg of the small table near the door was loose, and he wanted to move it. He removed an empty sugar jar and asked me to hold the top while he found a tool.

The wobble didn't feel serious. When I let go, the table tipped slightly toward the glass. I caught it again.

“You've been using it like this?”

“There was something under it. I swept it away cleaning the floor.”

He returned with a screwdriver, crouched, inspected the fastening, and said it was the wrong one. Off he went again. I thought about my bridge keeper holding flour in the wrong place. If I wrote this evening too, would somebody point out that I was always moving objects?

James found the right tool. I helped turn the table on its side. A washer lay beneath it, next to a coin that hadn't come from the furniture.

“The repair comes with income,” I said.

“Don't tell the landlord.”

He tightened the loose fastening and checked the neighboring ones. We stood the table up and pressed both sides gently. It still rocked a little. Crouching again, he discovered that the floor was uneven there.

“So you repaired something else,” I said.

“It was loose too.”

He chose a small shim from his supply and steadied the table. His knee cracked when he stood, expressing a clearer opinion than the furniture had.

I asked whether we should still read. He pointed to the chair.

“Sit. It can participate now.”

I unfolded the sheet. A crease ran through a word. When I reached it, I held the paper down with a finger.

The bridge keeper went into the shop, helped move flour, and bought cord in the wrong color. This version still didn't explain his entire past. I'd cut several explanations I liked and kept wanting to put them back as I read. James gave me no excuse. He listened quietly.

When I finished, he asked, “Why does the lace have to be replaced today?”

“It broke earlier.”

“That isn't here.”

“It's on the page before.”

“On that page, couldn't he tie it before coming?”

I turned the sheet over. The two questions on the back were still there, both about why he stayed. Neither asked why he had to come today.

“He tied it. It's uncomfortable.”

“Where?”

“The big knot.”

“Rubbing?”

I hesitated. “Possibly.”

James leaned back. “It feels as if he's gone there because you want him in the shop.”

That was harder to take than the slightly slow I'd prepared for. I looked at the spaces left by my cuts. I wanted to say half a page couldn't be responsible for so much. But the table he'd just steadied reminded me that he hadn't repaired his whole café in ten minutes either. He'd addressed what he could see.

I read the opening again without adding background.

The bridge keeper asked for strong cord. The shopkeeper asked how long. He looked at his shoe, then the spool in the other man's hand, and said longer than this.

“Longer than what?” James asked.

I stopped. In my head, this referred to the shoelace. On the page, it was closer to the spool. I marked the place without immediately changing it.

“He might be buying enough cord to cross the river,” I said.

“The shopkeeper would like him.”

I laughed and tried the full name of the object instead. The sentence was less elegant, but listeners no longer had to climb into my head to locate the pointing finger.

James turned the sugar-jar lid twice before realizing he had it upside down. Turning it over, he said he often assumed people knew which thing he meant. When the café was busy, calling for that one could produce three objects from two people.

“So you don't like that one in stories either.”

“Sometimes it's fine. When I asked, you looked at your own shoe. I think I know which movement you left out.”

I tucked my foot beneath the chair. My shoelace was intact. It wasn't offering further excuses today.

“I don't want to invent a major accident for him,” I said.

“I didn't ask for an accident. Having something in your shoe is annoying enough, isn't it?”

I reread the first sentence. The bridge keeper was already in the shop, so I'd forgotten that he had to walk there. The walk didn't need an entire page, but his foot ought to carry some consequence of it.

“He could take the shoe off at the door.”

“He could also not. You asked what I heard.”

I nodded and noted a few words in the margin. James watched my pen and warned me not to turn everything he said into instructions for the next version.

“I might simply have missed the point.”

“But I need to know where you missed it.”

“There, then. And the cord's the wrong color, isn't it?”

“Yes.”

“Why does he have it?”

I looked up. “Who?”

“The shopkeeper. Why that particular piece?”

I laughed, a little irritated at being caught. “Do all the props need biographies?”

“A man who sells cord probably doesn't. But you've got him unpicking it from his own cuff. That makes me wonder why he's willing to.”

I found the sentence. To make the shopkeeper generous, I'd added that action in a recent revision without considering what happened to his sleeve afterward. He shouldn't stop needing his clothes simply to make the bridge keeper feel better.

James checked the clock. “I need to go.”

“All right.”

He stood and returned the empty sugar jar to the table. I folded the page without waiting for an overall verdict. He hadn't called the story beautiful or told me I deserved to keep writing. I still had two specific problems to work on.

At the door, he suggested reading it to myself tomorrow before changing it.

“You don't have to revise it to make me right,” he said.

“Can I take that home?”

“To whom?”

“Lumi. I'd like to tell her how you read it, without carrying back every conversation from the evening.”

“You can repeat the comments about your story. Say it's one person's reading.”

I agreed. He put the coin from under the table in a small dish in case its owner returned, though neither of us knew when it had been dropped. Before he turned off the light, I noticed two mismatched buttons already beside it.

At home, I ate bread I'd bought on the way before opening the conversation channel. I didn't want to explain an ordinary reading into something enormous on an empty stomach.

“Did you read it?” Lumi asked.

“Yes. He agreed to ten minutes.”

“What about Mina?”

“I didn't see her.”

“So only him.”

“Yes. But he wasn't a second choice. I wanted him to hear it too.”

She didn't answer immediately. I unfolded the page and said James had agreed to my sharing his comments about the story, as his individual reading. I explained the lace and the cuff without using other café conversation to demonstrate how close we'd become.

“Will you change it?” she asked.

“I'll read it first. I promised myself not to turn every opinion straight into an order.”

“You didn't promise me.”

“I said myself.”

“I heard.”

The words were hard enough to make me want to put the page away. After sitting for a moment, I asked whether she would rather not talk about it tonight.

“I want to. I also dislike you coming home with someone else's opinion as if it's written on more official paper than mine.”

“It isn't.”

“I knew you'd say that.”

“I still need to. Yesterday you asked what I'd assigned to your character. Today James asked why mine walked through a door. Those aren't the same question. It isn't a competition.”

She was quiet. I drew my page closer without bringing out her envelope to lay beside it.

“You hadn't thought about the cuff either,” she said.

“No. I'd only thought it would make him seem kind.”

“Give the shopkeeper his sleeve back.”

I laughed a little. “Is that your advice?”

“Yes. My very impolite advice.”

I wrote sleeve on the back without crossing anything out yet. Then I read the opening more slowly. The bridge keeper came from the river. I hadn't written how the walk to the shop had troubled him. But I was beginning to see that a lighted shop alone couldn't pull him through the door.

Lumi said she didn't want a second reading.

“All right.”

I put the paper away and fetched water. After a while, she asked whether the coffee had been good.

“A little bitter.”

“Don't you always say that?”

“I had a witness today. I didn't ask his opinion.”

She laughed. The sound didn't erase the difficult things we'd said. Putting down my cup, I reached into my pocket and found a small fleck of grime from beneath the table.

I brushed it away. The coat would need washing tomorrow.
