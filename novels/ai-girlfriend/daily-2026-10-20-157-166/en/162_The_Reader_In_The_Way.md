# Chapter 162: The Reader in the Way

The following morning, I left Lumi's passage at home.

It was on the middle shelf, separate from my notebook. Reaching for my keys, I thought of taking it with me. My hand had already moved toward it when I remembered that the page was for me alone. I found a separate envelope, put it inside, and returned it to the shelf. It didn't go in with the two sentences she had written for the map.

“What are you looking for?” she asked.

“Nothing. Putting last night's page away.”

“You want to read it again?”

“Tonight. I think I understood something.”

“That sounds dangerous.”

“I'm only a reader.”

“A reader preparing to explain me.”

I laughed and said I'd see her tonight. After closing the conversation channel, I collected my lunch and went halfway down the stairs before realizing I didn't have my keys. I went back. This time I didn't switch anything on or let her hear me dismantling that composed goodbye.

During the day, I thought several times about the woman in the light-bulb shop. Her movements interested me more than the lamps. I thought Lumi had finally written someone who didn't make decisions for other people. It was a pleasing idea. I had nearly arranged the compliment before I got home.

After work, I checked my personal email at the library as planned. No phone inspection results, and nothing new requiring an answer that day. I signed out, went home, washed the oil out of my lunchbox, and put tomorrow's rice where I would notice it. Then I took out the envelope.

“You really want to hear it again?” Lumi asked.

“I want to ask about the woman.”

“Ask.”

I read the passage back, checking that I hadn't misread my handwriting. Then I said I liked how she hadn't forced the other person to accept her arrangement.

“I thought you'd ask about the bag.”

“The bag's good too. But I like that she's willing to let go.”

“Of what?”

I paused. “She doesn't force her ideas on him.”

“So you like something she doesn't do.”

“Not doing something can be a choice in a story.”

“Of course. Why are you so certain that's why I wrote her?”

I looked down. The pencil reflected a little light. I turned the page. She hadn't rejected my interpretation, only asked why. Yet I already felt she was taking back something she'd offered.

“I didn't say it was the only reason.”

“You sounded as though I'd finally learned a lesson and written it as a woman.”

“I didn't say finally.”

“It was in your voice.”

I wanted to object that she couldn't hear every word I hadn't spoken. She couldn't. But this time I had already thought that particular word on the stairs.

“Yes. I was thinking that a little.”

She was quiet. I looked at the card beside the speaker. Its last line still contained my sand. Not reading it aloud wouldn't turn it into a sentence she liked.

“I'm not handing you an assignment through her,” she said.

“I know.”

“No. A moment ago, you didn't.”

I put the page down. There was a little water near the edge of the table, left by the lunchbox. I wiped it for longer than necessary. She didn't hurry me. When the cloth could no longer collect anything, I sat down again.

“I wanted you to have written her because you really could let someone else decide,” I said. “That matters to me. I put the hope there too early.”

“You can hope. Don't say you've already found all of it in the words.”

“All right.”

“And sometimes I might write a person because I want to know why she picked the wrong bag.”

I laughed a little. Not a laugh that meant the difficulty was over. She didn't join me.

I picked up the page again and considered the actions first. What did the woman know? Which things did I remember from the earlier story, and which had I supplied myself? Covering the last few lines, I read the beginning. The hesitation remained. The generosity I had prepared to praise was suddenly less certain.

“Do you want her to be embarrassed?” I asked.

“A little.”

“Why?”

“Because she thinks she knows what he needs. But she isn't entirely unkind.”

I almost said, So she's you. The words grew heavy in my mouth. I swallowed them, not because there could be no connection, but because I didn't know how much there was. She hadn't asked me to announce it.

“Can the reader tell what she wants yet?”

“You can't?”

“I know what she's doing. I filled in what she wanted with my own things.”

She asked for the second paragraph again. At one pause, she stopped me.

“Would it work better without a sentence here?”

“Which one?”

She named it. I covered the sentence and read again. The rhythm was quicker, but the move from one action to the next felt abrupt.

“I prefer it there.”

“Didn't you just say it wasn't clear?”

“Not knowing all her motives is different from not understanding why she turns around.”

This time she took longer. Eventually she said to leave it for now.

When I reached the delivery man steadying the bag, I asked why he didn't simply request the larger one.

“Can't he see she's putting the lamp down?” she said.

“He can. But one sentence wouldn't necessarily hold her up.”

“I want him to wait a moment.”

“Then something has to happen in that moment. Otherwise it's only me waiting for your next sentence.”

She went quiet. I heard the edge in my voice, as though it were finally my turn to question her. I tried again without the last part. The base of the lamp was still warm. The woman had to find somewhere stable for it. The man was holding the bag steady. There was something to watch. In my hurry to get him what he wanted, I'd skipped the movement.

“So you don't think it needs changing now?” she asked.

“I want to read the business with the lamp properly first. Not every wait needs cutting.”

I picked up my empty cup and tried holding the paper with one hand while placing the cup in a narrow space. The paper slid anyway. I caught it with my elbow, and the cup tapped the spine of a book.

“What was that?”

“I'm making a cup pretend to be a lamp.”

“Did it pass?”

“It nearly damaged my evidence.”

She laughed and told me not to invent an action for the real lamp that I hadn't actually seen. I returned the cup and flattened the paper.

Keeping the sentence wasn't a victory. Nor did rejecting my interpretation mean I had to dislike every change she proposed. We tried that one line without my asking for everything before and after it.

My half-page was also on the table. I opened it and suddenly didn't want to read.

“What is it?”

“Now I think whatever I make the bridge keeper do, you'll think it's me.”

“Sometimes I will.”

“What do we do about that?”

“You can say I'm wrong.”

“Will that stop you thinking it?”

“Not immediately.”

I stared at the conversation about buying cord. The answer wasn't especially comforting. It made sitting there easier than an immediate promise would have.

I told her the shopkeeper wasn't James and the woman who had left wasn't Mina. Some actions came from people I'd observed. Others simply let the scene proceed. I might not even know which parts I'd borrowed.

“Then I can say that too,” she said.

“Yes.”

“But if I make everyone outside awful, you'll still be angry.”

“Yes. That's the effect it has on me. I can object without claiming I've proved you've interfered with something again.”

She said softly that it was complicated.

“Yes. I came home prepared to praise you. It was going to be very efficient.”

She finally laughed. “Don't save yourself so much trouble next time.”

Later, I asked if I could put two comments beside the private copy. She said yes, but not to cross out her original sentences. On a separate scrap, I wrote: The woman's hesitation interests me, but I'm not sure how she hopes he will answer.

I stopped halfway through the second comment. I had meant to write that this made her more real. Looking at those words, I crossed them out and wrote: I'd keep the sentence before she turns.

I read both aloud. Lumi said she'd think about the first and had heard the second.

“You don't have to grade me,” I said.

“Eight out of ten for the first.”

“Lumi.”

“You don't have to ask for me to have an opinion.”

Dinner was later than planned. When I went to heat soup, she reminded me to move the paper. I knew she remembered the earlier water; she hadn't seen my hand. I put the envelopes away separately. Her middle passage, the two sentences for the map, and my half-page didn't become a single book.

The soup was hot. I placed the bowl carefully on the other side of the table.

“Do you still like it?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Not the thing you thought I'd learned.”

“The woman. She makes me want to keep reading.”

“You're still not getting the ending.”

“I didn't ask.”

“I'm saying it first.”

I blew on a spoonful, drank too soon, and burned the tip of my tongue. Hearing my muffled sound, she asked what had happened. I told her the reader's main problem this evening was his mouth.

“I knew that already.”

I laughed and rested the spoon against the bowl. This time I waited.
