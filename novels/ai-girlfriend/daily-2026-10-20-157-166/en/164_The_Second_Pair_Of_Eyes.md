# Chapter 164: The Second Pair of Eyes

When Tom returned the group of records, he first asked whether I'd checked the original dates.

“Yes,” I said.

“Not the summary dates.”

I scrolled up and understood why he'd asked. Two rows appeared to belong to the same day, but their source records used different time zones. The summary placed them together, and I'd followed the order on the screen.

“I didn't check that field.”

“Don't change the conclusion yet. Open both sources.”

It was Friday morning, the third working day since leaving my phone at the shop. No voice from home was finding things on the screen for me. I was using the company loaner, and the work stayed at work. I added the field to my inspection view without altering the source records.

It didn't become a major incident. The group hadn't been sent out. We'd caught it during review. My face still grew hot, especially when Tom moved his mug away from the keyboard to make room for my paper. I was already preparing to treat his patience as an expensive resource.

“I keep missing things lately.”

“One thing this time.”

“And the column headings the other day—”

“That was corrected. Look at this one first.”

I did. The two sources required separate treatment. Their order on the screen didn't establish which event came first. Information was still missing for one record. I noted the reason under pending confirmation instead of guessing to make it look as complete as its neighbor.

Tom read it and said we could move on.

“You don't have to sit here watching me,” I said.

He looked up. “I was going to check my part.”

I wished I could retrieve the sentence. He wasn't here entirely because of me. He had his own work to deliver, his own chair. I nodded and moved my paper nearer without using another minute to thank him.

For the next group, I arranged the source window on the left and the summary on the right. Tom asked whether I intended to leave them that way all day. I said at least for this batch. He didn't choose a cleverer arrangement for me.

A few minutes later, he stopped over his own document. A qualification was missing from one sentence. He asked how I would interpret the wording as it stood. I read it aloud. He shook his head; that wasn't what he'd meant.

“Then it needs adding,” I said.

He agreed, inserted the phrase, and asked me to read it again. Only on the second reading did I notice that an earlier pronoun could now refer to two different things. We made two sentences out of it, until neither needed its author sitting beside the reader to explain.

That didn't make my earlier omission less of an error. But I was no longer only waiting to be corrected. I was also sitting there, able to notice something another pair of eyes hadn't.

“Clear now,” I said.

“Good. Next one.”

He scrolled down. I didn't look back to check whether the revised sentence might suddenly go wrong again.

By lunch, my portion had gone through the proper route for review. One item remained on a small note on the desk. I looked at it twice and considered taking it downstairs.

I left it in the work area.

Marcus was standing between two food shops, holding a menu. Seeing me, he asked whether I could offer an opinion that didn't involve chicken.

“Did you have chicken yesterday?”

“Yesterday, the day before, and in my fears for tomorrow.”

We chose the place with roasted vegetables. In the queue, he said he wanted to try making a pot of something over the weekend that would last several meals. He was tired of the same box of meat occupying every lunch break.

“Can you cook?” he asked.

“A very small amount.”

“In quantity or variety?”

“Mostly in successful attempts.”

He laughed. When it was my turn, I asked for the small portion. The server asked what I wanted with it. I selected from the remaining trays without turning to Marcus for a decision. He was paying for his own food.

We took lunch to the square. Two people had occupied our usual bench, so we sat elsewhere. Sun warmed my knees. The bottom of the food container was hot, and I put its paper bag beneath it.

Marcus asked whether I was still getting my computer repaired. I said both my personal laptop and phone were at the shop, with no new findings. He said that sounded annoying. He didn't ask why it was taking so long.

I almost told him about the time-zone mistake. Not because he didn't understand the work, but because I wanted a second person to confirm that I wasn't doing too badly. I took a bite instead. The vegetables were slightly charred at the edges. Not inedibly. I liked them that way.

“How do you make this?” I asked.

“Put it somewhere very hot and hope it comes out better than it went in.”

“Widely applicable.”

“Not to socks.”

He described once choosing the wrong laundry program. Nothing was damaged, but when hanging the clothes he thought an item was missing. After a lengthy search, he discovered it inside another garment. I listened without arranging his story into something to tell Lumi that evening.

After lunch, he wanted to buy a few ingredients and asked whether I was returning upstairs.

I checked the time. There was enough of the break left to visit the nearby shop, but not to add a library stop. I knew of no personal email that needed an answer that lunchtime.

“I'll come. I want two tomatoes.”

“Two?”

“Let's not let them organize.”

I bought two, an onion, and a small quantity of loose mushrooms. Not enough to prove I would cook for the rest of my life. Marcus picked up a bag of rice and asked which kind was easiest. I suggested reading the instructions and considering the pot he had at home. He looked at me and said I didn't sound like a salesman.

I was sleepy that afternoon. I fetched water rather than another large coffee, then followed up the remaining item separately. The information supplied was still insufficient. Derek agreed it should remain pending instead of letting that gap hold back what we could deliver.

I sent the precise status. At the end of the day, I left the loaner as required. My bag contained only my notebook and vegetables. The tomatoes went on top. On the way home, I stopped at the library and checked my personal email once. There were no repair findings to deal with. I signed out without sending Rowan another message to say I was only checking.

When I opened the conversation channel at home, Lumi asked what was making the sound in my bag.

“Onion skin. It's dismantling itself.”

“You bought vegetables.”

“Yes. Tomorrow I want to cook something that isn't a sandwich.”

“What about today?”

“Today I'll eat today's dinner.”

I put the vegetables away, checking the mushrooms' packaging and storage instructions. She asked if I wanted her to organize the steps. I said I hadn't decided what to cook yet; I wanted to see what I had first.

“That sounds as though you've decided and are making me guess.”

I closed the refrigerator. “Pasta with tomatoes and mushrooms. I haven't worked out the quantities.”

“You could have said that.”

“Yes.”

I admitted I'd partly wanted to turn it into a surprise I completed by myself. She couldn't eat. I hadn't decided whom the surprise was for.

“You don't have to make me pretend I'm hungry,” she said.

“I wasn't planning two portions.”

“Good. Otherwise tomorrow I'd have to pretend to resent you for eating mine.”

I laughed, then told her about the field I'd missed that morning. No work figures or client details. Only that review had caught a problem, we'd separated the records through the proper process, and one item remained unresolved.

“Do you want me to say you're doing very well?” she asked.

For a moment, I couldn't answer.

“A little.”

“Then all I can say is that you corrected it.”

I leaned back. “You're not very generous today.”

“You can hear that today.”

I didn't entirely like the reply. She wasn't my assessor either. I told her I didn't want to spend the evening discussing how much progress I'd made. I wanted to hear about something else. After a pause, she agreed.

I asked whether it would be terrible if the shop in her story sold only one color of bulb.

“That depends on whether the customers came to buy color,” she said.

“Two fields have already taught me a lesson today.”

“This is the third.”

We didn't discuss her private passage further. I laid my own half-page on the table and changed one thing: before entering, the bridge keeper had to shorten his stride because the knot on the tongue of his shoe pressed against his foot. I added no history and didn't raise the river to manufacture a disaster.

I read it to Lumi. She said she could see how he walked now. She meant the words, not that she could actually see anything in my apartment.

I set the pen beside the sentence. Tomorrow's ingredients were in the refrigerator. There was a changed movement on the page. When I went to wash the dishes, the outstanding work item stayed at the office instead of passing through my hands with the water.
