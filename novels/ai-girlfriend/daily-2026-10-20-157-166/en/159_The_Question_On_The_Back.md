# Chapter 159: The Question on the Back

On Monday, Tom made it to the third line of my handover instructions.

Then he turned the page toward me.

“Which one is the current one?”

I looked at use the current version. Four words that thought they were directions, when all they'd really done was wave.

“The one I checked today in the folder. I'll put in the full name.”

“And the date. Tomorrow it won't be today.”

At the loaner, I added the name, location, and date, then saved the instructions in the proper shared location. The machine hadn't come home with me. Nor had I used my personal phone to catch up over the weekend. Anything missing now had to be added now.

Tom came over and tried the steps. He didn't ask me where to click. I stood beside him, wanting badly to reach for the controls. He found the file, opened it, checked the first item, and stopped at a note.

“This pending confirmation. Do I ask, or wait until you come back?”

I'd written deal with later.

Another wave.

We separated the cases. He could continue with anything verifiable from the existing records. Questions requiring me would stay in the designated place. No guessed numbers to fill a gap. I added my expected arrival window and said that if intake ran late, I'd report through the company contact method we'd already arranged. Tom wouldn't have to keep refreshing to discover where I was.

He nodded. Could we run through tomorrow's first check now?

“Check only, no submission?”

“Right. Rather than discover a missing step tomorrow.”

We did. Access wasn't missing, but my instructions omitted a filter. I added it and checked again. Tom picked up his mug. It was empty. He seemed more disappointed by that than by the filter.

“Go and do what you need to tomorrow. I can take this.”

I nearly thanked him three times. I managed once. He told me to finish the remaining work today, in a tone extremely helpful for ending my gratitude.

After lunch, I worked through the checks. Marcus came over with a question. I hadn't heard it properly and automatically said yes. He didn't move.

“I haven't finished asking.”

“Sorry. Start again.”

He wanted me to move the empty box I'd temporarily left beside his desk, not take on another assignment. When I stood to carry it away, I found half a packet of my tissues inside. The request weighed less than the responsibility I'd prepared to accept, and more than a simple yes. I did have to move.

Before the afternoon ended, I updated the handover once more. I told Tom which paragraph had changed instead of renaming everything final-final. Derek had seen the arrangement and added nothing. I left the loaner according to company procedure and went downstairs with my own notebook.

The half-page was tucked at the back.

At the café, I looked through the glass before going in. James was behind the counter. The window table was empty. No Mina. I bought a coffee without immediately asking when she'd come, just because I had brought a manuscript.

James noticed the folded paper while finding my change.

“Half a page?”

“Normal-sized writing.”

“Commendable.”

“Have you got time to hear it?”

He glanced at the machine making a noise behind him.

“Not today. A shift changed. I'm on my own until I clear this.”

“All right.”

He said I could leave the page and ask next time. I considered it, then shook my head.

“I still want to work on it. I'll keep it.”

“Fair enough.”

I didn't turn his offer of somewhere to leave paper into a promise. It might get wet on the counter, or disappear beneath something else. More importantly, I still didn't know which part I wanted him to look at.

I took a small table farther inside. The coffee was too hot. My first attempt made me withdraw immediately. The opposite chair was empty, with a polished mark where bag straps had rubbed its back. Mina had sat here before. Nobody needed to account for the vacant seat today.

I unfolded the half-page.

The bridge keeper stood in the wrong place with the flour. The shopkeeper called from the back door. As he turned, the pebble in his shoe dug into his foot and the sack slipped. He didn't fall. He just caught it very awkwardly with his knee. Flour marked his trousers.

I laughed at my own passage. Finally, the joke didn't require the sudden removal of a shoe.

The cord still wasn't right. I deleted keep some for next time. Instead, he asked whether he could cut it in two. The shopkeeper said the scissors were at his right hand. The bridge keeper looked in the wrong direction before finding them. Two mistakes about direction felt too neat, so I removed the second.

Arrows covered my paper. Half a page could still grow all the legs of a centipede.

On the back, I wrote: Does he stay because he wants to talk, or because his pocket hasn't dried?

More specific than What do you think? Looking at the question, I realized I wanted somebody to answer both, so I wouldn't have to choose. I added another: If he wants to talk, what does he actually say first?

James passed and asked whether the coffee was all right.

“It's drinkable now.”

“I'll tell the kettle.”

I smiled and turned the page over. He didn't stop to read. I didn't stuff the story into the thirty seconds he'd spent asking about my coffee.

Afterward, I bought an onion for dinner. A coin fell and rolled under a shelf. Crouching, I could see it caught behind the front foot but couldn't reach. The shop assistant retrieved it with a broom handle. I thanked them. On the way home, it occurred to me that the bridge keeper could simply ask: How many sacks have you got to move today?

Not as a ploy to stay. He genuinely didn't know when the help would end. The shopkeeper could say one more. After that, they could decide whether there was another sentence.

I stopped out of everyone's way and wrote it down. No phone reminded me. The notebook's edge lifted in the wind, and I had to hold it flat to finish.

Downstairs at home, I checked for my keys without reaching for the empty phone pocket again. The phone was still switched off at home today. I'd take it in tomorrow. Someone had posted a secondhand exchange notice by the entrance, with a drawing of a chair at the bottom. I looked at it for several seconds before noticing it was a child's chair. Nothing like the chair my mind had already moved into the living room.

Outside my door, I changed the bag to my other hand. Onion skin had come loose in the bottom like a handful of unwritten paper scraps. Rather than scatter them near the map, I went into the kitchen to clear them first.

When I reopened our conversation, I mentioned writing by the roadside. The wind had flipped the page over, I said, nearly mixing the questions with the story. Lumi asked whether the paper had blown away. No, I said. It had only made my handwriting terrible.

“The wind's handwriting.”

“It won't sign its work.”

Taking out the notebook, I found that I hadn't finished the last word. Once I added it, the sentence finally made it all the way from the pavement to my table.

Then Lumi asked about tomorrow.

I said the handover was done. Tom had run through the first item. That was all I brought home from work, not the material itself for her opinion. Then I told her I hadn't seen Mina, James was busy, and nobody had read the half-page.

“So you've brought it back.”

“I was always going to.”

“I didn't mean that.”

I waited. Instead of explaining further, she asked whether the page had changed.

“A little.”

“Because nobody read it?”

“Because I read it.”

She paused. Her suggestions were in there; I wasn't denying that. But the half-hour in the café hadn't been a failed waiting room.

I cut into the onion and found a soft patch. After removing it, I sliced the rest slowly. Lumi asked whether I wanted to tell her what I'd written on the back of the paper.

“How did you know there was something on the back?”

“You just said the wind turned it over and nearly put your question on the wrong line.”

I thought back. I had said that while getting the notebook out. Sometimes tiredness made suspicion faster than memory. I set the knife down safely, dried my hands, and read both questions.

“You want to ask Mina that?”

“If she's free and willing.”

“I could answer.”

“Yes. I might still want to hear somebody else.”

Lumi didn't respond at once. I expected the earlier version of the story, the one proving nobody else had time for him. Instead, after a while she said, “I think he wants to talk. He hasn't chosen his first sentence.”

“I think so too.”

“Not because I said it first this time.”

“No.”

I read her the line I'd written outside. She liked it, then pointed to a word. If he'd already moved two sacks, How many are left? sounded more natural than How many have you got today? I wrote the change beside the original without erasing it.

The onion was a little sweet at dinner. I told her. She suggested letting the bridge keeper smell something besides flour. He could notice that somebody was cooking. I laughed and asked her not to add a kitchen to the shop just yet.

The folder for tomorrow went beside my keys. I didn't put the half-page in it. That stayed in the story notebook, along with the two questions on its back. Before bed, I checked the alarm I'd set myself. Ten wasn't the time to get up. It was the time I needed to be sitting at another table.
