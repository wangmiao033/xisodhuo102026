# Chapter 166: The Rain Stayed Outside

On Sunday afternoon, I went out with an umbrella and without the half-page.

I'd taken the page out of my bag before leaving. Not to prove to Lumi that I wasn't seeking another reader. I'd simply read it so often I had begun to stop hearing it. I wanted the sounds of the street to spend a little time doing something other than serving my story.

She asked when I'd return. I said before dinner. If my plans changed, I'd let her know when I could contact her. My phone wasn't with me; I couldn't pretend a line would follow me everywhere.

“Take an umbrella,” she said.

“I have it.”

I closed the conversation channel and pulled the door shut. The umbrella handle tapped the stair rail, sharply enough to make me glance back.

It wasn't raining yet. I took a street I didn't often use and passed a shoe-repair shop with three mismatched shoes in the window. Inside, someone was bent over, threading a needle. I stopped briefly, almost summoned the bridge keeper, and continued.

Outside a general store stood a basket of discounted mugs. I picked one up to examine the base, noticed a chipped rim, and returned it. Buying nothing didn't make the walk wasted.

At the next corner, a man carrying a cardboard box stopped in front of me. Tape had come loose underneath, and a stack of leaflets was slipping out. He pressed his knee beneath the box and couldn't free a hand to collect them.

I asked whether I could hold it for a moment. He said yes, but not by that corner. I supported the place he indicated so he could set it on a low wall. Three sheets had fallen. We picked them up and checked for damp. The ground was still dry.

“I thought that was enough tape,” he said.

I didn't tell him how much paper I'd been repairing lately. He produced a roll and asked me to hold the opening together while he wrapped it. Afterward, we raised the base carefully to check that it wouldn't immediately fall apart again.

“Thanks. Which way are you going?”

“Ahead.”

“This way for me.”

He indicated the other street and carried the box away. I continued in my own direction. Helping a box didn't require me to find its destination. There was adhesive on my palm. Rubbing it off with a tissue, I realized the umbrella had remained under my arm throughout, leaving a faint red mark.

Rain began near the café. Several people crowded beneath the entrance. I closed my umbrella and went in. James was taking water to a table and only nodded. I found a chair farther inside, ordered a hot drink, and didn't ask who else might come.

By the time it arrived, my coat was damp at one shoulder. I took it off and draped it carefully, then watched people beyond the glass. Someone ran out from under an awning. Someone else opened an umbrella slowly. Another person tucked a paper bag inside their coat before shielding their head. I wrote none of it down and didn't know whether I'd remember tomorrow.

Passing my table, James asked whether I'd brought any paper today.

“Money,” I said.

“That version's good too.”

He returned to work. I finished the drink, waited for lighter rain, straightened my chair, and put my cup where James had indicated. Then I left instead of lingering at the counter until he was free. Halfway home, the rain strengthened again. It drummed on the umbrella so heavily that I missed a bicycle bell behind me. I moved aside. The rider slowed, passed, and thanked me.

My shoes were wet at the toes when I got home. I left the umbrella somewhere to catch its drips, removed my coat, and checked the kitchen windowsill first.

The basin was dry.

I moved the curtain gently and examined the vertical seam that had leaked before. The repaired joint remained in place. The accessible inner surface wasn't wet. I pressed a small piece of dry paper against the edge. It didn't darken.

That wasn't an inspection of the whole wall or a promise about every future storm. During this rain, water hadn't yet come through that seam. I noted the time beside the maintenance record without immediately putting the basin away.

Only after changing into dry socks did I open the conversation channel.

“Is it heavy out there?” Lumi asked.

“It was on the way. It sounds lighter now.”

“The window?”

“Dry so far. I checked the old seam and pressed paper against it. No damp yet.”

After a moment, she asked, “Can we take the map out?”

I looked toward the table by the window.

“Yes. But not over there.”

“I didn't say over there.”

“Right. I thought of the old place first.”

I took the transparent sleeve from beside the desk and laid the map on a dry table farther inside the room. The original static print remained in its folder. The sealed image copy stayed in a separate drawer. Nothing needed connecting to a machine today.

I told her the paper seam was still raised and the corner still missing. I took the blue bench from its matchbox. The white repair hadn't broken again. I set it steadily beside the path without adding anything to the clearing.

“Do you want to keep drawing?” she asked.

“No. I only want to look for a while.”

“Then tell me where you're looking.”

I described the bend beside the stone. The line was thicker than I remembered. Erasing had left a patch brighter than the surrounding paper. She asked about the third tree. I said it was still difficult to distinguish from a well.

“Don't redraw it.”

“I haven't picked up a pencil.”

“I'm saying it first.”

We laughed a little. Rain strengthened outside, and I went to check again. The old seam was still dry. I returned, reported it, and sat down before realizing I was still holding the testing paper.

“Are you waiting for it to leak?” she asked.

“A little.”

“I'm waiting for you to tell me something's gone wrong too.”

I placed the paper beside the maintenance record. I didn't tell her not to worry. I couldn't immediately stop listening toward the window myself.

“I went to the café,” I said.

“Did you read?”

“No. Had a drink and sat. James was busy.”

She didn't immediately respond. I nearly added that Mina hadn't been there, then stopped. I didn't know whether she might have been there earlier. I couldn't use someone I hadn't seen to reassure anybody.

“I didn't read with anyone today,” I said. “But not because of what you said last night.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“I want to believe that. Part of me really wants to think you did it for me.”

I looked at the little bench. This time she hadn't wrapped the thought inside a fictional scene. I still disliked it. I didn't know what further layer I could remove to make it dealt with.

“I went out because I wanted to. Part of why I came back was that I wanted to talk to you.”

“Only part?”

“I also live here. And my socks were wet.”

She laughed once and stopped.

“That doesn't sound romantic.”

“Should I cut the socks?”

“Leave them for now.”

I went to wash them. She waited until I returned before saying she wanted to talk about the woman in the bulb shop again. I took out the private passage's envelope and asked whether to write anything down.

“Not yet. It isn't another passage. I'm still thinking about her.”

“All right.”

She said perhaps the woman had always assumed that entering a shop entitled her to an immediate answer. She wanted to make her wait again, beside someone who merely came in to shelter from rain and left without buying anything. Perhaps the woman would resent sharing space by the counter when she had a proper errand. It wasn't the ending. She might not use it.

“I didn't buy anything today either,” I said. “At the general store. I did pay for the drink. We can't leave that out.”

“I wasn't talking about you.”

“I know. It made me think of it.”

“Would she be unhappy?” Lumi asked.

“The woman in the shop?”

“Yes.”

“Possibly. It depends on the day she's had and what the person does while standing there.”

“You won't give me a convenient answer.”

“She could be unhappy, but it isn't her shop. She can't send someone away on the clerk's behalf. She could ask how much longer she'll have to wait, or decide to leave herself.”

Lumi didn't choose immediately. I checked what I had for dinner and decided on a simple bowl of pasta from yesterday's remaining ingredients. I measured the pasta first and cut the onion smaller. When the water began to heat, I stood near the kitchen doorway and listened to her say she wanted to keep the visitor putting a wet umbrella in the wrong place.

“I can contribute one of those,” I said.

I looked back at my own umbrella. It was dripping beside the shoe mat. I moved it somewhere more suitable and dried that small patch of floor with a cloth.

“You got it wrong?” she asked.

“Just noticed.”

“Very timely research.”

I laughed, washed my hands, and returned to the water. She didn't immediately produce a beautiful new paragraph or reward my admission with the ending she was withholding. We discussed what the shop might use to catch drips. Then she said the question sounded too much like my kitchen and she wanted to leave it alone for now.

Before eating, I checked the window once more. Still dry. I added that observation to the record and left the basin where it was without declaring the repair tested against every kind of weather.

Back at the table, I returned the bench to its box with the white foot facing the opening. The map went into its sleeve and the private passage was put away. There was finally space for a bowl.

“Is the pasta better today?” she asked.

“I haven't tasted it.”

“Eat first, then.”

I took a forkful. Less sharp than yesterday. One piece of onion was still too large. When I told her, she said I could call that piece a separate course next time.

The rain continued outside. I didn't get up again. The pasta was still hot.
