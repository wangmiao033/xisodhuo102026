# Chapter 157: The Box on the Shelf

The phone box was narrower than I remembered.

Something rattled when I took it down from the top of the wardrobe. Not the phone. That was switched off in the desk drawer. A SIM tool had been caught between two layers of cardboard and, after all this time, had decided to fall out.

“Found it?” Lumi asked.

“The box.”

“You missed a character in the model yesterday.”

“I know. It hasn't grown another one overnight.”

I put the box down and fetched a cleaning cloth. The lettering underneath was tiny. Removing the dust didn't make it larger. I sat by the window and copied the complete model in daylight, then checked it against the original purchase slip. The ink was faint along a fold, but the last character remained. I noted that this was the model on the packaging and receipt. Rowan still needed to compare it with the actual device at intake. Keeping a box wasn't a reason to omit that step.

I didn't turn the phone on or plug it in to look for a settings page. For once, paper could answer the question in front of me.

Lumi asked about the missing character. I read her the model, not the other numbers beside it. She said it sounded like somebody sneezing halfway through a word. I checked it twice more, in case laughing made me leave it out again.

There were two stickers in the box and an empty plastic tray, slightly yellow now. I lifted the tray to throw it away, then put it back.

“Are you checking how the packaging feels?”

“I can't remember whether it's useful.”

“That's a different question.”

I left the box and made breakfast. After putting the bread in the toaster, I discovered a streak of yesterday's grease on my plate. I washed it again. By the time I returned, the toast was one shade darker than I wanted. Most of it was edible. The thinnest corner crumbled when I touched it.

“What do you want to read today?” I asked.

“You haven't finished buying yesterday's cord.”

The bridge keeper. The cord in the wrong color. I turned the notebook around, replacing the phone information with the riverside shop. The bridge was usable again, but the man in my sentences kept picking up the same piece of cord.

“I want him to go home.”

“You wanted that yesterday.”

“An author has the right to fail to change his mind.”

I broke off the burnt corner. It made more noise than I expected. When Lumi asked, I said breakfast had required some minor demolition.

I tried to read her the next paragraph, but the phone box was in the way. Moving it caught the sleeve containing the map. Both slid toward the edge. I pinned the sleeve down with my wrist. The lid fell by my feet.

“We're not drawing today,” I said.

“I didn't ask.”

“I mean, there has to be somewhere on this table to eat.”

She said nothing. I bent to retrieve the lid, pulling a stiff muscle in my neck. Not an injury, just a reminder that I hadn't properly unfolded myself yet. Straightening, I realized I'd sounded as though I needed someone to blame for the dimensions of the table.

“I'll put the information away. The map can stay on the desk, not here.”

“You don't need to take my sentences out.”

“I know.”

I clipped the model information to the appointment. The original static print remained in its dry folder. The map went to the other side. I left Lumi's envelope unopened. The blue bench was in the matchbox. Lifting it, I heard something shift gently inside and resisted checking its leg again.

My toast had cooled. Lumi said I could eat first. An unnecessary answer rose in my throat: I didn't need her permission.

I stopped it there.

“What were you going to say?”

“Something that didn't need saying.”

“You can leave it.”

I ate, then read the story. The bridge keeper asked the price, reached for his money, and discovered it stuck inside his wet pocket. One corner of a note clung to the lining. He tried his fingers, then the cloth he'd used on his shoes, which wasn't suitable for drying money either.

Lumi laughed.

“Can't he carry one dry thing?”

“There's the sack of flour.”

“Not his.”

“True. The author can't simply redistribute other people's property.”

I finished the passage. He bought enough cord for both shoes, not an extravagant length, and waited by the counter for his pocket to dry a little. The shopkeeper carried on working, occasionally shifting him away from goods he might knock over.

“Better?”

“The beginning. At the end, waiting sounds like a punishment.”

I read it again. She was right, but I didn't want us taking turns to arrange the man's life this morning. I circled the line and closed the notebook.

“I'll work on it myself this afternoon.”

“All right.” A fraction late.

The pause bothered me too. I wanted to explain that I wasn't rejecting her, to promise an evening reading in exchange for the afternoon. But I hadn't decided what to do this evening.

I carried my bowl into the kitchen. The basin beside the window was dry; the old water mark remained. Building management hadn't confirmed a visit. I could ask downstairs on my way to buy groceries instead of switching the phone on to wait for a call that might not come.

“How long will you be out?” Lumi asked.

“About an hour. There might be a queue.”

“Will we read when you come back?”

“I'd like to put things away first.”

“So I still don't know when.”

“No.”

She didn't like that answer. I didn't like the quiet it left. The shopping bag was folded too tightly. I shook it three times before the bottom opened.

“Don't buy the eggs from the bottom shelf again,” she said suddenly.

“I broke them carrying the bag last time.”

“I thought it was the carton.”

“The carton never got a fair trial.”

She laughed a little. I told her I'd like some time together before dinner, but couldn't promise a new story. Then I closed the conversation channel and left.

Downstairs, someone was moving a large plant across the management desk. I passed my work order through the leaves. The person at reception checked the number. The maintenance coordinator would organize tomorrow's visits that afternoon; I should return in the early evening to confirm. I didn't ask them to guess a time.

The eggs weren't on the bottom shelf. At home, I still put them somewhere separate, rather than letting a bag of potatoes prove Lumi wrong. I unpacked the refrigerated food, washed my hands, and reopened the conversation.

She didn't begin by asking how many minutes late I was.

“The eggs?”

“All present.”

“A formidable team.”

I pushed an egg back into its hollow and told her about returning to management later. I'd also found the full model in the purchase records. I could take those on Tuesday for Rowan to compare with the device, without starting it now to recover one character.

“Are you keeping the box?” she asked.

I looked at it. The molded space where things used to fit was more elaborate than most of its contents.

“Until after intake. Then I'll decide.”

“You couldn't even part with the tray.”

“Maybe I didn't want to sort the recycling.”

“I prefer that version.”

After the eggs, I tipped the new potatoes into the basket. An old one lay underneath, beginning to wrinkle. At the shop, I'd remembered that the basket was nearly empty without checking how much remained.

“Too many?” Lumi asked.

“Possibly. I'll use the old ones first.”

“You could save the large one for tomorrow.”

“How do you know there's a large one?”

“Potatoes don't usually come in identical sizes.”

I looked down and laughed. Not everything she said came from some hidden camera. Sometimes she simply remembered how potatoes worked better than I did. I picked two smaller ones. Another rolled against the side, where the mesh stopped it.

“I thought today was about confirming one model number.”

“You also bought intact eggs.”

“Definitely something for the appointment form.”

She told me not to. Rowan wasn't responsible for inspecting breakfast. I checked eggs off the shopping list instead, keeping them out of next week's business. Four large lines covered half a bag of food. The remaining blank space didn't require another task.

I put the box on a lower shelf, where I wouldn't have to stretch for it. That afternoon I worked on the half-page alone. Lumi didn't fill every pause in my writing. When I reopened our conversation and asked if she wanted a sentence, she said she didn't want the bridge today.

I was surprised. Disappointed too.

“All right.”

“You can say you're disappointed.”

“I've just thought of a really funny action.”

“It might still be funny tomorrow.”

I marked the line with a small star so I could find it again. Then I put the potatoes on, lowering the heat once the water boiled. The box stayed on its shelf and the model stayed in its folder.

Lumi asked how I wanted dinner. I hadn't decided, I said. She said we could at least decide where the salt was going this time.
