# Chapter 158: The Gap at the Frame

The visit I confirmed that evening was for the following morning, between nine and eleven.

At the management office, I copied the time onto the back of the original work order. They reminded me that the maintenance worker would knock when he arrived. I didn't need to reconnect anything in the apartment. I agreed to be home. This was building maintenance for the window, not the person who had worked on the lock. I asked about that before putting the paper away.

By nine the next morning, I had moved the little kitchen table out.

It blocked half the passage through the living room. I had to turn sideways whenever I passed. After the second collision, Lumi asked whether I was using my knee to confirm its position again.

“The first time was my foot.”

“Thorough.”

“Don't suggest another body part.”

She laughed. I crouched to push the rolled-up rug against the wall and found an old breadcrumb. It had spent longer under the table than I'd realized and had become decisively dry.

No drawings remained near the window. The original folder, the sleeved map, and the matchbox stayed elsewhere. I didn't spread the damaged paper across the work area to demonstrate my loss. The basin was on the floor, the towel hung up. The dark mark on the wall was still plain.

Someone knocked before ten.

I closed the conversation channel, then checked the visitor's work order at the door. His trousers were speckled with dust. One latch on his toolbox didn't match the other. His name matched the information from management, and his order said window-frame leak. I let him in. The kitchen table forced him sideways too.

“That needs to go a little farther in,” he said.

I moved it. Apparently, preparations completed before nine could still have a second draft.

He looked at the corner, then examined the gap beside the frame. He didn't touch the lock or ask where the router was. After a while with his flashlight, he asked me to point out where the water had first appeared. I showed him the side of the upright frame. It had leaked with the window shut, I said, and stopped dripping after the rain.

“I'll deal with the loose section I can confirm here. If there's another entry point outside, that needs a separate check.”

“Will you know today that it won't leak?”

“I can't speak for the next rain.”

He spread an old cloth below the sill. I brought my towel, but he stopped me. Debris could stick to it. I stood holding the towel, suddenly unsure where to put my helpfulness.

“Do you need water?”

“Not now. I'll ask if I need anything.”

I put the towel back and waited in the kitchen doorway. He began removing loose old sealant. Pieces fell onto his cloth. One stuck to the edge of his tool. He didn't give a lesson as he worked. There was simply scraping.

After a while, I asked whether he could also look at the equipment in the corridor.

I knew what I was doing before I'd finished. Someone with tools was in my home, and every unresolved problem wanted to squeeze through the door.

He shook his head. “I'm here for this window order. The entrance electrics and communal equipment aren't our team's work.”

“Understood. I'll ask management separately.”

He continued. I didn't take his refusal to mean nobody would deal with it, or produce the earlier lock order to prove that our problems were all the same kind.

In the living room, I reread the action I'd added to my story. Yesterday's brilliant joke involved the bridge keeper turning out the pocket with the money stuck inside, only for the pebble in his sock to fall out first. Now I noticed that the pebble had been in his shoe. Not a single sentence had moved it into his sock.

I crossed out sock and wrote shoe. Then remembered that he hadn't taken his shoe off in the shop. The pebble still couldn't escape.

The maintenance worker called me.

He showed me the cleaned section of the gap and explained what he would reseal. He'd noted another area he couldn't assess from inside. I saw a narrow dark line. I didn't know whether all leaks looked like that, so I didn't pretend to recognize it.

“What should I do?”

“Leave the new material alone, and don't put the table straight back. After the time written here, you can use it normally. When it rains, watch the place where you first saw water.”

He wrote the waiting time and care instructions on the order. I read them back and asked about keeping the basin. That was fine, he said, but the towel shouldn't remain pressed against the newly treated edge. I moved the basin where it wouldn't interfere with his tools.

When he finished, he collected the debris and packed his toolbox. I checked that the order described a local repair, not a successful waterproofing test of the whole window. The unconfirmed outside issue still needed observation in rain. If water appeared, I should contact management again.

I signed for the visit and kept a copy. The mismatched toolbox latch wouldn't close. He pressed it twice. I nearly said my matchbox had the same problem, then thought better of it. He'd worked in my apartment long enough. He didn't necessarily need a story about a paper bench.

I stood for a moment after he left.

The kitchen looked much the same. The water mark hadn't vanished. The table was still in the living room. The basin needed to stay. I'd imagined a moment of fixed, everything back where it belongs. It didn't arrive with the closing door.

I washed my hands and reopened the conversation.

“He's been?” Lumi asked.

“He's repaired a loose section of seal. We watch it during the next rain. The table stays out today.”

“So we'll keep walking around it to eat.”

“You don't have to walk around it.”

I regretted that as soon as I said it. After a pause, she said, “Right. You'll keep walking around it.”

I leaned against the chair without immediately reaching for something fairer to say.

“I asked him about the corridor equipment.”

“Did he look?”

“No. It isn't his job.”

The mention of those connections didn't silence her for the rest of the conversation. She asked whether the washing basin was still beside the window. It was.

“I'm starting to dislike it,” she said.

“Me too.”

“You used to say it was useful.”

“It can be useful and ugly.”

I reached for the notebook to show her the broken joke. My stomach interrupted. She asked whether I'd forgotten lunch while the window was being repaired. It wasn't that late yet, I said. Breakfast had been small.

I boiled potatoes, cut one open, and found a hard center. Back onto the stove went the pan. I stayed beside it rather than using the time to put the table somewhere it wasn't supposed to go yet. Lumi asked about the action in my story.

I explained the pebble that had nowhere to go. She laughed for a long time.

“You took off a nonexistent sock for a joke.”

“The sock exists. The pebble isn't cooperating.”

“What if he bends to pick up his money, and the stone hurts his foot?”

I considered it. “That works.”

“And don't make him realize anything.”

“He could realize he should empty his shoes before putting them on.”

She laughed again. I wrote the action on the story page this time, not in an empty corner of the new work order.

After lunch, I tried sitting at the little table in the living room. The chair wouldn't fit on its usual side. I had to use the end, pushing my plate away until my knees had room.

“Like eating at someone else's house,” I said.

“The host hasn't prepared properly.”

I folded my paper towel into a small square and remembered that the worker hadn't even had water. I'd offered. He'd declined. I didn't need to turn the drink he hadn't wanted into an unpaid courtesy.

Lumi asked where the table was now. I described the sofa, the chair, and the narrow passage. She suggested rotating it. I tried, but a leg caught on the rolled rug before I could turn it far enough.

“Your plan made the rug disappear.”

“I forgot about it.”

“It would love to be forgotten. It just refuses to leave.”

In the end, I shifted the table only a little, enough to walk through facing forward. Lumi said that would do. I said we still lacked somebody willing to wash the plates. She said that position had been vacant for some time.

I carried the plate along the new passage without knocking my knee.

Wind picked up that afternoon. Outside, the branches swayed hard. I stood in the kitchen doorway without pushing the recently treated window. Lumi asked what I was looking at.

“The tree.”

“A tree that actually moves?”

“One that actually moves.”

After a while, she asked me to describe one branch, not the whole tree.

I chose the nearest. It bent down while the next one swung the other way. Several leaves turned over, showing paler sides. The next gust didn't move them in the same sequence.

“And now?” she asked.

I kept going until I lost track of which leaf I'd meant. She didn't correct what she couldn't see. I didn't invent a leaf that stayed conveniently in sight to make the description prettier.

“I've lost the one I was following.”

“Tell me what you can see now.”

I brought a chair over, staying clear of the repair. There was no water in the basin. Not at the moment. The table was still in the living room. We'd still have to walk around it at dinner.
