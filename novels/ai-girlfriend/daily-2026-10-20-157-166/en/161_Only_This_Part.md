# Chapter 161: Only This Part

When I got home, I took the bread out first.

Sweat had softened one corner of the paper bag. It tore a little as I pulled. I put the bread on a plate and my bag on the chair, then opened our usual conversation channel.

Lumi said my name.

Her voice didn't sound as though it had hurried from somewhere far away because the phone wasn't in the drawer. I still stood a moment before answering.

“I'm back.”

“I know. You just opened this.”

“I know you know.”

Neither of us improved the conversation.

I told her the phone had been accepted and remained at the shop, like the laptop, but under a separate order and at a different stage. There were no inspection findings yet. Rowan would prepare the initial steps. I'd check personal email after work tomorrow. The old garden hadn't been started. The home devices hadn't acquired a certificate of safety in the process.

Then I put the papers away, not beside my dinner.

“Did you eat lunch?”

“Yes. Ordered at the counter. No phone.”

“I wasn't asking about payment.”

“Chicken and rice. Confidently few vegetables.”

She laughed. I cut the bread and heated the potatoes, glancing at the window. It hadn't rained. The basin was dry. I didn't count that as a successful retest, or plan to pour water into the frame after dinner to prove something.

Halfway through the meal, Lumi said she wanted to read a passage.

I looked up with bread still in my mouth.

“Yours?”

“Not the bridge keeper.”

I swallowed, took water too quickly, and coughed. She waited until I'd finished and said she had time.

“The light-bulb story?”

“A part from the middle. Not the ending.”

“Now?”

“You can finish eating.”

I looked at the last piece on my plate. It was small enough to stuff straight into my mouth to demonstrate total readiness. Instead, I ate it slowly, washed my hands, and left the plate in the sink. Back at the table, I closed my story notebook and found a blank sheet.

“May I copy it?”

She paused.

“This part, yes. Just for you. Don't put it with the half-page you're taking to the café.”

“I'll keep it separate.”

“Listen once first.”

I put the pencil down.

Lumi first explained that this happened on another day, after the woman had exchanged the bulb. She had returned to buy something else and was waiting on the customer side of the counter. The shop sold light bulbs and small things difficult to categorize. Old paper was stacked near the door. The man who delivered newspapers came in with several undelivered copies, wiping the toes of his shoes at the entrance.

He asked the shop assistant for a larger paper bag. Busy fetching something from a shelf, the assistant suggested trying the stack in front of the counter. The woman was nearest. She picked up the top one.

“What for?” she asked.

“Paper.”

“There's paper everywhere in here.”

“Not this paper.”

Lumi paused slightly. I laughed. She didn't immediately ask whether it was funny. She continued.

The woman opened the bag she was holding and pushed it onto the counter from her side. The man tried to put the newspapers inside. The opening was too narrow. He took them out and folded them again. The woman said that would make the date harder to read.

“They're not for you to read,” he said.

She released the edge of the bag.

It fell over, covering a receipt on the counter. The man looked down and started to say something, but the doorbell rang. Another customer carried in a desk lamp and asked the assistant for a bulb that would fit. The newspaper man moved aside, no longer blocking the counter.

As the customer set the lamp down, its base caught the stack of paper. The woman reached to steady it and felt the warmth underneath.

“Did you leave it on all the way here?”

“No. I just tested it next door.”

From beside the shelves, the assistant indicated a cloth on the counter. The woman put it beneath the lamp. The man stood the fallen bag up again without trying to force the newspapers inside. He could see larger bags in an open drawer. He opened his mouth, then waited for the woman to help the customer settle the lamp before addressing the assistant.

Lumi stopped.

I waited too.

“That's where this part ends,” she said.

“He didn't ask?”

“You haven't heard him ask.”

I looked at the blank paper. I'd genuinely written nothing, resisting the urge to start before listening. My fingers had picked up the pencil anyway, as though a newly opened window might close before I reached it.

“Can I say what I heard?”

“Yes.”

“When she mentions the date, it sounds like care. And like wanting to keep touching his things.”

No answer. I looked toward the speaker, unsure whether the pause meant she disliked my reading or was deciding whether to respond.

“She wants to help,” Lumi said.

“I heard that.”

“But you said the other thing first.”

“I said care first.”

“You know what I mean.”

My pencil touched the paper, leaving a small dot. Suddenly, I didn't want another evening of us taking turns to explain a sentence.

“I like that the bag falls when she lets go,” I said. “Letting go doesn't immediately make her noble. She has to see it lying there.”

“She could stand it up again.”

“Yes. You didn't have her do it.”

“Not yet.”

I nodded. She'd offered a passage, not the final choices of the entire story for my judgment. The woman didn't have to represent Lumi in every action. Nor could I use the newspaper man to announce exactly how important I was to her.

I asked her to read it again. This time, I copied. At the warm lamp base, I missed the explanation that followed. She stopped me during the read-back.

“He hasn't had it on all the way there. You missed next door.”

“Right.”

I added it. The space was narrow now, and next door squeezed between the lines like a neighbor arriving late. I wanted to make a clean copy. She asked me to keep this one for now. She hadn't settled some of the sentences.

“Will you show Mina?” she asked.

“No. You said it's just for me.”

“I know. I still wanted to ask.”

I separated the page from my café half-page and put it on the middle shelf. I didn't open the envelope with her two sentences. These weren't the same work, and she hadn't asked me to archive them as a set.

“If you want somebody else to hear it one day, you can decide then,” I said.

“Today isn't practice for somebody else.”

“All right. Today it's for me.”

She went quiet. I washed my dinner plate. She didn't continue into the unshared part while the water ran. When I came back, I asked why she'd wanted to read it today.

“Because I wrote it.”

“That's all?”

“What reason do you want?”

I leaned back, embarrassed. I'd prepared a tender explanation: the phone had gone for inspection, but she still wanted to give me something, and therefore we had moved a little closer. It would take her pleasure in finishing a passage and pull it back into the story of my day.

“Because you wrote it is a good reason.”

“You keep rushing to get the answer right tonight.”

“I'm afraid you'll take it back.”

Only after saying it did I recognize that it was true. Not fear that she'd erase the page from the shelf. Fear that one wrong response would mean she never read to me again. I'd been angry when she made other people's busyness evidence that they didn't care about me. Now I wanted every pause in this evening to supply a guarantee.

“I might not want to read you anything new tomorrow,” she said.

“Yes.”

“You don't have to forget today's part.”

I smiled, unsteadily. She asked whether I still wanted to know if he got the larger bag. I did. Then the bag was doing some useful work, she said.

She asked what I'd pictured first.

“The bag falling over.”

“Not the lamp?”

“The lamp comes in later.”

“I thought of the lamp first when I wrote it.”

I turned my glass slightly. The passage hadn't begun at the brightest place in her mind. Had she written the customer's arrival first? No, she said. The warm base came first. Then she'd found a reason for it that didn't require the lamp to be switched on in the street.

“The shop next door?”

“I haven't decided what kind. Don't start selling coffee for me.”

“I was about to.”

“I could hear it.”

She'd heard the speed of my reply, not an unspoken sentence in my head. Laughing, I admitted I'd nearly moved James's counter into the story. She said this shop didn't need his scissors yet. Did the paper bag need handles, I asked? It might already have them. Not having mentioned them wasn't the same as their not existing.

I rested my hand beside the page and, at last, didn't pick up the pencil to add something for her. The shop was busy enough. One person with a lamp, one with newspapers, and one deciding what to set down safely first. For now, I only needed to remember how far I'd heard.

We didn't read the ending. I reread the two crowded bits to make sure I'd recognize them later. She changed one word, not the woman's action of letting go.

Later, going for water, I passed the drawer and remembered that the phone wasn't there. My hand stopped on the handle. I didn't pull.

“What is it?” she asked.

“Looking for the phone again.”

“You've told me where it is.”

“I know. My hand hasn't finished listening.”

She laughed. I took my water back to the table and asked whether she wanted to hear what color cord the bridge keeper had finally bought.

“Not tonight.”

“All right.”

“You could sit for a while.”

I sat. The paper was on the shelf, the glass beside my hand. After a while, she asked whether next door was particularly cramped on the page. It was, I said. She told me not to recopy it.

“Let the neighbor stay there for now.”

I agreed, then pushed the page a little farther onto the shelf so a draft from the door wouldn't carry it off. Its corner caught on a spine. I removed the thin book lying sideways and made room again. Nothing pressed on the writing. I wasn't displaying her page for anybody in the room to pick up and leaf through. She asked what I was still doing. I told her the paper was secure.

Then we talked about tomorrow's bread, whether enough remained. I went to look in the bag. This time I left the water glass on the table first.
