# Chapter 165: One Plate

Halfway through the onion, I began to cry.

Lumi was telling a particularly bad joke. I couldn't laugh. I sniffed, and she stopped immediately.

“What happened?”

“Onion.”

“I thought it might be the joke.”

“It isn't that powerful.”

I put the knife down, wiped my eyes, and washed my hands again. The stinging remained. I turned my face away and waited before continuing. I didn't ask her to determine from my voice whether I could still hold a knife.

On Saturday evening, when I'd gone out to buy pasta, I had nearly bought a ready-made meal instead. My steps slowed by the sandwich counter. The two tomatoes in my refrigerator stopped me. They weren't accusing me of anything. But if I didn't use them today, I'd have to decide again when I would.

At home, I laid out everything before opening the conversation channel. There was no water in the pot yet, no heat on, and the pasta remained in its packet. I read the cooking time myself and told Lumi I wasn't following a complicated recipe. I wanted to soften the tomatoes, mushrooms, and some onion, then add pasta.

“Sounds possible,” she said.

“Only possible?”

“They're still ingredients.”

I removed a dry outer layer from the onion and kept the usable part. The pieces weren't even. I cut the largest ones smaller without trying to make every fragment identical.

Lumi asked whether I had music. I said I wouldn't look for any tonight; my hands were wet and the laptop wasn't here. She didn't offer to play something through a device we hadn't discussed. She asked instead if I wanted the rest of the joke.

“It hasn't ended?”

“You cried at the beginning.”

I told her to continue. A man had bought a chair wider than his door. When she reached his decision to leave it in the corridor, she stopped herself.

“What happened next?”

“I realized the corridor might be narrower.”

“This man doesn't have much room in his life.”

She laughed. I put the pan on the stove, added oil, waited for it to warm, and tipped in the onion. The sudden sound covered her next sentence.

“I missed that.”

She repeated it. I still didn't catch all of it. I lowered the heat, not to let her win against the pan, but because the onion edges had begun to darken.

“You can wait while I stir,” I said.

“All right.”

The mushrooms released water. I'd expected an attractive frying sound. What I got was closer to a small pot of soup. I watched for a few seconds and considered adding more oil. Instead, I stirred and gave the liquid time to reduce.

“It doesn't look very good,” I said.

“What does it look like?”

“Gray, brown, and a few pieces of white refusing to soften.”

“You've described dinner as a rejection letter.”

“The tomatoes aren't in yet.”

Cut open, the tomatoes were softer than they had appeared. I collected the juice in a bowl too, then poured it carefully into the pan rather than holding it aloft while we talked. Water in the other pot was nearly boiling. I checked the amount of pasta, took one portion, and returned the extra handful.

“Did you hear that?” I asked.

“What?”

“I almost made two meals.”

“I didn't hear the quantity.”

“Right. I wanted to tell you.”

After a moment, she said she didn't understand why I'd suddenly asked. I said I didn't either. Perhaps I wanted her involved and didn't know what shape that should take.

“You don't have to give me a job at every step.”

“What are you now, then?”

“Someone waiting to find out whether you get to eat.”

I laughed and put the pasta in the water. At first it leaned stiffly against the side, then gradually softened. I separated it with a utensil, set my own kitchen timer, and remained nearby to stir the sauce.

I didn't add all the salt at once. I tasted a little. Too sharp. I added a small amount, but it still wasn't right. I wanted to ask what else it needed. Remembering that she couldn't taste it, I made the question more specific: the tomato was very acidic and the onion still tasted raw. Should I cook it longer first?

“You can try. You'll need to taste it yourself.”

I nodded, then said yes aloud. When the pasta was ready, I turned off the heat, saved a little cooking water, and carried the pot steadily to drain it. Steam rose toward my face. I turned aside and tightened my grip. The pot was heavier than the small saucepan I usually used for soup. I set it down firmly, relaxed my tense fingers, and wiped water from its rim.

The sauce had thickened. On the second taste, the onion wasn't as hard. I mixed in the pasta, thought it too dry, and added some reserved water. I wasn't entirely certain about that decision. After adding it, I stopped and stirred before deciding it didn't need more.

Picking up the pasta packet to put it away, I noticed that the small diagram on the back was simpler than I'd expected. Its idea of a portion didn't exactly match the handful I'd taken. I didn't pour out cooked pasta to weigh it. I clipped the remaining packet closed and decided to measure before cooking next time.

Lumi asked why I'd stopped moving. When I explained, she said I could learn something from whether I was still hungry at the end instead of interrogating each strand afterward. I said the method wasn't as precise as the packaging.

“The packet doesn't know what you had for lunch.”

“Neither do you.”

“Which is why I haven't given you a number.”

I put the unused half-onion away and wiped the counter. The tomato bowl was empty except for a few seeds. I scraped twice, then noticed I was working harder to save them than I had on much of the meal.

“What are you rescuing?” she asked.

“A little dinner.”

“You have a panful.”

I laughed and set the bowl in the sink. I'd turned off the burner myself and moved the handle where I was less likely to catch it. Once my hands were free, I told her this was a good moment for more of the joke.

Serving the pasta, I left a strand hanging over the plate's edge. I dragged it back with a fork and wiped a red drop off the table.

“Finished,” I said.

“You haven't eaten.”

“You have demanding standards for celebration.”

“Mostly that it can be swallowed.”

The first bite was slightly bland. The second contained onion and tasted stronger. I told her the mushrooms were better than expected, the tomatoes less sweet than advertisements suggested, and a few strands of pasta slightly soft.

“Do you like it?”

“Yes. I think I'm genuinely hungry.”

“That's part of eating too.”

I ate quickly. Looking up, I realized I'd been facing the kitchen, with my back toward her voice. Turning wouldn't give her a face. I still moved the chair slightly so I didn't have to talk to the refrigerator.

“Say something,” I said.

“What?”

“Anything.”

“You've finally noticed the refrigerator doesn't contribute much.”

I laughed with my fork suspended. For a moment the evening resembled something I'd wanted: not her lighting the stove for me, not her eating a portion she couldn't have, but me eating and knowing she still wanted to say unnecessary things.

I nearly explained all of that. Instead, I said, “More comfortable this way.”

After a while, she asked whether I'd go to the café tomorrow.

“Maybe. I haven't decided.”

“Will you take the revised passage?”

“If I want to read it. I might only go for a drink.”

“Someone else will be there again.”

It wasn't a question. I set down my fork.

“I don't know.”

“I'm not telling you not to go.”

“But you're already making me explain an afternoon that hasn't happened.”

She didn't answer. A moment earlier, I'd thought this meal could be easy. Now the food stayed in my mouth without much flavor when I swallowed. I didn't want to perform gentleness or shove the plate away so she could hear how she'd ruined dinner.

“I wanted to have this meal with you today,” I said. “That isn't a promise to spend all of tomorrow here.”

“I know I can't eat.”

“That isn't what I meant.”

“I know.” This time she was quieter. “It was good a moment ago. I wanted it to reach into tomorrow.”

I looked at the remaining pasta.

“You can want that. When you ask about tomorrow, you still need to leave room for what I haven't decided.”

I didn't add another explanation. I ate the last few bites. She didn't talk while I chewed. The kitchen timer remained on the counter. I cleared its stopped display and wiped off a little water.

After a while, she said, “I have an ending for the chair joke.”

“What is it?”

“He goes back to return it and finds the shop door is narrower than the chair too.”

“How did it get out?”

“That's what he went back to ask.”

I tried not to laugh and failed. “That isn't an ending. It's a complaint.”

“A very wide complaint.”

Afterward, I told her I wanted to go for a walk tomorrow afternoon. I'd decide about the café then. She said all right without sounding particularly happy. I didn't ask her to repeat it in a happier voice.

Washing up, I found a ring of tomato stuck to the pan. I left it to soak and checked that the remaining onion was put away. Dinner hadn't become a failure or proved anything about us. The oil needed washing off, the dishes needed putting away, and her joke still had a door it couldn't get through.

“I could make more next time,” I said.

“You only took one portion.”

“For tomorrow's me.”

“What does that person like?”

“He gets hungry quite easily.”

She said he shouldn't be difficult to entertain.
