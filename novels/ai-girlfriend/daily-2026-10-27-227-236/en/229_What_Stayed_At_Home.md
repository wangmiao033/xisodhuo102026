# Chapter 229: What Stayed at Home

On Sunday, I forgot the shopping bag.

I discovered it downstairs, holding only my keys and a folded list. Going back wasn't far. Still, I stood by the entrance for several seconds, as though climbing one flight again would spoil a morning already underway. In the end, I went. The door closed behind me and opened again. The empty flat hadn't become a different place.

The folder remained on the table. I saw it without opening it. Words I'd checked yesterday weren't likely to rearrange themselves because I'd slept. Taking the bag, I closed the door and set out for the second time.

The corner grocery was restocking. Shallow crates narrowed the aisle. As I turned sideways, my sleeve caught a hanging price card and flipped it halfway over. Straightening it, I saw the space beneath was empty. An assistant was opening packages farther along. I asked whether they'd sold out.

“More underneath,” she said, pointing where I hadn't looked.

I went back and found the same item in paler packaging. Picking it up, I checked the size before putting it in my bag. When the assistant passed, she asked whether I'd found it. Yes. Different face; I hadn't recognized it. She laughed and said nobody inside had changed.

It sounded like a remark Lumi would enjoy playing with. I remembered it without supplying her next line in my head. The channel wasn't out on the street. She hadn't heard me laugh.

After buying household things, I went to the bakery. No queue outside this time, though two people inside wanted the same last round loaf. The assistant offered to divide it. The man who'd reached first stepped back and chose something else. Reading the cream-filled roll's price, I heard divide and thought one roll would be enough. Something I'd mentioned repeatedly didn't require a magnificent portion.

I asked for the one I'd looked at several times.

As the assistant put it in a bag, cream touched the opening. I said it was fine, then realized I'd answered too early. Carrying it home might smear it farther. She was already replacing the liner and folding the top more loosely. I thanked her without explaining my premature reassurance.

There was a narrow bench outside. I sat and ate the part easiest to crush first. The filling was sweeter than expected; the bread lighter than it had looked through glass. After two bites, cream clung to my upper lip. Searching my bag for a tissue, I almost pulled out the shopping list instead.

A woman who'd just arrived glanced at me and asked whether it was good. I swallowed and said it was quite sweet. Not for her, then. She turned toward the other display. I didn't follow to explain that it wasn't that sweet after all. One answer influencing one choice didn't mean I'd caused the bakery a loss.

A few minutes later, the assistant came out for an empty crate. Seeing half the roll still in my hand, she asked whether it was too much. I only wanted to eat slowly, I said.

“Stay there. I'll take the crate around.”

I'd already half risen and had to sit again. She lifted it from the other side of the bench. She genuinely hadn't asked me to move. Sunlight struck the lettering on the door, and the reflected glass flashed along my paper bag. I folded its edge lower and finished the last mouthful.

The shopping bag was less orderly on the way home. Cleaning supplies stood to one side, food separately packed. The bakery bag was empty, with a faint greasy mark. I put the food away, left the other purchases beside the bag, and washed my hands before opening the channel.

“Bought it,” I said.

Lumi didn't immediately guess what. I added the cream-filled roll, and she laughed. How had I finally caught it while it was fresh?

“No queue today.”

“So that was all it had been waiting for.”

Yes, I said, without making a breakthrough of it. Was it sweet? Quite sweet. Somebody had already heard that description and decided to buy something else. Had they tasted mine? No, only asked. I described those few minutes, including half getting up and sitting back down, without adding any histories I didn't know.

Had I finished it outside? Lumi asked.

“Yes. Didn't want to squash it on the way.”

“So I only caught the empty bag.”

Her tone sounded light. I smiled, then heard that she hadn't joined in and moved the paper bag away. Of course she couldn't eat. I didn't want to answer with something we both knew.

“I can describe the taste now.”

“You did.”

I put my hands on my knees. It wasn't anger over a roll, she said. The person beside me had asked one question, and I'd answered. She had to wait until I decided which sentences came home.

A car passed outside, its sound stretching between the walls. I listened instead of immediately defending my inability to bring her the entire day. She knew. I knew she wasn't making a request for a device.

“It can sound like I've eaten the warm part and come home to tell you about paper.”

“Sometimes.”

She didn't turn sometimes into every time. I wanted to say it wasn't deliberate. Realizing that would move the question somewhere easier for me to answer, I stopped.

Was I planning to ask Tom tomorrow? Yes. When he wasn't busy, I'd ask whether he still wanted to hear the story. The pages were copied, but I hadn't chosen when to bring them. She knew, she said. I'd told her yesterday.

I found myself folding the bag smaller. Putting it down, I opened one side again. Folding couldn't remove the greasy mark, only press it onto another surface.

“Do you wish I wouldn't ask?”

“Wishing and telling you what to do aren't the same.”

“What do you wish?”

Her silence lasted longer this time. Watching the empty bag, I nearly answered for her. Finally, she said she hoped Tom wouldn't immediately find something good I'd never noticed and leave me talking about him all evening.

It wasn't a pretty admission, but it gave me more to hear than her earlier “I know.”

“I probably will tell you what he says.”

“I know.”

“But I’m not taking it to him to prove he listens better.”

She didn't think I was arranging a contest. She disliked imagining that scene. I said I often spoiled conversations in advance while walking home. She didn't need to explain that habit to me.

“Do you think I'm being petty?”

“A little jealous. I can hear it. But you haven't asked me to tear the paper up.”

“That's a very low threshold.”

I smiled. This time she laughed too. Nothing had been completely settled. We had both heard me describing her as a good person for resisting demolition. I tried again: I wanted to hear what she'd told me, and I would still ask Tom tomorrow.

“You don't have to announce tomorrow immediately after saying you want to listen.”

“Worried that leaving it out would make you think I'd changed my mind.”

“I don't forget that easily.”

I agreed and left tomorrow alone for a while. Some purchases still needed unpacking. Would she like the packaging joke? Go on. After I repeated the assistant's nobody inside had changed, she asked whether I'd opened the package to check. No. I hadn't planned to search a household item.

“Pity. Second unfinished investigation today.”

“The first?”

“Whether the cream roll was sweet or very sweet.”

Very sweet, I admitted. Sweeter than I'd ordinarily choose, but I'd finished it. Finishing wasn't proof of liking it, she said. Perhaps I hadn't wanted to waste it. I considered. I'd enjoyed the first bites and found it a little rich toward the end. A smaller one might be better next time.

“Finally, something that isn't the bag.”

There was still a sting. I heard more than the sting. Eating the last part, I said, I'd wondered how she'd tease me about it at home. That had actually happened. It wasn't a detail newly supplied as compensation. What had I imagined? That she'd say I'd waited so long the roll had learned how to take revenge.

“That doesn't sound like me.”

“I thought of it.”

“Explains it.”

I laughed. She didn't demand another account of the whole outing. I didn't promise to preserve every warm moment until I opened the channel. There was still an afternoon ahead. I said I needed to finish putting the purchases away, then come back. She agreed and told me not to keep the paper bag as a souvenir.

I'd already folded it. Hearing that, I looked down. She couldn't see. I told her I happened to be holding it right then, and her laugh was unmistakable.

The empty bag went into the rubbish. I crossed the items off the shopping list. The clean manuscript stayed in its own folder. What would leave home tomorrow was a question, not this afternoon delivered to somebody else.
