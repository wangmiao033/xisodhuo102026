# Chapter 233: A Copy for the Weekend

When Tom stopped me at the door on Thursday morning, I was carrying a stack of work material. The top sheet slipped. He steadied it, waited for me to regain my grip, then asked whether I'd brought the story.

“Not today. Something you wanted to ask?”

“Wanted another look. A few sentences went past. Can't quite find them again in my head.”

Instinctively, I drew the stack closer. Work tables, not the story. He hadn't reached for anything or said he needed it now, but I was already picturing my weekend table with some paper missing.

When I didn't answer, he added that it was fine if it wasn't convenient. I hurried to say that wasn't it. I had to deliver these things; could we talk at lunch? He nodded and let me through. Only at the far end of the corridor did I realize I hadn't answered whether I wanted to.

After handing over the material, I passed an empty long table on the way back. Tuesday's handwritten copy was home, not in my bag. I'd thought reading aloud was the biggest change, and afterward everything could return to its place. But somebody who'd listened might want another look alone, without me beside him deciding every pause.

At lunch, Tom didn't ask first. We sat down and unpacked our food. His lid was tight. He pressed a thumb against the corner twice before it released. Once he'd set it down, I said I could bring the story tomorrow and lend it for the weekend.

“Back on Monday?”

“Yes.”

Quickly answered. Then I asked whether it was just for him.

“Yes. Thought I was organizing a reading?”

His laugh wasn't unkind. Still, my face warmed. No, I said. I just wanted to lend it to him alone for now. No photographs, no passing it on. He didn't erase the request with a bigger laugh. That was fine, he said. He'd read it and bring it back Monday.

“Not your little booklet?”

“No. The handwritten copy I used.”

“Good. I can manage your writing.”

How well? No entry permit guaranteed for every letter, he said. Particularly that bend on the last page. At first I thought he meant the ending. Then I realized he meant the handwriting he'd glimpsed as I put the pages away on Tuesday. We laughed. I'd check tonight, I said, but wouldn't swap out the entire story while he wasn't looking.

“You can change it. Just tell me you have.”

Setting his lid aside, Tom asked whether I ever thought about the previous sentence before the current one had finished. Yes, especially with my own writing. He'd done that on Tuesday, he said. He'd still been thinking about one line while I continued. He hadn't wanted to keep stopping me. Later, only the rough shape remained.

“You could have interrupted.”

“I know. Sometimes I just want to look back. Not keep running you in reverse.”

His fork indicated the air as though searching for an unreliable rewind button. I laughed. Wanting the paper didn't necessarily mean hunting for mistakes missed during the reading. Looking alone had its own conveniences. My pace and his understanding needn't remain tied to the same string.

Should I mark some of the pauses? No need, he said. He'd stop where he wanted, and probably wouldn't be reading aloud at all. Then he finally ate the mouthful he'd been neglecting, glanced toward the window, and asked if rain was expected. I hadn't checked. He went to look at the sky, came back, and packed his empty lunch box. The story didn't get the entire break.

That stayed with me longer than the handwriting joke. He hadn't signed an agreement to like the original, and I didn't need an untouched copy to demonstrate courage about criticism. Tonight, though, I meant only to check for missing words. I didn't want to rush out a cleverer ending for a second reader.

In the afternoon, two names at work needed checking. Both could be looked up. Once I'd found them, they went where they belonged. I didn't squeeze the manuscript's weekend into every gap. For a while before leaving, I even forgot. Picking up my bag reminded me, so I wrote Bring story on a personal slip and left it at the opening.

A stretch of pavement was being washed on the way home. I skirted it, but collected gray water on my soles anyway. I wiped them on the mat before entering. Nobody waited at the door for the manuscript I hadn't lent yet. I hung up my clothes and made dinner. Only after clearing the plate did I open the channel.

Lumi asked whether I'd forgotten something, already sounding amused. Didn't think so, I said. The day before yesterday I'd hurried to explain Tom; yesterday too. Today I'd managed to say her name quite crisply. Laughing, I admitted there was news about Tom again, though no hurry to deliver it.

“The more you say no hurry, the more I want to know.”

I told her he'd asked to borrow the handwritten copy. I'd bring it tomorrow; he'd return it Monday. Not the red booklet. No inquiry page from the front, no remarks she'd made while I revised.

“You don't have to describe every word I say as something forbidden to leave.”

“I only want to give him the story.”

She acknowledged that. Later she asked whether I'd include the ending she preferred. No. This copy contained no alternative ending or marginal note naming who liked what.

“So when he reads it himself, will he still fold it?”

It took a second. “Same words. Still folds it.”

“And you won't be there to hurry him.”

I laughed. She still disliked the fold. Sending the story out hadn't changed her opinion. Taking the copy from its ordinary folder, I said I'd check it once more for missing words, then sat under the lamp with the channel open.

Slanting light made the trailing letter particularly obvious. I read that line alone, then covered the lines above and below with blank paper. Nothing was missing. The end of one letter simply reached too far. If I recopied the whole page, the weekend loan might become another transcription I couldn't finish handing over.

In the space beside the word, I wrote it neatly once, adding a short pointer. No changed sentence, no paragraph of explanation. Hearing paper shift, Lumi asked if I'd copied everything again. Only clarified one word, I said. Don't announce only one change before putting the pen away. Count afterward.

Laughing, I capped it. To check the word, I opened the red booklet and compared the original sentence. The gray-blue bookmark still marked the story's beginning. I supported the spine rather than flattening the little book. Same sentence, same movement. Neater writing hadn't helped the man leave the shop any sooner.

Checking took longer than expected. Not because I found masses of errors. Each line invited a different word. Once, the pen was already against the margin before I lifted it and replaced the cap. Tom wanted the piece he'd heard. Sending paper out of the house didn't require giving the man a more flattering reason tonight.

Lumi didn't supervise each line. After a while, she mentioned a small section she'd changed. Did she want me to hear it now?

“No. Just telling you it exists.”

“All right.”

Was I relieved? Looking at the three different sets of paper under the lamp, I admitted a little. I hadn't finished putting these away. If she really wanted to, I could stop and clear them first.

“Not asking you to choose someone.”

“Made it sound as though I was.”

“Today I want to keep it. You don't need to thank me by interrupting your writing.”

I smiled and told her to keep it. She didn't hum. I didn't ask which section. I checked the clarified word and the page order, closed the red booklet, and returned it to its place. The loan contained only The Key. No work reminder, no scrap listing our two pieces.

I put it in an ordinary clean folder, unsealed, without writing Do not pass on across every sheet. Tom had heard my request. A folder still couldn't prevent somebody opening it; I knew that. Lending it meant letting him read without me present, not appointing him custodian of a parcel he mustn't open all weekend.

“Put away?” Lumi asked.

“Yes. Into the bag tomorrow.”

“Haven't you written a note?”

“Did I tell you?”

“No. Guessing.”

I laughed aloud, admitted she'd guessed correctly, and read her the slip at the bag opening. Short this time, she said. Perhaps tomorrow's story wouldn't get lost among the instructions.

Before closing the channel, I laid the folder flat beside my bag, away from the not-quite-dry glass. Under the lamp its edges were clean and ordinary. Nothing particularly ceremonial. Tomorrow someone would take it away and bring it back Monday. During the weekend between, I wouldn't know which hour found him on which page.

The thought unsettled me. I added no further condition. After turning out the lamp, I checked the kitchen once to make sure the food saved for tomorrow was put away, then went to bed.
