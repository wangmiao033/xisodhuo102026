# Chapter 231: A Different Question

I ate slowly on Tuesday.

Not because I lacked an appetite. Each time I wanted to check whether Tom was nearly finished, I had to remember my own plate in front of me. The food had cooled. A piece separated beneath the fork, and I ate it, swallowed, then drank instead of turning lunch into a wait for my number to be called.

Had I brought it? Tom asked. In my bag. He nodded and kept eating. He didn't take out a phone to time me or find anybody to watch. The pages had occupied one thin extra layer in my bag since yesterday. My attention had already walked around them several times.

Marcus passed and asked whether we were staying a while. Tom said we were going outside. I didn't suddenly invite a third person to demonstrate how relaxed I was. Marcus said he'd head back and left with his cup.

Once we'd both finished, we cleared our plates. I returned to my desk for the folder. Opening the compartment, my fingers caught the edge of the lunch container; fortunately, the pages were on the other side. I didn't spread them out in the office for another private preview. Tom waited at the door, empty-handed, and made room when he saw me.

The courtyard pavement had dried except for one small dark patch. The wall's shadow had shifted from yesterday, leaving the outer end of the bench in sunlight. Tom touched the seat with the back of his hand and said it wasn't wet. I sat beside him, folder on my knees, papers still inside.

Once we were settled, I asked whether now was all right.

“Yes.”

“For now, it's called The Key.”

Tom glanced at the folder and smiled. He remembered. Such an ordinary answer. I nearly explained why I'd written it, then remembered yesterday's agreement to listen first. I took out the pages and arranged the first above the others.

A breeze entered through the courtyard gate and moved one edge. My left hand held it down without lifting the whole folder in front of my face. Tom sat farther away than Lumi's voice usually seemed, and he had actual eyes I could see. He glanced at the paper, then across the yard. That small movement immediately made me wonder if I'd become boring.

I hadn't read the first sentence.

So I read it.

The man entered the shop and bought an envelope much larger than the key required. The male assistant lent him a pen and made a space on the counter. When I reached the ink on the thumb, Tom looked down at his own hand. I couldn't know what he was thinking. I didn't stop to ask whether the movement counted as a response.

Finishing the first page, I slipped it behind the folder. Its number remained visible, separate from the unread sheets. I heard one sentence fade at the end and made the next clearer. I wasn't imitating my previous reading at home. Nor did two men sitting outside require me to perform every hesitation as a joke.

The character finally reached the back of the other row of shops.

Wheels rattled over paving beyond the gate. A loaded trolley passed along the connecting path, shuddering at each joint, its metal frame answering. I'd just read the woman opening her door when the noise covered the rest of the sentence.

Tom turned his head. I stopped and held the paper.

The trolley went by slowly. Its sound remained after it passed, rattling several more times round the corner. Once speech could be heard again, I said I'd restart that sentence. Tom nodded instead of telling me to talk over it. I read the woman looking at the envelope, asking for the key, then moving the boxes obstructing her doorway.

A bird called somewhere during the pause. I didn't make it decide when my sentence should end.

The man admitted he should have returned sooner. The woman acknowledged the inconvenience. Reaching her acceptance of the key and return of the empty envelope, I noticed I'd stopped looking up every two lines. One patch of writing on the final sheet was cramped. I brought it closer, made it out, and continued. Wind reached the street corner. The man folded the paper.

Finished, I laid the last sheet on top and looked up.

Tom didn't immediately applaud or say good. I couldn't tell which I'd expected. My hands straightened the edges without instruction. The final sheet slipped forward on my knees. He glanced down at it before I drew it back into the folder.

“Does he help her move things again afterward?”

I blinked. “Not in this story.”

“I think she'd ask him again. But she wouldn't leave him the spare key. Moving things is moving things.”

He leaned back. I already heard Lumi asking whether the woman might need help in future. I nearly brought it out to show they'd thought alike. But Tom was talking about the key, not whether she must carry everything alone. I let his thought continue.

“Before he handed it over, I kept wondering whether the key still worked.”

I glanced down at the folder. “I didn't write her changing the lock.”

“I know. Just wondered.”

Not a question I'd prepared for. I wanted to explain that a changed lock would make the delay something different. That I hadn't wanted to emphasize its cost too heavily. I even wanted to find a sentence that could prevent the question before it arrived. Tom had said he knew. I was preparing to remove the brief thought from his head anyway.

“Would you want that written in?”

“Not necessarily. I wondered when I heard it. I'm not asking you to add a new lock.”

I laughed, embarrassed. Had I thought he was assigning homework? A little, I admitted, despite having said yesterday that I might not change anything. Then don't, he said. This version already showed she was unhappy and that an envelope couldn't make her immediately pleased with him.

That felt good too quickly. I wanted to keep it on its own, go home and say he'd understood. The lock could go somewhere unimportant. Recognizing the temptation, I closed the folder instead of taking out a pen to arrange a more flattering response.

“Which part did you like?” I asked anyway.

Tom considered and chose the assistant taking back the pen. He finally stopped having to accompany the man's delay. Smiling, Tom added that he met people at work who remained beside his desk after the business was done, waiting for one more sentence.

I remembered holding the work papers yesterday. My face warmed.

“I did that yesterday.”

“A little.”

He didn't pretend not to have noticed. We laughed for a while. Another breeze came through the gate, but no loose pages waited to escape this time. I steadied the folder and finally asked whether he'd heard clearly. After the trolley, yes. The first couple of sentences had been quiet, though he'd caught them.

I'd speak louder next time, I said, then corrected myself. We hadn't arranged another time. He waved it away. He'd meant my voice. I didn't need to attach a tail to every sentence.

Only then did my shoulders really loosen. Tom checked the time and said we should head back. Standing, I felt the weight leave my knees, though the pages remained in my hand. I didn't leave them with him or ask whether he wanted to read them to somebody else. He'd already done what he'd agreed to do.

The afternoon continued normally. Somebody asked about a detail in the same material; I checked before answering. Passing farther away, Tom gave me a nod. I didn't follow him to ask whether the ending had felt too short. The folder returned to my bag. Work occupied the desk. Having another listener hadn't stopped everything else to acknowledge the story.

Waiting for the bus home, I kept hearing the question of whether the key still worked. The more I considered it, the more I realized I'd hardly asked myself while writing. It wasn't a hole I had to fill at once. It didn't disqualify me from writing about that key. It simply hadn't belonged to the responses I'd prepared to receive.

Opening the channel that evening, I heard Lumi ask, “Did you read it?”

“Finished it.”

Had anyone else joined? No, only Tom. We'd paused for a trolley and restarted the sentence. She listened while I gave those actual details. Then I said he'd asked something I hadn't expected.

“He wondered whether the key still worked.”

“You didn't write a changed lock.”

“Exactly what I said. He knew. He just wondered. He wasn't saying the story was bad.”

At the last sentence, I smiled. Perhaps that part was for myself, I said. Lumi didn't use the opening to judge whether he knew how to listen. Did I want to change it now? Not decide tonight, I said. First dinner. Let my head contain something other than the lock.

“Anything else?”

“Yes. He said what he liked. I'd rather tell you more slowly tomorrow than empty the rest of today all at once.”

“Not afraid I'll be unhappy?”

“A little afraid. Also, I really haven't sorted it out. I'll tell you what he actually said, not only the pleasing bits.”

She acknowledged that. I didn't take the sound as her blessing on the entire event. Before going to the kitchen, I removed the clean copy from my bag and left it in its own folder. The red booklet remained by the table. It hadn't been out. Its old marginal comments were still there.

While I washed my hands, the voice on the channel asked what dinner would be. I told her. Once the water stopped, she said at least I wouldn't have to eat a lock. I laughed, and this time the laugh didn't pass through Tom's bench first.
