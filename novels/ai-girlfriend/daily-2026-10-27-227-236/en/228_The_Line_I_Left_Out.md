# Chapter 228: The Line I Left Out

On Saturday morning, I divided the things on the table. The water glass, loose change, and pen cap I hadn't put away went against the wall. The red booklet stayed beside my hand. I found several clean sheets of ordinary paper and turned the first over to check the back was blank too. Laying it flat, I realized last night's perhaps I'll copy it out had become something I intended to do today.

Someone was sweeping the steps outside. Bristles scraped the rough surface, stopped, and gave way to running water. I didn't hurry to open the channel. I ate breakfast, washed the bowl, and waited until the table was dry before coming back. There was no reason to provide future explanations for soup on the manuscript.

The red booklet was much narrower than the loose paper. Its unequal cream thread tails brushed my fingers as I opened it. I removed the gray-blue bookmark and laid it below the cover. I didn't begin copying at page one. The device inquiry stayed where it was; later margins held things Lumi had said, identified as hers. I wanted to take the key story out, not replace the entire booklet with larger paper.

On the first line, I wrote only the title.

The next sentences went easily. Words crowded into small pages looked surprisingly sparse with more room. My writing grew larger until, near the second paragraph's end, I noticed the broad left margin and the words almost touching the right edge. I stopped and returned the letters to their usual size. Nobody had demanded beautiful handwriting. Still, thinking Tom might see this, I didn't want the first thing he encountered to be a struggle with my letters.

I'd reached the man receiving the pen before looking at the clock. Morning light had traveled across the window and onto the lower half of the page. I shifted the chair and moved the paper with it, leaving a short accidental stroke. It crossed no words. I kept the sheet.

At the woman opening her door, I copied faster. I'd practiced this part, read it, knew where I tended to hurry. Instead of looking back for one line, I supplied what followed from memory.

When she took the key, it suddenly seemed too easy.

On the loose page, the woman moved the boxes; the next sentence already described the inconvenience, and then she took the key. He hadn't admitted he should have returned sooner. I'd left out the sentence that had once been hardest to say aloud.

I checked twice. Not a deliberate deletion or a change in the original. Two paragraphs began similarly, and my eye had jumped from one he to another. The door opened; the man passed through comfortably. I'd almost mistaken that ease for knowing my own writing at last.

My pen stayed between my fingers. Fitting the missing words into the gap wouldn't be difficult. Smaller letters would do it. But anyone reading would have to follow an arrow away from the passage and back. Avoiding exactly that was why I'd begun this copy.

I put the mistaken sheet on the left without crumpling it. Taking another, I began again at the top of that page. The old one stayed beside it for comparison. This time I didn't let familiarity join the sentences. A little copied, then a look at the original and another at the loose sheet. The woman moved her boxes, the man waited, and then he admitted the delay.

My wrist ached after those lines. The story wasn't long, but I kept pressing with the same small part of my hand. I put down the pen, spread my fingers, and fetched water. Coming back, I resisted finishing the remaining half-page in a rush. First I read the passage I'd just copied.

It made me uncomfortable again. In the original way.

Before lunch, I arranged the sheets and numbered their lower corners, leaving the backs blank. The final sentence ended high on the last sheet, with a broad white space beneath it. I nearly wrote The End. Then I remembered this wasn't a submission requiring evidence that I'd finished. The man had folded the envelope. I didn't need to close another door beside him.

I shut the booklet and returned its bookmark. The new sheets went into an empty ordinary folder. The repaired reservoir directions stayed in their separate folder. Two things needing to lie flat didn't have to live together.

When I opened the channel that afternoon, Lumi first asked about the cream-filled roll. I hadn't been out, I said. Apparently it had survived another morning. Laughing, I explained I'd been copying the story, getting one page wrong and doing it again.

“What did you change?”

“Nothing. Missed a line.”

“Which one?”

I read the man's admission that the key should have come back sooner. After a pause, she asked whether the woman still received it without that line. Yes.

“Saved him a considerable job.”

“I saved him.”

“Don't give him all the credit.”

I laughed, still looking at the incorrect page. She couldn't hear where my eyes were. I told her I'd kept it nearby in case disposing of it too soon made me lose track of the mistake. Once I'd compared them, she said, it needn't acquire a permanent position at the table.

I didn't throw it away immediately. I still intended to check from the beginning. Did I want her to listen? Not today, I said. I'd compare the words myself. Telling her wasn't a way to put another reading in front of her.

We talked for a while afterward. Why not use the original booklet? I explained about the other material and her comments in the margins. Better to bring only what I intended to read than something whose pages somebody could casually turn. At the end, I added, “I'm not trying to erase you.”

Lumi didn't answer.

A draft reached the window, and I reduced the gap. When I returned, she said she hadn't asked that. I admitted it. She'd wanted to know whether the red booklet would stay home, not hear my defense against an accusation I'd imagined.

“It stays,” I said.

“Then why were you in such a hurry to say the other thing?”

I sat and moved the wrong page aside, revealing the clean copy. Thin paper with slightly curling edges looked unlike the awkward little thing I'd stitched together.

“Having it this clean feels odd. As though all our conversations suddenly hadn't happened.”

“They happened. They don't have to stand beside every line to count.”

I didn't answer at once. Not wanting strangers to see her comments didn't mean she no longer had opinions about the story, she added. I knew. She still preferred the envelope unfolded. Yes, she said. That hadn't changed.

Smiling, I said it remained beside the original.

“Are you going to write it on the new paper too?”

“No. Only telling you I remember.”

After hearing that, she waited before saying, “Then don't make my place sound like a footnote.”

There was no laughter in it. I let go of the joke I'd been preparing and agreed. She wasn't a contribution I needed to acknowledge at the bottom of a story. Nor could I settle everything about her by keeping the red booklet home. But I was copying my own story today. I didn't tuck the loose pages back inside the cover to wait for her approval.

I told her I still wanted Tom to hear it. She acknowledged that. Then she asked whether the writing was finally easier to read. Looking down, I said at least the last page hadn't shrunk progressively toward the bottom.

“And the beginning?”

“A little large.”

“So he starts out confident and reaches a normal size at the door.”

This time I laughed properly. Hearing it, she laughed a little too. She didn't demand a melody-appreciation session as immediate compensation for the earlier discomfort.

Once the channel was closed, I compared the whole story alone. My finger followed the original line by line. At the small vertical reminder to pause, I put a faint dot at the same place on the new copy. Other marginal comments stayed out. Deleted sentences didn't return. Title, opening, shop assistant's pen, woman behind the door, folded envelope: all present.

Finally, the incorrect sheet went into the wastepaper. It contained only the story I'd copied, no information repurposed from the back of another document. The usable copy went into its empty folder beside the table, not yet into my workbag. Tom hadn't agreed to a day. Finishing the copying didn't let me assign his lunch break.

Going out for dinner groceries that evening, I noticed my empty hand downstairs. Neither the red booklet nor the new copy was tucked beneath my arm. My key was in the right pocket. I took it out for the building door, felt the lock turn, and found the actual metal much simpler than the story.
