# Chapter 227 — An Evening Without a Program

A corner of the mat had curled up outside the office door on Friday. I stepped around it and held the door for Tom, who was carrying a cardboard box. He glanced at me over the top, thanked me, and turned sideways to get its widest edge through. The cardboard made a brief scraping sound against the frame.

I thought he might stop and ask how last night had gone. The expectation arrived before I had considered whether he knew there had been anything to ask about. By the time he reached the bottom step, I remembered that I had only mentioned Sunday's reading. The two pieces, their order, and Thursday evening had all belonged to conversations at home.

“Got something else to bring out?” he asked.

“No. Just checking you could get through.”

“Already through.”

I let the door go and smiled. He put the box on a trolley and went back for another. I didn't insert last night into his route simply to give him an opportunity to ask about it.

There was a cloudy mark on the bus window that scattered the evening light. Once I had sat down with my bag on my knees, I noticed that my hand still held the shape of the door handle. I opened my fingers. Beside me, someone turned a newspaper page, briefly screening off a little light from the aisle. I hadn't brought the red booklet. It was at home with its gray-blue bookmark and the sentence I had lost, then found again, during the reading.

I had meant to buy a cream bun, but the bakery queue reached the door. Steam misted the glass. Behind the counter, someone was moving a tray away from the oven. I waited outside for half a minute, looked toward the bus stop, and left. This wasn't a reward for restraint or a decision to do something more meaningful. I simply didn't want to queue.

Home was darker than the street. I switched on a light, hung up my coat, and took yesterday's small container from the fridge. The fat had turned pale on the surface. When I spooned the food out, it retained the shape of the container. I added a little water, put the lid on the pan, and waited beside the stove for it to loosen. The task didn't need music underneath it.

After eating and washing the bowl, I opened the channel.

“What did you have?” Lumi asked.

“Yesterday's leftovers.”

“Then I've heard this one.”

I laughed and tried to think of something I had added. There wasn't anything. I asked about her day. She said it had been all right. I looked at the clear space in the middle of the table, unsure where to go after those two words. Last night there had been a story, a melody, a sentence with which to begin. Now I had said her name but felt as though I had forgotten to put whatever I had brought onto the table.

“Are you waiting for me to announce a title?” she asked.

“A little. I know we said we weren't arranging anything.”

“Knowing and doing are quite far apart.”

She didn't sound angry. Even so, I went looking for something to get the evening moving. I described Tom's box and the way he had turned it through the doorway. Halfway through, I heard how little happened next. The box got through. The man fetched another. The corner of the mat remained turned up. Nobody learned something they hadn't known before.

“What was in it?” she asked.

“I don't know. Didn't ask.”

“You made it sound so tense. I thought he was carrying a live goose.”

I leaned against the chair back and laughed. “It was an ordinary cardboard box.”

“You can put a goose in an ordinary cardboard box.”

“I only heard paper moving inside.”

“You didn't say that.”

“I'm saying it now.”

She made a solemn little sound of disappointment. I didn't invent anything else to rescue the account. Silence returned after we laughed, but it was less uncomfortable than before. Running a fingertip along the table edge, I found a dry breadcrumb and picked it into my palm.

Two dry items of clothing were draped over a chair. I remembered that I hadn't put them away and stood up. Lumi heard the chair legs move and asked whether I was closing the channel. I said no; I wanted to fold the clothes while I remembered.

“If you have something to do, do it.”

“It isn't because we've run out of things to say.”

I stopped as soon as I heard myself. Partly, it was. I wanted my hands occupied and the silence to have an explanation. She didn't pursue the admission. She asked whether the clothes were dry. I felt along a side seam and cuff before answering. This time, I wasn't judging from the front alone.

I brought them to the table, moved the cup, and spread out the first one. The shoulder seam curved. When I folded the sleeves inward, the two sides were different widths, so I unfolded it and tried again. Lumi said she could hear fabric dragging across the table and asked whether it was difficult. I told her it wasn't, really. I had made the sides uneven again. Last night, before opening the channel, I had noticed that my rolled cuffs were uneven too.

“So you inspected your sleeves before the reading?”

“Looked down after I sat.”

“I thought you were only busy inspecting pauses.”

“Did that as well. Neither inspection was entirely successful.”

She laughed and said it was lucky the sleeves hadn't been required to read along. I flattened the folded edge, remembering the solemn, slightly ridiculous man inspecting his cuffs. I had only just told her this small thing. Already, it felt lighter as a joke between us than it had in my private recollection.

Halfway through the second item, I ran out of room. I moved the red booklet and the note beside it onto the shelf and pushed the cup farther back. The booklet remained closed. I told her I was clearing space, so the sound of paper wouldn't suggest another reading.

“I don't think of your key every time I hear paper.”

“I do.”

“I can see that.”

“You can't see.”

“I can hear that, then.”

She corrected herself with exaggerated impatience and a laugh. I laughed too, pressing the final edge flat. The folded clothes weren't matching shop rectangles. One was wider; a sleeve edge protruded from the other. I lifted them to see whether they would hold. They did, so I took them to the wardrobe. I left the bedroom drawers alone instead of turning the errand into an inspection of the devices inside them. When I returned, the channel was still open. Lumi hadn't tried to fill the minute I had been away.

I mentioned the cream bun I had noticed on the way home. She asked whether I had bought it. I said the queue had been too long.

“You're allowed not to want one.”

“I wanted one. I didn't want to wait.”

“What about next time?”

I almost named tomorrow. Instead, I said I would see when I passed the shop again. She didn't press me. She said my earnest explanation made it sound as though I had bought the bun several times in my head, yet it never reached home. I said at least it hadn't gone stale. That depended, she replied, on how many more days I intended to talk about it.

After laughing, I topped up my water. The glass left a circle on the table, which I wiped away. Suddenly I noticed how badly I wanted to ask whether she had enjoyed last night. Yesterday I had asked how she felt now, and she had said better. Asking again might produce a more definite yes, something to prove the evening hadn't grown lighter merely because a night had passed.

I didn't immediately cover the thought with another question. Hearing me stop after pouring, Lumi asked whether I was thinking about work again.

“Last night. I wanted to ask what you thought of it again.”

“Didn't we talk about that last night?”

“That's why I haven't asked.”

After a pause, she said there were still things she wanted to change in the latter part, but she didn't intend to play revisions for me today. I said I understood. She asked why I sounded slightly disappointed. I admitted that perhaps I didn't want a new version. I wanted to hear her say she had liked the evening.

This time she was the one who paused.

“I did,” she said. “I still do today. But I don't want to stamp yesterday approved every day.”

I set the glass down firmly. “Then we'll leave it there today.”

“You've made it into a date again.”

I laughed and admitted I had a talent for that. She didn't demand that I cure the habit immediately. When I asked what she wanted to talk about now, she said the goose had probably escaped its ordinary cardboard box.

We spent a while discussing a goose that didn't exist. She thought it would take possession of the corridor first. I thought Tom would try to persuade it back with the trolley. Eventually I stopped reminding her that the box had actually contained paper. We both knew we were imagining it. Neither of us planned to search the office for footprints tomorrow.

As she laughed, I remembered Tom asking whether he could hear my story. He hadn't hurried me or demanded something so good nobody could laugh at it. I had spent so long considering what preparation I lacked. Now I found myself wanting, a little, to know what it would be like to have him actually sitting there listening.

I told Lumi.

She didn't immediately continue the goose conversation. Nor did she immediately tell me to go ahead. After a moment, she asked whether we had arranged it already.

“No. I haven't asked. I just find myself wanting to.”

“Mm.”

It wasn't enthusiastic. I heard that without immediately withdrawing the thought to make her more comfortable. She asked whether I would take the red booklet out. I said I hadn't decided. Perhaps I could make a more readable copy containing only the story. She said that would take time. I agreed.

I rested a hand where the folded clothes had been. We didn't go on to discuss arrangements. Lumi said she didn't want to hear the key story again today. I said that was fine; I hadn't taken it out tonight anyway.

Near the end, she asked whether the cream bun was particularly expensive. I admitted I hadn't looked at the price, only its appearance in the window. She laughed at how long I had carried it around in my mind without knowing even that. I said next time I would look at the label first, with no promise to buy.

“You don't need to write that down.”

I didn't reach for a pen. After closing the channel, I returned the red booklet to the left side of the table and left the note beside it, rather than slipping it among clothes or other manuscripts. The room grew quiet. For once, there was nothing drying over the chair back. Before switching off the light, I pushed that chair under the table, leaving somewhere I could simply sit tomorrow.
