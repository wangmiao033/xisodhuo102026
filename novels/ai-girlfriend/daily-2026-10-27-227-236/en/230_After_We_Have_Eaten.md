# Chapter 230: After We Have Eaten

On Monday morning, the clean copy stayed home.

When I lifted my workbag, the folder lay within reach at the table's edge. I'd decided to ask Tom. Bringing the manuscript seemed the natural next step. But if I had it with me, I might follow the question with Since it's here, how about now? Instead, I took what I needed, reached the door, checked the bag's clasp, and didn't go back.

The bus arrived a little late. People crowded the front entrance. An umbrella tip touched my trouser leg, leaving a barely damp mark. I moved farther inside and held the rail. The stop announcement interrupted the Do you still want to hear it I'd prepared. By the time I found a seat, it had become something more courteous, so courteous I seemed to have declined on his behalf.

At the office, I began with the work in front of me. Last week's finished material hadn't lost another column simply because it was Monday. New tasks occupied a different page. I spent the first part of the morning dealing with them instead of lifting my head whenever Tom passed.

Eventually, he did stop beside my desk to ask where the afternoon material was. I told him and stood to retrieve the top copy, checking it wasn't an older version. Pulling it free dislodged a clip underneath. He bent to pick it up, and I offered the pages. There was half a second in which to say something else.

I didn't.

He waited to leave while I stood holding the edge of the paper. Looking up, Tom asked if there was anything more. At last I let go. No. I'd speak at lunch. He nodded and took the material. Nothing dangerous had happened. I'd merely made something simple resemble another awkward document awaiting him that afternoon.

Marcus arrived at lunch first. He weighted the corner of a napkin and said it was windy by the window. I sat across from him. Hunting for a place with nobody else around would make me seem to be planning a secret. Tom came later, carrying food hotter than usual. As soon as he'd set it down, he went for water.

We talked briefly about the morning. Marcus ate quickly. Before taking his plate away, he checked that the afternoon arrangement remained unchanged. Yes, I said. Tom nodded. Once Marcus had gone, I looked at Tom's lunch and remembered his request not to read while he ate.

I wasn't reading now. Surely I could ask a question.

“That story you asked about,” I said. “Would you still like to hear it?”

He was lowering his fork and looked up. “The key?”

“Yes. I'd like to read it now.”

“Sure. Today?”

I nearly agreed and searched afterward for a way to explain the manuscript was at home. As though accepting today was the only way to prove the invitation genuine.

“Didn't bring it. Tomorrow, after we've eaten?”

Tom glanced outside. A bench stood in the small courtyard behind the office, where few people passed. Sometimes somebody took a cup out there. Leaves lay on the seat now. Sun reached one edge while the wall shaded the other half.

“There?” he asked.

“If it doesn't rain. Only you. I wasn't planning to invite anyone else.”

He nodded. “How long?”

I gave the rough duration I'd practiced, not a precise minute count. Tom considered. There would be enough time after lunch, but we shouldn't run into the afternoon's work. I agreed. If something came up, we'd rearrange rather than have him listen while dealing with a task.

“Do you want me finding faults, or just listening?”

I hadn't prepared for that. Whatever you like would be easiest. Lowering every expectation might make it less troublesome for him. But I didn't mean whatever. I wanted him to hear it, and I didn't want him to feel he owed me a review, preferably a flattering one.

“Listen first. If something occurs to you, you can say. I might not change it on the spot.”

“Of course. It's not my manuscript.”

He answered so promptly I smiled. The thing I'd circled all morning wasn't something he intended to take responsibility for. Finishing his last mouthfuls, Tom pushed away the empty plate and asked whether I'd bring the little booklet. I'd made a separate copy, I said. Only the story.

“Fine. Don't forget.”

“The pages or lunch?”

“Either. Don't want to discover I'm attending a class halfway through eating.”

I laughed and agreed. As we stood, he caught the napkin lifted by the breeze and dropped it in the bin. He didn't announce tomorrow's event to anyone nearby. Taking my cup back, I passed the bench and noticed a decorative cap missing from one end, exposing the fastening beneath. The seat wasn't loose or sharp. I didn't attempt a repair.

That afternoon, I wanted to check the clock more often than usual. Not to leave early. Seeing Tom reminded me of the arrangement and made me wonder whether he was thinking about it too. When he brought me a question later, it concerned work entirely. I hesitated before catching up. Which part hadn't I heard? The first sentence. Could he repeat it?

I dealt with the actual question without adding an account of tomorrow's nerves. The story didn't need to claim every conversation to prove he'd agreed to hear it.

The sky had darkened by the time I left. At home, I hung my coat and went to the folder, then realized I was checking whether paper was still inside. I drew out one corner, saw a page number, and replaced it. The red booklet lay nearby with its gray-blue marker showing. Neither object had been appointed the other's replacement.

After dinner, I opened the channel and told Lumi. Tomorrow after lunch, the little courtyard behind the office, another day if the weather was unsuitable. Tom had agreed to listen. Nobody else had been invited.

“You asked today.”

“Yes.”

“As smoothly as you imagined yesterday?”

I laughed. “Not really. This morning I nearly refused to let go of his work papers.”

“He thought something was missing?”

“Maybe. All I know is he asked if there was anything else.”

I described the actual exchange at lunch. I didn't turn every glance at the afternoon clock into evidence that Tom was equally eager. Would I explain the ending she preferred? Lumi asked. No. I wouldn't introduce two versions beforehand. In this one, he still folded the envelope.

“What if he asks?”

“I can say I considered another ending. Doesn't mean I need to carry your remarks over too.”

After a pause, she said she wasn't afraid of people knowing she had opinions. I understood. I wanted to read this version on its own. She acknowledged that without demanding I hide her name or announce whom I'd read it to first.

Standing to clear the dishes, I said I'd be away from the table briefly. Was I nervous? Yes, but the dishes really were waiting in the kitchen. Running water filled the room. I didn't shout over it. Back with dry hands, I put the ordinary folder into a separate section of my workbag, away from the lunch container.

Hearing the zip, Lumi asked whether the manuscript was packed. Yes. The red booklet wouldn't come. Then don't pack her sounds for the week merely to make things convenient, she said. There was a joke in it and some discomfort. I didn't interpret it as a concealed prohibition on leaving home.

“I haven't recorded anything. Not planning to.”

“I know. I meant don't use everything else to explain those pages.”

I put down the bag. She was right. I'd nearly prepared to tell Tom why writing the story had finally helped me sit and hear somebody else. He'd have been handed an evening of private life before the first sentence.

“Only the story.”

“A genuinely short introduction this time.”

Smiling, I said perhaps only the title. I could ask him to sit down first, she suggested, rather than begin upon reaching the bench. Exactly the sort of thing I might do when nervous. I'd remember. I didn't need to rehearse looking natural as well.

Later, we talked about the wind after lunch. I described a napkin being caught without calling it freedom or escape. Would tomorrow's pages fly too? The folder would support them, I said. If it became windy, we'd go back. I wasn't competing with weather.

“Don't chase the paper while reading.”

“He might run first.”

She laughed. I relaxed a little at the sound without using it to ask whether she now liked the entire arrangement.

Before the channel closed, she said, “Tell me how it actually went when you come home. Don't tell me a successful version in advance.”

I agreed. The bag stood beside the chair, the paper inside. For the first time, I didn't need to compose my question for Tom. Tomorrow's opening was already written. Before its turn came, there was still a lunch to eat properly.
