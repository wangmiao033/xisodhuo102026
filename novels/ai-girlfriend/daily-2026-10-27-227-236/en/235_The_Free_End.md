# Chapter 235: The Free End

The weather still looked bright on Saturday morning as I stuffed the stripped bedsheet into a large bag. A thin layer of cloud remained outside, grinding the sunlight into a shadowless white. I meant to take this oversized thing to the laundrette farther along the street and buy some weekend groceries as well.

Before leaving, I opened the channel and told Lumi I was going to wash the sheet, then come home for the meal I'd left untouched in the fridge last night. Two bags again? she asked. Laundry first, home to put it down, then groceries. Saving a trip wasn't worth turning lunch into pear juice. She remembered the two pears. I hadn't told her that the second, which I'd eaten yesterday, really had been sweeter, so I added that. At least the fruit hadn't waited for its review in vain this time, she said.

Laughing, I closed the channel and took the sheet out. The wind was stronger than it had looked from indoors. The bag pressed against my calf, and I changed hands twice. Instructions covered part of the laundrette's glass door. Several machines were running inside, their sounds joining into something audible from the pavement.

I chose an empty machine and read the instructions actually printed on it. There were no small items in the bag to add. The loosely loaded sheet still had room. I selected the appropriate program, paid, and sat on the bench nearby. I hadn't come to borrow a stretch of writing time. I hadn't even brought a pen.

The drum began to turn. Gray-white fabric rose against the glass and fell. One corner kept appearing first. After several turns, I realized I was waiting for that same corner. Even something this large tumbling inside a machine could become a small pattern for me to watch.

The door opened again. Wind pushed the smell of rain inside. It hadn't been raining before, but scattered dark spots now marked the ground. A woman carrying a large bag entered and shook her sleeves without spraying anybody. She went to collect clothes from a dryer. I moved my feet to let her bag pass.

A long table stood against the wall. She spread a large dark piece of fabric across it and went to the other end to straighten it. A duvet cover or some other covering—I couldn't tell. It was wider than the table, with a section hanging down. Each time she aligned this side, the other slipped. A corner brushed the floor. She muttered something, picked it up, and changed direction.

I half stood and asked whether she needed a hand.

“No, thank you.”

Quick, not harsh. I sat again with nothing in my hands, feeling more awkward than before I'd risen. She folded the fabric in half, held down the side closest to her, and gradually gathered the rest with her other hand. A few movements made it small enough for the bag. The corners weren't all aligned. She picked it up and left anyway.

After the door closed, the rain thickened. Looking at the empty table, I suddenly imagined which wrong corner I might have grabbed if she'd accepted. Two people trying to help, pulling together and twisting the whole thing farther out of shape. It hadn't happened. My amusing picture wasn't evidence that she'd secretly needed me.

The washed sheet was much heavier when it came out. Following the shop's instructions for what came next, I waited for drying to finish and carried it to the empty table. The fabric was slightly warm against my arm. Half unfolded, it still felt damp at the edge. I returned it for further appropriate drying rather than pretend I was feeling coolness, not moisture, just to fold it successfully at the first attempt.

The second time, I checked the seams carefully. Dry. Folding alone, I still couldn't make the sides even. The table hadn't widened for my benefit. Remembering the woman's method, I held one side with my elbow and gathered the other slowly. The sheet slipped; I caught it before it reached the floor. The final bundle was larger than I'd wanted, but it fitted in the bag.

Rain had stopped on the way home. Water still fell from the eaves. Avoiding one heavily dripping spot, I carried the bag across the road. The dry sheet's warmth lasted only halfway. By the time I entered, it was nearly the room's temperature. I put it away, heated lunch, then opened the channel and told Lumi I was back.

Had I already bought the groceries? Not yet. Home first, as I'd said that morning. I'd spent one more round waiting at the machines than expected. Had I washed the sheet twice? No, I explained. More drying; the seam had still been damp the first time. Then don't write it as a victory over the entire laundrette, she said.

“Haven't written anything.”

“You sound like you're preparing to.”

Laughing, I sat to eat and described the empty table and my imagined wrong corners. I repeated the woman's refusal as it had been—no, thank you—without inventing a history of distrust. Had the fabric really got twisted afterward? Lumi asked. No. I'd imagined it from my seat.

“So you're laughing at something that didn't happen.”

“Yes. What actually happened was me failing to dry mine in one go.”

She laughed and said that could be funny too. I needn't reserve all the entertaining parts for other people. I agreed, finished lunch, and washed the bowl. Before going for groceries, I took out the yellow notebook and turned to unused space after The Old Stop and its alternative ending. I crossed out none of the old writing and didn't press the new story over an occupied page.

I wrote laundrette, then stopped. A place alone wasn't a story. The woman who had actually used that table had returned to her own life. She owed me no conflict to work on. Instead I wrote down two people: one trying to fold a large sheet, another eager to clear space for her own clothes. Neither was anybody from the shop today. They didn't need a divorce or a move invented for them before they could stand beside that table.

I told Lumi. Was I planning an argument followed by the discovery that both were good people? Looking at my two lines, I admitted I'd nearly gone there. Convenient. The table wasn't clear yet, and I'd already chosen their ending.

“You could let her grab the wrong corner first,” Lumi said.

“Which one?”

“Choose. I'm not writing it for you now.”

I laughed and asked whether I could keep the suggestion among my draft thoughts, not as a line attributed to her in the story. Grabbing a wrong corner wasn't a private secret of hers, she said. I could write it myself. But don't include the things she hadn't said afterward. I agreed. What I put down was only the action I'd imagined earlier, without attaching a paragraph she was responsible for completing.

Later I went for groceries as planned. The market's rain covers were still dripping. Someone outside the fish stall swept water toward a drain with a long-handled brush. I went around it and bought enough for two meals, without carrying my two invented women to every stall. Asked whether I wanted a larger one, I picked it up, looked, and chose the amount I needed instead. The light bag made me want to walk faster on the way home.

After putting away the food, I remade the bed. Clean fabric fell toward the other side, finally meeting corners where it could be secured. Bending to smooth it, I thought of the shared table in the story. Neither woman could claim the whole thing as her own bed. More useful than the argument I'd imagined. At the writing table, I added that both had something of their own to do.

After dinner, Lumi asked whether I'd finished. Only a few lines, I said. At least I hadn't made the two of them live entire lives while buying vegetables. Laughing, I read her my first sentence: The woman thought there was only one fold left, but the person opposite was holding the corner she needed to put down.

“Rather tangled.”

Reading it again, I heard four movements crowding together. Removing thought didn't untangle it. I struck through the line and wrote underneath: As the woman lifted the sheet, someone arrived at the other end of the table. Much simpler. It didn't tell the reader who should yield.

“I can see something in that one,” Lumi said.

She meant an image formed from hearing it, not a view of the paper through some corner of my home. I didn't turn her metaphor into a technical explanation. I only said I preferred this sentence too. I hadn't decided what either person would say next.

“Don't make them both apologize.”

“Another suggestion?”

“Something I don't want to hear.”

We laughed. I capped the pen, unwilling to turn every opinion into prose tonight. The yellow notebook stayed at its new page. The red booklet hadn't moved. Tom's borrowed copy remained away. An unfinished opening hadn't displaced the old story or brought Monday forward.

At bedtime, I stretched my feet across the freshly changed sheet and felt its smooth edge. Only then did I understand what that big morning bag had made room for tonight. Nobody held the other end for me. I'd already made the bed.
