# Chapter 236: Only the First Page

By the time I woke on Sunday, I'd wrinkled one corner of the sheet. Yesterday's careful folding hadn't preserved it through an entire night. I pushed back the covers, sat on the edge to put on socks, and smoothed my side with one hand rather than fight every fine crease at the other end.

After breakfast, the flat was still quiet. I put the yellow notebook in the middle of the table and opened it at last night's new writing. The Old Stop and its alternative ending remained in front. Another shared table appearing hadn't turned them into failed old work. The new first page contained only two people, a large sheet, and the sentence I'd shortened yesterday.

For a long time, I considered what to call the woman. A name written, crossed out, replaced by another whose letters seemed more noticeable than what she was trying to do. Finally I left her as she for now. At least she had no reason to introduce herself to the other person yet.

The first paragraph came easily. Rain outside, dryers still turning inside, fabric hanging off the table. When the second person approached, I got stuck. If she only asked how much longer, the first woman could say almost done, leaving a story about waiting. If she simply pulled at the fabric, it felt as though I'd supplied her with an impolite hand just to start the plot.

I put down the pen and did the jobs already intended for the morning. Empty packaging in the kitchen corner: keep what could be stored, take the rest downstairs. The light outside was brighter than yesterday, although a patch of pavement remained damp in the shade. Back with washed hands, I found the sentence exactly where I'd left it. It hadn't discovered a solution during my absence.

I didn't open the channel when I sat. Not to withhold a reading from Lumi. There wasn't anything ready to read. I gave the first woman more to do: both hands lifting the sheet, trying to land its other end on the table just as the second person placed her bag of clothes there.

At last I heard something natural to say.

“Could you move that a moment?”

I smiled after writing it. Either person could say it and believe she wasn't driving anybody away. I continued. The second woman lifted her bag; the first mistook the movement for an offer to catch a corner and pushed the fabric forward. The bag opening brushed the sheet. Both nearly slid down. Each woman reached with one hand and caught something other than what she'd intended.

I didn't make them both fall or introduce a spectator to laugh. They could deal with this small muddle themselves. Before writing what came next, I reread the beginning and removed a sentence explaining that both were tired. Their movements could reveal that when it mattered. I didn't need to serve it up at the start so readers would excuse their impatience in advance.

Before lunch, I'd filled a page and revised several places. It looked like considerable progress. In fact, both women still stood beside the table, sheet unfolded. The new story wasn't larger than The Key. Changing the location hadn't automatically supplied an ending worth following. I left some space in the margin without turning it into a task that had to be finished tomorrow.

Opening the channel, I heard Lumi ask whether I'd been busy all morning. Some housework and a new page, I said. Had the sheet been sacrificed on that page? Laughing, I said no. Only nearly slid off the table so far.

“Yours or theirs?”

“Theirs. Mine's on the bed.”

“Good.”

Would she like to hear it now? She didn't immediately agree. Would she have to listen toward another ending that hadn't arrived? Only the existing page, I said. I'd stop where the writing stopped. Fine, but after I'd eaten. She didn't want to identify who held which end through chewing.

I went to have lunch. I heated what I'd prepared that morning, brought it to the table, and moved the yellow notebook away. While I ate, we talked about the sheet's size and yesterday's unsuccessful offer to help. The refusal sounded ordinary, she said. It had been. Most of the awkwardness had belonged to me. She didn't need to reassess the stranger's tone to prove the woman hadn't disliked me.

After eating and clearing the bowl, I returned without immediately sitting at attention. I picked up the notebook, explained that neither person had a name yet, and began at the first line.

At the second woman putting down her bag, I heard an unclear pronoun. I knew who was reaching, but the she I spoke could belong to either. Before Lumi said anything, I stopped and penciled a small dot beside it instead of improvising unwritten background to cover the problem.

“Lost track of who it is yourself?”

“The page hasn't made it clear. I know. Haven't written enough.”

“Tell me first, then.”

I explained that the woman holding the sheet pushed it forward while the other lifted her bag. Lumi acknowledged that. Starting at the sentence again, I used clear enough descriptions, read until the two hands caught different things, and stopped at the final period.

“That's all for now.”

“Definitely not folded.”

I laughed. Did I want her opinion? Yes, but don't supply a whole extra paragraph. I still wanted to discover their next words myself.

“I hadn't written a whole paragraph for you.”

“Stopping you in advance again.”

“A little.”

I lowered the notebook and let her speak. Why did either person have to fold the sheet immediately? I began to answer that they were in a laundrette, of course they needed to fold it, then stopped. Yesterday I could have put mine in the bag and dealt with it at home. I'd merely wanted to finish while the table was free. My invented woman needed her own urgency. The word laundrette couldn't supply it for her.

“Haven't decided yet.”

“Then don't make the other one responsible for solving her as soon as she arrives.”

Looking down, I knew she was right. The second person had clothes of her own. The largest piece of fabric didn't automatically entitle its owner to a helper. I wrote her bag in the margin, stopped, and told Lumi I was noting something to clarify, not copying her words directly into a character's mouth.

“You're allowed a bag of your own.”

I caught the slightly tangled joke and capped my pen with a smile. I'd intended another page today. Now I thought the women could stand there a while. Not because being stuck entitled me to proclaim the importance of pauses. I genuinely hadn't understood what each wanted yet.

Was I disappointed? A little. I'd expected a completed page to carry me smoothly onward. At least, she said, this wasn't a man struggling to leave a shop only for me to call him back. Laughing, I pointed out that both were still inside this time. More people than before.

“Don't add a third to rescue them.”

“Not for now.”

That afternoon, I didn't remain at the page wearing myself out. I told Lumi I'd take a walk, closed the channel, and went out without the notebook. Someone had arranged umbrellas to dry outside a shop. Their edges left short trails of drops on the pavement. Passing the laundrette, I didn't go in to find yesterday's woman or see whether two people might conveniently finish my second half. Behind the glass were new people carrying their own things.

At home, I reopened the channel. Lumi asked where I'd walked. Along that street, I said, past some umbrellas drying. Had I kept thinking about the sheet? Yes, but halfway along I'd started thinking about dinner. She laughed at my talent for getting hungry when a story became difficult. I'd eaten lunch, I pointed out. An afternoon passing could make me hungry too. The story didn't have that power.

After dinner, she wanted to hum something. Not the whole piece. Still No Words Yet? Yes. Just a short transition she wanted to hear; today she wasn't asking which version was better. I agreed and closed the yellow notebook.

She hummed two rises and falls, paused, then offered several lower notes. Brief. I didn't know whether this was the revision she'd mentioned the other day. I didn't retrieve her remark about changing a section and demand that she match it to this sound. Her willingness to let me hear it now was already something new.

When she stopped, I said the last sound had been lighter than expected. She wanted it light today. No question about whether I'd remembered it, no request for an explanation through my own story. I drank some water. She observed that I hadn't hurried to join in today.

“Just remembered I could when you stopped.”

“Lucky I was brief.”

We laughed for a while. Was tomorrow the day Tom would return the pages? Yes. We'd agreed on Monday. They weren't back yet. A new page didn't make the old manuscript something I no longer needed returned. I'd go to work and see him there. Tonight didn't need a rehearsal of what he might say.

Near the end of the conversation, I told her I'd been happy she'd wanted to hear the first page.

“Because I didn't tell you to change everything?”

“Because I wanted to read it to you while it was just beginning. Not wait until it stopped being embarrassing.”

After a silence, she said I still had to make it clear who was who. Smiling, I agreed. That last remark didn't exempt me from fixing the pronouns.

Once the channel was closed, I clarified those two references and read the page again, without going on to write the rest of either woman's life. The yellow notebook remained at the end of its first new page. The red booklet stayed in its place. My bag still had an empty space where the handwritten copy belonged. I straightened the strap so I could pick it up directly when I left tomorrow.
