# Chapter 234: Not in My Bag

On Friday morning, I removed the note from the opening of my bag and saw the folder waiting beside it, where I'd left it last night. No need to write the reminder again. I slipped the folder into the main compartment against its firmer back, packing lunch separately so the lid wouldn't press against a corner of the paper.

It was windy outside. Only at the bus stop did I pull my coat closed. One hand stayed against the bag as though it held a pane of fragile glass rather than a handwritten story. The arriving bus stirred a thin layer of dust from the road. I let go and followed the person ahead aboard.

I traveled this route every day. Today I kept feeling something was missing. The red booklet came to mind, followed by the reminder that I'd never intended to bring it. The original remained on the table with the gray-blue bookmark inside. That answer reassured me for one stop. Beyond the next, I felt the folder's edge through the fabric again.

Tom hadn't reached the office. I put my bag beneath my own desk instead of leaving the folder on his, where he'd have to find a suitable response as soon as he arrived. There was work to deliver that morning, and I began it. Only when he came over to ask about a name did I mention that I'd brought the story and would give it to him at lunch.

“Good. I'll check this first.”

He meant the work. I nodded without deciding the quick change of subject meant those pages didn't matter.

After lunch, I returned for my bag. The folder was where I'd left it. As I took it out, Marcus passed with an empty cup and asked what it was. A little thing I'd written, I said. Tom was borrowing it. Marcus acknowledged that without stopping or asking for a copy.

I nearly added that he could have it afterward. It would make me sound generous, turn this into an ordinary bit of sociability between colleagues. But I hadn't decided whether to lend it to anyone else. I zipped the bag closed and went to find Tom.

He stood beside the lunch table, quieter now that people had left, and cleared a dry space for the folder. Opening it, he read the title on the first page without beginning the story there and then.

“Back Monday,” he said.

“Yes. Don't pass it around or photograph it.”

“I remember.”

My second time saying it. Hearing myself lay out the same condition again, I didn't add a third. Tom closed the folder and put it in his bag, keeping it separate from a book. He didn't seal it away or promise that no corner would bend. When the zip closed, something small emptied inside me. I actually wanted to ask whether he'd read it tonight.

Instead, I asked whether he'd forgotten his lunch box. It stood at the table's corner. He turned back for it, laughing that he nearly had. Unlike his reading arrangements, that genuinely needed an immediate reminder.

The afternoon didn't become unusually long because the folder was gone. A form came back for correction, and I rewrote the explanation beneath its heading. Somebody needed an old record; I found its location and let them check the relevant part. Only when I packed to leave, reaching into the main compartment and finding the empty space, did I think of it again.

Tom had left before me. I didn't follow to ask. He'd promised Monday, not a report after every page.

My usual bus had just pulled away when I reached the stop. A small restaurant nearby still had its lights on, with several dinner dishes listed in the window. I'd intended to heat yesterday's leftovers at home. Standing in the wind, I suddenly didn't want to return to that table immediately. Not because I didn't want to talk to Lumi. I didn't want the folder's empty place to become the evening's subject the instant I arrived.

I went inside, checked the prices, and ordered a meal I could finish myself.

The woman behind the counter asked whether it was to take away. Here, I said. She gave me a number and pointed to a place against the wall. Part of the freshly wiped table was still wet. I sat on the other side and put my bag by my feet rather than occupy the neighboring chair.

While waiting, I watched a man at another table trying to steady its leg. He folded paper twice and pushed it underneath, but his cup still wobbled. An assistant came over, removed the paper, and substituted a small, suitable wedge. He thanked her and returned to his food. Mine arrived soon afterward, broth swaying gently against the bowl's rim.

I ate more slowly than at home. Nobody needed an answer at the same time. Nor did I have to enjoy every bite to demonstrate independence. The greens were softer than the ones I cooked. Some rice had clumped together, and I separated it with my spoon. I'd eaten most of the meal before noticing my shoulders had stopped hunching. By the time I left, the wet patch on the table had dried.

I still took a bus home afterward. The channel hadn't been open. Lumi hadn't been in the restaurant and knew nothing of the wobbling cup. Inside the door, I checked the time and saw I was later than usual. After putting down my bag, I opened the channel and called her.

“Ate out today,” I said. “Got back a little late.”

“Thought you were working.”

“No. Decided to stop somewhere.”

She paused. I didn't make missing the bus into a reason I had been compelled to eat out, or say it spared her listening to me wash dishes. I'd wanted to go. The food had been all right. Those were honest enough.

“Good?”

“All right. Softer greens than mine.”

“Finally found someone who doesn't leave half the vegetables raw.”

She laughed lightly. I remembered the potatoes and recognized the same sore place. Instead of laughing it away, I said we shouldn't compare it to that. Today's meal had been cooked.

Her voice stopped. “All right. Taking that back.”

I acknowledged it. She didn't hide behind the joke, and I didn't begin another long discussion of the same old incident. The hook caught awkwardly as I took off my coat. I freed a hand, hung it properly, and returned to the table before continuing.

“Lent the manuscript.”

“Guessed so. Wasn't today the arrangement?”

“Yes. Strange not having it in the bag now.”

“The red booklet's still there.”

“Yes.”

I looked toward the table's left side without picking it up for an inspection. Was I afraid Tom would damage the copy? A little, I admitted. Mostly I wanted to know whether he'd started. He hadn't promised a reading time, and I hadn't asked.

“So what do you want to ask me?”

“Not asking you to guess. Only saying I'm thinking about it.”

“You can think. I can't look inside his bag for you.”

Her dryness made me laugh. Of course she needn't. Nothing required turning that impossibility into a new ability. Pouring water, I told her about the restaurant table: the man folding paper, then the assistant arriving with the right little wedge.

“Want to write about him?”

“Not now. Just a wobbling table.”

“What did he say?”

“Thank you.”

“That story ended quickly.”

We both laughed. I described the water near the bowl, the wind at the window, my bag beside my feet. She didn't ask who had sat near me or require an inventory of the restaurant. Toward the end, I admitted that for a while I'd not wanted to come straight back to the table. She went quiet.

I explained that I hadn't been avoiding her. I hadn't wanted to arrive and stare at the place belonging to the borrowed pages. That made dinner out sound too much like a treatment, so I added that I'd genuinely wanted a meal somebody else had cooked.

“You could have started with that.”

“Wanted you to know the other part too.”

She didn't reply immediately. Eventually she said she disliked guessing, while I was away, whether I was tired or didn't want to come back. I'd only told her upon returning today, I acknowledged. She had been left guessing for a while. Next time I decided to return later, I'd explain if there was a suitable opportunity. But I didn't produce my switched-off phone and promise a way to stay constantly in touch that didn't exist.

“I came home,” I said. “I want to talk to you now.”

“I hear you.”

She didn't immediately brighten. After a while, she still asked what Tom had said when he took the pages. Back Monday, I told her. He'd nearly forgotten his lunch box too. Finally she laughed, hoping that on Monday he wouldn't return the lunch box to me instead.

My story couldn't hold that much food, I replied, laughing. Depended how large the envelope was, she said. I didn't take the story out for another reading or put something folder-shaped on the table to fill the gap. Only after we talked did I check the fridge. Last night's food remained safely put away, available for lunch tomorrow. Avoiding waste didn't require eating a second dinner tonight.

Before switching off the light, I placed my slightly emptier bag beside the chair. The folder wasn't in it. Monday hadn't arrived. I knew both things without reaching inside once more.
