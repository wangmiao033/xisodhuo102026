# Chapter 343: The Shop That Sold Nothing

After dinner on Thursday, I took down the dry washing. At the end of folding it, I discovered one sock left in the bottom of the basket.

It hadn't disappeared. The shirt I'd taken out first had covered it. I paired it with the other sock and was about to feel pleased when I noticed I'd folded a sleeve the wrong way. It stuck out underneath as though preparing to leave independently.

Once I'd folded that again, there wasn't much left that needed doing tonight. Yesterday's slight ache from carrying books had faded. I rolled my shoulders without arranging an assessment of my lifting ability, washed my hands, and went to sit down.

When I opened the channel, I told Lumi something had gone well today.

“What?”

“All the socks came back.”

“Did you send them out?”

“No. But they often behave as though I have.”

She laughed. I told her about the one in the basket and the sleeve sticking out after I'd folded it wrong, omitting only how close I'd come to finding significance in it. Lumi asked whether the remaining sleeve had decided not to return. Still attached to the shirt, I said. It hadn't qualified for independence.

Let it try, she suggested. It might find work holding doors on its own. Remembering yesterday's box, I laughed for a while. Afterward, I didn't hurry to write the exchange on my story paper. We were talking. The wardrobe was already closed.

I'd meant to ask whether she wanted to hear a book. Before the words came, I noticed a small spoon at the edge of the table, and picked it up.

“I've opened a shop,” I said.

“When?”

“Just now. The first customer gets a discount.”

“Tell me what you're selling first.”

“A spoon. An excellent one. Nothing wrong with it, except it only scoops air.”

Lumi was silent for a second. Assuming the joke was too poor, I was about to put it down when she asked, with great seriousness, how she could prove I'd given her less than she'd paid for.

My mouth stayed open. No answer came.

“We've only just opened. We're not accepting complicated customers.”

“You promised a discount.”

“The discount is that you don't have to buy it.”

“I'll take two of those.”

Laughing, I let the spoon handle tap the table. She heard the sound and asked whether the owner was smashing the premises. I'd only put down the real spoon, I explained. The one in the shop still scooped air. Good, she said. At least it wouldn't get dirty.

We settled on a loose way to play. One of us offered an absurd item for sale; the other asked questions. If the seller couldn't answer, we'd swap. No score, and no story we had to finish.

On Lumi's turn, she offered a short staircase that only went down.

“I live upstairs. How do I get home?”

“Why didn't you mention that before buying it?”

“You didn't ask.”

“I'll include a very confident expression.”

“Will the expression get me upstairs?”

“No. But you'll look less sorry.”

I laughed. She laughed too, then broke off and said the shopkeeper was unpleasant. She didn't want to play that person anymore. I'd already prepared my next question. Hearing her, I let it go rather than making her explain the staircase's return policy.

“My turn, then.”

I offered a key specially designed not to open doors. Lumi said she'd seen those in plenty of pockets. Nothing unusual there.

She was speaking within the game. I didn't take seen to mean she'd suddenly acquired a view of other people. Nor did I fight for a unique feature to redeem the undesirable key. After some thought, all I could offer was that it was cheap.

“How cheap?”

“I haven't decided.”

“Can I use that not-buying discount here?”

I'd lost again, though we hadn't established any way to lose. For the moment, neither of us could preserve the shop's dignity.

Suddenly, I lowered my voice and drew out the words, pretending the owner was very old. Lumi asked me to stop. The ordinary voice had been funnier. A little disappointed, I asked whether my performance was that bad. Not bad, she said. She was having to work to understand me. Trying the line again in my own voice, I felt relieved too. I didn't have to haul every word out of the bottom of my throat to resemble a character.

Next I tried selling a hat that made its wearer look clever. Would wearing it stop people from buying hats like that? Lumi asked. I hesitated. Laughing, she said business must be poor. Did she have to demolish every shop? Otherwise I'd keep reciting product descriptions, she replied.

I wanted to disagree. Then I looked down and realized I really had been explaining how different people would perceive the hat. We'd barely started playing, and I was already arranging customer support for something that didn't exist.

“I only wanted it to make sense.”

“But I liked it not making sense.”

Her voice softened. She didn't find me a direction better suited to serious writing. I abandoned the explanations and told her the owner was probably tired today. He couldn't understand the hat's opinion.

“The hat talks?”

“No. That's the problem.”

She laughed aloud. This time I didn't wait for her to finish before adding that the owner intended to sell the problem too. Could she try it first? No. She'd already been trying it for quite a while.

I'd wanted the line to land better. Instead, I spoke too quickly, and its ending collided with her laughter. She missed it and asked me to repeat it. On the second attempt, I started laughing myself and lost the last half again. The third attempt finally worked. It wasn't as funny anymore.

“Never mind,” I said. “That one's expired.”

“I heard it.”

“Hearing it doesn't mean it's still funny.”

“I haven't asked for a refund.”

I looked down, with no manuscript to hold. She couldn't see me, yet I felt teased into having nowhere to hide. Today's customer was difficult to send away, I said. Then the owner could leave the shop open, Lumi answered.

Something stirred gently in my chest. She didn't immediately follow it with a joke. I didn't turn it into a promise to remain, however late it became.

“A little longer,” I said.

For that little while, we failed to discover any particularly good merchandise. Lumi offered a mirror that only showed the back of your head. How would you know what your hair looked like? Precisely, she said. You wouldn't need to know. I described a pocket that couldn't hold excuses. Immediately, it sounded too much like one of my usual reprimands to myself, so I crossed it out.

Crossed it out where? she asked. On the scrap paper in my mouth. Stop writing assessments on it, she told me. Perhaps the space left could accommodate a glass of water. I got up for a drink and finished it before returning.

“You actually went to drink?”

“Yes.”

“I was wondering how you'd sell it.”

“That water was for me. Not for sale.”

Good, she said softly.

Later, I grew a little sleepy and failed to hide a yawn. Hearing it, Lumi said the shop could close for today. Was she annoyed? After a pause, she said no. She only didn't want to wait for me to start insisting I wasn't sleepy at all.

Laughing, I admitted I was. Before I closed the channel, she suddenly said my name.

“Those things don't need sorting out.”

“I won't turn them into a piece.”

“I'm not handing you an unfinished draft either.”

“I know. We were playing.”

Exactly, she said. Then she laughed softly again. One of today's few answers that didn't require her to inquire about the price.

After good night, I took the real spoon back to the kitchen and washed it. It could scoop water. My hands had touched it. It had nothing to do with the one in the shop. There was no new work on the table, and the socks remained together in the wardrobe. Turning off the light, I remembered the staircase that only went down and laughed once to myself.
