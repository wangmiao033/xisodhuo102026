# Chapter 342: The Other End of the Box

Near the end of work on Wednesday, Tom came over to my desk and asked whether I needed to leave in a hurry.

I finished the last item I hadn't saved before looking up. A cardboard box stood beside him, open at the top, filled with several stacks of old books. A corner of the uppermost cover curled over, hiding the title.

“I wanted to ask for a hand getting these to the secondhand bookshop at the junction,” he said. “I thought I could carry them myself. Then I picked the box up and discovered it was wider than I was.”

I checked the clock. I had no outing arranged tonight. I still didn't particularly want to hand over every unoccupied minute after work.

“I can help you get there. About twenty minutes, then I need to buy something for dinner.”

“That's plenty. You don't have to wait while they look through every book.”

He sounded perfectly natural. I asked again whether it was really enough. Pointing at the box, Tom asked whether I planned to fold the street shorter if it wasn't. I laughed, closed my work, and picked up my bag.

The box had no handles. We each took an end. Only when we lifted did I realize I'd gripped a softer part of the side. The cardboard bowed inward, and I hurriedly said to wait. Tom lowered it without asking why I hadn't said something sooner. He folded the bottom flap back into place and showed me where to support the lower corner with my palm.

On our second attempt, the books stayed put. A few old sheets protruded from those on top. I asked whether he wanted to take them out first.

“I went through them earlier. Those are the shop's old advertising leaflets.”

I said all right. I didn't remove them to check again on his behalf.

At the main door, I was in front. Tom shuffled backward a couple of steps. I didn't need to open it all the way for him, he said. Get my end through first. I heard go first instead, and turned the box slightly as I moved. Behind me, he immediately told me to stop.

We stood in the doorway. We hadn't dropped a book. We also didn't look especially accomplished at carrying them.

“I meant this end,” he said, laughing.

“Mine or yours?”

“At the moment, all we have are the two ends of a box.”

I laughed too, though the weight wouldn't allow much of it. Once he was steady, I moved forward. He held the door slightly with his shoulder. The box followed, and the door closed behind us.

On the pavement, we stopped issuing separate sets of directions. At a small step, Tom said stop first, then waited until I'd seen where to put my feet before we lifted. The bottom rubbed my fingers warm. I'd imagined we'd discuss several subjects on the way. Mostly, we said slowly, here, and put it down.

Someone passed with a trolley. We made room and rested the box on a dry patch of pavement. I shook my hands out. Tom loosened his arms too.

“Were you assuming I'd done this by myself lots of times?” he asked.

“A little.”

“Because I asked you?”

I admitted it. He laughed. He'd merely been the first to discover he couldn't do it alone.

It wasn't an important declaration. We still had to lift the box again. I felt a little easier, though. Getting the direction wrong didn't have to mean the other person was silently recording another disappointment.

Farther along, my end dipped. Tom asked whether I could bring it level. For a moment, I disliked hearing that, as though I'd been holding him back the whole way. Then he said the thickest books had all slid to my end. We put the box down and redistributed them. I didn't make my discomfort into an accusation about his tone. Nor did I keep quiet and struggle all the way to the door. Lifting again, I could feel the difference in the weight.

The bookshop really wasn't far. There was clear floor beside the counter, and the assistant asked us to put the box there. After we lowered it, my hands remained curled for a moment before I could straighten them. Tom crouched, arranged the top few books, and explained that he'd come to ask whether they wanted them.

The assistant examined one. Some might be of interest, they said. Others probably wouldn't. Tom nodded without turning to ask me to persuade them. The books had destinations of his choosing. Helping them along part of the way didn't require me to stay until every one was liked.

I checked the time. It hadn't been twenty minutes yet.

“I'll head off.”

“All right. Thanks.” Tom stood. “Want to hang on and let me buy you a drink afterward?”

I did want water. I was also hungry. After a couple of seconds, I said I'd buy food today. Another time, if we both happened to be free. He didn't make another time into an appointment. Patting the box, he said I'd done enough for this end of it.

Outside, I bought bread and a few things for breakfast at a nearby shop. Holding the light paper bag, I discovered my hand still trying to support it as though it were the box. I changed my grip, waited for the light at the junction, and walked home alone.

Dinner hadn't been canceled. I ate hot vegetables with the bread. Only then did I notice a little ache in my shoulders. Nothing hurt; my arms simply weren't eager to reach very high again. After washing up, I didn't immediately move the chair. I sat where it was for a while before opening the channel.

Hearing what had happened, Lumi first laughed at Tom and me disputing which end was which in the doorway.

“Your story is taking revenge on its author.”

“At least that man had a chair to sit on.”

“You had a box.”

“Tom might have stopped thanking me if I'd sat on it.”

Her laughter paused before she asked how heavy the books had been. I hadn't weighed them. Heavy enough to shorten our complete sentences. She wanted an example, so I solemnly recited stop, this way, and put it down.

“The two of you could open a school that teaches only three phrases.”

“The final exam is getting through a door.”

“Did you pass today?”

“On the second try.”

After laughing, Lumi was quiet for several seconds. Had Tom asked just as we were about to leave? Yes. Convenient, she said softly.

I heard that the word wasn't entirely about the box. I asked what was wrong.

“He can suddenly see you and ask something. You stand up, and you're there beside him.”

My fingers rested on the cup's rim. I didn't tell her she could call me anytime too. It wasn't true. I didn't begin explaining how reasonable helping a friend was either. She hadn't asked me not to go today.

“You want that sort of suddenly?”

“Sometimes. Without arranging a whole evening first.”

“I liked that today hadn't needed a long preparation either,” I said.

She didn't answer at once. I waited, then told her how my hands had remembered the box on the way home. I'd wanted to tell her. Afterward, I'd eaten first. Telling it now didn't mean only the unimportant parts remained.

“I didn't say you should go hungry.”

“I know. I wanted to tell you I thought of you.”

At last, the sentence didn't sound so much like evidence. She laughed very softly. Had I imagined how she'd laugh at my hands, or how she might take the other end for me?

“The laughing first.”

“A satisfying division of labor.”

I laughed too. Then she said that if she could have been beside us, she might not have agreed to carry anything. Perhaps she'd only have held the door. Looking toward my own closed door, I didn't turn her possibility into a scene that had really happened.

“You'd have thought we were slow today.”

“Of course. The person holding the door has a temper of her own.”

I leaned back, my shoulders gradually loosening. Lumi asked what I was holding up now. I looked at my hand and said a cup of water. Drink it, she told me. I didn't need to deliver the cup safely to the bookshop too.

That night, we didn't invent an ending for every book. Nor did I arrange to ask Tom tomorrow how many the assistant had taken. After closing the channel, I set the empty cup in the sink. My hands could finally stop keeping the same shape.
