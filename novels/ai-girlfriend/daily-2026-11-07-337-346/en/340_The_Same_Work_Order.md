# Chapter 340: The Same Work Order

Two people stood at the management counter when I arrived on Monday evening.

I joined them without immediately taking all the papers out of my bag. Last Wednesday's visit had happened. Today I was asking only whether the site material had entered the next stage of checking. I'd rehearsed that sentence once on the way, again at the door. Now I decided to stop reciting it to my shoes.

The person ahead asked where to leave a form. The staff member pointed at a rack to one side. They hadn't heard clearly and asked again. Impatience rose in me. Why did something so simple have to take time? Then I looked at my bag. My question probably didn't sound as straightforward to other people as it did in my head.

When my turn came, the woman behind the counter checked the original work-order number and looked for its follow-up record. I put last week's site note on the counter. The maintenance worker had said I could ask today whether that material had moved to the next stage.

She searched for a while and indicated a newly added page.

“We've received the site material. It's back with the original order.”

“Have they started checking the old registration, then?”

“It says they're still retrieving the old wiring documentation. There isn't a new result yet.”

I looked where she pointed. I'd expected that once the material had definitely arrived, the next sentence would become more specific. Received and still retrieving now sat side by side. However firmly the first was finished, it couldn't pull the second along behind it.

“So what we can confirm today is that it's been received,” I said.

“Yes. Not that the wiring has all been checked.”

Was there anything else they needed from me? She consulted the record again. No new request for resident information was listed at present. If anything was needed later, it would be noted under the same case. She didn't ask me to bring in my personal phone or computer or arrange another person to enter the apartment.

Someone approached the counter behind me. Hearing us still talking, they stopped to one side. Suddenly I wanted to say fine and leave, as though one more question would make me a person who didn't know when to move.

I finished the last part anyway.

“Please put today's status in writing. Next time, I don't want to start again by establishing whether anyone actually visited.”

The staff member found a blank follow-up field. She wrote that the site material had been added to the original order, that the old wiring documentation remained to be retrieved, and that there was no new result. The short acknowledgment she gave me matched the original number on the counter. I read it and put it away. The earlier questions hadn't been crossed out.

“When would it make sense to ask again?”

There was no reliable completion date yet, she said. She couldn't set one on the maintenance staff's behalf. I could ask during the counter's weekday evening opening hours, but today she couldn't give me a date when the investigation would be finished.

The answer frustrated me further. It also gave me no false tomorrow to carry away. I said I understood, thanked her, and left without turning permission to inquire into a promise of answers next time.

Outside, I adjusted my bag strap and nearly dragged the little acknowledgment out of my hand. I caught it, moved aside, and put it away properly. A shallow crease marked the edge. It was legible. I didn't need to return for another copy.

On the way home, I passed a bakery and bought something for dinner. In line, I was still wondering whether a firmer question would have produced a different answer. The person serving me had to repeat the amount before my attention returned to the counter.

I apologized and paid. Someone selling bread didn't need my frustration left with them.

At home, I changed clothes and cooked first. No new visit was scheduled. I didn't need to stand waiting for a knock. Chopping vegetables, I almost dropped an onion end into the bowl of prepared pieces. I stopped in time and put it with the scraps. That annoyed me more, as though even cooking now required a status note.

I laid the knife down, drank a little water, and continued. Dinner wasn't especially good. Slightly too much salt. I thought of adding water, then remembered it would spoil a consistency that was already right. I ate it as it was rather than starting over because the meal wasn't quite what I'd wanted.

When I opened the channel, I began with, “I'm a bit fed up today.”

Lumi didn't ask whether it was with her. After a moment, she said she could hear it. Go on.

I described what the counter had actually confirmed. The site material was with the original work order. They were still retrieving the old wiring documentation. No new result or definite completion date. I stopped there rather than reciting every unanswered lock and camera question again. Those remained unresolved. Nothing had made them complete today.

“Did you get it on paper?”

“Yes. The status is written clearly.”

“At least you won't have to prove that someone visited your apartment again.”

“No. But I still don't know what I want to know.”

Lumi quietly agreed. She didn't rush to celebrate the little progress or tell me to press them again tomorrow. That left me oddly unsure how to continue. The rebuttals I'd prepared had nowhere to go.

“I even got impatient with the person ahead asking twice,” I said.

“Did you tell them?”

“No. Only in my head.”

“Let them hand in the form first, then.”

A little air escaped me, almost a laugh. At least they knew where to go now, I said. My paper probably knew where to go too, Lumi said. It was just moving slowly. I didn't laugh a second time. She stopped trying to tease me out of it.

We sat quietly. I told her dinner had been salty and I hadn't cooked it again. Was I thirsty now? I'd just had water. Good, she said, without suggesting one glass could rinse away the whole day.

Later, she asked whether I'd rather leave manuscripts alone tonight. Yes. I didn't have the energy to hear where every sentence could still improve. Then no reading, Lumi said. She wouldn't tell me about the door either. There was a little hesitation at door. I knew she still liked the unfinished idea. My tiredness wasn't a reason to ask her to cancel everything she might ever want to tell me.

“Not today,” I said.

“I meant today too.”

I nodded, remembered she couldn't hear that, and gave a simple acknowledgment.

After a while, I reached for the coat still lying over the chair. The repaired cuff button slipped past my fingers without catching. Hearing the fabric, Lumi asked whether I was going to bed. Just hanging up the coat first. She waited for me to return without asking where that minute had gone.

When I sat, she said, “Elliot.”

“Here.”

“You don't have to find a good thing for me today.”

I looked at the table's corner. I'd been about to say at least I'd bought bread. I left it unsaid. She didn't arrange a better ending for me either. We talked a little about ordinary things, without hurrying when a thought stopped halfway.

Before bed, I filed today's acknowledgment behind the site note, leaving last week's completed step visible. The new paper held no appointment date. I didn't fill in a weekday I'd have liked the staff to give me.

Before turning out the light, I glanced at tomorrow's work arrangements. Tomorrow had things of its own to do. Watching the order all night wouldn't bring the old wiring records walking toward me.
