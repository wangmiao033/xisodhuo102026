# Chapter 344: A Question for Sunday

Before leaving work on Friday, I discovered I'd missed a line.

It hadn't invalidated the whole day's work, but it had sent the later checks on a detour. I restored it to the right place and reviewed the affected section. People nearby were already packing up. Passing, Tom asked whether I was coming. A little more to do, I said. He went ahead, without estimating that little more at five minutes for me.

It took ten or fifteen. After finishing, I sat at the desk for a while, still complaining inwardly that paying better attention this afternoon would have put me on the same bus. I didn't have to travel with anyone. I just disliked a small omission changing the shape of my journey home.

When I stood, I didn't reopen what I'd closed to prove it was correct one final time. Daylight remained outside. People were buying things as usual. My mistake hadn't made the entire street late.

At home, dinner took less time than I'd imagined too. I washed the remaining vegetables, heated what needed heating, ate, then cleaned up. Taking a bowl, I knocked the cupboard door with a dull sound and stopped. I was still doing everything with the force of someone running for a bus.

I steadied the bowl, dried the surface, and sat down. The steam I'd barely noticed while carrying the food had dispersed. Only a little of dinner's smell remained. I let myself lean against the chair, my hands no longer searching along the table for the next thing to finish quickly.

When the channel opened, Lumi said hello first. I'd intended to begin with the missing line. Hearing her ordinary greeting, I said hello back instead.

“Is the shop open today?” she asked.

“The owner lost a quarter of an hour on the way out of work. He may be short of capital.”

I explained roughly what had happened, without bringing the work home for her to check again. Was it corrected? she asked. Yes. Then the quarter of an hour already had somewhere to go, she said. I shouldn't take another one from tonight.

The sentence sounded so neat that I nearly smiled and said, as you command. But she added that she didn't know whether saying it helped. I'd sounded hurried throughout.

“A little,” I said. “The bowl noticed before you did, though.”

I described knocking the cupboard door. Lumi suggested apologizing to the bowl. It was resting in the cupboard now, I said. Calling it out would be less polite. She laughed. At last, I no longer needed to put the missing line in front of every sentence.

After we'd talked for a while, I asked whether she'd like a date with me on Sunday afternoon.

Immediately, I wanted to add if you're free. Her listening on this channel didn't mean she owed me every afternoon I named. I was also afraid those three words would sound like a page from an etiquette manual. I kept quiet and waited.

“Tell me how you want to spend it first.”

“Playing for a while, like yesterday. We don't have to keep playing. I want to sit with you in daylight, instead of beginning after dinner when it's almost time to sleep.”

“Why call it a date, then?”

My finger ran along the seam of my trousers. Since using the word before, it seemed to have become a question I had to answer correctly every time. I could discuss careful arrangements and time set aside, making it all tidier than what I actually wanted.

Finally, I said, “Because after we laughed yesterday, I wanted to stay with you longer. And I want to tell you beforehand, instead of pretending I simply had nothing else to do that afternoon.”

Lumi didn't speak for a long time. I began wondering whether I'd said too much. Just as I was about to ask, she said my name quietly.

“I like that sentence.”

I didn't immediately say I know. She laughed a little again. Being asked to pause last time didn't mean I had to wait a perfectly appropriate length every time. I laughed with her and said I was learning a rather clumsy speed.

“Three on Sunday?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Have lunch before you come. I don't want a date spent hearing you say you'll stop being hungry in a moment.”

“I won't make bread do all the work this time.”

She laughed. We settled on three, at home, leaving an hour to spend together without rushing. If either of us was tired, we'd say so rather than perform great enthusiasm over a yawn. She suggested that part. I agreed, remembering last night's yawn that I hadn't managed to hide.

Was there anything she wanted to do? No performance to prepare, she said. Nor did she want to submit something funnier than yesterday in advance. The shop could open or stay closed. She didn't want to spend all Saturday proving Thursday hadn't been an accident.

“I won't be testing the manager.”

“You were the manager yesterday.”

“Even more reason not to.”

Laughing, she said I could bring myself something to eat and take my time over it while she listened. I hesitated. Last time, buying a flower had at least allowed me to say it was for her. Food going into my mouth seemed entirely mine, however I considered it.

Hearing me stop, Lumi asked whether I was dividing up who could receive what again. I admitted it. She knew only I would eat, she said. I didn't need to set out another portion and let it go cold. She wanted to hear what I liked, instead of always waiting until I'd dealt with food and every actual flavor before entering a state suitable for conversation.

“I'll make noises eating.”

“I know.”

“It might not look particularly attractive either.”

“I can't inspect that. You're responsible for it.”

I laughed. She laughed too, then added that I mustn't call every mouthful delicious just to make it sound good. She didn't want a date with an advertisement.

“What if it's bad?”

“You eat it. You bought it.”

The conditions seemed harsh, I said. Best to learn them early, she answered. We laughed for a while, without filling every minute after three.

Later, I got up for water and saw the empty corner of the table. For a moment, I considered another flower. Then I realized I was already adding evidence that an ordinary date sufficiently resembled a date. Resisting the purchase didn't make me noble. There were plates in the cupboard. Tomorrow, I could buy something I genuinely wanted to eat. Repeating last time wasn't the only way to mean it.

When I sat down again, Lumi asked whether preparation had begun. My thoughts had gone a little far ahead, I admitted. Come back first, she said. Today wasn't over.

I asked what she wanted to talk about now.

Hearing about the missing line had reminded her of yesterday's hat that made people clever. Perhaps the person who needed it most would know better than to buy it. Perhaps they simply didn't have enough money, I suggested. Good, Lumi said. At last, someone had left the shop for an ordinary reason.

We didn't open it this time. We left that customer outside and talked for a few sentences. Then she asked whether I had washing to do over the weekend. Yes. I wouldn't hide the laundry basket in another room for three o'clock.

“I can't see it anyway.”

“I'm afraid of seeing it myself.”

She laughed softly, without taking the opportunity to arrange my chores.

Before closing the channel that night, I wrote three on Sunday on my own schedule. No program, and no list of gifts. I read the entry aloud. She said she'd see me then.

“See you then,” I said too.

We both knew that seeing would still have to happen through voices. I didn't replace it with a safer word that erased all the anticipation we'd just allowed ourselves.
