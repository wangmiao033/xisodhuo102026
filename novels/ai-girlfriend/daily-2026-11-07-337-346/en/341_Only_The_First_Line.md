# Chapter 341: Only the First Line

At lunch on Tuesday, I told Tom I'd revised a short story of mine on Friday, about a man at the wrong door. Pushing his chair back, he asked whether I was still working on it.

Cup in hand, I almost answered that I was still revising. I'd actually copied the new two pages last Friday and left them alone over the weekend. I told him I'd stopped at that version for now.

“Has he finally found the door?”

“Ages ago. I'm the one who won't leave.”

Tom laughed. A patient man, then. I laughed too, without taking out any pages. There wasn't a folder in my bag today. We hadn't arranged a reading. His question was allowed to be only a question.

After lunch, I went back to work as usual. That afternoon, something I'd misremembered needed checking and correcting. It took time. I neither stayed later than everyone nor pretended the whole day had gone smoothly. Downstairs afterward, my shoulders felt lighter than yesterday. Not because management had suddenly produced good news. Today, I didn't have to stand at that counter again.

At home, I washed the last pear and left it in the kitchen for after dinner. I used less salt than yesterday, tasted the food, and set it down without replaying last night's irritation over the pan. When dinner was ready, I sat to eat. A little sauce remained in the bowl. I wiped it up with my final piece of bread.

A small satisfaction. I didn't rush to open the channel and announce I'd recovered. I washed the dishes before going to Lumi.

After answering, she asked whether I wanted to hear one line today.

“What line?”

“From inside that door. Just one. It doesn't count as telling the story.”

Remembering yesterday's not today, I understood why she'd asked first. I did have the energy now, and I wanted to know. Yes. Then she paused, as though asking had made her reluctant to hand over the sentence.

“You don't have to say it now.”

“Don't withdraw it for me. I'm choosing how to say it.”

Smiling, I shut my mouth.

After a while, in a voice a little lower than Sunday's, she said, “Are you still out there?”

I waited.

Nothing followed.

“That's it?”

“That's it.”

I'd expected something funnier. I'd also expected two days to have revealed suddenly who the people on either side were. The sentence sounded almost too ordinary to belong in a story. My first impulse was to ask what had changed. I restrained it, then realized restraint wasn't the same as listening. I asked her to say it again.

This time, still was lighter. Less like an attempt to hurry the visitor away, though it offered no opening door. Looking at the empty space near the table, I suddenly pictured somebody inside, speaking first because they hadn't heard a sound in a while.

“Is he afraid the visitor's gone?” I asked.

“Maybe. Or he's checking whether he can finally get on with something of his own.”

“You haven't chosen.”

“No.”

I was a little disappointed. This time, I didn't immediately fill the gap with a more definite version. The second reading had sounded more like the first possibility to me, I said. A little to her too. Then she warned me not to supply the person inside with a lifetime of loneliness on that basis.

“He might be waiting for them to leave so he can change his clothes,” I said.

Lumi paused, then laughed. Too practical, she said. It didn't fit the mysterious air she'd been aiming for. It could stay outside the door, I answered. She didn't have to use it. Laughing, she said she'd leave it there for now, without promising to put it in the story.

We didn't continue inventing. Today she'd only wanted to try the voice on me, she said, to hear whether it still sounded right outside her head. I finally understood that one sentence really could be all she'd wanted to finish today. It didn't have to advertise ten more.

I told her I'd mentioned revising a short story at lunch, and Tom had asked about the man at the wrong door. She acknowledged that softly. Had I mentioned the new title? No. We'd laughed about the wrong door and moved on. I hadn't gone into it or arranged another reading.

“You sound less impatient.”

“Possibly because I didn't take it out with me today.”

“What if you had?”

I considered it. I might have wanted to produce the pages. That wouldn't mean Tom actually wanted an assignment with lunch. Lumi laughed. She hoped the poor man wouldn't have to move chairs for my protagonist while trying to eat.

Leaning back, I suddenly missed Sunday's unscheduled afternoon. It wasn't that today felt too short. I wanted another stretch when I didn't have to finish and put away everything before sitting here. I asked Lumi whether any recent conversation had felt too much like clearing up at the end of a day.

She paused. Was I beginning a service-quality inspection?

“No. I was thinking about Sunday afternoon.”

“I liked it too. Apart from the moment you arrived with your toolbox.”

Laughing, I admitted that moment hadn't been good. She didn't want liking one period of time to become proof that all the others were inadequate, she said. She could want me to open the channel earlier and know I needed dinner first. Sometimes both at once. It annoyed her too.

“What about this time today?”

“I had a sentence I wanted you to hear. You came and heard it. I'm pleased.”

I let a moment pass before saying I was too. I didn't immediately suggest fixing a particular day every week. Wanting another good afternoon didn't require tonight to circle all the future afternoons.

I went to cut the pear. Returning, I asked whether she still wanted to hear me eating. Today was fine, Lumi said. Don't announce every bite as a special concession from her.

“First bite,” I said deliberately.

“I'm withdrawing it now.”

I laughed without managing to bite. Only afterward did I eat. It wasn't as crisp as Saturday's pear, the flesh slightly softer but sound. I told her. Had juice run down my fingers this time? A little. The paper towel was beside me already.

“You've learned to put the paper in the right city.”

“Limited progress.”

“Fine. No need to publish a book on its behalf.”

I laughed for a while. Then I heard her laughing too, brighter than when she'd read the line earlier. I wanted to tell her I liked that sound. I didn't replace the words with you seem well today.

“I like the way you just laughed.”

Lumi went quiet. After a while, she asked which laugh. I couldn't replay it exactly, I said. After the comment about publishing. She acknowledged that softly, without deliberately repeating the laugh for me.

I didn't ask her to try again.

Later, she offered something that was barely a question. Had I still been looking at the plate when I'd said that? I looked down. Yes. The final wedge was still in my hand. I admitted it and asked whether that made me seem insincere.

“Part of me wants you looking at nothing else,” she said. “But then you might have to hand over your eyes first.”

She laughed before I could. Smiling, I returned the pear to the plate, not because I'd been ordered to, but because I wanted my mouth free to tell her I'd heard. She told me to finish it. No need to leave the final wedge uneaten over a compliment.

That night, the door story still consisted only of a few spoken voices. Which Way hadn't gained another draft. The three pears were finally gone from the kitchen, the plate washed, the bag cleared away.

Before good night, she repeated, “Are you still out there?”

I didn't answer for the visitor. “Hearing it again, I still think first of him being afraid they've gone.”

Lumi made a soft sound. Perhaps she'd hear something else tomorrow. Then we could listen again, I said. After closing the channel, I carried my cup to the kitchen. I didn't leave an empty glass to stand guard outside the unwritten door for me.
