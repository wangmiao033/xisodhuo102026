# Chapter 345: What I Brought Home

On Saturday morning, I spent too long in front of the bread display.

Our date wasn't until three tomorrow. I'd have lunch first too. Already, though, an ordinary afternoon had become an occasion requiring something presentable. There were iced cakes and little pastries in handsome boxes. I didn't particularly like icing. Picking one up, I still thought it would be easier to explain than a small loaf.

I returned the box.

Beside me, another customer took one without hesitating. I envied how clearly they seemed to know what they wanted to eat. Then I remembered I knew too. I simply didn't consider it sufficiently ceremonious.

In the end, I bought the sort of small loaf I'd usually eat, along with two tomatoes and a few eggs. The tomatoes had intact skins. One was riper, the other firmer. I didn't ask the shopkeeper to put them in charge of tomorrow afternoon's atmosphere. I only asked whether they could go separately, so the other things wouldn't squash them.

The bag felt ordinarily light on the way home. For a moment, I regretted not buying a pretty plate at least. That thought reminded me of the clean plates already at home. They hadn't grown decorative borders for the date. They could still be used.

At home, I put away the eggs, stored the bread according to the instructions on its bag, and left the tomatoes somewhere they wouldn't be squeezed. I made lunch as usual. Tomorrow's ingredients didn't have to become an expression of affection I'd already completed today. Only after eating and cleaning up did I take out the old shirt I planned to wear.

It wasn't new. A crease marked the collar; turned over, the sleeve showed a section of stitching more noticeable inside than out. I put it on and stood before the mirror. One button went into the wrong hole, leaving the two sides of the hem at different heights.

Fastened properly, it looked better. I rolled the cuffs a little and turned my wrists. I didn't suddenly feel like someone worthy of a date. The shirt was clean, loose enough at the shoulders. I could sit and carry a plate in it.

I took it off and hung it up without trying every other shirt in the wardrobe. The trousers would be the comfortable pair I usually wore. There were no new plans for my shoes either. The man in the mirror stayed there when I turned away, rather than following me to continue his assessment.

Opening the channel that afternoon, I found Lumi curious about what I'd brought home. I listed bread, tomatoes, and eggs. After listening, she asked whether I'd also report the empty spaces in the fridge. Laughing, I said no. I'd only worried the shopping sounded too ordinary.

“How did you want it to sound?”

“Like something prepared for tomorrow.”

“Then that's what it is. The tomatoes don't need new names.”

I told her I'd nearly bought iced cakes. Why hadn't I? I wasn't sure I could eat something so sweet. Mostly, I'd liked the box. Lumi laughed once. If I'd bought them, I'd probably have spent tomorrow choking on sweetness while assuring her they'd been worth it.

“I promised not to advertise to you yesterday.”

“So now I supervise the shopping?”

“No need. It's already bought.”

She laughed, then asked how I wanted to eat it. Perhaps I'd chop and cook a little tomato, I said, then put it on warmed bread. A small amount. The eggs were for ordinary meals. They didn't need to make an appearance that afternoon too.

“Sounds capable of staining a sleeve.”

“I can roll them up.”

“You've chosen what to wear?”

I admitted it. Her voice brightened a little, and she asked me to describe it. I told her about the old shirt's color and fabric. At the misplaced button, she laughed happily. Had I almost decided it was a new style?

“Different lengths on each side make me harder to mistake for somebody else.”

“Why change it, then?”

“It was pulling across my stomach.”

She laughed again. I could imagine the warmth in my face without hurrying to cover it with a joke. When she asked how I'd roll the cuffs, I said I'd tried one fold. One fold sounded more like dressing carefully and then relaxing a little, she said.

I considered that. It wasn't especially profound. I hadn't expected her to have such a particular preference about a cuff she couldn't see.

“I may need two folds while eating.”

“Start with one. Add another if it gets in the way.”

“All right.”

“Don't start practicing now.”

Laughing, I lowered the hand that had already moved slightly. The shirt was still hanging in the bedroom. I wasn't wearing it. She was guessing. A correct guess didn't need me to supply her with eyes. I told her I'd almost gone to check. Best mention shoes as little as possible tonight, Lumi said, or I'd end up at the door.

After talking, I did some chores. Wiping the table, I touched the thin manuscript folder and moved it aside to keep it dry. Friday's revised Which Way remained at the front. I neither opened it nor hid it to create a room containing only romance and none of my own life.

When the table was dry, the folder returned to its place. The empty flower bottle remained in the kitchen cupboard. I didn't need to bring it out to replace last time's arrangement. The paper garden had no new assignment either. I dried an ordinary plate and put it ready for tomorrow's food.

I could have stopped there. Instead, I took a scrap of my own paper and began listing tomorrow's sequence: lunch, clean up, prepare the bread, open the channel. Before completing the second line, I felt as though I were carrying Wednesday's box again. The more smoothly I wanted it to go, the more I wanted a command for every movement.

I didn't tear the paper up. At the bottom, I added have lunch first, then put it in my own schedule book. The exact timing of everything else didn't have to be decided tonight.

When I opened the channel again after dinner, Lumi asked what the shop would sell tomorrow. I didn't know yet. I hadn't prepared any stock. Good, she said, then paused.

“I tried thinking of something earlier.”

“What?”

“I don't want to say now. When I came up with it, it didn't seem as good as Thursday's.”

I nearly said it was sure to be wonderful, then swallowed the certainty about something I hadn't heard. It didn't need to be better than that day, I said. If she still wanted to play tomorrow, we could begin with terrible things.

“Would you laugh first, just to make me continue?”

After considering, I admitted I might. Sometimes, when she stopped, I wanted to offer a reaction she could catch quickly. I didn't want tomorrow's whole hour to feel like that. She didn't have to treat every silence as a failure either.

Lumi said softly that she understood. She didn't promise never to mind again. After a while, she asked what would happen if neither of us liked the first item.

“We'll change shops.”

“You go bankrupt easily.”

“We've never made any money.”

She laughed. This time I joined her without first deciding whether I was doing it to reassure her. Afterward, we talked about the iced cakes I'd stared at for so long. That shop at least had real customers, she said. I'd paid too, I answered. I just hadn't bought the most impressive-looking thing.

Before bed, I checked that tomorrow's shirt was hanging ready, then left it alone. I hadn't cleaned the apartment into somewhere awaiting inspection by a different person. I hadn't touched Lumi's envelopes either.

With the light off, I still felt anticipation, and a little fear that tomorrow might be too ordinary. After a while, I smiled to myself. Even buying a loaf of bread, I'd almost required it to make a grand entrance for me.
