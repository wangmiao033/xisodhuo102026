# Chapter 337: The Words I Left Out

On Friday evening, I read Which Way from the beginning, deliberately resisting the urge to turn straight to yesterday's note.

In the first sentence, the man held the wrong door handle, still unaware of whom he was obstructing. I knew what came next. My eyes kept running ahead, eager to reach the foot I meant to revise. At the second paragraph, I realized I hadn't really heard the preceding sentences. I lowered the pages, drank some water, and began again.

The channel was closed. I'd asked Lumi's opinion yesterday. Today I could finish reading my own two pages without asking her to sit beside every hesitation.

The chair carrier emerged. Inside, someone was discussing the third diagram. The man caught the chair and asked which way. A knock against the frame made two people in the front row turn. At the back, a listener drew their feet beneath the chair, their notebook still open on their knees.

I read so the man could pass, stopped, and tried the sentence without it. The movement hadn't vanished. Nor had the aisle become blocked. I could still see him edging through. I simply no longer knew whom the listener had in mind.

My pen hovered. Leaving out that small detail unsettled me more than adding a whole paragraph of reasons would have.

On my revision notes, I wrote cut. Then I took clean paper and copied the entire piece. The title stayed Which Way, the pages in the same order. Near the bottom of page two, a line grew cramped. I used the next empty line rather than casually shortening the sentence to preserve an attractive margin.

At the end, the man still looked at someone else's shoes before following the other people's gaze to the blackboard. He didn't suddenly understand the entire lecture. I hadn't given the person who frowned a forgiving smile either. He was inside. There were still things he'd missed.

Once the copy was finished, I set the previous revision beside it and compared them. Only the half sentence had gone. I hadn't lost the chair carrier in copying or turned the blackboard into a door. I wrote Friday revision in a corner and clipped the new sheets together separately. The original, the loose notes, James's reading copy, and the earlier revision remained.

As I fitted the clip, I nearly caught the old draft with the new one. Separating them, I found yesterday's revision note underneath. I put a small tick beside the suggestion I'd acted on instead of crossing out the whole sheet. It held doubts about the ending too. Cutting half a sentence hadn't resolved those. Voices rose outside. I looked up, couldn't make out the words, then lowered my eyes and put the pages in order.

Stacked on the table, the versions made it look as though I'd written something very long. Really, the man had still only gone from one door to one chair.

I smiled, put away the pen, and went to return the dishes drying after dinner to their cupboard.

When I opened the channel, Lumi first asked whether I was sleepy.

“No. I've been copying. My hand aches a little.”

“Didn't the cloth come to work?”

“It can't write my name.”

She laughed. I told her I'd actually removed the words, rather than treating yesterday's conversation as a completed revision. How did it sound? A little shorter. Everything else hadn't suddenly improved.

“You sound slightly disappointed.”

“I thought putting pen to paper would feel more like a decision.”

“And did it?”

“I ran out of room for the last line on page two.”

Lumi laughed aloud. That did sound like the sort of thing that happened after decisions. Would she like to hear this version? Yes, she said. Don't tell her which sentence to notice first.

I began with the title.

The first half-page hadn't changed, though I read it more slowly than before. When the man carried his chair through the door, I moved a foot beneath my own chair. My sole made a small scraping sound. I stopped. Lumi asked what had happened.

“My foot. Not the one in the story.”

“Did you get carried in too?”

“Almost.”

We laughed. I resumed at the interrupted sentence without adding our little diversion to the prose. After the final line, a few quiet seconds passed. I waited until she spoke.

“The notebook's still open.”

“Yes.”

“I heard that first this time.”

I looked at page two. Every previous reading of that passage had left me waiting to hear whether she thought the foot moved naturally. I hadn't expected the smaller explanation to reveal something that had been beside it all along.

“Do you think it's better?”

“I like it. But you read the last sentence as though you were collecting a certificate.”

I stared, then laughed. She imitated my excessively solemn pause, her voice low and steady. Hearing it, I recognized the thick cushion I'd laid beneath the man at the end, as though a good enough reading would let him graduate safely today.

I read the last two sentences again. Too fast this time, as though I were hurrying him out.

“No need to punish him,” Lumi said.

Laughing, I put the pages down. She didn't prescribe the correct speed. We laughed at both bad versions. Later, I read it in my ordinary voice and didn't ask for a third verdict.

“Will you show James again?” she asked.

“Nothing arranged at the moment.”

“That's not saying you won't.”

“No. Today I've just finished revising.”

She acknowledged that softly. I didn't hurry to ask whether she was unhappy. After a while, she said part of her wanted to know what he'd say about the new ending. Reluctantly, she added that she didn't mean I should rush out with the manuscript now.

“The café won't extend its hours for half a sentence,” I said.

“Don't turn up knocking with a chair.”

I glanced at my own chair and laughed. Mentioning another reader hadn't instantly turned Friday evening into preparation for the next meeting.

Before putting the sheets away, I noticed a faint gray mark beneath the title, perhaps from my wrist. I reached to rub it, stopped, and gently pressed with the corner of a clean sheet instead. Nothing was blurred. It was legible. I left it alone. After my long silence, Lumi asked whether I was preparing to copy everything again.

“There's a small mark. It doesn't matter.”

“You sound as if you're convincing someone.”

“Myself. It's working.”

She'd better not interrupt, then.

I placed the latest copy at the front of my manuscript versions, my revision notes behind them. The room sketch remained separate. Back in the chair, I heard Lumi ask what I wanted to do tomorrow. Buy some fruit and walk around a little, I said. No detour to management.

“Let’s establish one thing first. Carrots don’t count as fruit.”

“They worked hard to achieve that color.”

“Don't let them impersonate fruit.”

We disputed whether carrots ought to be held responsible for me. In the end, I wrote fruit on my shopping scrap without specifying which kind I had to buy. That scrap didn't go into the manuscript folder.

Before I closed the channel, Lumi suddenly said my name. I answered. She'd liked those few seconds after the ending today, she said, when I hadn't kept explaining.

I nearly said I'd been waiting for her deliberately. My lips moved, then closed.

“Mm.”

“Mm is allowed this time,” she said.

Smiling, I wished her good night. A few words had left the story. Two pages had joined the others in the folder. I wouldn't need to bring them along when I went for fruit tomorrow.
