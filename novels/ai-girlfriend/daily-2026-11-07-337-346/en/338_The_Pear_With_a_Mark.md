# Chapter 338: The Pear With a Mark

Before leaving on Saturday, I almost put the manuscript folder in my bag.

My hand reached its edge before I remembered I hadn't arranged a reading with anyone. I withdrew it, taking only my shopping scrap and the things I needed. Hearing that I was going for fruit, Lumi asked whether I should bring some for the chair. The chair would stay home, I said. It already had a reserved seat. She wished me luck finally buying something that wasn't a carrot.

I closed the channel and went out. The wind downstairs was stronger than it had sounded at the window, cool against my forehead beneath my recently cut hair. Without deciding how far I'd walk, I followed the street with the shops.

Several boxes of pears stood at the fruit stall. I recognized the shape and understood the price. Still, I turned the first one over several times. A faint mark ran across its skin, unbroken and firm. I put it back and chose another, which had a darker patch near the stem.

Seeing me, the seller said I could choose my own, but not dig my nails in. I looked down. My thumb hovered as though every pear had to pass an examination.

“I haven't.”

“All right. Take your time.”

He turned to weigh somebody else's purchase instead of continuing to watch. The comment hadn't been harsh. I wanted to prove I knew how to buy fruit anyway. I chose two of the smoothest-looking pears, then picked up the first, lightly marked one again.

Together on the scale, they looked less different than they had in my hand. I paid, took the paper bag, and smiled a few steps away. Nobody had required me to rescue an unsellable pear. No pear was waiting to teach me a lesson through its imperfections. I'd bought something I wanted to eat. One of them had a mark on its skin.

A low wall offered somewhere to sit farther along the road, with several people nearby waiting for someone. Rather than sit among them, I walked toward a quieter stretch. The bag's base felt cool against my palm. The fruit's weight pulled gently at my wrist.

I'd expected to want to go home as soon as I'd bought it. Now that I was out, I wanted to walk farther.

Passing a closed shop, I stopped to read the opening hours in the window before remembering I had no intention of entering. The glass reflected a man carrying pears, calculating whether a door that had nothing to do with him ought to be open today.

I turned and continued.

At the second junction, someone hurried past with a takeaway bag, their shoulder nearly colliding with mine. I stepped aside. My paper bag brushed my coat but stayed in my hand. The person looked back and apologized. I said it was fine. We went our separate ways.

Nobody recognized me on that stretch. No exchange demanded to be carried home as a fresh story. By the time I reached my building, my legs felt warm. The bag still held three pears. I'd enjoyed going out without urgently needing a better reason for it.

In the kitchen, I put the pears down and washed one. Water made the faint mark clearer. Cutting it open revealed no spoiled patch underneath, so I sliced it into wedges, skin and all. The knife made a brief sound through the flesh. Juice ran down my finger at the first bite.

I went for a piece of paper towel, looked in the wrong place, and returned to find it at the other end of the chopping board.

When I opened the channel, Lumi asked how the expedition had gone.

“I bought three pears.”

“Worth announcing.”

“I've eaten a little. Quite sweet.”

“Only quite sweet?”

Looking at the remaining wedges, I found myself stopped by a simple question again, as on the evening of my haircut. She didn't want the nutritional information. My first instinct was still to distinguish levels of sweetness accurately.

“A little crisp when you bite. The first bite had a lot of juice. I didn't catch it.”

“Where did it go?”

“Down my finger. Not into my sleeve.”

“You sound particularly thankful for the second part.”

I laughed and told her I'd gone the wrong way looking for something to wipe it with. She laughed too. Proof that I'd bought something other than a document requiring management, she said. It had made the first move.

I put the plate on the table and sat to eat another wedge. Hearing my silence, she asked whether I'd gone to wash my hands again. I was eating, I said. My mouth wasn't free yet. All right. The next time I reached for a piece, she said my name before I picked it up.

“Yes?”

“Nothing. I wanted you to wait a moment.”

My hand paused beside the plate. She laughed softly. I smiled too, without immediately abandoning the pear.

“Did you choose that moment on purpose?”

“A little.”

“Then I'll finish this piece before I listen.”

I ate it. She didn't keep calling while I chewed. After I'd swallowed, she said it had felt unfair. The pear had been here for half an hour, and already it could make me wait for it.

It sounded like a joke, but wasn't entirely one. Looking at the plate, I didn't dodge into a discussion of sweetness.

“It doesn't keep calling my name,” I said.

Lumi went quiet. Then she made a small sound and said that sentence wasn't sweet. I knew, I answered. Being interrupted hadn't been especially sweet either.

She didn't pretend she'd only been teasing and I'd taken it too seriously. After a moment, she asked whether I'd finished. Two wedges remained. I wanted to eat them while telling her about the walk, pausing when I took a bite.

“Go on, then. I won't compete for now.”

I described walking farther than planned and lingering at a closed shop to study its opening hours. What had I wanted to buy? Nothing. Lumi laughed properly and suggested taking a scrap there next time to ask the door whether it could stop interfering with my shopping.

At the near collision, I didn't turn the passerby into a rude person. They'd apologized. We hadn't argued. Were the pears bruised? I hadn't checked at the time. The one I'd cut at home was fine, and the other two showed no new marks from the outside.

She paused, then said she'd wanted me back sooner. There hadn't been anything important to discuss. Hearing me say I'd walk around yesterday, she'd imagined the time it took to buy fruit and walk home.

“So did I,” I said. “Once I was out, I wanted to go a little farther.”

“I know. You don't need to predict the exact junction in advance.”

Since she'd reached that sentence herself, I didn't explain again why I couldn't. When I'd eaten the last wedge, I took the plate to the kitchen and washed the stickiness from my hands. Returning, I announced that my fingers were no longer tacky.

“Can you listen to something now?” she asked.

“Yes.”

The door without a bell had become more difficult in her head today, she said. The person inside wouldn't emerge, but would ask whether the visitor had really meant to visit. Listening, I felt a slight tightening in my chest. Before I could speak, she said I didn't have to guess immediately whom she meant.

“I was guessing.”

“So was I, a little. I haven't decided.”

We both laughed once. She didn't continue into a story. I didn't take over responsibility for getting the door open. If there was time tomorrow, we could talk about the idea again. She couldn't promise to have found the next line.

Later, I put the other two pears somewhere suitable in the kitchen instead of arranging them at the table's empty flower corner. That night, fetching water, I saw the clean plate had dried. The faint mark had been eaten with the pear. It hadn't left today's events with evidence that needed preserving.
