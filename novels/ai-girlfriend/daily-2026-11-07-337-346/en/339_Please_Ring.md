# Chapter 339: Please Ring

On Sunday afternoon, I brought the thin story collection to the table, opened it, then closed it again.

I did want to read. But as I turned to the bookmark, I was already using a book to fill time before asking how Lumi wanted to spend it. The third story's title was there. We hadn't begun the text. I closed the book and let that remain true for now.

The clothes I'd washed earlier were hung up. I'd eaten lunch. Wiping the table, I found a watery patch I'd left behind and wrung out the cloth properly before clearing it. The bare surface left me slightly unsure what to do. The paper garden remained in its place, the blue bench in its matchbox. I didn't bring them out to furnish the afternoon.

When I opened the channel, Lumi asked whether I'd gone for another walk. No. Some things around the apartment that morning. I'd nearly started reading just now.

“Nearly?”

“I wanted to ask you first.”

“If I wanted to do nothing, could you sit still?”

“I could try.”

She laughed. We really didn't begin anything immediately. I heard a small rustle as I pushed up my sleeve, decided the movement didn't warrant reporting, and put my hand down. Lumi didn't ask either.

After a while, she said, “Please ring.”

I automatically looked toward the front door before remembering yesterday's nonexistent one.

“There isn't a bell.”

“Then why are you knocking?”

Quick and impatient. I laughed. This person had certainly become harder to deal with today. She hadn't worked out who it was, Lumi said. Perhaps the person inside didn't remember that they'd never installed a bell.

“The visitor could say, I'm here to fit one.”

I heard my own excitement as the words came out. An easy next step had appeared: the door opened, a toolbox came in, and the difficulty became a job. I was already imagining the look on the person inside when they discovered the visitor hadn't brought any tools.

Lumi didn't answer.

“Only an idea,” I added.

“I know.”

Her voice drew back. Looking at the blank paper beside me, I discovered I'd picked up my pen, though I hadn't written anything. Putting it down made the awkwardness clearer.

“You don't like it?”

“I haven't reached my second line, and you've made him a repairman.”

“I thought we were thinking together.”

“I wanted you to hear how annoying he was first.”

I leaned back. She hadn't forbidden suggestions or dismissed mine as terrible. It hurt a little anyway. I'd thought I'd helped. The door had finally begun moving in my mind.

“I'll take that line away for now,” I said.

“You don't have to pretend you didn't think of it. Just don't carry on writing it for me first.”

All right. I neither hid the pen nor took it up again to prove I was still ready. After a long silence, Lumi said the person inside would also ask why the visitor hadn't left.

“He says please ring, but doesn't want anyone to stay?”

“Maybe. He wants the person to know he's home. He doesn't want to open the door.”

This time, I didn't immediately ask why. She tried two versions herself. One sounded too hurt, the other like a deliberate trap. Neither pleased her. She stopped, saying she still preferred the opening line for now.

How many times had the visitor knocked? I asked. She hadn't counted. A second knock had sounded only in her head; she hadn't described it to me. I admitted I'd imagined the visitor waiting a long time already. Perhaps only a few seconds had passed, she said. Being told to ring made them feel long. We actually left those seconds empty, saying nothing. I began laughing and admitted I wanted to knock again. Not yet, she said. She wanted to hear how long the person inside could bear it.

I imitated her earlier tone. “Please ring.”

“Too polite.”

I tried again, pressing hard on every word. Lumi laughed. Now I sounded ready to approve visitors for the entire street. I laughed too. A third, casual attempt came closer to her original irritated, not-quite-awake delivery.

“That one's all right,” she said.

“At last.”

“Don't be proud. You haven't even got a bell.”

We laughed, but the uncomfortable part hadn't entirely gone. I still wanted her to try the repairman's version. She seemed to hear it, asking whether I was waiting for her to come back and use it.

“A little,” I admitted.

“It might be good. I don't want to go that way today.”

Looking at the shelf, I suddenly remembered my reluctance to change the ending of Which Way the previous week. I'd wanted Lumi to remain even if I didn't take her suggestion. Now someone else held the story, and I was quick to wonder what was left for me to do if she didn't want my line.

“Then we won't go that way today.”

“You sound as if you're granting leave.”

I gave an unhappy little smile. “I'm learning how to put a suggestion down without packing the rest of myself away with it.”

She answered quietly without declaring the lesson complete.

Later, fetching water, I saw the two pears remaining from yesterday. I asked Lumi whether she'd like to hear some pear-cutting, then laughed at myself. She wasn't sure she wanted a second performance from a pear, she said. I could eat if I wanted. I didn't need to organize entertainment before doing it.

I washed one, sliced it, put the pieces on a plate, and returned to my seat. She waited until I'd taken the first bite before asking whether it was still sweet. Yes. Yesterday's might have been crisper, though perhaps I'd simply cut this one thinner.

“I'm relieved you're allowing a pear an inconsistent performance.”

“It didn't enter the competition.”

“Is it too late?”

Laughing, I shook my head, then told her I was doing it. She'd heard the laugh, she said. It didn't need subtitles. I stopped explaining, finished a wedge, and asked what she'd like to talk about if we weren't doing stories today.

She wanted to know why I'd closed the book.

I explained the part I'd left unfinished earlier. After a pause, Lumi asked whether keeping her company always required something in my hands. Sometimes, I said. A book, a manuscript, a glass. They were easier than simply asking whether she wanted to spend time with me.

“Ask now, then.”

“Do you want to spend time with me?”

“We already have.”

I laughed. Then she added yes. Softly, without withdrawing the joke. I settled the plate securely instead of hurrying to find an activity to follow that sentence.

The afternoon didn't race past. We talked about my pointless study of the shop's opening hours, then argued over why a door that didn't want to open had such a serious voice for informing people of it. I suggested one possibility. She disagreed. She tried another and laughed before she could finish. We selected no ending.

Before dinner, I returned the collection to its place, the bookmark still at the third story's opening. I put away the blank sheet too. None of Lumi's lines had been written on it. Her half-formed idea remained in conversation, without even a name.

As I prepared to close the channel and cook, she suddenly said again, “Please ring.”

“I'm going out to make dinner, not coming in.”

“Then work something out when you come back.”

She was laughing. I agreed, then closed the channel as usual, without establishing a password I had to say on returning. Reaching for the pan, I was still smiling. At the difficult voice, not at having finally found a toolbox that would open her.
