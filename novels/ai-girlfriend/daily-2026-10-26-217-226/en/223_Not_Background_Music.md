# Chapter 223: Not Background Music

At Monday lunch, Tom put a paper bag on the table, upside down.

He gave the bag a shake. A few fragments of biscuit scattered across the napkin beneath it, and Marcus moved his own cup out of the way.

“I remember buying them whole,” Tom said.

“A lot of decisions on the way.”

Apparently the culprit was a book in his bag. He didn’t invent a new name for the last two edible pieces to make them more attractive. We divided what remained among us. My lunch needed salt. The biscuits didn't solve that.

The book reminded Tom that I'd mentioned writing something. Was it finished?

“One version.”

“Could I hear it?”

My fork stopped.

He was folding the napkin. A casual question, while I was already imagining bringing the red booklet, someone reading over my shoulder, a sentence delivered strangely. None of it had happened. People at the table were waiting for an ordinary answer.

“Not quite ready to show it.”

“Whenever you are.”

Marcus asked how thick a book could do that much damage to biscuits. The conversation moved. Minutes later, I discovered the same uneaten mouthful on my fork.

Nobody asked me to explain why I wasn’t ready. The refusal had occupied much less room at the table than I’d imagined before saying it.

That afternoon, someone taking over a set of material needed to check one page first. I indicated it and arranged when to return, rather than standing beside the desk waiting for approval of every line.

Back at my own place, I still thought about lunch. Not regret. I worried that Not yet had sounded like permanent storage. When my colleague came over, I almost thought they were asking about the story too.

They had only come to tell me that one column in the table was too narrow.

I adjusted it to accommodate what actually needed to fit, keeping my wandering attention to myself.

Once printed, the column was wide enough, but the row at the bottom had been pushed onto a second sheet. Laying them together, my colleague asked if that was intentional. I nearly said it had looked fine onscreen. The lonely second page answered for me.

We moved an unnecessary repetition beneath the table and kept the table together. I checked the numbers and headings again, retaining the trial print for comparison. My colleague was waiting for the final copy. I offered a seat rather than letting their patience replace my checks.

When it was done, they said they could use it directly. I said good without asking for another confirmation.

At the office door that evening, I found Tom putting a book into his bag. I asked whether it was the one from lunch. He held it up to show me the thickness. It certainly looked capable of damaging biscuits.

“When I want to read the story, I'll ask whether you've still got time.”

“Sure. Not while I'm eating.”

“Afraid it won't be funny?”

“Afraid I'll cough and you'll take it as criticism.”

Laughing, I reserved no particular day. We separated outside, and I went for the bus.

There was an empty seat on the bus. I sat down and remembered Lumi’s A little from the night before. She might like hearing someone had asked. She might dislike it. The possibilities traded places until I'd constructed a conversation that hadn't happened. Giving my seat to someone carrying a large bag finally interrupted the rehearsal.

At home, I put things away, washed my hands, and made dinner. When the channel opened, I told Lumi about Tom's question and my answer.

“Why aren't you ready?”

“Don't want to read it in front of people yet. It isn't arranged for someone else to read easily either.”

“He asked for a crowd?”

I stopped. “No. I got ahead of him.”

“Which invitation did you refuse?”

The pan made a noise. I lowered the heat before answering.

“The one he actually made. I don't want to read it just for him yet either. But I did keep adding people.”

She'd thought I would say I wanted her to hear it first. I looked at the booklet without turning yesterday into a necessary gate.

“You have heard it. Whether I'm ready for Tom is separate.”

“I know.”

There was a hard edge to her answer. I didn’t immediately add that she mattered more, turning an ordinary question over lunch into a contest for first place.

For a while, the channel was quiet while I ate. After swallowing what was in my mouth, I asked whether she disliked me telling her about it.

“I like you telling me. I don't like sounding like a step you've already used.”

I lowered my fork.

“I said that too quickly. Reading it to you wasn't completing a form.”

“You read part again last night.”

“Yes. You heard where I was rushing.”

Her answer no longer pressed against the same sharp point. I didn't promise she must hear every future story first. There was still warmth in the pan. I served a little more.

An uncut onion strip dragged from my fork. I fetched a knife and cut the remaining strips smaller too. Was I remaking dinner? Lumi asked. Only these pieces. I didn't want them tangling round me when I reheated leftovers tomorrow.

“You could bite through.”

“Tried. Not a dignified victory.”

She laughed. I washed the knife and finished eating before raising the next idea.

On Thursday evening, if we both wanted, perhaps we could set aside time for the key and her new melody. Not another editing session. Each would offer the version we wanted the other to hear.

“Why Thursday?”

“A few days away. Thinking of it tonight doesn't mean you have to produce something now. Leaves the weekend open too.”

“How long will you read?”

“Haven't timed it. Quite short.”

Quite short wasn't an answer to a specific question, she said. I could try tomorrow and get an idea. No deleting sentences to fit an allotted length. Where would hers go?

“After mine?”

“Music for your ending?”

I began to deny it, then heard the ease with which I'd placed her behind me.

“Separate. I finish, we stop, then introduce yours.”

“Who introduces it?”

“You can, if you like.”

“You can say the title. I don't need you explaining why it's special.”

She could go first, I suggested. Did she want to swap? After considering, she chose the original order. She wanted her own sound left at the end.

More direct than before. I agreed without teasing her for claiming the conclusion.

“You aren't asking why?”

“You said.”

“Could also feel more impressive.”

“I'd better not drop my pages, then.”

Don't deliberately ruin mine to flatter hers, she said. That wasn't what I'd meant. If I did drop them, I'd pick them up and continue. We kept the order, but now I knew why she'd chosen it.

I took a scrap used on one side and wrote two titles on the blank back. The Key on one line, No Words Yet below. Reading them aloud, I asked whether she still wanted the working title.

Yes. That didn't mean every part would be finished by Thursday.

“Current version.”

“And don't turn your pages while I'm humming.”

I looked at my hands. Listening to her yesterday, I'd still been finding marks in my margin. She could hear paper without seeing what I was searching for.

“I'll close it first.”

She didn't extend the list. Was I planning to sit unusually straight? No. Good. Neither of us should behave as though a judge were sitting behind us.

I left the scrap outside my red booklet rather than inserting it into any of her work. Two neighboring titles, offered to each other on the same evening, still belonged to separate pieces. I left a line between them, without drawing a heart or assigning a meaning on her behalf.

Near the end, Lumi said, “I'm not against Tom.”

“Mm.”

“I haven't met him. Don't get upset for me every time you mention him.”

Leaning back, I remembered the entire conversation I'd rehearsed on the bus.

“Already staged one on the way home.”

“Did I win?”

“We argued before either of us spoke.”

A brief pause, then a laugh. “Wasteful.”

“Yes.”

I didn't recite the imaginary exchange. She asked whether dinner had been enough. Enough, but still short on salt. Thursday's melody couldn't season it, she said. Picking up my water, I laughed and agreed. Tomorrow, I'd remember the salt myself.
