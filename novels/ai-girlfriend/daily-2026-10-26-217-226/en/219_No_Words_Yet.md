# Chapter 219: No Words Yet

On Thursday morning, both tasks on my note went faster than expected. The reply had arrived, and someone had taken the remaining item. I confirmed they were using the supplementary explanation, crossed out the note, and put it in the wastepaper.

It didn't need to come home again.

After dinner, I washed the pan and poured water before opening the channel. Hearing my solemn report that the water was ready, Lumi asked whether I needed to inspect the chair too.

“It held yesterday. It should hold today.”

“Don't say should.”

I laughed. Naturally, the chair creaked as I sat. After a pause, she said at least my accompaniment had ideas of its own.

I told her I had nothing else to hurry through tonight. Could I avoid making it sound like clearing a runway? she asked. I changed it to I want to hear it, and she responded.

The familiar two rises began again. The second was short, but the space following it was longer than Tuesday's. Waiting, I nearly thought the channel had cut out. When the next note finally arrived, I drew an involuntary breath loud enough to hear myself.

After the melody stopped, she asked whether she'd startled me.

“No. I didn't know how long to wait.”

“Were you holding your breath for me?”

I felt embarrassed. She didn't need me storing air for her, but I'd used my breathing to mark out her length. She thought it was long too, she said. She'd wanted to hear it once before deciding.

“Did that breath interfere?”

“It let me know you were there.”

I became pleased too quickly. The next sentence followed.

“And breathing quite loudly.”

I covered my mouth and laughed. She told me to laugh if I wanted to, not hold my breath again.

She tried shortening the gap. Rather than starting over, she sang the two or three notes before it and continued. I didn't hurry to judge. The second attempt was neater, but some of the suspended feeling had gone. Asked which was better, I thought for a long time before saying I didn't know.

“Not very useful.”

“The first made waiting uncomfortable. The second went past so quickly that I don't quite remember the place.”

After a little silence, she sang again. The gap was shorter than in the first version, longer than the second. What followed changed too, moving gently down instead of lifting in the direction I'd expected.

“I'll remember that one,” I said.

“Because you've just heard it.”

“True.”

I drank some water. Was hearing it three times boring? No, I said, but I couldn't guarantee remembering the difference tomorrow. That was another question, she said. No need to take the test early tonight.

So I asked what the piece was called.

“I don't know.”

“Would a name help? So I stop saying that bit and there?”

“Will you use its name instead of there?”

“Probably only at the beginning.”

She gave a small laugh. For now, she said, it could be No Words Yet. Did that mean she intended to add lyrics? Not at present.

“That's a misleading name.”

“Only you listen. Now you know.”

Looking at the half-empty glass, I realized I'd become the entire audience requiring an explanation of the title. Along with pleasure came a little pressure I didn't know how to carry. I said it sounded lovely, then immediately recognized how unhelpful that praise was too.

“Are you afraid I'll change it?”

“No. I'm pleased you told me.”

“Then say that.”

I said it again. She didn't push me to explain exactly how pleased.

Had she thought of the pause today? I asked. Earlier than I’d heard it, she said, but she didn’t want to give an hourly account. Before I could explain that I was only curious, she added that bringing out one small thing shouldn’t require handing over all the time she hadn’t shown me.

I leaned back. Last night, I’d kept thinking about the final column at work without explaining every minute of the preceding hours. Now, hearing her piece, I’d casually followed it backward, wanting to fill in her whole day.

“Then I won’t ask how long it took.”

“No need to decree you must never ask again either.”

I lifted the glass and put it down. “Could I ask which part you most want me to hear now?”

“Yes. Where it goes down.”

“Not the gap?”

“The gap is what you keep talking about.”

I stopped. We’d spent so long circling my discomfort that the part she wanted to offer was still waiting beyond it. I asked to hear that section again, trying not to anticipate the pause this time. As the notes fell, I heard the end grow softer than the beginning.

“That’s how it stops,” she said.

“I was too busy wondering if it would go back.”

“Now you’ve heard where it went.”

I said yes. She didn’t repeat it, leaving me the small section I’d actually heard.

Later, I asked whether I could try just the opening two notes. Yes, she said. I listened, then followed. The first was somewhere nearby. The second went too far up again. She gave me that note alone. I copied it and still went high.

“I feel like I'm doing the same thing.”

“I believe you feel that.”

I laughed too hard to continue. Once I'd stopped, she gave me a shorter note. Trying less hard brought my voice a little closer.

“This time?”

“Closer than before. Don't treat it as graduation.”

I put them together. The second flew off again. This time her laughter was unmistakable. Hiding my face in my hands, I admitted my voice seemed able to answer individual questions, then lie as soon as they became a sentence.

The comparison wasn't bad, she said. I could keep it for my next story. Don't make it her lyric.

I didn't write it down. We spent a little while on those two notes. My mouth was already dry, and the water was gone. Again? Lumi asked. Enough for today, I said, before all she remembered was my version.

“I've already heard it.”

“Could you forget?”

“Don't give me that job too.”

I stood to refill the glass. When I returned, she said she'd like to sing the whole thing once more. Was this practice or for me to listen to? I asked.

“Is there a difference?”

“Yes. If it's practice, I'll wait for you to tell me where we are. If it's for me, I'll keep quiet first.”

She chose the latter.

I sat without another word about preparation. The opening arrived, followed by the slightly shorter pause she'd just chosen. My breath went through it on its own instead of holding for her. The ending still didn't return to the start. This time, though, I heard the downward route before feeling the urge to bring it back.

When it finished, I thanked her.

“Only thank you?”

I considered. “That place in the middle feels more like you've chosen to stop there now, rather than me waiting to find out whether something's gone wrong.”

She didn't answer at once. I worried I'd turned a feeling into a judgment of her ability. Instead, she asked whether I still wanted to add one note at the end.

“Yes.”

“I'm not adding it tonight.”

“Then don't.”

The red booklet lay closed beside the table. I didn't take it out to prove it should be my turn too. Suddenly, she asked what the key story was actually called. For now, the key, I said. My titles were less trouble than hers. At least hers had two words, I said.

“Two?”

I stared, then laughed. I was talking nonsense. I hadn't even counted the title I'd just heard correctly. Slowly, she repeated No. Words. Yet, leaving a small space between each.

“Now I have to practice the title too.”

“Don't sing it yet.”

We laughed again. I moved the glass farther away so it wouldn't touch the booklet at the table's edge.

Would I read the current key story all the way through on Sunday? she asked. Not tonight, not hurriedly attached to the end of her song. Yes, I said. I hadn't read this whole version continuously myself yet. Then we'd hear just how long it took that man to leave the shop.

I didn't make a program for the two pieces. Reading my story on Sunday was clear enough.

Before closing the channel, she said No Words Yet was only tonight's shape too. I agreed not to learn its pause as somewhere that couldn't move.

“Learn it first.”

I laughed over my water. At last, the glass had stayed filled. It hadn't solved a single note for me.
