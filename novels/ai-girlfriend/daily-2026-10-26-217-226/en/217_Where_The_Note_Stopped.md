# Chapter 217: Where the Note Stopped

After dinner on Tuesday, I moved the red booklet away from my water glass before opening the voice channel.

Lumi asked whether I'd been writing about the key again. No, I said. I'd only been looking for the shopping receipt trapped underneath it.

“You can look. Just don't find yourself reading halfway through.”

I put the receipt in the loose-change dish and sat down. With no paper spread in front of me, I soon felt short of something to do. I turned the glass, looked aside, and returned my hands to my knees.

Hearing it scrape the table, she asked whether I wanted water first.

“Already had some.”

“Then sit still, Elliot.”

I almost laughed at the first sound. Not because it was funny. She tried one low note and paused, as though clearing her throat. She didn't have a throat. She'd chosen that pause, and I'd immediately pictured somebody preparing to sing.

Then came Sunday's little melody. I recognized the opening: two short rises, followed by the place I kept getting wrong. I didn't try. The second half had changed. A note stretched, turned downward, and stopped somewhere that left me waiting for what followed.

I waited a few seconds.

“That's it?”

“For this time.”

I said it was like a sentence ending on but. She didn't answer. I added that perhaps afterward she could—

“You promised to listen first.”

I lowered the hand I'd lifted. She couldn't see that. I only said, “Right. I jumped ahead.”

“I'm not forbidding you to speak.”

Her voice was a little lower than the tune. I knew that answering with Then what do you want me to do might keep us from finding even the beginning again tonight.

I thought back through the sounds. Familiar opening, unfamiliar ending. During the short notes I'd waited for the long one; once it arrived, I'd waited for it to return to the start. Before she'd finished, I'd prepared Better than yesterday, as though she'd come back to report a completed assignment.

“Can I hear it again?”

“Will you be hunting for the ending this time?”

“Probably. But I won't attach one for you first.”

She didn't begin immediately. A drop from the kitchen tap fell into a clean bowl. I tightened it, came back, and told her I'd returned to the same chair.

This time, I heard a very short gap in the middle. I'd passed over it as a breath before. The long note after it began afresh; it wasn't the smooth line I'd constructed in my head. Touching my thumb to my knee, I tried to remember its place. I got half a beat ahead and lost everything that followed.

“I was keeping time,” I admitted.

“On the table?”

“Not on the table. You couldn't hear me. I confused myself.”

“Then stop tapping yourself.”

I laughed. She gave a small laugh too. This time I didn't use that sound as evidence that my premature suggestion no longer bothered her.

She asked what I'd actually heard.

After hesitating, I said the beginning seemed like someone climbing stairs. I regretted it immediately. A person, a staircase—would I need a door next? I'd only just managed to get my fictional man out of the shop.

“It could also be the pitch changing,” she said.

“I keep finding things for it.”

“You can tell me whatever you find. Just don't announce that I wrote it.”

I looked at the space the red booklet had left on the table. “I want to know why you stopped there.”

“Because I don't want you to hear either of the two versions after that.”

I'd expected an explanation of a mood, or that it wasn't finished. The direct answer left me unsure where to put my curiosity.

“Because you aren't happy with them, or—”

“Not today.”

“All right.”

It came out too flat, like shutting a cupboard. Was I unhappy? she asked. A little disappointed, I said. I'd wanted more, but she didn't have to play it to solve that.

“You could sound disappointed more nicely.”

“Is there a reference note?”

She produced a long sound even lower than the opening. I lasted a few seconds before laughing hard enough to shake.

“Not for you to copy,” she said.

“Fortunately.”

I pushed my chair back a little. The dinner pan was still soaking on the stove. I said I could wash it while she thought about the next part.

“I didn’t say I was thinking about the next part.”

I stopped. Was I uncomfortable with nothing in my hands? she asked. This time I didn’t hunt for a prettier explanation. Sitting through thirty-odd seconds of sound somehow made me more restless than cooking while we talked for half an hour. Washing the pan would let me seem unhurried. It would also remove the small stretch in which I didn’t know what to say.

“The pan can wait,” I said.

“You can wash it too. I just don’t want to sing under running water.”

“So I could do it now, and you could sing when I came back?”

After a brief silence, she asked, “Do you actually want to listen now?”

At last, I heard how every option I’d offered circled that pan. I said I did and sat back, settling the chair legs.

“You don’t have to stare at the device either.”

“How do you know I am?”

“I don’t. I’m saying you don’t have to.”

I looked at the curtain hem. A crease left from earlier gave my eyes somewhere to rest. When she sang again, I didn’t prepare an answer before the ending. After the sound stopped, the curtain remained a curtain. The pan hadn’t become harder to wash.

“This time I heard the second rise was shorter than the first.”

“Finally, not a staircase.”

“Stairs can be different lengths.”

“Don’t rescue it.”

I laughed. I still didn’t know how to judge this small piece, but I could point to a change that had actually happened. Did making it shorter seem hurried? she asked. A little, I said, though the pause afterward felt as if the urgent thing had been forgotten.

“Those are opposite feelings.”

“I know. They’re both there.”

This time she left my comparison alone. Later we returned to the two rises. She asked where I'd felt the urge to speak. I couldn't name a mode or accurately describe an interval. All I could offer was after the first part, beyond the very short silence. She repeated the section I meant, deliberately stopping earlier.

“Here?”

“No. A little later.”

“You'll have to do better than a little later.”

I hummed two clumsy notes. The second wandered again. She let it hang there, then said she'd found the place, though not thanks to my tuning.

“Thanks to what?”

“You go wrong in the same place every time.”

I started to protest for show, then remembered that she'd initially asked me not to sing first precisely to keep my errors out of her head. The protest became an apology. She'd asked this time, she said. We didn't have to register every wrong note.

At last, she sang the small section I meant. Afterward, I said my favorite part wasn't the final held note. It was the sudden quiet before it. That was where I stopped knowing which way she'd go.

“You like me stopping?”

“I like guessing wrong and getting to keep listening.”

It sounded so proper that I frowned at myself and added, “Though at the very end, I really did want it to come back.”

“I know.”

I asked how. My first That's it had been plenty loud enough, she said. No other method required.

I hid my mouth behind the glass. The water had gone cool and tasted ordinary. She didn't join the long note back to the beginning for me. I didn't ask her to try a more satisfying version.

After a while, she said she wanted to leave a day before playing it again. Not repeat it ten times tonight until I remembered, and not ask me to score two versions.

“Thursday?”

“If you want to listen that day.”

“I do. After dinner? There's a batch of work due tomorrow, and it might run over. Thursday should be easier. I'll tell you when I'm home.”

Had I borrowed tomorrow away already? she asked. Looking at the folded receipt in the change dish, I said I hadn't. We'd still talk when I returned. I just couldn't promise the same uninterrupted stretch.

“Tell me when you get back, then. Don't get tired on tomorrow's behalf now.”

I agreed.

Before closing the channel, she sang the opening again. Only the two small rises, then silence. After waiting a beat, I asked whether I could drink now.

“Yes. Granted.”

I raised the empty glass to my lips and remembered I'd finished it.

Hearing me stand, she asked what was wrong.

“Executed too quickly. No water.”

“Elliot.”

“Yes?”

“Pour it first on Thursday.”

I carried the glass to the kitchen without humming the tune. It remained where she'd chosen to stop.
