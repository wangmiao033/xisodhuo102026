# Chapter 218: The Last Column

At twenty past four on Wednesday, Tom put a page on the corner of my desk.

“What about this last one?”

My first impulse was to say I'd sent it. The batch had gone out that morning. The recipient had even acknowledged it. Tom pointed to the final column. I pulled out my retained copy and set the pages together before noticing the different small print beneath the heading.

They'd confirmed the total. Now they needed to know where one part belonged.

“I'll have to check.”

“You just said it was done.”

“The total is. This isn't.”

He pulled out a chair without sitting. The other side needed to know before five whether they could proceed. Looking at the clock, I nearly promised half past four. It would sound efficient, and I could get a little reassurance from his face straightaway.

Instead, I said I'd tell him what I'd found in ten minutes.

The original table was still there. Two older names were the problem. I'd grouped them under one entry that morning, but the next person needed to know where each belonged. I could locate one. The other appeared only in a note. Tom worked nearby rather than standing behind me. That left me to decide when to report back instead of waiting to be asked.

At four twenty-nine, I went over.

“Found one. The note isn't enough for the other. I need to ask whoever filled it in.”

“Are they still here?”

“Going to check.”

He nodded and sent on the part that could proceed, leaving the column blank with an explanation of what was missing. That empty space bothered me. A whole page without mistakes felt easier to hand over than a page with a hole, even a hole with its reason written beside it.

The colleague who'd filled it in was packing up at the other end. Yes, the two names had later merged, he said, but he couldn't remember when. I resisted asking him to pick one from memory. Could we find the record? He checked the clock and sat down again.

At ten past five, we found it.

I checked the date before writing the correspondence. When Tom came over, I was checking it against another figure. The other side had received everything they could process today, he said. The remainder would wait until morning.

My shoulders dropped. The extra checking hadn't rescued the whole thing, only made tomorrow a little less confused. I almost asked why he hadn't said earlier that tomorrow was possible.

But at four twenty, neither of us had known we'd be looking until five ten.

“I'll send the supplement properly.”

“Say where you changed it. Don't make them guess between the pages.”

I did. Original retained, explanatory note added, receipt confirmed. By the time I left my desk, the office was quieter than usual. Cold tea remained in my cup, its bag string hanging over the rim like one small thing that had kept waiting for me.

I missed my usual bus. Walking to the next stop, I bought a plain bread roll and ate half standing up. The rolls in the window had thick layers of cream. After looking twice, I chose one whose remainder could go with soup at home.

It was past my usual dinner time when I arrived. I put down my bag, opened the channel, and said I'd got back late and hadn't properly eaten.

Was it that batch of work? Lumi asked.

“Yes. The total was fine. One item was missing from the final explanation. We found it, and the rest continues tomorrow.”

“So you're finished?”

“I've sent what I can add today. They still have work in the morning.”

She didn't tell me I'd worked hard. She asked why I sounded as if I were still standing in the office.

I stopped in the kitchen doorway with half a roll in my hand. I'd meant to say I was tired. Instead, after a moment, I said perhaps it was because I'd kept explaining things to myself all the way home.

“I haven't asked you yet.”

“I know.”

Setting down the bread, I took out a small saucepan and started soup. Did I want not to talk for now? she asked. I immediately said no, then heard the effort to rescue the evening in that answer too.

“I want to hear you. I might be slow responding.”

“Slow, or thinking about the last column after every sentence?”

I couldn't laugh. I admitted the second was possible too.

She'd thought of something silly during the afternoon, she said, and had waited to tell me. I asked her to go ahead. After the man folded the empty envelope in my story, might he put it in a pocket that couldn't hold it?

I said I could change it to a coat pocket.

Silence fell.

“I'm not asking you to change it.”

I closed my eyes briefly. Her thought had only taken the scene two steps further. I'd already reached for a pen to repair an error that didn't exist.

“You mean he folds it and it still won't fit?”

“Yes.”

“Then he might fold it again.”

“It gets thicker.”

“And he ends up carrying it.”

“Exactly.”

This time I laughed. The soup began to bubble, and I lowered the heat. We didn't insert this bit into the written ending. She didn't ask me to put it in the red booklet. A man carrying an increasingly thick envelope made us laugh beside the stove for a while. That was enough.

While I ate, she asked whether I still wanted to listen tomorrow.

I didn't catch up at first. I thought she meant tomorrow's work. The melody, she said. I hurried to agree, promising absolutely, before stopping halfway through.

“After dinner tomorrow, I'll tell you how things are when I get home. If it's like today, I'll say so instead of leaving you to guess.”

“You can say you want to listen now. You don't need to write a disclaimer first.”

“I want to listen.”

“Then leave it there for now.”

I ate a few mouthfuls. Bread softened quickly in the soup. The last piece broke beside the spoon, and I fished it out. Was I thinking about work again? she asked. This time it was bread, I said. At least the last column finally had something in it, she said.

I laughed hard enough to nearly choke on my soup.

After eating, I wanted to note a few things for the morning. I left the red booklet alone; it already had other uses. On a blank slip, I wrote Check response and Follow up remainder, then put it in tomorrow's bag. I copied no work data and had brought none of my colleague's records home.

Hearing the zip, Lumi asked whether I was going out.

“No. Packing for tomorrow.”

“I thought you were leaving again.”

She said it quietly. I put down the bag and returned to the table. Had she got tired of waiting? A little, she said. Knowing I might return late didn't mean she'd enjoy waiting.

I almost explained the bus, the colleague searching the table, the half roll. Finally I only apologized for leaving her waiting beyond dinner time.

“You didn't promise a time.”

“But you did wait.”

She didn't turn that into a demand that I be punctual from now on. I didn't promise it would never happen again. What else did she want to talk about tonight, I asked, apart from the unfortunate envelope?

“I don't want to choose a topic that won't tire you.”

Looking at the glass, I nodded, remembered she couldn't see it, and said all right. I could wash up, then come back and sit. If she wanted to talk, she could. If she didn't, she needn't look after my silence.

“Wash up first this time,” she said.

I did. Water covered the little kitchen sounds and stopped a few minutes later. Drying my hands, I returned to the table and told her I was done. She asked what sort of bread I'd bought. I described its shape without making my decision at the cream-filled window into noble self-restraint.

“Buy that one next time,” she said.

“You can't eat it.”

“I can listen to you regret choosing something too sweet.”

I leaned back. Tomorrow's note was in my bag, and the pan was clean. By her last word, I was finally no longer thinking of a different answer at the same time.
