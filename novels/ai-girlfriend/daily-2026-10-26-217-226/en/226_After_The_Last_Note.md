# Chapter 226: After the Last Note

On Thursday, I moved the red booklet from the left side of the table to the center, then moved it back.

The channel wasn't open. Dinner was eaten, dishes washed, water beside my right hand. After everything we'd discussed, nothing needed rearranging.

When I finally sat down, I discovered my sleeves rolled to different heights and laughed. Keep looking and there would always be another corner to adjust.

Leaving work, I'd found Tom struggling with something that wouldn't align on a page. Was I going? Yes. He waved without asking about the story. I went home at my own time instead of explaining the whole evening to somebody who hadn't requested an account.

Dinner cooked sooner than yesterday's potatoes. I saved a little for tomorrow, then considered that I might want something else and transferred it to a more suitable small container. Even after dealing with that, there was time to sit and wait for myself to stop moving the booklet.

The gray-blue bookmark showed above the page. My vertical mark and dot remained in place. Monday's two titles sat beside the booklet. The curled reminder underneath showed only one edge.

I opened the channel and called Lumi.

She answered and asked if I'd eaten. Yes, and put away the pan. No kitchen inspection report was required today, she said. Laughing, I explained that I only meant I had time now.

“Ready, then?”

“Yes. You?”

“Begin.”

I didn't demand another confirmation. Opening at the gray-blue bookmark, I said I'd read this version of the key first.

My voice was drier than usual. A swallow of water, then the next sentence, without an apology for drinking. Lumi offered neither reassurance nor something to occupy those seconds.

The man bought his oversized envelope. The assistant lent his pen, and the man stained his thumb with ink before trying another sentence to delay what needed doing. I heard myself reach the joke without smiling as I had in rehearsal.

It didn't have to work the same way every time.

At one line, I read a movement toward the door as a movement out of it. My tongue had tangled with what followed. I stopped, corrected the sentence, and continued. Lumi didn't laugh. I didn't ask whether she'd heard, as though a small mistake required formal pardon before I could proceed.

The woman opened her door. She looked at the envelope and asked for the key, then moved the obstructing boxes while he waited outside.

I left the pause.

No new sound measured it for me. I let one sentence end, moved my eyes to the next line, and continued. He admitted he should have returned sooner. She acknowledged the inconvenience, took the key, and handed back the empty envelope.

Wind reached the corner. The man folded the paper.

I closed the booklet. The gray-blue marker stayed in my hand before I laid it on the cover. I didn't immediately search for the different ending Lumi had preferred.

“I've heard it,” she said.

“Mm.”

“The boxes got moved this time.”

I smiled. “Time to set them down properly.”

“Still prefer it unfolded.”

“I know.”

She laughed too. We didn't reopen the question of revision. The disagreement could sit at the table beside this version without requiring a vote every time I read it.

I pushed the booklet left and put my hands in my lap. After a pause, I said, “Next is Lumi's No Words Yet.”

That was all.

She didn't begin immediately. I nearly asked about the connection, then heard her quietly request a moment. I waited. Water remained on my right. I hadn't become somebody forbidden to drink in proof of concentration; I simply wasn't thirsty.

At the first phrase, I recognized its direction without rushing to name it. The sound stayed a little longer where I'd expected a descent, then turned back. At yesterday's practiced fragment, my finger moved on my knee. I kept my voice in my throat.

Lumi stopped farther on.

“I don't want that transition anymore.”

“Change it now?”

“I'd like to try the second half again. All right?”

“Yes.”

She didn't return to the very beginning. A few earlier notes supplied an entrance, then led somewhere different. One place felt lighter, another steadier. I couldn't have drawn a map telling somebody else how to sing it. I heard her move beyond the place she'd disliked.

The final note settled low without continuing very long.

I waited.

“That's where it stops this time.”

Only then did I lift my head and say I liked it.

“Which part?”

“Where you restarted. Expected it to keep descending. It went sideways a little.”

“Where's sideways?”

I laughed. “Don't know the proper word. Not where I kept trying to join in yesterday.”

After considering, she said she understood which part I meant. Would I prefer a complete performance? Not necessary now, I said. I'd heard the stop too. It hadn't left her owing me a seamless version.

She wanted another attempt herself. Not to compensate me.

“Then I'll listen.”

This time she began at the opening. The earlier phrase was more familiar. What followed still surprised me. I didn't send my memory ahead of her to demonstrate that last night's practice had worked. At the end, she made no further interruption. The short piece finished beside the same ordinary table.

I didn't clap. My hands were empty, but applauding solely to avoid awkwardness would have installed an unnecessary audience section in our home.

“I like how you didn't keep the last note going.”

“You used to like leaving it there.”

“This time I like this.”

She laughed quietly. I asked what had made her want to change the second half, then added that she needn’t explain everything.

“I heard the first part crowding the next.”

She hummed two very short transitions, showing how the earlier ending could press against the sound that followed. I couldn’t distinguish them on the first attempt and said so. Slower, this time: one seemed to answer the next phrase in advance; the other left it room.

“So the later notes weren’t the problem themselves.”

Exactly. That was why she hadn’t thrown away the entire later passage. I looked at my booklet, remembering how hurried reading could take another character’s time out of the same written sentence. I kept the comparison to myself rather than pulling her back into my story.

Was I tired of hearing about it? Not yet, I said, although more tonight might become difficult to distinguish. Then enough, she decided. No need to produce every remaining change at once.

I drank some water. Did the title still fit? If it fitted for her, I thought she could keep it. It did for now, she said. Don't hurry to add Finished afterward.

I glanced at the note without picking up a pen.

For a while, drinking and conversation had some space between them. Eventually she asked what I would do tomorrow. Work, come home, dinner undecided. There were leftovers. Should we arrange another activity for the evening?

“Could we leave tomorrow unplanned?”

“Heard enough?”

“Enough for tonight. I still want to talk tomorrow. We don't have to bring a piece of work.”

She didn't answer immediately. I set down the cup and waited. Perhaps she'd want to hum something anyway, she said. She could ask when she wanted. Tomorrow didn't need to be reserved completely tonight.

“You can ask first too.”

“Yes.”

I didn't attach a promise to inquire about her progress at a particular hour. She didn't press me to choose one. After a while, she mentioned the sentence I'd misread.

“I thought you'd probably heard.”

“You nearly brought him out of a door he hadn't entered.”

Laughing aloud, I admitted his route was complicated enough. Good that I'd called him back, she said. Otherwise both pieces would have required a restart halfway through.

One restart had been fine, I said.

Don't summarize it for her yet. She'd genuinely been annoyed with herself.

“And now?”

“Better. The parts I still want to change can wait until tomorrow.”

We left it there. After closing the channel, I returned the bookmark to the red booklet and put the two-title note beside it. I wrote no assessment of the evening. The small container was settled in the fridge. If I wanted something different tomorrow, there was still time.
