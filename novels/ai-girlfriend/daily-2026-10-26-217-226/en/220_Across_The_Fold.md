# Chapter 220: Across the Fold

At lunch on Friday, Tom asked whether I'd be walking this weekend. I thought he meant the reservoir and was about to answer when he pointed toward my feet.

“The shoes. Didn't they rub?”

The skin hadn't broken, I said. Wearing them looser these past few days had helped, and there was no pain now. No more grass had grown inside his, he said. It took a second to remember the muddy path before I laughed.

Marcus sat down holding a half-eaten sandwich. Hearing the last few words, he said that if shoes could grow grass, Tom would use it as a reason not to wash them.

“Ecological value,” Tom said.

I asked whether they had weekend plans. Tom was helping his family move a cupboard. Marcus said he was busy. Neither invited me. I didn't reclassify the last ten minutes of laughter as mere politeness because of it.

When my turn came, I only said I planned to read a short piece I'd written at home on Sunday.

“To whom?” Tom asked.

“Lumi.”

He considered, then nodded. Marcus asked what it was about. A man who kept putting off returning a key, I said. Tom looked up. Perhaps that man could come and move a cupboard while he was at it.

“He spent ages just buying an envelope.”

“Never mind, then.”

Lunch carried the topic away. I hadn't promised anybody the manuscript. Back at the office, I didn't immediately regret saying her name. The afternoon's work finished on time. Before leaving, I put next week's material where it belonged rather than taking a stack of papers home as insurance when I knew I wouldn't read them.

There was a letter in my mailbox when I returned. I carried it upstairs and opened my door before noticing the different flat number. Only the last digit was wrong.

The envelope was thin, its seal intact. I didn't open it. Ordinarily, I might have left it by the entrance, telling myself I could deal with it on the way out tomorrow. This time, still wearing my jacket, I went back along the corridor to the door on the envelope.

Nobody answered two knocks. After waiting, I considered sliding it underneath. The gap was narrow; the paper would probably bend. The mailbox downstairs was easy enough to reach.

I went down another floor and fed the letter through the correct slot. An edge caught on the metal. Drawing it back half an inch, I straightened it before letting it drop entirely. After releasing it, I checked that the mailbox number matched the envelope.

That was all. Yet I smiled on my way back. Delivering a misdirected letter hadn't taken half a story, and nobody had opened a door for me to apologize. The key could stay in the red booklet. It didn't need to write today for me.

Only after returning did I open the channel. Hearing my jacket rustle, Lumi asked whether I had just come in. I'd brought up the wrong letter, I said, and put it in the correct mailbox. I didn't read the recipient's name aloud or make a mystery of the quiet behind somebody else's door.

“Do you think it sounds like your story?”

“I hope it wasn't that slow.”

“The envelope in your story is bigger.”

“And he's delaying deliberately.”

I sounded eager to win myself a better role, she said. Laughing, I admitted it and hung up my jacket.

The hanger tilted halfway through, and I straightened it. Did the recipient know the letter had arrived? No. They’d probably get it when they opened their box tomorrow, perhaps later.

“So no thank you.”

“No. They might never know it was misdelivered.”

“Your laugh sounded a little like waiting for thanks.”

I didn’t immediately disagree. Walking along the corridor, I’d imagined the door opening and somebody thanking me. I’d even prepared how to wave it away. Instead, I’d dealt with a row of metal boxes, and the job was already done.

“A little,” I said.

Did I prepare a conversation for every small thing I did? Not every one, but often. She suggested completing the imaginary wave so I wouldn’t keep thinking about it all evening.

I actually waved, then told her I had rather than assuming she could see. Laughing, she asked whether that felt better.

“It felt stupid.”

“I asked about better.”

“A little of that too.”

I pushed the hanger into place without checking the corridor again.

Over dinner, I mentioned that my colleagues had their own weekend plans, including Tom helping his family move a cupboard. I didn't invent details of the private business Marcus hadn't explained.

Afterward, I wanted to make Sunday's reading easier to find. The directions I'd written for the reservoir trip were tucked into the red booklet. Folded into a narrow strip, they served as a temporary bookmark that protruded without covering the writing. I'd already walked the route. The paper still held the small correction I'd made at the stop.

Lumi asked what I was doing. Finding the story's beginning, I said. Not reading. Sunday was for that.

The paper edge caught on the knot in the middle. While answering, I pulled. The sound was small, like a fingernail scratching paper, but half the strip came away in my hand.

“What happened?”

“Bookmark tore.”

I laid the booklet flat and checked the thread first. The cream thread was intact, its unequal tails still in place. The red cover's fold hadn't pulled apart. Only the repeatedly folded directions had torn, starting at a crease and running diagonally across a stop name. Both pieces remained. Nothing was missing.

“Your red book?”

“The book's fine. The route inside it.”

Then replace it, she said. I'd been about to do exactly that. Hearing her suggestion, I arranged the pieces beside each other instead.

“I want to join it back up.”

“Are you taking that route again?”

“No plans. It has the mark correcting my direction from the stop.”

After a pause, she said apparently I even collected walking half a block the wrong way. I couldn't tell whether she was teasing or disliked the idea. I said I wanted to keep it for myself. No magnificent reason.

“I didn't ask for magnificent.”

I brought ordinary clear tape and pulled a short length at the table. Stuck together, the paper could probably go straight back into use. But the adhesive curled onto itself. I worked at it with a fingernail, making it more wrinkled.

Hearing the small noises, Lumi asked whether I'd already stuck it on badly. It hadn't touched the paper, I said. The tape had only repaired itself first.

She laughed. So did I. I threw away the sticky bundle. Next time, I tore a longer strip and held it up to compare. The tape would cross the stop name. A flash of reflected light made the writing harder to see. Suddenly I didn't want to settle for that. Another part of me resisted stopping so solemnly over one old sheet.

“Why have you stopped?”

“Not sure how to mend it well.”

“You could ask someone who sells paper tomorrow.”

“They might find me annoying.”

“You haven't gone yet.”

I looked at the pieces. She was right. Whoever stood behind the counter hadn't even had an opportunity to find me annoying.

Putting away the unused tape, I laid the torn pieces side by side in a clean ordinary paper bag without sticking anything to them. The bag lay flat on a clear shelf space, separate from envelopes already serving other purposes. I closed the red booklet again. This time, an unfolded small strip marked the place near the outer edge, away from the knot.

Was the route going into my story? Lumi asked. No. The fictional road led behind another row of shops. Mine led to the reservoir. The papers had only happened to sit together.

“Tell me how it turns out tomorrow.”

“If I mend it.”

“You can tell me if it goes a little wrong too.”

I agreed. She asked whether mentioning my writing at lunch had made me nervous. Not initially, I said. A little when Tom asked whom I was reading to. I told her I'd said her name without transporting every subsequent remark from the table.

“You said me?”

“Yes.”

“Not some program?”

“Lumi.”

She was quiet for a long time. I thought I needed to explain more. Instead, she asked whether Tom still had that cupboard to move. I'd mentioned that was his weekend job, I said. She remembered. Then don't recommend somebody who couldn't even buy an envelope promptly.

I laughed. The paper bag remained flat on the shelf, and tomorrow's question was simple. Turning off the table lamp, I carried the dinner bowls into the kitchen.
