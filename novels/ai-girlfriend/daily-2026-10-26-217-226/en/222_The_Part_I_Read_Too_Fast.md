# Chapter 222: The Part I Read Too Fast

On Sunday morning, I meant to put the repaired route sheet away. I carried it to the table and stopped.

The creases remained. Yesterday's mend felt slightly thicker beneath my fingertip. I laid it flat and checked for lifting edges, leaving the old crease alone. The patience I'd learned while repairing it made me slower. It hadn't made me an expert overnight.

The gray-blue bookmark marked the key story in my red booklet, clear of the thread knot. The repaired route lay flat in its ordinary folder, open on the left side of the table. I left it there and made breakfast.

Before the breakfast dishes had dried, I was silently rehearsing the opening. Familiar sentences didn't fit my mouth quite as easily as my eyes. Two neighboring sentences began with the same movement, as though someone were tugging the character's arm back and forth.

I changed one and put the pen farther away. I meant to read this version today. An empty afternoon wasn't a reason to imprison the man at the counter again.

Starting from a middle page, I found my thumb kept covering a word. The little booklet wouldn't open flat, and I didn't want to force it. I put a clean empty saucer on one corner. It slid against my pen cap with a noise that made even me look up.

Three adjustments later, I removed it.

Something small enough for a pocket demanded a surprising amount of table. I moved my chair closer, supported the spine with my left hand, and turned pages with my right. Slower, but less of a fight.

At the doorway scene, somebody downstairs dragged something across a floor. I stopped. Minutes later, I discovered I was wiping the table while the manuscript remained open at the same place.

I sat down and finished the passage without opening the channel to recruit Lumi. I couldn't identify the awkwardness afterward. I put a question mark in the margin and left the decision until evening.

That afternoon, I bought groceries for two dinners. The person ahead of me spent a long time searching for change. I'd switched the bag to my other hand and was preparing to become impatient when the cashier asked about another pocket.

The money was there. Nobody applauded. We moved forward a step.

On the bus home, I thought of my fictional shop assistant. He wasn't responsible for rescuing anyone, but every answer didn't have to sound like an eviction. I didn't rewrite him around the encounter. Getting off, I checked beneath my seat for the shopping.

After dinner, I washed the pan and cleared the table. No special refreshments had been arranged to accompany the story. Water on the right, open booklet in front, open folder holding the route on the left.

When I opened the channel, Lumi asked, “Does he get out of the shop tonight?”

“You already saw him out last week.”

“You picked up a pen again. Anything could happen.”

Laughing, I admitted changing a repeated phrase. Had the ending changed? No. He still folded the empty envelope.

“All right. I won't stop him at the corner yet.”

I announced the beginning and read the first line.

The opening went better than expected. I didn't lower my voice theatrically when the assistant lent his pen. He didn't need an invented identity supplied by my performance. At the man's complaint about the oversized envelope, I heard myself smile and continued.

At the woman by the storeroom, I began to hurry.

Her extra journeys occupied few lines. Reading them aloud, I felt every pause might sound like I hadn't decided what happened. I ran the boxes, the waiting, and her return together. His admission hurried after them.

Finally, the wind slapped the envelope against his hand. I read the fold, stopped, and reached for water.

Lumi said nothing immediately.

Two swallows. I set the cup down rather heavily. “Still prefer it unfolded?”

“Yes. That wasn't what I was thinking about.”

“Which part?”

“After she moves the boxes.”

I found the lines. Why had I suddenly sounded as though I were catching the last bus?

“Worried it was slow.”

“I was still at the door. You'd reached the street.”

Too specific for me to claim I'd read everything the same way. Trying the transition alone, I wanted to attach the next line before finishing the second sentence.

“Can you hear it?”

“Yes.”

My fingertips found the uneven thread tail beside the page. I'd let the man wait when writing him. Reading, I hadn't allowed him the time.

“That bit makes me uncomfortable.”

“Because he finally has to speak?”

“And because she won't finish it for him. I want to get past it.”

Lumi made a quiet sound of acknowledgment. I looked at my booklet without handing her the story as a demand to identify whom I really meant.

“Change it?” she asked.

“Not the words yet. Could you hear that part again?”

“From her opening the door.”

I found the place. A pause after her question about the key. At the box blocking the doorway, I added no theatrical panting. One sentence ended before the next began. His wait felt longer than it looked on paper. I nearly asked whether it was too long, stopped myself, and continued.

When he admitted the key should have come back sooner, I finally didn't rush to supply her answer.

Afterward, Lumi said, “Now she has time to listen.”

I smiled. “Didn't finish moving the boxes last time.”

“Probably still carrying one while you apologized.”

Leaning back, I laughed. The uncomfortable exchange hadn't vanished because we'd found something funny. I could finally read through it.

I drew a short vertical line in the margin as a reminder. At the second mark, Lumi asked what I was doing. I explained. Any more, she said, and he'd leave the shop to find himself surrounded by roadblocks.

I crossed out the second and kept one.

The morning's question mark was there too. Beside it, I wrote Pace. Sometimes the trouble wasn't inside the story. Sometimes my mouth was simply in a hurry.

“Your pages sound quieter now,” Lumi said.

I told her about the saucer and nearly knocking the pen down. She laughed, then advised against holding every page in the air for silence. I was supporting the booklet, I said, but hadn't raised it above my head.

“Better not. You sound enough like you're taking an oath already.”

I returned my elbow to the table.

“Which reading did you prefer?”

“The second.”

“Because I paused?”

“Because I heard it. Don't start testing me.”

I capped the pen and agreed. Quiet settled beside the table. I left my water alone this time.

Would the woman ask the man to help again? Lumi wondered.

“It isn't in this story.”

“Have you thought about it?”

“Only now. Maybe. I don't want to add her asking again just to make him feel better.”

“I wasn't thinking of him. She might genuinely need someone to move those boxes.”

I looked at what I'd written. So determined to prevent him hiding inside help, I'd almost made her somebody who had to manage alone forever to earn the right to be angry.

“I can think about that later. Not adding it today.”

“Her boxes are already moved today.”

I shifted the open folder so I could close the booklet. Hearing paper, Lumi asked whether I'd written down another of her remarks. No. Yesterday's repaired route. How was the mend? Still visible, still noticeable to a fingertip. It hadn't split farther.

“Will you take it out again?”

“If I go that way. Nothing arranged.”

Its job sounded easier than my story's, she said.

“Why?”

“Doesn't have to make everybody like its ending.”

“It sent me half a block the wrong way from the bus stop.”

“You added that part yourself.”

Laughing, I put the sheet away. The booklet was still open when I returned. I didn't read it a third time. We talked about getting up tomorrow, then about the person who'd finally found the change. I described the half-minute I'd seen without inventing a history of hardship or recovery for a stranger.

Near the end, Lumi asked whether getting me to reread the passage counted as keeping me there.

“It counts as asking me to make something I was already reading clearer.”

“You could say a little.”

I looked at the short vertical line.

“A little.”

She sounded pleased and didn't ask how much longer she could keep me. I closed the booklet and took my cup to wash. That small admission hadn't been written on the page. I still remembered it when the water started.
