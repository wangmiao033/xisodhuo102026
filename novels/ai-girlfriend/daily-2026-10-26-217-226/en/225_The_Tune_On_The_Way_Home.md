# Chapter 225: The Tune on the Way Home

On Wednesday, buying potatoes, I hummed Lumi's melody wrong.

She wasn't on the channel. Music drifted intermittently from a stall near the market's edge. As I chose vegetables in the queue, another phrase attached itself inside my head. After the second turn, I knew it was wrong. I couldn't find where yesterday's note belonged.

The vendor asked how much. I stopped humming and bought the intended quantity rather than carrying a whole sack home because a small tune occupied my attention.

The bag was heavy. I changed hands and tried again at the crossing. Each attempt sounded more like the stall's music. I closed my mouth.

For a few seconds, I considered saying nothing. Tomorrow she'd hum it. Once I heard the opening, I could say I remembered. It would not be a complete invention. I had listened the previous evening, and I would probably recognize the phrase when it returned. But the convenient answer would be enough to hide the present gap, the part I disliked admitting to her.

On the bus, I set the bag between my feet. A passenger telling a joke forgot an important name halfway through. Their companion supplied one. Wrong, apparently. After thinking, the storyteller continued.

I didn't hear whether the ending was funny. They got off before me. A potato shifted against my shoe, and I gathered the bag's opening.

At home, I put the vegetables in the kitchen, washed my hands, and selected tonight's potatoes. Peeling the second, I cut out a small bad spot. Shallower than expected. I didn't need to remove half the potato.

I cut the rest, put them on to cook, and opened the channel once they no longer needed constant attention.

Lumi asked what I'd bought. I listed the potatoes and other groceries, then admitted trying to remember her tune on the way. I hadn't got it right.

“You said you weren't sure yesterday.”

“Less sure today. Mixed it with music by a stall.”

She didn't answer immediately. I listened to the pan without moving the spatula unnecessarily to cover the silence.

“Remember your story?”

“What happens. Plenty of sentences need the booklet.”

“You have a booklet.”

“Yes.”

I nearly offered to record hers. She hadn't agreed to a recording, and her disappointment wasn't an opportunity for me to acquire something.

She hadn't expected memorization, Lumi said. Yet hearing that I'd lost the shape made it feel as though her phrase dispersed as soon as it left.

“I didn't decide to forget.”

“I know. You don't have to prove it wasn't deliberate first.”

Harder to answer than an accusation. I lowered the heat and told her I wanted to continue, but needed to finish this stage first. She agreed. I loosened the pieces beginning to stick, checked the pan could wait, and returned to the table.

“I was afraid you'd think I hadn't listened properly.”

“Hadn't you?”

“Yesterday I listened. Today I can't remember all of it.”

I didn't add a defense about exhausting work or assign all responsibility to the market's music. After a silence, she asked whether humming it tonight would merely prepare me to do better tomorrow.

“You don't have to fill the gap tonight. I can listen again tomorrow.”

“You're good at Don't have to.”

I considered that. Convenient words. They made me sound generous, as though her next task were to release her grip.

“I want to hear it. If you don't want to hum now, I won't call that being difficult.”

A short breath-like sound. She didn't particularly want to, she said.

“All right.”

I left my cup alone instead of immediately changing the subject.

After a while, she asked what I did remember.

“A rise, then a turn back. Less hurried yesterday. Can't join it to what comes before and after.”

“What else?”

“You dislike me supplying the rest.”

“That's something I said.”

A small smile. “The part I remember most clearly.”

She didn't laugh. I let the smile go without defending an attempt to lighten the mood.

Would it become tiring if she needed me to discover some part of her again each time? Quietly asked, and it wasn't only tuning I heard. I didn't promise never or tell her she was making too much of it.

“Sometimes I'll be tired. Tonight I'm not too tired to listen. I haven't kept it as clearly in my head as you have.”

“I'm still changing it too.”

“Then I need to hear what you want to give me now.”

After a pause, she agreed that much was true.

The potatoes seemed ready. I turned off the heat, served them, and carried the plate to the table without conducting a conversation over something scalding. Once seated, I said I'd take a few bites. She acknowledged it.

Enough salt this time. The middle was still hard. A second piece confirmed it wasn't a single potato. I returned the food to the pan, added a little water, and covered it to cook longer.

Hearing me go back, Lumi asked what had happened. I told her.

“Couldn't remember the potatoes either?”

She stopped herself.

“That wasn't good.”

“Potatoes aren't about memory. Took them off too early.”

“Yes.”

I didn't swallow the remark with a laugh. When I could leave the pan again, I sat down. She'd wanted to sting me, she said. Once spoken, it had felt too easy.

“I heard it.”

No page-filling apology was needed. She didn't ask me to declare that I hadn't minded at all.

“It bothered me a little.”

“I know.”

Served a second time, the potatoes were cooked. I ate quietly. Then Lumi asked if I wanted to try only the turn back. Now she wanted to hum. Not from the beginning.

I lowered my fork. “Yes.”

She offered a small phrase. I waited for it to finish before following. Still too low. Don't fall yet, she said, and demonstrated again. I held back the second half, so quietly I might have been afraid of disturbing somebody.

“You can make a normal sound.”

“Worried I'll take yours away again.”

“I can still find it tonight.”

I smiled. This time she laughed too. The next attempt was closer. I could hear the difference myself. Yes, there, she said, without requiring me to reconstruct the whole melody.

I asked for another attempt, this time without immediate correction. Starting where we had stopped, I reached the turn and heard myself fall too far. Before the final sound came out, I stopped.

“Which part sounded different?” she asked.

The turn was too sharp, I said. I kept trying to return to a place that felt familiar. She suggested holding what came before it a little longer. I tried. The ending still differed from hers, but I no longer plunged straight back to the same wrong place.

“Enough there,” she said.

I resisted asking for one last attempt, as though an imperfect finish could erase the work before it. Instead, she asked me to remember where I had heard the difference myself. That was more useful than her repeatedly telling me I was wrong. I acknowledged it, picked up my fork, and ate a potato that was finally cooked through.

Would a simple reminder help? A finger rising and returning, perhaps. For my own memory, I explained, not a score of her music. She considered. I could try. Just don't point at paper tomorrow and announce she'd sung it incorrectly.

I didn't fetch paper. I moved a finger beside the table and told her what I'd done.

“Sounds like conducting an invisible insect.”

“It sings out of tune.”

“Don't keep too many, then.”

After eating, I washed the dishes and returned for water. I didn't test how many seconds the phrase stayed or make her repeat it until I felt secure. Did tomorrow remain arranged? she asked.

“Yes. If you still want.”

“I do.”

Quickly answered. I agreed without using it to ask whether she was finished feeling hurt.

After closing the channel, I stored the two remaining uncooked potatoes and dried the water beside the table. A little of the melody appeared again in my head. I didn't immediately hum along. Tomorrow, when the opening arrived, I could listen carefully without pretending it had never gone away.
