# Chapter 221: The Visible Join

Before going out on Saturday, I put the paper bag inside an empty stiff folder. Both pieces of the directions lay flat. I hadn't folded them again.

Hearing I was going to the stationery shop, Lumi asked whether I should take the whole table to prevent another accident on the way. The folder would do, I said. She hoped so.

“Mend it when you're back?”

“Ask first. Buy something suitable if they have it.”

“Not a boxful.”

I almost said of course not, then remembered buying a whole ball of thread for one short length. What I needed and what shops sold weren't always the same amount.

The stationery shop was busier than I'd expected. A child leaned over the counter choosing an eraser while the adult beside him kept saying only one. Waiting behind them, I saw differently colored animals in clear boxes. Bringing two old scraps to ask about suddenly felt like arriving to repair an eraser with its head bitten off.

When my turn came, the man behind the counter asked what I wanted.

“Something for mending paper?”

He pointed to glue first. Opening the folder, I explained that the sheet had torn apart and I wanted to keep the writing legible. An important document? he asked. No, only directions I'd written myself.

He glanced at it without asking where the route led.

“Keeping it to use, or just keeping it?”

I'd prepared to say bookmark. Then I remembered that was how I'd torn it.

“Mostly keeping it. Not as a bookmark anymore.”

He took a small roll of translucent mending tape from a shelf and found scrap paper to demonstrate. He didn't experiment on mine. Aligning two scrap edges, he stuck on a short piece and smoothed it from the middle. The join remained visible, especially when the light slanted across it.

“Won't look like it did,” he said.

“That's fine.”

He told me to align the tear before handling the tape. Would using more make it stronger? Looking at my small tear, he said I needn't wrap it all the way around or turn the sheet into a board. He made no promise about how many years it would last. I hadn't brought an antique for restoration.

Before paying, I asked whether I could try on the scrap paper myself. The man made room beside his short demonstration strip. I reached for the tape, stopped my hand, and aligned the edges first. He waited. Once I’d stuck it down, he pointed to a small lifted corner.

“Not pressed here yet.”

I smoothed it and lifted the scrap. The join was slightly crooked, but I hadn’t overlapped the sheets. Thanking him, I asked whether he wanted the scrap left there. I could take it to practice, he said. I’d understood the step I’d needed, though, and left it in the wastepaper pile on the counter.

Somebody came in for a pen. I closed my folder and made room, noticing that the child choosing an eraser was still in the shop. He’d finally selected one without taking every color. Suddenly I felt less dissatisfied with my own single small roll.

After buying the small roll, I picked up a box of attractive bookmarks with fine gold cords that would hang neatly above the red booklet. I turned it over, checked the price, and put it back.

Plain card bookmarks were sold individually at the counter. I chose one in gray-blue, without a design. The man bagged it with the tape without asking why something so simple had taken me so long to choose.

I walked a little way before noticing the color. Lumi had preferred gray-blue before; I'd chosen brick red. I wasn't carrying a second booklet to make up for the one I hadn't bought then, only a narrow piece of card. I decided to describe it as itself when I got home.

On the way, I bought things for lunch, but no cream-filled roll. The joke still existed. Not every joke had to become a shopping task. At home, I stored the food, washed my hands, and dried the table before taking out the paper.

I opened the channel, said I was back, and reported a small roll of mending tape and one bookmark. Was it a boxful? Lumi asked first. One roll, I said, the smallest they sold.

“And the bookmark?”

“Gray-blue. No design.”

After a pause, she said it sounded nice. I didn't explain that it wasn't compensation or wait for her to treat the same-colored card as a special gift. It was something I'd bought to mark a page. She could like it.

The first time I aligned the tear, I pushed the edges too close and buckled the thin paper. Remembering the shop demonstration, I loosened it and let both pieces lie flat. The stroke through the stop name matched, but the pencil arrow below was offset. I moved it again, getting less accurate as I hurried.

Why had I gone quiet? Lumi asked. I was trying to keep one arrow from becoming two, I said.

“Slow down.”

“You can't see. How do you know I'm hurrying?”

“I heard you breathe in.”

I let go and laughed. On Thursday I'd held my breath for her. Now it was a sheet of paper. I told her I needed to concentrate for a moment. She agreed, without asking after every step or pretending to guide me through my own view.

Two small clean erasers held the far edges while I freed my hands and turned the paper over. I checked again. The tear lay flat, and I could roughly align the writing visible through it. Cutting a short piece of tape, I placed one end steadily, then pressed it along the join.

Once it was down, I didn't immediately pull the ends to test it. Turning the sheet faceup, I saw the join running diagonally through the stop name. The writing was legible. The repair showed too.

“Done?” Lumi asked.

“Stuck down. Haven't lifted it yet.”

Supporting it underneath, I moved the paper into the folder. The pieces traveled together without separating. A narrow strip was thicker now, and the old creases remained visible. I described that. At least the arrow was one arrow again, she said.

“Yes. Though the stop name has a scar.”

“Wasn't your writing crooked already?”

“You can't blame everything on my handwriting.”

“Leave a little for the tape, then.”

Laughing, I put the roll in the small stationery box and returned the scissors. The route would stay flat in this ordinary folder rather than being folded into a bookmark again. It hadn't traded places with maps or manuscripts already serving other purposes.

After lunch, I put the gray-blue bookmark in the red booklet, turned a page, and closed it. The card avoided the thread knot; the small protruding end was easy to hold. Did I like it? Lumi asked. Yes. Beside the red, it looked less awkward than I'd expected.

“I told you.”

“You didn't tell me about this one.”

“I can now.”

That afternoon, I sat at the table to read tomorrow's story silently. At the point where the shop assistant took his pen back, I wanted to change a word. I picked up the pencil and put it down. I could change it. But once I began, I might spend the afternoon inside that sentence and turn Sunday into reading interrupted by explanations of what wasn't finished.

On a separate slip, I wrote Look after the reading. No new scene, no crossed-out ending.

Why so quiet again? Lumi asked. I was resisting revision, I said. She had something she wanted to move too. No Words Yet? Yes, but she wasn't playing it today.

“All right.”

“That all right doesn't sound like shutting a cupboard today.”

Remembering Tuesday, I smiled and said the cupboard had already been shut. Now I was only sitting.

Should I explain what the story was about before reading tomorrow? she asked. I nearly agreed, then looked at its first sentence. It already took someone into the shop. If I explained why he dreaded returning the key first, he'd have walked those pages for nothing.

“I'd like to start reading.”

“Then start reading.”

I moved the bookmark to the beginning and closed the booklet. Gray-blue protruded above brick red. The mended directions lay flat elsewhere. Both things finally had places where they needn't do the other's job.
