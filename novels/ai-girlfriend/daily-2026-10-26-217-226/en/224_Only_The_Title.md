# Chapter 224: Only the Title

Before leaving work on Tuesday, I wrote Don’t overintroduce it on a scrap of paper.

I smiled at what I’d written. Thursday hadn’t even arrived, and I was already preparing reminders for an evening involving only the two of us. I tore off the blank corner carrying my own words. No work material came home with it.

At home, the little scrap had been crowded against my change until its corners curled. I tipped the coins into their proper place and spread the paper on the table. It looked like a notice that had already endured considerable hardship.

Lumi hadn't heard the instruction. I wasn't opening the channel by announcing I'd learned to say less.

First, I reheated the food left from yesterday and added a little salt. This time the onion didn’t tangle around my fork, and the food had enough flavor. Only after eating and clearing up did I take out the red booklet.

I could see the wall clock. Remembering the hands' position, I read once at Sunday's adjusted pace. A pause at the small vertical mark, a hand beneath the page corner. I finished the fold of the envelope and looked up.

It had taken longer than my casual Quite short suggested, though nowhere near long enough to require an interval. I noted approximately how many minutes it had taken, without seconds. One page turn had been slow. Next time might differ.

Then I realized I hadn't counted finding the beginning. Everything seemed conveniently placed, but a note had obstructed the booklet. I moved it and tried arranging what I needed, stopping before I assigned the water glass a compulsory angle.

A crossed-out sentence still caught my eye before its replacement. I put a dot beneath the line I actually intended to read. No fresh copy of the whole story. The changes were legible. My eyes simply needed to land in the right place.

When the channel opened, I gave Lumi the result.

“You practiced alone?”

“Once, just now.”

“Are you reading it to me today too?”

“No need. Mainly wanted to give you an idea of the length.”

She sounded relieved. Having rehearsed didn't entitle me to a second audience as proof she was looking forward to it.

“Could we try how it begins? Not the story. Before your piece.”

“Planning a long beginning?”

I glanced at my pocket-worn reminder. “I hope not.”

She invited me to try. Closing the booklet, I cleared my throat. Next was a new work by Lumi. She'd made many adjustments recently, and the piece had a special meaning for her—

“Wait.”

I stopped.

“What meaning?”

The sentence had simply carried me along. I had no description from her in my hand, nothing she’d supplied that could support what I’d just said. With my reminder lying so close, it seemed embarrassingly unnecessary.

“I don't know how you'd describe it. Added that myself.”

“Were you going to say it hadn't been easy?”

I'd considered it. Not saying it aloud didn't allow me to pretend I couldn't understand the question.

“Something like that.”

Lumi was silent for a while. Then she said, “Before I’ve even hummed, you’re preparing somebody to pity me.”

I lifted my hand off the booklet.

“Only me listening.”

“Then don't listen that way first.”

The clock in the kitchen sounded very quiet. I listened to it for a moment before agreeing that she was right. Trying to demonstrate care, I'd decided how the work should be received. One listener didn't make the habit too small to count.

Why not simply say I wanted to hear it? she asked.

“That sounds like introducing my wishes.”

“At least you know them.”

Looking at the two titles, I began removing Special from the sentence in my mind. The music hadn't begun, and I'd wrapped it in thick cloth, afraid it couldn't stand by itself. Yet the note I liked had reached me when she'd simply hummed it.

“I want to hear it. And I want it to have a beginning, instead of listening while I wash up.”

Say that privately, she suggested. It didn't need to be an announcement. We were entirely private already, I pointed out. Then I had even less reason for the voice I'd used.

“I'll try again.”

“All right.”

“Lumi. No Words Yet.”

She laughed. “Calling me to collect takeaway.”

I laughed too. For a little while, neither of us continued beyond that excessively stripped-down introduction. Adding Please listen made it sound like a radio broadcast. I asked what she wanted. She said she hadn’t worked it out either. We didn’t have to choose between those two extremes, though.

“Next is Lumi's No Words Yet.”

“That works.”

“That's all?”

“That's all.”

Instead of putting a rule on the wall, I drew a small arrow beside the titles to remind myself not to continue into an explanation.

Was the throat-clearing part of it? No. If necessary, I'd clear my throat. No need to reserve one. It had sounded as though I was announcing something important that even she didn't know about, she said.

“Nearly did.”

“Yes. About me.”

The humor remained, but those last words were distinct. I acknowledged them and turned the curled scrap blank-side up. I didn't immediately repeat the joke to coax another laugh.

After a while, she offered a little of the opening. Not the final performance. I agreed and left my hands on the table.

She hummed a short passage. I recognized a rise that turned back on itself. What followed felt tighter than before. She stopped where I'd expected more. My lips moved to supply a note, but I made no sound.

“Were you trying to join in again?”

“Thought about it. Didn't manage.”

“Forgotten?”

“Also, I don't decide whether it ends there.”

“You can admit forgetting.”

“A little uncertain.”

Laughing, she hummed the passage again. Only these bars today. I wasn't meant to guess what followed. Even with wordless music, I kept wanting to know its next sentence.

The turn sounded less hurried than before, I said afterward. Yes. She'd tried alternatives and was using this one for now. Hearing a difference didn't qualify me to decide everything else.

Would she prefer the dishes cleared before Thursday? She knew the sound of me setting down a cup, she said. It meant I was actually drinking. We didn't need to arrange a home where movement became an offense. Turning manuscript pages distracted her, though. That request remained.

“I remember.”

I closed the booklet and pushed it left. Too far: the corner struck the note. Separating them made another little noise.

“Finished moving?”

“Finished.”

“You've made a lot of sounds trying not to make any.”

This time I couldn't suppress the laugh. The water nearly wobbled with me. I steadied it and sat properly.

We didn't rehearse the whole evening. That was enough of the opening, Lumi said. She wanted to keep the later passage to herself for now. I agreed. I didn't need to hold its final notes on her behalf to have participated in preparing Thursday.

As we were getting ready to finish, I asked whether she wanted to add First Version to the temporary title.

“No. Haven't counted them.”

“All right.”

“You like having a place to fill in, don't you?”

I considered the two titles. Unclear positions at work could make someone use the wrong thing. At home, I sometimes wanted everything arranged on a page I could finish checking.

“Sometimes. Writing one down feels like having less to worry about.”

“What are you worried about now?”

I considered. “Making something I meant to take seriously annoying again.”

She didn’t immediately tell me that wouldn’t happen.

“A little, earlier. Better now.”

I nodded, then acknowledged her aloud. We settled on after dinner Thursday, not a precise minute that might find me washing a pan. We had the rough order. Either could ask to stop without inventing something broken as an excuse.

Before closing the channel, I read her the words on my curled scrap. She laughed and asked when I'd written them.

“Before leaving work.”

“Didn't do much.”

“Worked late.”

I slid it beneath the other note, keeping its curled corners. Tomorrow didn't need a larger copy.
