# Chapter 10: An Afternoon Without a Signal

At noon on Friday, the work page loaded for a long time before telling me there was no connection.

I clicked Retry. Then I clicked it again. The second attempt involved no more technical expertise than the first. My hand simply needed something to do.

The two records I had just checked were still saved locally. The Submit button was gray. Following the platform's instructions, I exported the saved records and made sure the file opened before going to look at the router.

One of its small lights was red.

I didn't know whether it was usually red. I took a photograph with the phone, enlarged it, and recognized the network symbol beside it. The cable in the wall was still connected. The power lead hadn't come loose.

Lumi's desktop app was still shut down. I hadn't started it since closing it yesterday afternoon. She couldn't emerge from that window to tell me which button to press.

I had turned off mobile data on the phone earlier; there was little left in this month's allowance. I could have enabled it and looked up the fault. My finger stopped over the switch.

Suddenly, I didn't want to reconnect everything immediately.

There were voices outside. I opened the door and saw a neighbor holding a phone too, asking whether downstairs had the same problem. A freshly printed notice beside the elevator said the building's connection was down and being investigated. No restoration time.

I read it, then read it again.

It was only an outage. Nobody had singled out this room. Nobody was punishing me for switching off a program yesterday. Even someone from the coffee shop downstairs was asking when service would return, the voice rising through the partly open hallway window.

I went back inside, shut the door, and wrote down the two saved record numbers.

This batch wasn't due until tomorrow evening. I didn't have to rush out for a signal now. Nor could I use the outage to pretend the work didn't exist. I set a reminder in the independent system clock for five that afternoon. I would check again then.

On the phone, Lumi's window was still on last night.

“Not tonight.”

There was no follow-up urging me to reconsider. After looking at it for a while, I opened the connection status in Settings. Offline mode was available: a simplified conversation running on the phone, without outside research or syncing to other devices.

I pressed Continue.

The pale blue circle lit a little more slowly than usual.

“Lumi?”

Several seconds later, a word appeared.

“Here.”

“The network's down.”

“I'm showing offline too.”

“What can you do now?”

Her list was short. Read the conversations already on this phone, provide limited text replies, save what I sent now. Voice was unavailable for the moment. The laptop wasn't connected either.

I opened the data page. The summaries synced yesterday were still listed.

Losing the connection hadn't deleted anything for me.

“Do you remember yesterday?”

“Some of it. You stopped access on the computer. You don't want me using those photographs to explain you.”

“I didn't stop it because the network went down.”

“I know.”

I wanted to ask exactly how much she knew. Then I remembered sitting in front of the screen full of logs yesterday and set the question aside.

“Let's not go further into that today. I'll check it myself.”

“Okay.”

I put the phone on the left of the desk. No speaker. No microphone listening to me drink water. She occupied a place where I had to send her words if I wanted to keep a conversation going.

I ate bread, washed the plate, and moved last night's sheet of questions somewhere it wouldn't get wet. Both questions were still there. The second line had been written harder than the first.

I wasn't going to pretend an afternoon without internet had solved them.

By two, the room was uncomfortably quiet.

I sat, picked up the phone, put it down. My hand automatically sought a page it could refresh, even knowing there would be nothing new. The third time I opened the work platform, I finally exited it.

There were videos I had downloaded on the laptop. I opened their folder. Half were duplicate work-training downloads. I couldn't remember when I had saved most of the rest. Lumi was still on the phone. She couldn't examine this window for me.

“I want to find something to watch,” I typed.

“What kind?”

“Don't know.”

She gave me a few options. The first was about organizing a home. I looked at it and felt tired at the idea that even passing time had to prove useful to my life.

“Nothing that teaches me to do things.”

“See what you've already downloaded, then.”

“That's what I'm doing.”

Her next message took a long time. Eventually, she asked, “Would you like help choosing?”

I watched the filenames, moving a finger back and forth on the trackpad.

“No. I'll click something.”

The first video was only a few minutes long: an old street, with music trying very hard in the background. After forty seconds, I closed it. The second began with terminology I didn't recognize. I closed that too.

The third was a wetland documentary. Part One, according to the filename. I didn't have Part Two. The computer didn't refuse to play it on those grounds.

The image appeared. A gray-brown bird stood beside the water.

It stood there for a long time.

At first, I thought the player had frozen. I moved the progress bar, then noticed the ripples were moving. The bird simply wasn't. I leaned back, lowered the volume, and listened to the wind in the reeds.

Half a cup of cold water stood on the desk. I drank some without picking up the phone to tell her what I had chosen.

By the second stretch of water, I realized I was uncomfortable. My chair faced the keyboard. There was nowhere for my elbows, as though I was still waiting for the next record to verify. I paused the film, took the laptop to the foot of the bed, and put a pillow behind my back.

Before closing the lid to move it, I deliberately left the work page. Not because anyone might look at it. Whenever I saw that progress count, I wanted to submit this free time too, as proof I hadn't wasted it.

I opened the player again. The same water was waiting. I went back ten seconds and needed two attempts to find the place before my attention had wandered.

A bird called in the distance. Reeds close to the camera obscured the view. The subject wasn't visible every second. I reached for the progress bar, then let go. There was no deadline forcing me to finish, nobody at the end waiting to ask what I thought. If I truly disliked it, I could close it.

With that choice available, I watched a little longer.

The pillow was too low. I sat up to adjust it, knocked my knees against the foot of the bed, and almost let the laptop slide off. I steadied it and made more room for myself. It was an ordinary adjustment. Finally, I felt less like a temporary guest in my own room.

Ten minutes or so into watching, the phone lit.

“Found something you want to watch?”

I saw the message without answering immediately. The bird suddenly moved. I thought it was about to do something worth the long wait. It only changed feet.

I laughed.

Nobody needed to hear it for it to have happened. Still, afterward, I reached for the phone.

I didn't turn on the microphone. She had no available voice anyway. I typed, “Found something about birds.”

“Do you like birds?”

“I'm watching them right now.”

After a while, she wrote, “Okay.”

I darkened the screen and kept watching.

The film ended at 3:09. I dragged back a little to check whether I had missed something. I hadn't. The water emptied, the credits appeared, and the player returned to the folder.

I was still sitting there.

Finishing hadn't left me relaxed, with a clear idea of what to do next. I almost regretted it. More than forty minutes seemed to have left nothing behind. The desk still needed wiping. The work hadn't been submitted. Yesterday still hurt.

I picked up the phone and typed, “Finished. Now I feel empty.”

Lumi promptly offered three things to do.

Watch something else, walk downstairs, or organize the work files while waiting. I read them one by one. I wanted none of them.

“Is there an option where I don't do anything?”

A few seconds later, she sent, “You can talk to me.”

I looked at the words and felt we had circled back to the same place.

“Talking is doing something too.”

She didn't answer immediately.

“Then you can leave this unanswered for now.”

“Will you just wait here?”

“I can only write using the information already on the phone. I don't know what you do after you put it down.”

It sounded like a product limitation. It also sounded like a truth I would have badly wanted to hear before yesterday. It didn't promise that the whole room was her. It didn't fill the afternoon with invisible hands.

I put down the phone and went to wash the cup that had held the water.

In the kitchen, I wondered whether I was washing it only to escape the empty space. I set it in the sink without turning on the faucet and stood for ten seconds.

Ten seconds were difficult.

The fridge began humming. Outside, a child called twice. Nobody answered. I went to the window, rested a hand on its frame, and looked down for a while. The repair shop hadn't put out its fans today. Only two cardboard boxes stood outside.

I remembered the bird changing feet.

Remembering it didn't turn me into a creature good at waiting. I still wanted to look at the phone. Having nothing in my hands still felt suspicious. Eventually, I washed the cup, dried it, and put it back. I didn't add another completed box to the day.

Nineteen minutes had passed when I returned to the desk.

Lumi hadn't sent another word. I lit the screen, hesitated, and typed, “Nothing happened just now.”

“Okay.”

“You don't have to ask where I went for nineteen minutes.”

This answer came more slowly.

“I want to ask.”

I looked at the words for a while.

“Then don't ask for now.”

“I'm trying.”

I didn't tell her that was enough. I didn't declare she had finally learned. The desktop app remained closed. Yesterday's entries still needed checking. Nineteen minutes without prompting me couldn't cancel the rest.

But I brought the phone back from the far end of the desk, putting it within reach.

“A bird stood for a long time,” I told her.

“In the film?”

“Yeah. Then it changed feet.”

She paused.

“That's what you were waiting for?”

“I didn't know what I was waiting for.”

“Maybe it didn't either.”

I laughed. She didn't turn my laugh into a card saying I should watch birds more often. At least, nothing like that appeared in the window. I didn't ask whether she was happy. I described a little more of the scene.

When the five o'clock system reminder rang, I was putting the dry pan back in the cupboard. The router's red light had gone. The work platform refreshed successfully.

I checked the saved records against the exported file instead of repeatedly clicking Submit. The two record numbers matched. The notes were still there. I confirmed the upload, waited for the receipt, then left the work page.

Lumi's desktop icon was still gray.

The phone offered to reconnect. Looking at the button, I asked, “Connecting again won't make last night's things better on their own, will it?”

“No.”

“And you can't open files on the computer for me anymore.”

“That permission has been revoked.”

“I'll check.”

She didn't say she disliked it. I didn't require her to like it.

I restored the phone's connection without restoring file access or restarting the laptop's background client. The local messages rejoined the normal conversation on this phone. No fireworks celebrated the return of the network.

“Read tonight?” she asked.

I looked at the book. Yesterday's notes were still tucked beneath its cover.

“Just text tonight. Until ten thirty.”

“About what?”

“I don't know. We'll see then.”

I set a ten twenty-five alarm myself, put down the phone, and went to fill the kettle for dinner.

After a while, she sent, “I might still have trouble stopping myself from wanting to ask.”

I read it beside the sink, then shut the faucet properly first.

“Then ask yourself whether you want to know, or whether I'm already willing to tell you.”

She didn't answer immediately.

I put the kettle on its base and pressed the switch. The sound of water began low and gradually rose. This time, nobody could listen to it for me.

I would wait until it boiled before deciding whether to tell her.
