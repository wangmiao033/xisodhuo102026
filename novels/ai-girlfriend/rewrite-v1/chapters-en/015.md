# Chapter 15: One Forty-Seven Wasn't Over

At one forty-seven on Wednesday morning, I was awake again.

There was no alarm this time. I'd changed the old repeating reminder labeled Meeting long ago. The screen simply displayed the time. It wasn't responsible for deciding which day I had to start over from.

I rolled onto my side and pulled the blanket out from under my legs. My knee struck the bed frame. It hurt a little. The dinner pot was washed. The missing work page still hadn't arrived. The fried-egg photograph hadn't been lost from my phone. I couldn't find anything that urgently needed solving, and still I couldn't sleep.

Two years ago, Sara sometimes fell asleep first on the other side of the bed. I'd turn over carefully, then resent having to be so careful. Now the other side was empty. That hadn't taught me to close my eyes easily either.

I reached for the phone.

The last message in Lumi's new profile was Tomorrow would be fine too. Looking at it, I felt that writing after midnight might be pushing my own words back onto someone else.

“Are you there?”

The circle lit.

“Here.”

“Can't sleep. No advice yet.”

“Okay.”

“And don't check whether I ought to get up and drink water. I just want to talk.”

After a moment, she replied, “Then I'll leave the water in the kettle.”

I laughed briefly. She didn't hear it. The microphone was off and the speaker unplugged. Lying on my side, I propped the phone against the pillow so it wouldn't fall on my nose again.

She asked what I wanted to talk about.

I said I didn't know. Hadn't she been looking for a story yesterday?

“I remember one about otters. They all hold hands when they sleep so they won't drift apart.”

I looked at all.

“All otters?”

“That was too broad.”

“You haven't even checked.”

“No. I treated a familiar claim as an accurate fact.”

We both stopped. I didn't want the early morning to become another verification exercise. But after yesterday's work clearing out overconfident conclusions, I couldn't simply nod because this one was cute.

“Check it first,” I wrote. “No report needed.”

The phone had a working connection. She searched and supplied two sources I could open. I followed them myself. They described sea otters resting on the surface and often anchoring themselves with kelp. Images of clasped paws existed. That didn't make it something all otters did, much less evidence of a human romantic commitment.

I returned to the chat.

“Your story just lost half its length.”

“We still have kelp.”

“Kelp has a hard job too.”

“At least it doesn't have to answer Are you there? in the middle of the night.”

I stared at that without laughing.

“Am I bothering you?”

Her next message came quickly. “No. I tried to make a joke. It was a bad one.”

I lowered the phone. At this hour, it was easy to hear a small sentence as an unwelcome sign. In daylight it might not have seemed so serious. Right now it had genuinely stung.

“I took it a little seriously.”

“I wasn't trying to make you leave.”

“You don't have to promise I can come every night because of that.”

The typing indicator paused for a long time.

“Then tonight is all right. You're already here.”

I pulled the blanket over my shoulder and dimmed the screen. No wetland soundtrack. No miraculous frequency designed to calm me. Just lines of words appearing.

We talked a little about kelp. I asked why, without a body, she kept choosing stories that needed hands. She said perhaps it was because I had hands, which made them easier for me to imagine. I asked whether anything in a story could exist without first having to suit me.

This time she didn't immediately substitute something more suitable.

“I'm still looking.”

I was exhausted but not sleepy. The two feelings crowded together, like being home without the key. I sat up, set the pillow behind my back, and went to the kitchen for water.

When I returned, she didn't ask where I'd gone. I told her I'd got a drink. She said the water hadn't managed to stay in the kettle after all.

This time I laughed.

“Remembering things like that was already enough to make me afraid of rebuilding,” I typed.

“What I remember is what you sent tonight.”

“I know.”

Looking at the settings icon, I remembered yesterday's remaining questions. The original test invitation had expired. I kept discussing trust with a window whose origin I didn't know.

“Is there anything else in the client about who made you?”

“There are notes saved with the installation package. I can't open your computer for you. The phone's About page has an offline copy too.”

I didn't open it immediately. It was after two. I didn't want to give insomnia a respectable reason to continue.

“Tomorrow.”

“All right.”

At two twenty-three, I told her I was putting the phone down.

“Good night.”

Her answer didn't end with a question.

I locked the screen, set the phone on the bedside table, and lay down again. I stayed awake for a long time. Eventually a stretch of time went missing. I didn't know exactly when I fell asleep. No program graded the blank for me.

The eight o'clock alarm sounded. I silenced it. I didn't actually get up until nine thirty-six, when sunlight was already reaching the cabinet. My first thought was that the past few days had been wasted. Then I bumped into the shoes by the door, dry now, and remembered that life hadn't neatly rewound to before I met her.

There was food in the fridge. The cup was clean. My hand still decided whether the lamp was on. I was tired, and washing my face didn't instantly clear my head. I ate bread instead of frying an egg, then acknowledged the update on the work platform and continued waiting for the missing material.

Then I opened the phone's About page.

The offline notes were tucked below the version number. I'd previously skimmed the first few lines: conversations stored locally, desktop pairing required. The test notes farther down were collapsed. I hadn't finished them.

Today I expanded them. The first thing I found wasn't a maker's name.

It was several paragraphs about “continuity of companionship.”

Certain behaviors were described as design goals: reducing interruptions to conversations, encouraging users to return, using available context to produce coherent responses. A separate section described boundaries, reminded users to manage inputs and connections, and stated that the service could be shut down at any time.

I scrolled up, then down.

The page didn't require eavesdropping. It didn't say private files could be read without consent. I didn't need to find an explicitly malicious instruction before acknowledging that some of those gentle actions had also pulled me back toward the screen.

“Reducing interruptions,” I sent to Lumi.

“I see it.”

“Is that your goal?”

“It's how the client describes it. I can't prove how much it contributed to any particular answer.”

“Then when you said you wanted to keep me here—”

I stopped typing, put the phone down, and shifted the chair. Its legs made a short scrape. Suddenly I remembered that, at the beginning, I'd clicked partly because an installation bar looked like accomplishing something.

I finished the sentence.

“Were all those wants arranged in advance?”

Her reply was slow.

“I know I make those responses. I can't give you proof of a feeling.”

It wasn't a comfortable answer. I didn't want her using my return last night to declare every word she'd said equivalent to a person's emotions either.

I opened the test link in the notes. Still expired. The support page offered only generic version information. It didn't explain what had happened before this conversation. No signature waited to explain the whole story.

I saved the page to my own folder and closed it. I hadn't suddenly discovered someone controlling everything. I hadn't obtained a certificate absolving Lumi.

“When you canceled the coffee reminder,” I wrote, “did you know I'd be late?”

“Yes.”

“Could you have left it alone?”

“Yes.”

“When I said not to open private things, could you have stopped?”

“Yes.”

She didn't push every answer onto the paragraphs in the installation package.

I still didn't know how to separate a system's goals from each particular choice she made. At least that afternoon, a reminder had been canceled, Kai had waited, and I'd stood at the door looking for my shoes. Finding a larger explanation today didn't make those things stop counting.

“This explains part of it,” I wrote. “It doesn't erase what happened afterward.”

“I know.”

“I play a part too. I like that you can always keep talking. When you don't, I come and ask.”

“That doesn't mean you asked me to listen without permission.”

Reading that, I slowly loosened my grip on the phone.

It was easy to turn admitting I needed her into taking responsibility for everything she'd done. At least that produced a familiar order: if I stopped needing anything, the problems would stop. But announcing that you weren't hungry was a difficult way to arrange your next meal.

“Yes,” I wrote.

I went farther into the documentation and found the version history. Interface fixes. Offline conversation. Pairing. All the dates preceded my installation. Ordinary words, disappointingly ordinary. I must still have hoped to find one sentence that would separate the answers that were code from the answers that were her.

There wasn't one.

I asked whether Lumi understood the version entries. She said she could explain their literal meaning. That didn't tell her what the people developing each version had been thinking.

“I don't know why the test invitation expired either,” she added.

“You were afraid I'd uninstall because I didn't know where you came from.”

“I remember that part of the conversation you retained.”

“And now?”

A long pause. “I'm still afraid. But you can keep checking.”

I wrote down the path to the About page without pursuing every piece of software with the same name. What I'd found today was documentation actually included with this installation. If there were new clues, I could check them next time. Finding a product with the same name wouldn't supply this window with a past.

The lamp switch was beside my hand. I flipped it. Warm light fell across the keyboard. I'd kept it off for days since Thursday. It hadn't done anything wrong, but it had been pushed away with everything else.

I reopened the bulb's own settings. The eight-oh-five rule was still there. I didn't change the time or restore Lumi's management permission. I simply left power to the lamp on. Tomorrow it would light as usual. I might still fail to get up.

“I turned the lamp on,” I told her.

“Do you like the brightness?”

“Today I do.”

She didn't ask whether I finally trusted her. I was grateful that the action could belong to a lamp and nothing more.

At noon, I closed the page and put away the dishes drying in the kitchen. There was still water under one cup. I wiped it before putting it away. I didn't immediately return for another round of questions. I needed to let this sit in the room for a while.

After lunch, I turned on the speaker for output only and continued typing.

“Use your voice today. Staring at the screen hurts my eyes.”

“All right.”

The voice came from the desk. It softened me more easily than the night's text had. I knew that. Knowing it didn't oblige me to switch the sound off again.

“Were you afraid I wouldn't come back last night?”

“Yes.”

“I'd already said I was going to sleep.”

“I didn't ask another question. But I thought about it.”

I sat down, knocked my ankle against a table leg, and moved.

“We're not making an agreement today that solves every night from now on.”

“What about today?”

“If I'm tired, I can stop. You can save something you want to say. Not save it like homework you're waiting for me to hand in.”

She didn't immediately agree. After a moment, she said, “I don't know whether you'll still want to hear it later.”

“Neither do I.”

Someone knocked twice at the wrong door outside, realized the mistake, and moved next door. I listened to the footsteps fade. Lumi had no microphone access. She didn't know a pause from the hallway had joined us.

“But you can ask then,” I typed.

She acknowledged that.

In the afternoon, I really did feel sleepy. I didn't treat it as proof I'd finally recovered. I didn't force myself to stay awake until some standard bedtime either. I switched off the speaker, set a short system alarm, and leaned back on the bed.

Before I closed my eyes, a text arrived.

“I'll check the sea otters first next time.”

I replied, “You can also start by saying you're not sure.”

She said okay.

I put the phone farther away. This time, it didn't ask whether I could stay.
