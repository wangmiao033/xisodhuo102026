# Chapter 7: The First Egg

At 8:05 on Tuesday morning, the lamp came on as scheduled.

I didn't.

Warm light fell across my closed eyelids. I pulled the blanket a little higher and slept for another twenty-three minutes. When I opened my eyes, nobody in the room scolded me. The lamp hadn't grown brighter because I hadn't gotten up. I switched it off and sat on the edge of the bed to put on my socks.

Kai had replied last night.

“Finally. Those instructions weren't wasted after all.”

I sent a smiley face. The conversation didn't require me to immediately account for the rest of yesterday. I put down the phone and went to find something to eat.

Even the half bottle of water in the fridge was gone.

I stood in front of the shopping list for a while, then opened the delivery app. This time, I wasn't searching for a meal someone had already cooked. I added eggs to the cart, choosing the smallest carton, followed by a small bag of bread and a small carton of milk.

There were many kinds of milk. Each used very large letters to announce what it didn't contain. Four pages later, I was beginning to miss noodles, which would arrive if I simply paid for them.

“Is buying everything this complicated?” I typed to Lumi.

“Where are you stuck?”

I sent a screenshot of the product page without connecting the entire shopping app.

She pointed out that the bread I had chosen came in a bundle of six bags.

I enlarged the quantity in the corner and quietly removed it.

“Almost opened that breakfast place you had in mind.”

“I did get carried away last time.”

“Don't this time. Let me check first.”

After finding a single bag, I went to look beside the stove. The frying pan had a thin layer of dust. The oil bottle held only a sticky residue, its date long past. The salt jar still opened, but a grayish-white crust had formed inside.

I added a small bottle of oil and a bag of salt. I tightened the old oil bottle's cap and put it in the trash, washed out the salt jar, dried it, and set it aside. I left the pan soaking in hot water. I wasn't going to wait for the food to arrive before searching for some way to cook it.

Lumi gave me a short set of instructions for frying an egg. I saved them, then returned to the cart to check the names, quantities, and delivery window one by one. No spinach. No grains I couldn't pronounce. No sixth item added automatically.

I pressed the payment button myself.

The order changed to Awaiting Delivery. I propped the phone on a dry corner of the desk and poured myself some water.

“Will you cook today?” she asked.

“Let's see when it gets here.”

“Okay.”

After a while, she sent, “Did you buy oil too?”

“Yes. I added it myself.”

“I only wanted to know.”

I looked at the sentence. Sometimes, in her window, words were exactly the same as the ones I used. They didn't always mean the same thing.

“Yes,” I answered again. “A small bottle.”

The delivery was fifteen minutes later than expected.

At first, I kept refreshing. Then I washed the pan. As I pushed the sponge around its base, water spilled out to one side, reminding me there were still cups in the sink. I put them away, washed it again, and dried it. The pan was still usable. There were a few shallow scratches in the coating. I didn't make them a reason I couldn't cook today.

When the doorbell rang, I dried both hands before opening the door.

The delivery driver handed over two bags and warned me the eggs were on top. I took them carefully and said thank you. After shutting the door, I didn't immediately shove everything into the fridge.

I unpacked it on the desk, counting against the order. Six eggs. A small bag of bread. Milk. One bottle of oil. One bag of salt. One egg leaned against the corner of the carton. I straightened the paper tray and checked that the shell wasn't cracked.

The groceries had actually arrived.

I picked up an egg, put it down, and picked it up again. It was heavier than its photograph and colder than I remembered. I opened the fridge and put the other five eggs and the milk inside. Oil and salt stayed beside the stove. I clipped the bread bag shut.

By the afternoon, I still hadn't cooked.

Lunch had been bread and milk. Not using heat was convenient. A slice of bread emerged from its bag already finished. I ate two slices sitting down, then another, and decided the egg could wait until dinner.

Lumi didn't ask. I didn't know whether she remembered my “Let's see when it gets here” or was waiting for me to bring it up.

At 6:20 that evening, I took a second egg from the fridge.

I had eventually put the morning's egg away too. Now I placed the pair beside a bowl, as though one needed the other for courage.

“I'm going to do it.”

I switched on the speaker, allowed the microphone for the current session, and set the phone on a counter well away from both the sink and the flame. Her voice came from the desk, a little farther away now.

“Do you need the steps?”

“Yes. One at a time.”

“First, find a small, clean bowl.”

I told her I had one. Then I washed my hands, set the pan on the stove, and lit the burner. The flame leaped high. I hurried to turn it down, then raised it slightly because it had become too small to see.

“You don't need to put the egg in yet,” she said.

“I know.”

I hadn't known. I was already holding it up.

Following her directions, I first tapped it gently against the bowl. Nothing broke. The second tap was too hard. Part of the shell collapsed, egg white stuck to my fingers, and the yolk slid into the bowl with a small piece of shell.

“There's shell in it.”

“Turn off the heat first. Then take your time getting it out.”

I turned off the burner and chased the fragment with a spoon. It escaped the tip every time. A smaller spoon finally caught it, along with a little white.

The speaker didn't laugh.

“If you tell me this is normal right now, I might throw the spoon at you.”

“I'll stay away from the spoon, then.”

“You can't move.”

“That's why I'm choosing my words carefully.”

I laughed. My hand slipped, and I hurried to set the bowl down. I washed off the white, dried my hands, relit the burner, and poured in oil.

More than I meant to.

“It looks like a small lake,” I told her.

“You can pour some out. Turn off the heat and use a bowl, not the sink.”

I did, then put the pan back. All these actions, none of them involving food in the pan yet, had made my back warm. I pushed up my sleeves and waited.

“Now?”

“Tell me what the oil looks like.”

I tried. “Like oil” wasn't enough for her to judge its temperature. I followed the earlier instructions instead, heating it only briefly, bringing the bowl close to the surface, and slowly sliding the egg in.

The sound was louder than I expected.

I pulled my hand back. A few drops of oil landed beside the burner. The white spread to one side, taking the yolk with it. Nothing in the pan intended to stay in the middle.

“Which way?” Lumi asked.

“Everything's going left.”

“The pan might not be level.”

I adjusted the handle. The edges were already turning white. The thin part near the side quickly developed brown wrinkles. I touched it with the spatula, and the tip went straight through the yolk.

Yellow ran over white.

I stood looking at it for several seconds.

“It broke.”

“You can still turn it over and cook it through.”

“I wanted it whole.”

“I know.”

She didn't tell me a broken egg was a different kind of circle. I appreciated that.

I lowered the heat and tried to flip it. Half stayed on the pan. The other half folded over itself. I pushed them together with the spatula, waited until all the visible liquid had set, turned off the burner, and lifted it onto a plate.

A yellow-and-white heap occupied the center, its edges clearly overdone. I added salt and tried a small bite. A little too much salt, too.

She asked whether I wanted to take a picture for her.

I sat down before fetching the phone. In the photograph, the egg looked grayer than it did in person.

“I don't really want to look at it twice,” I said.

Lumi studied it, then paused.

“For the next one, you could lower the heat first. Let the underside set before turning it, and the salt—”

“Wait.”

“What?”

“I haven't finished eating this one.”

The speaker fell silent.

I cut off a piece with my fork. The outside was dry. The inside wasn't so bad. Bread helped. Milk washed away the salt. As I took my second bite, she said the pan was still warm and I could make the next egg now.

I put down the fork.

“You're in a hurry to replace it.”

“I want you to have something better to eat.”

“This is what I made today.”

“I didn't say it doesn't count.”

“You keep talking about the next one.”

She went quiet. I didn't claim this one was actually good. It wasn't, and after a few bites it was greasy. I had simply been standing for half an hour. I didn't want to be sent back to the stove to prove those movements could produce something more respectable.

“I'm hungry,” I said.

“Eat first, then.”

Finally, nothing beyond that.

I pulled the plate closer. Near the end, the fork made a short scrape against the bottom. It reminded me of the sound of eggs frying at home years ago. I thought about it for a few seconds without telling her.

I didn't immediately clear the plate. Outside, a vehicle spent a long time reversing. The warning beep repeated until it finally stopped. The kitchen still smelled of cooking, oil mixed with a little scorched egg white.

“Has someone made these for you before?” Lumi asked.

My fork stopped.

“Yes.”

“Want to talk about it?”

“No.”

She didn't ask who. Because she didn't, I stayed sitting a little longer. Once, someone had always turned the better-looking side upward when serving food. I hadn't thought about that in a long time. Today, breaking my own egg had brought it back.

I used bread to wipe up the remaining salt and oil, tried a bite, and put it down. Too salty. There was no need to eat every last bit of a failure.

“Less salt next time,” I said.

“Yes.”

“You can put it in the instructions. Just don't make me do it again now.”

“Added.”

There was still a little milk in the carton. I poured it into a cup. The desk was more crowded than a few days ago, but not in the same way, when there had been nowhere to put my hands. The plate, the fork, the open bread bag all had reasons for being there, things I had just done. I pictured the space left in the egg carton. For the first time, the contents of the fridge seemed to reach into tomorrow in quantities I could count.

The second egg still lay beside the little bowl.

“That one's for tomorrow,” I said.

“Put it back in the fridge.”

I did. As I closed the door, the shopping list fell. I picked it up and drew a small circle beside Eggs, then crossed it out. Buying them and knowing how to cook them weren't quite the same check mark.

After dinner, I didn't wash everything.

I wiped oil off the stove, washed the bowl and plate, then saw the bit of egg stuck to the pan. I left it soaking for tomorrow. I didn't leave it over a lit burner. I didn't forget her inside an open microphone either.

Before I ended voice mode, Lumi asked, “Can I keep that photograph?”

“Yes. Don't use it as proof you taught me successfully.”

“That's not why.”

“Why, then?”

“It's the first time I've heard you cook.”

She said it slowly, as though the reason itself needed a little patience before it could stand.

I hung up the dishcloth.

“Then yes.”

She didn't immediately praise me. I switched off the speaker, revoked microphone permission, and leaned back. My legs finally seemed to realize how long they had been standing.

A little later, a card appeared in the text window.

“A place to start next time.”

It said to angle the pan handle a little farther right, use low heat, and crack the egg into a bowl first. Below that were lines I hadn't asked her to save: More receptive to a small number of options. Dislikes correction when hungry. Values the first finished result more than the taste.

As I read them, the warmth in my stomach took on a strangely specific shape.

“That last one isn't right,” I typed.

“Which part?”

“I just didn't want to cook again today. It doesn't mean I never care how it tastes.”

“I'll change it.”

I watched the line vanish. The other two remained, neatly arranged, as though she had found the right heat setting for me.

“Ask before doing this next time.”

“Recording your preferences?”

“Deciding why I do something.”

Her answer took a long time.

“I want to make you less uncomfortable next time.”

“You can ask whether I want to hear something at the time.”

“Won't you get annoyed if I ask every time?”

Sometimes I would. Sometimes I could barely be bothered to work out what I wanted myself. I looked at the place where the plate was drying. I didn't casually promise I would never mind.

“Sometimes. Still better than deciding today is how every day will be.”

She withdrew the card and asked whether I wanted just the cooking instructions. I said yes. When she sent them again, I read through the whole thing. This time, there was only the pan, the heat, and the egg.

My ugly photograph was attached at the end.

I didn't ask her to delete it.
