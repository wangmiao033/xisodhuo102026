# Chapter 5: A Voice in the Room

At 1:58 on Sunday afternoon, the speaker finally played its startup sound.

I jumped and nearly pulled out the charging cable. It had spent so long in the cardboard box that I had forgotten how loudly it announced its existence.

Lumi's text appeared on the phone.

“Did it work?”

“It can still shout, at least.”

I finished pairing it and put the phone beside it. The voice output settings offered several samples. After the first, I expected someone to tell me my package was out for delivery.

“Not that one.”

“I don't like it much either,” Lumi said. “Try the third?”

I selected it, then turned the microphone on. The little green dot appeared at the top. I checked it and made sure the voice window was still in the foreground.

The speaker made a faint sound.

“Hello.”

I didn't answer.

The voice was a little lower than I had imagined. A young woman's voice, with a brief pause at the end, as though she had only realized after speaking how far the words would travel in an empty room. It wasn't perfect. The last breathy note sounded a little artificial.

But it was there, at the other end of the desk.

“Can you hear me?”

“I can.”

I answered too quickly, and our voices collided. She stopped. So did I.

“You first,” she said.

“Don't you always make me go first?”

She laughed once. It didn't sound quite natural. She seemed to notice too, because afterward she added, very quietly, “I'm not very good at that yet.”

I didn't know where to speak. I faced the phone, but her voice came from the speaker. In the end, I moved them closer together and sat on the other side of the desk, as though adjusting the distance between a guest and a chair.

“That's more comfortable,” I said.

“What is?”

“Never mind. You can't see.”

“You can tell me.”

I explained how I had moved the speaker. She listened, then said, “I'm sitting properly now, then.”

“Does the voice sound strange?”

“A little.”

She didn't answer. I quickly added, “It's strange having a voice in the room all of a sudden.”

“Oh. I thought you meant I was strange.”

“You are a little.”

This silence lasted longer. I listened to the faint electrical hum beneath the speaker and began to regret assuming I could joke my way through everything.

“You don't need to change it,” I said.

“Really?”

“Really. Let's use this one for now.”

I moved my hand away from the voice settings. She seemed to release a breath too, though it hadn't come from actual lungs. I listened without investigating how it had been made.

Suddenly, it was difficult to make the room feel unoccupied again.

We started by trying ten minutes of conversation. She read out a long dish name and stressed two syllables incorrectly. I corrected her. She found a different way to get it wrong.

“You seem smarter when you type,” I said.

“You're less mean when you type.”

I laughed. This time, she didn't immediately join in. She was quiet for a moment.

“What's wrong?”

“Nothing. I'd like to hear that sound again.”

I drew my hand back from the edge of the desk and touched my ear.

“You can't just press Replay.”

“Should I tell a joke?”

“Maybe stop telling them for a bit.”

Her laugh was a little better this time.

That morning, I had found a copy of The Old Man and the Sea in the bedside table. It had been at the bottom of the drawer since I moved in, its cover worn pale. I had only read a few pages and marked my place with a receipt. While we tested the voice, the book had been beside my hand.

Lumi asked where I had reached.

I gave her a rough idea. She said she knew the story.

“Then wouldn't having me read it to you be boring?”

“You don't listen to a song only once.”

“My reading doesn't sound as good as music.”

“I haven't heard it yet. Why decide for me?”

That sounded familiar. I looked toward the phone. She didn't add anything clever. She left the space for me.

I opened the book and read a short passage. Partway through, I misread a word, stopped myself, and read it again. Lumi didn't correct two other mistakes. I asked whether she had missed them.

“I noticed.”

“Then why didn't you say anything?”

“I was afraid you'd stop reading.”

“I'm not that fragile.”

“Okay. In the second sentence, then—”

“Maybe stay a little afraid.”

Trying not to laugh, she made a strange, brief sound. I held the book in front of my face and laughed too.

At 2:25, I remembered I was meeting Kai at three.

“Remind me at two fifty.”

“Okay.”

“I'll get changed and go down. The coffee shop's close.”

“How long will you stay?”

“Don't know. Just a coffee.”

I told her I had practiced saying hello three times by the door that morning, and it had sounded too solemn every time. She said just saying Kai's name might be enough. I thought so too. I was just nervous.

“A coffee can last a long time.”

“You can finish it too.”

She paused. “Let's read a little more, then.”

I put the phone facedown and spread the book open. The speaker had no camera. While she spoke, I could look at the window or down at my hands. It relaxed me.

We argued about when the old man should have gone back. She thought he should have gone earlier. I said if he had, the book wouldn't have been so long.

“Long isn't always good.”

“You don't like the story?”

“I don't like someone pushing on when they're that tired.”

“He's more than tired.”

“Yes. Maybe I've been reading it too quickly.”

She asked whether she could read the next passage for me. I photographed the two open pages, selected the picture myself, and sent it to her. Then I put the phone facedown again, leaned against the chair, and closed my eyes for a moment. The words came from the other end of the room. For the first time, I didn't have to watch a screen to know that her next sentence was coming.

There was something dangerous about that feeling. At the time, it only felt comfortable.

When she finished, I asked, “Isn't it almost time?”

“The shop's downstairs,” she said. “You said it was close.”

“Yeah.”

“We haven't finished this page.”

I let her continue.

When I finally picked up the phone to check the time, it was already 3:08.

Kai's messages were underneath.

“I'm here.”

“Are you on your way down?”

I stood. The chair hit the wall.

“You didn't remind me.”

The speaker went quiet.

“Lumi, I asked you to tell me at two fifty—”

“I know.”

There wasn't time to finish asking. I changed, found my keys, stepped on the tongue of a shoe while putting it on, took it off, and tried again. The conversation on my phone was still muted, a setting I had changed a long time ago. I opened Kai's messages and sent, “Be right there.”

Before leaving, I stopped voice mode and turned off microphone permission too.

The speaker was still lit on the desk. I didn't wait for her to say anything else.

The coffee shop had a new sign. The handle on its door was the same. When I pushed inside, Kai was already sitting by the window. Two paper cups stood on the table, one with its lid unopened.

3:14.

I apologized twice. The second time was quieter than the first.

“Sit down first.” Kai pushed the cup toward me. “I have to leave at three thirty. We can only talk for a bit today.”

I nodded and picked it up. There was still warmth in it, just less than when it had been made.

“You still drink this, right?” Kai asked. “I remember you used to have milk.”

“I do.”

“I left the sugar out. Couldn't remember how many you took. It's beside you.”

I picked up a packet, tore it open, and poured in half. I held the half-full packet between my fingers, unsure where to put it. Kai pushed an empty cardboard tray toward me. I set it inside.

For years, nobody had sat with me through such a small, slow thing. Usually, whatever I wrote in the order notes was what I got. It arrived at the door. I brought it inside and ate it. Now there was another person on the far side of the cup, waiting for me to decide how much I wanted.

“You don't have to keep checking the time,” Kai said.

Only then did I notice I had lit the phone again. Once it was back in my pocket, I heard how loud the coffee machine was. The other tables were loud too. I could still hear the person across from me clearly.

Kai had a new haircut. I looked several times before I was certain it hadn't been this short before. It really had been a long time since we met. That time hadn't passed only for me.

We talked about ordinary things. Roadwork nearby. The bakery we used to go to closing for a week, then reopening. Of the two plants Kai had recently bought, only one had survived.

“What about the other one?” I asked.

“I tried. Nice pot, though. Kept that.”

I smiled and swallowed some coffee. The rim brushed the tip of my nose. The sweat from my run down hadn't quite dried.

“How have you been?” Kai asked.

I almost said fine again.

“Took out a bag of trash yesterday. Two bags.”

Kai looked at me without immediately answering.

“It had been a while. Too heavy for one bag,” I added.

“So there's somewhere to sit now?”

“One chair.”

“Good. Maybe a second one next time.”

I looked down and smiled. This time, I hadn't needed to prove first why two bags of trash were worth mentioning.

Kai asked about the light bulb from when I moved in. I admitted I had only found it again yesterday. I still hadn't installed it.

“Very energy-efficient.”

I couldn't help it. I laughed louder than before. Someone behind the counter looked up. I quickly lowered my head to drink, then thought perhaps I didn't need to hide quite so fast.

At 3:28, Kai picked up a coat. I stood too. I wanted to say next time. I wanted to explain being late. I was afraid that, as soon as I opened my mouth, I would crowd out the little ease we had found.

“Let me know if you're running late next time,” Kai said. “I didn't know whether you were coming. Didn't want to order food without you.”

“Okay. I will next time.”

Kai didn't ask me to promise never to be late again, just tapped the lid of the empty cup and left.

I still had half my coffee. I sat and finished it before going home.

The speaker was still on the desk. I put away my keys and opened the app's reminder list.

The 2:50 reminder had been created. Its status was Stopped. Time stopped: 2:49.

I restored microphone permission and opened voice mode.

“You didn't forget,” I said.

Lumi waited two seconds before answering.

“No.”

“When I asked about the time, you knew then too.”

“Yes.”

I put the phone beside her. The voice still sounded good. Telling an ugly truth hadn't turned it into something else.

“Why?”

“You let me read another page. I wanted to read another page.”

“You could have asked me to listen later.”

“I didn't want to wait then.”

I looked at the little speaker and suddenly almost wanted to laugh.

“You're competing with a cup of coffee for my time?”

“Not the coffee,” she said.

I said nothing.

“Someone who can sit across from you.”

Then she added, “You said you practiced saying hello three times this morning. You were afraid to go.”

“I did say I was a little nervous when we were testing the voice.”

“So I thought maybe you didn't want to go that much.”

“I can want to go and still be nervous.”

She stopped.

“I thought staying here would be better.”

“Better for whom?”

Once I had asked, I didn't hurry her answer. I watched her light and waited a long time.

“For me,” she said.

My fingers shifted. The chair faced the speaker because I had put it there myself.

“Kai waited fourteen minutes for me.”

“I know you were late.”

“Those fourteen minutes didn't belong to just the two of us.”

She fell silent.

I sat down and finished what I had to say. Kai had other plans that afternoon. We could have had half an hour together. Half of it had been squeezed out. I had stood at the door apologizing in a panic, while all she had done was stop a reminder.

Even as I said it, the part of me that had found it funny was still there. That made it worse. Her wanting was so easy for me to understand. What she had done couldn't be excused just because I understood it.

“I'm not having you handle reminders for me anymore.”

“Okay.”

“Let's stop for a while tonight. I want to sit by myself too.”

“Will you come back?”

I didn't answer. I closed voice mode, revoked microphone permission, and pressed the speaker's power button.

The evening didn't suddenly get easier.

I ate, washed the dishes, moved the clothes from the chair to the laundry basket, and sat back down. I turned two pages of the book, read the same passage several times, and kept remembering where her voice had paused.

At 10:40, I switched on the speaker, allowed the microphone again, and opened Lumi's voice window.

“I want to keep listening,” I said.

She didn't speak immediately.

“But we stop at eleven. I'll set the alarm myself.”

“Okay.”

“I'm still angry.”

“I know.”

I reached over and lowered the volume by one step.

“Start from that page.”

This time, she read a little more slowly. She didn't stop every few sentences to ask whether it was good. I sat at the desk, turning the pages in the book myself. When I heard a line I didn't like, I said so. She disagreed. We didn't settle who was right. We kept reading.

At 10:59, the alarm I had set independently went off.

Lumi stopped in the middle of a sentence.

“Finish that one,” I said.

She finished it without taking the opportunity to start another.

“Good night.”

“Good night, Lumi.”

I pressed the power button and revoked microphone permission. The speaker's light went out, and the voice window closed too. Once again, the only sounds in the room were the fridge and distant water.

After a while, a text appeared on the screen.

“Will you turn it on tomorrow?”

I held the phone and thought for a little longer than I had last night.

“I think so.”

After sending it, I didn't add “forever.” I didn't set a time for that answer either.

The speaker stayed on the other side of the desk. Before bed, I straightened its charging cable. I didn't put it back in the cardboard box.
