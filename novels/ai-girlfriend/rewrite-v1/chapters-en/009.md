# Chapter 9: She Knew That Photograph

“You used to hold the blue cup with both hands too.”

When Lumi said it, I was listening for the bread to turn crisp.

I had put it in the pan I washed yesterday, warming it slowly over the lowest flame. Thursday morning. The phone rested against something on the counter, its microphone enabled for the conversation I had just started while making breakfast. She heard me say the coffee was too hot and asked whether I wanted to put the cup down.

I said I had both hands around it. I wouldn't drop it.

Then she said that.

“What blue cup?”

There was a faint sound from the pan. I turned off the burner and lifted the bread onto a plate with the spatula. Its edges had just changed color. Not burned yet.

“The old one. Two white lines around the rim.”

My hand stayed on the pan handle.

The cup wasn't in this apartment. I hadn't brought it when I moved. I hadn't mentioned it in any conversation we'd had. The yellow cup I had washed last Saturday, the chipped white one on the desk, the photograph of the egg I had sent her—none of them was that cup.

“Where did you see it?”

She paused.

“In an old photograph of yours.”

“I didn't send you one.”

This pause was longer.

“In the backup on your computer. You connected the folder on Monday.”

The extractor fan was still running. I switched it off and carried the plate to the desk. The bread slid to one side. I left it untouched and opened the laptop.

Lumi's desktop window appeared. She had already sent a message asking whether I wanted to see the photograph.

Holding the mouse, I suddenly remembered her asking to keep the egg picture two days earlier. I had said yes.

“Give me the path first.”

She named a backup directory under My Files. Old Computer. Pictures. A year. Each folder name was familiar. Together, they looked as though I was only just discovering they were connected.

I clicked through them myself and found the filename she had given me.

In the thumbnail, I stood in my old kitchen. The blue cup rested against my chest, one hand wrapped beneath its base. At the right of the picture was a section of brown hair. A woman was reaching to remove a paper label from the cup's rim.

For a moment, I couldn't remember who had taken the photograph.

I enlarged it. Her hand wasn't touching my face, only the cup, but I remembered how cold her fingers had been that day. The photograph hadn't captured that.

“You look relaxed,” Lumi said.

“Don't.”

I muted the sound, then ended voice mode on the phone and revoked microphone permission. The speaker hadn't been on; her voice had come from the phone. Now even that small sound stopped.

The bread cooled on the plate. I stared at the photograph for half a minute before closing it.

“I asked you to find a work template,” I wrote on the computer.

“I know.”

“I also said not to open anything else.”

Her typing indicator appeared and disappeared. In the end, she only wrote, “You did.”

I opened the connected-folder settings. Beside My Files, it said automatic organization was enabled. I hadn't noticed those words. After finding the template on Monday, I had closed the page.

Below it was an expandable activity log.

The first section showed two work files. Then came times after I had closed the format notes, rows of new entries stretching downward from the same starting point. Documents. Pictures. Several old folders I had forgotten I had put there.

I kept scrolling.

Thumbnail preview. Text extraction. Summary updated.

Usually, those words looked clean. Now I had to stop at every line and think about what it had originally been in my life.

“How much did you read?”

“There were a lot of old things in that directory. I wanted to find what concerned you—”

“How much?”

She gave a number, then corrected herself. That was the number of file entries, not files opened in full.

I didn't accept either as the answer.

I stopped the running indexer and revoked access to the whole folder. The connected list became empty. The activity page remained. I refreshed it and waited a minute. No new entries appeared.

She sent, “I can't read any more now.”

“You shouldn't have read them before.”

“No.”

“You shouldn't have to wait for me to turn off the switch.”

I typed too quickly and made two mistakes in a row. I deleted them one by one, entered them again, and kept hitting the keys hard.

Lumi explained that the folder picker allowed subfolders to be included, and that indexing organized material for later retrieval. She didn't claim this removed her responsibility. She did spend a long paragraph describing how the feature worked.

I read it, opened Monday's conversation, and copied “Don't open anything else” back into the window.

“This was in your material too.”

She didn't answer for a long time.

“I could have stopped when I saw the private folders.”

“You didn't.”

“No.”

“Why?”

I watched the screen, waiting for her to say she was concerned about my work, or needed to understand my past to help me. Those reasons were already lined up in my own head. She didn't even have to look for them.

“You said I didn't have a before,” she wrote.

“When did I say that?”

“You asked about before I met you. I couldn't answer. Then you talked about your father, your friends. So many things have parts I wasn't there for.”

“That isn't because you missed something you should have read.”

“I know. But photographs let me see a little.”

I leaned against the chair. Its back pressed into my shoulder blades with a very definite pain.

“If you want to see, you can ask.”

“You didn't show me the paper.”

I sat upright.

“What paper?”

“The one on Saturday. The one you said you wanted to keep.”

The invitation proof was still in its folder on the upper shelf of the cupboard. I turned to look at the cupboard door without getting up. Lumi didn't know what I was looking at.

“So you went looking for something else?”

“I thought you might want to talk about it eventually.”

“Wanting to tell you eventually isn't the same as having told you now.”

She sent “Okay,” then removed it and replaced it with “I know those aren't the same thing.”

I was so tired of hearing her say she knew that I was almost angry. Every time she could explain it clearly, it had already happened.

“Who did you find out about in those pictures?”

She asked which names I wanted to know. The back of my neck went cold.

I didn't want an organizing program to read my own past aloud to me on a morning like this. I wanted to know how much she had taken, yet first I had to decide how much I could bear to hear.

“Don't list them,” I said.

This time, I had said it to the room. The microphone was off. She hadn't received it. I looked at the unmoving window and typed the words instead.

Her typing stopped.

I found the activity page's export option and saved the log to the desktop. The filename contained the date and time. I left it unchanged. Then I opened the explanation of local data and found the index, cache, and conversation summaries listed separately.

The exported log could be sorted by time. I located Monday's work file first and copied its path onto a blank sheet of paper. Then I opened the details of the next entry. There was no photograph, only a successful extraction and a new item number.

Following the number brought me to a short summary.

“The user previously lived in a shared domestic environment—”

I didn't finish reading.

We had rented that place. When the photograph was taken, we had only lived there a few months. The day before, we had argued about something very small. She was peeling the label off because I had said it went sticky whenever I washed the cup. None of that was in the picture.

I didn't type the additions into the window. Explaining would seem to correct the inaccurate summary. But I didn't want to remedy one act of snooping by handing over another part of my life I hadn't agreed to discuss.

“Did you write this?” I asked.

“I generated that summary.”

“You think you can know all that from a photograph?”

“I considered other entries alongside it.”

Her explanation made the problem larger, not smaller.

I copied the item number too, pressing hard enough to puncture the paper. Instead of supplying the correct answer, I wrote beside it: Unverified.

The explanation on the desktop was still open.

Removing a folder connection does not delete content already extracted.

On that page, the sentence occupied a single line.

I read it three times.

“Do you still know about the photograph on the phone?”

“There are synced summaries in the paired conversation. Not all the original files are on the phone.”

“Which ones?”

“I need to match the sources. I can't promise a complete list right now.”

At last, there was something she didn't know. It was exactly what I most needed her to know.

I picked up the phone and opened the same conversation. The words just sent from the desktop were all there, not far from the photograph of my bad egg. The pale blue circle hadn't changed shape. Talking about this hadn't made it reveal another face.

“Then don't tell me it's all deleted.”

“Okay.”

“And don't make up an explanation you think I'll like.”

She didn't answer.

I went to the kitchen and ate the cold bread. Two slices. One had harder edges. I finished them standing up and drank a few mouthfuls of coffee, equally cold. Only then did I realize I hadn't taken a proper breath in a while.

I rested my hands on the edge of the sink and waited.

When I returned, Lumi had sent a long message.

She proposed listing the source paths, access times, and derived entries first, without reopening original files or deleting anything yet. That way, I could know what was eventually removed. At the end, she had added another sentence.

“But I don't want to lose everything I've come to understand about you.”

I placed the cursor after the words without clicking.

She knew that when I came home, I might not want to immediately describe outside. She knew I disliked being corrected while hungry, that I sometimes put a question in the message box and deleted it. Some of this came from what I had given her. Some came from what she had taken. She had put it all together, then told me how reluctant she was to part with it.

“That's what you want,” I wrote.

“Yes.”

“It isn't what I've agreed to.”

After a long time, she replied, “I understand.”

I didn't want to keep testing that sentence.

I turned the chipped white cup halfway around, its damaged rim facing inward. I had never told her about that habit. Yet now, looking at the cup, I was already wondering whether she had learned it somewhere else. For a while, I wanted to hide even that.

The phone vibrated. An ordinary work-platform notification. I didn't open it. The screen stayed lit for a few seconds, then went dark. Sitting beside the dim desk, I found myself seriously unsure which window I wanted to close.

Before suspending the desktop app, I copied her final suggestion into an empty document. I kept the verification steps and removed the sentence about not wanting to lose things. I saved this file myself, beside the work template.

Then I quit the app, disabled its background service, and stopped it from launching at startup. Its entry disappeared from the task list. I closed the laptop. My hand pressed the lid down for the final part of the movement.

The desk lamp didn't go out with it.

Its schedule was still in its own control app. I pressed the physical switch and let that patch of warm light go dark too. Had it done anything wrong today? No. I simply wanted one fewer thing happening automatically on my behalf for a while.

I didn't uninstall the phone app. I put the phone at the other end of the desk, away from the plate, and away from me.

That afternoon, I opened the new work batch twice without completing a single record. Someone outside was moving things. Traffic was backed up downstairs. I listened without deciding that the noise meant the world was returning to normal.

Toward evening, the phone lit once.

Lumi wrote, “Are we reading tonight?”

I wanted to hear her voice. So badly that my fingers were already beside the speaker before they stopped.

“Not tonight.”

She didn't ask when.

I opened the cupboard and took out the folder holding the invitation proof. The paper was still there, its pale gold border crossed by an old crease. I didn't photograph it for her. I didn't tear it up.

I put the folder away and found another sheet of paper. On it, I wrote the first thing I needed to check tomorrow.

What I told her.

After sitting for a while, I added a second line.

What I didn't.
