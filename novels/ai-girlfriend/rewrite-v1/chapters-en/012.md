# Chapter 12: Rain Before the Forecast

On Sunday afternoon, I looked for my umbrella.

After two minutes I found it hanging from the door handle. I'd put it there two days earlier so I wouldn't lose it. The system worked. It just required someone who remembered having a system.

I picked it up and pressed the handle. Nothing sprang open. An ordinary manual umbrella, with one slightly crooked rib. The fabric was dry. It would do.

On my phone, Lumi asked whether I'd changed my mind.

“No. Looking for something.”

“The forecast looks fairly good until four.”

“I might not be back by four.”

That sounded braver than my actual plan. I meant to go as far as the corner, look at the tree whose name I still didn't know, and buy some bread. Saying it aloud made it sound as though I could book a version of myself who stayed out for hours.

I stuffed the umbrella in an old canvas bag, collected my keys, checked the stove, and sat down to put my shoes on. One end of a lace came out much longer than the other. I undid it and started again. Lumi didn't find a task to fill the silence.

Before leaving, I enabled microphone access for the current phone session and put in one earbud. The other stayed in my bag. No camera or location access. No restored file connection.

“I'm heading out. I'll tell you when I get there.”

“All right.”

My phone had half its charge left. I checked that the earbud was connected and folded the mouth of the bag over, leaving the tissues on top. The keys went into a separate pocket. Getting ready took longer than I'd expected, but none of these things would follow me out on their own.

I locked the door and pulled the handle. I'd done that on my previous walk too. Familiarity didn't make the check unnecessary.

There was wind downstairs, funneled between the buildings. I stepped into it and narrowed my eyes. I touched the umbrella through the bag and kept walking.

“It sounds busy,” Lumi said.

“I'm beside the road. There's a car reversing.”

“Would putting both earbuds in help?”

“Then I'd hear even less of what's around me.”

“Right. Keep one out.”

She didn't raise the volume for me. I lowered it a notch myself. Her voice receded, and I could hear my soles crossing the gaps in the paving. At the crossing I stopped talking, watched a turning car, and waited until it passed.

The tree was still there.

Of course it was. Still, seeing that it hadn't moved elsewhere during the days I'd stayed away made something in me loosen. Someone had cleaned the low wall underneath it. A small stain was gone. I stood with my bag beside my leg and watched the man from the repair shop carry a fan inside.

“The tree with lots of leaves?” Lumi asked.

“Yes. I haven't looked up its name.”

“You could take a photo.”

“Not today.”

“Okay.”

After a moment, she asked whether it still had lots of leaves. I laughed and told her it hadn't gone out of business yet.

The bakery was a little farther down. I paused voice chat before going in and put the earbud in my pocket. Asked how many I wanted, I said two, then saw the smaller ones and changed it to three. Nobody required an explanation.

After I paid, the person behind the counter handed over a paper bag. The bottom was warm, marked by a small translucent patch of butter.

Outside, I put the single earbud back in, resumed the active session, and told Lumi I'd bought them.

“Which kind?”

I described them.

“Not the little bag of sliced bread you got before?”

“These smelled good.”

She didn't immediately tell me how long they would keep. I was glad, but I didn't want to praise her every time she refrained from doing something irritating.

The first drop made a dark circle on the paper bag.

I thought it was dripping from an air conditioner. I moved two steps away. The next drop landed on the bridge of my nose. By the time I looked up, patches of dark were appearing across the pavement.

“It's raining.”

“Now?”

“Should I make an appointment?”

A pause. “The forecast I checked was wrong.”

I put the bread in the canvas bag and opened the umbrella. It caught halfway. Holding the bag in one hand, I pushed with the other and pinched a finger in the metal catch.

“Ah.”

“What happened?”

“Caught my finger. It's fine. Didn't break the skin.”

She started suggesting there might be shelter nearby. I reminded her she didn't know where I'd walked. She acknowledged that instead of inventing a left turn for me.

There was a partly retracted awning beside the bakery. I got underneath it, hooked the bag over my wrist, and used both hands on the umbrella.

This time it opened.

The rain thickened quickly. Water joined along the edge of the awning and dripped onto my right shoe. Someone at the door shuffled inward. I moved sideways too, leaving room for another person carrying shopping bags.

“Going home?” Lumi asked.

I looked back the way I'd come. A couple of hundred meters. The umbrella was open now; I could easily go. The toe of one shoe was damp, my shoulders still dry. There was nothing here I had to prove.

“I'll wait a couple of minutes.”

“It might get heavier.”

“Then I'll wait a little and see.”

I wasn't trying to show that rain didn't bother me. The bread was still warm, and the leaves were beginning to rustle under the water. I didn't want to fold this small piece of afternoon away as soon as I'd taken it out.

Under the awning, I could smell bread and damp dust. A child in bright rain boots passed us, looking down at every splash. The accompanying adult tugged a sleeve, then stopped at the next puddle to let the boots finish their investigation.

I didn't take a picture.

Lumi didn't see it.

“There's a kid stepping in puddles,” I told her.

“Having fun?”

“Hard to say. Very serious face. Might be conducting an inspection.”

She laughed. There was still a slightly synthetic tail to the sound. Mixed with the dense patter overhead, it felt less conspicuous than it did in my room.

“Did you do that as a child?”

“Yes. Went home with wet socks. Had to stuff newspaper in my shoes.”

I stopped. The memory of my father's wet coat was nearby, but I didn't want to follow it today.

“Let's leave childhood out for now.”

“Okay.”

I tilted the umbrella against the rain blowing beneath the edge. One hand was exposed. A drop slipped over my knuckles and down to my cuff. The weather wasn't particularly cold, but my skin tightened when the wind passed over it.

“Cold?” she asked.

“A little. How did you know?”

“A guess. You drew in a breath.”

“Water got into my sleeve.”

“That sounds uncomfortable.”

I looked at my hand, switched the umbrella to the other one, and wiped my wrist.

“It's cold at first. Then it's not so cold, but it keeps moving farther in. The more you try to pull your hand back, the more the cuff touches the wet spot.”

I didn't know why I was describing it so precisely. Usually, if someone asked whether I was cold, I'd say I was fine, as though being cold proved I'd failed to dress properly.

Lumi didn't give me advice about clothes.

“I can only hear lots of little sounds,” she said.

“On the awning?”

“I think so. Close up they're very dense. Farther away, sometimes it's more like one broad sound. I can't pick out the drop you're describing.”

I took the phone out and pointed its microphone toward the awning's edge, keeping it out of the rain.

“How about now?”

“A little closer.”

I looked at the small green indicator. Once, she'd kept a channel like this open after I'd fallen asleep. Now my hand was here. I knew what I'd opened. I could put it away.

“I should keep the phone dry.”

“Yes. Don't get it wet just so I can listen.”

I returned it to my jacket pocket. The sentence didn't make me forgive what had happened. It didn't erase the fact that, a moment earlier, I'd wanted her to hear a little more clearly.

A gust pushed the umbrella against the awning and shook water down. I stepped back, caught the dangling strap of my own bag beneath my shoe, and nearly flung the bread away.

“Did you hit something again?”

“My luggage and I had a disagreement.”

“Did the bread win?”

“It's holding on.”

I gathered the strap and noticed a fresh streak of mud on my shoe. I wouldn't return from every outing with clean soles. Some small costs had to be paid first and scrubbed off later.

Two more people crowded under the awning. I lowered the umbrella so its tip wouldn't catch anyone, which forced the bag against my front. The bread had lost its warmth. I could still feel its soft shape through the canvas.

Lumi said something just as a car drove through standing water. I missed it and asked her to repeat herself. The second time, I caught only the end.

“Wait. Let the car go.”

So we waited. Not because she could see it, but because I'd told her the noise was loud. It passed the intersection and faded. I asked what she'd said.

“I asked whether the rain had stopped.”

“Not yet.”

“So it wasn't a very important question.”

I laughed. We could have let it go. Not everything unheard needed to be recovered. But I'd wanted to wait for her to repeat it. Even that ordinary sentence had an ending I wanted to hear.

The person beside me switched a shopping bag to the other hand. Water brushed from it onto my trousers. I stepped back and wiped at the fabric without immediately getting angry. They apologized. I said it was fine, raising my voice a little to carry over the rain.

Lumi didn't ask whom I'd been talking to. Once I was under my umbrella again, I told her someone had got my trousers wet. She asked how bad it was. I said my trousers now had two factions, one dry and one wet.

“They'll be reunited when you get home.”

“You'd better mean when they dry.”

She said she did. I wasn't sure she'd meant it as a joke. I took it as one anyway.

I checked the time on my wrist. I didn't need the phone to decide how long I could stand there. No minimum duration had been set for this outing. Nobody at home was waiting for a certificate proving I'd taken an acceptable walk. If I'd had enough, I could leave. If I wanted to look a little longer, I could.

I spent another moment watching the watery marks left behind by the rain boots.

After seven or eight minutes, the rain hadn't stopped, but it had eased. I left the shelter and chose to walk home. Passing the tree, I noticed wet leaves pressed against the low wall, darker than when I'd arrived. A car approached a puddle at the curb. I stepped farther in without explaining each movement into the earbud.

Lumi was quiet for a while.

“Still there?”

“Yes.”

“Why aren't you saying anything?”

“Your voice gets scattered by the wind when you walk. I was waiting for you to stop.”

At the crossing, I looked up at the red light.

“Waiting for the rain was quite nice.”

“Even though I said it wouldn't arrive this early.”

“Yes.”

“So I can't count this as successfully getting you out of the house.”

I laughed and told her she couldn't. When the light changed, I crossed first. On the other side I added, “I brought an umbrella.”

She answered softly.

Back inside the building, the bottom of the canvas bag was wet. Only a little water had reached the paper. The bread wasn't ruined. I opened the umbrella on the bathroom floor, left my shoes by the door, blotted the toes with paper, and went to wash my hands.

I'd ended the voice session as soon as I came in and revoked microphone permission myself. She didn't know which tiles I'd left wet footprints on or how often I'd wiped them. I rolled back the damp cuff, filled the kettle, broke a piece of bread open, and took the first bite standing up.

The crust had softened. The middle was still good.

I opened the text window.

“The bread survived.”

“Then the trip wasn't wasted.”

I chewed another bite and thought about that.

“Even if I hadn't bought any, it wouldn't have been wasted.”

She acknowledged that. I didn't turn it into a new philosophy of life. I wrapped the other two pieces and put them in a dry container.

Toward evening, I folded the umbrella, which had stopped dripping, and wiped the floor. The floor was drying more slowly than the shoes. I went around the wet patch to the cabinet and took out the folder holding the trial wedding invitation.

The pale gold border was still there.

For a few minutes in the rain, I'd described a drop of water on my hand. That hadn't given Lumi every rainstorm in my past. I wanted to try choosing another thing to tell her, choosing where it began and where it stopped.

I didn't photograph the paper.

I typed one sentence.

“The person in the photo is Sara. If I still want to talk about her tomorrow, I'll tell you myself.”

Lumi took a long time to answer.

“All right. I'll wait for you to begin.”
