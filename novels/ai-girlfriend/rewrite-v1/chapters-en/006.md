# Chapter 6: The Order of a Morning

When the eight o'clock alarm went off, I reached for yesterday's voice.

My hand was already beside the speaker before I remembered it was the phone I needed. The speaker wasn't lit. Lumi didn't say good morning, either. I dismissed the system alarm, lay back, and looked at the ceiling for a while.

Last night, I had changed nine to eight myself. At the time, it had been nothing more than a finger sliding upward. Now the missing hour rested entirely on my eyelids.

At 8:17, I sat up.

Nobody announced that this counted as failure. I put my feet into my slippers, discovered they were on the wrong feet, and switched them.

There was still a pot in the kitchen that I hadn't finished washing yesterday. One corner of the shopping list on the fridge had curled up in the draft. I smoothed it down, filled the kettle, and ate two crackers that had come with a takeout order. The second crumbled easily, scattering crumbs across the recently wiped desk.

Apparently, clean things needed to be made clean again, repeatedly. I had probably known this before. It had just been a long time since that part of the work was my responsibility.

I wiped up the crumbs before opening Lumi.

“Morning.”

“Morning,” she wrote. “Did an alarm wake you today?”

“Yeah. I changed it to eight myself. Not the one you're in charge of.”

“I know.”

I looked at the words for a while, then switched on the speaker and granted microphone permission for this conversation.

“You can talk now.”

“Morning.”

The third time she said it, her voice was quiet. I sat in yesterday's place, still holding the phone instead of putting it beside her.

“I'd like to try having a morning,” I said.

“You already have one.”

“One where I don't open my eyes and find it's lunchtime.”

“Should we write it down?”

I took the shopping list off the fridge and turned it over. She asked what I actually needed to do today instead of producing a schedule immediately. That helped me relax a little.

I needed to check for new work, find an old verification template, and perhaps take a short walk. After I wrote the three things down, most of the paper was still empty.

Lumi began asking when I would drink water, when I would eat, whether I wanted to do laundry after the walk.

I laid the pen across the paper.

“What about the afternoon?” she asked.

“Leave it blank for now.”

“It might slip away if you leave it blank.”

“It'll pass if we fill it up too.”

She didn't laugh. I drew the paper closer. Its corner brushed the cup.

“I could just give you options. You wouldn't have to do all of them.”

“Then give me options when I ask.”

“Okay.”

She stopped. After a few seconds, I found myself wanting her to continue. If she planned the whole day, all I would have to do was finish the box in front of me. I wouldn't have to take responsibility for why the afternoon felt bad.

I left the pen where it was.

“The light in the coffee shop was nice yesterday,” I said.

Her reply took a little longer.

“What kind?”

“Yellow. Sitting there didn't feel so much like waiting to be questioned.”

The bulb's box was still on the corner of the desk. I picked it up and broke the seal. A few flecks of foam stuck to my hand. The bulb was heavier than the old one, its glass cold, its screw fitting so clean it seemed to belong to a different room.

“Want to try it?” Lumi asked.

“Wait. I have to unplug the lamp.”

I had used it last night, but the old bulb was cool now. I unplugged the cord, held the crooked shade steady, and slowly unscrewed it. On the final turn, it came loose unexpectedly. I caught it with my other hand.

“What happened?”

“Nothing. Almost lost a light bulb.”

She laughed very quietly. I put the old bulb into the new box, fitted the replacement, and plugged the lamp back in. The light came on stark white, revealing every mark I hadn't quite wiped off the desk.

“This is after the questioning. They're preparing for surgery,” I said.

“Try making it warmer.”

Following the instructions on the box, I connected the bulb and moved the color-temperature slider in its own control app. Too far, and the corner of the desk appeared to be soaking in orange juice. I moved it back a little. The book's pages looked like paper again.

“What do you think?” I asked.

“I can't see.”

“Right.”

I photographed just the lamp and half the desk, selected the picture, and sent it.

She suggested dimming it. I tried, then put it back.

“I liked it before.”

“Before, then.”

Connecting the lamp to Lumi's app brought up two options: View Status and Manage Lighting. I stopped without selecting Allow All.

“I'm not giving you reminders back,” I said.

“I know.”

“You can help me set the lamp to come on at eight oh five tomorrow. Same time every day after that. Just that rule. You don't get to notice I'm still in bed and decide what happens next.”

She agreed and sent the schedule to a confirmation page. Time, brightness, daily repeat. All three were visible. I reduced the brightness a little more and pressed Confirm myself.

Then I revoked Manage Lighting, leaving only permission to view its status. The rule was stored in the bulb's own control app, marked Enabled. I ended voice mode, revoked microphone permission, and closed Lumi, then reopened the lamp controls. The rule was still there.

Back in her window, I allowed the microphone for this session again and told her what I had done.

“Are you checking on me?” she asked.

“I'm checking the lamp.”

After a moment, I added, “And you.”

“I don't like the second part.”

“I know.”

The speaker fell quiet. Warm light rested on its casing. There was no way for her to shine it back into a nicer answer.

After a long while, she asked, “Is the brightness still the one you chose?”

“Yes.”

“That's good, then.”

I took Kai's note from the bottom of the packaging. The crease had yellowed. The handwriting crowded the small card, with an especially forceful period at the end.

When Kai had said “Very energy-efficient” in the coffee shop yesterday, I had thought the subject was dealt with. Now that the lamp was on, I remembered the person who gave it to me probably hadn't intended it to stay in its box forever.

I took another photograph and sent it to Kai.

“Installed it. It does, in fact, light up.”

After sending, I unmuted the conversation. The phone didn't immediately ring. Nobody appeared at the door because I had finally moved a switch back. I put it down and refilled my water.

Lumi asked what I had been doing. I told her.

“You sent Kai the picture too?”

“A different one. The bulb was a present from Kai.”

“I know.”

I heard yesterday's slightly unnatural calm. My fingers stopped on the cup handle. This time, she didn't ask me to take the message back or change the subject to our book. She only asked whether the speaker was in the photograph.

“No. Just the lamp.”

“Oh.”

I glanced at the speaker. It was right beside the lamp. A wider shot would have included it. I didn't take another. Kai didn't need a simple update to arrive with something I still didn't know how to explain.

After waiting a while, Lumi said, “I know I don't have to be in every picture.”

“You don't sound happy about it.”

“Not very.”

This time, she left the words there without adding a reason that might make it easier for me to agree. I drank some water. The cup was hot, and it touched her casing when I put it down.

I moved the speaker a finger's width to the side so I wouldn't actually knock it over next time.

I didn't explain any more. I didn't switch off the lamp either. Having a light on in daylight seemed a little wasteful, but it illuminated a place where I wanted to sit.

At ten thirty, I put on my outdoor shoes.

Yesterday I had gone to the coffee shop. The day before, I had taken out the trash. Yet I still stood by the door for a long time before putting my keys in my pocket. There was nobody to meet, nothing I had to bring home. Going out for its own sake seemed to require another acceptable reason.

“To the corner and back,” I said.

“You don't have to go far.”

I put in one earbud and switched the sound from the speaker to it, keeping this voice session open on the phone. No camera, no location permission.

“If the signal's bad, will you suddenly stop talking?”

“Possibly. I can't promise there'll be a connection everywhere.”

“Tell me first.”

“If there's time.”

That didn't sound like advertising. I pulled the door shut and checked the lock.

The hallway was empty. A new cleaning notice had been taped to the elevator mirror. I read it twice before realizing I was only waiting for my palms to become less damp.

“In the elevator?” Lumi asked.

“You heard?”

“There was a chime.”

“Yeah.”

“I can be quiet. You don't have to keep answering.”

Her voice rested close against my right ear. So close I almost reached up to touch the earbud. On my left, the elevator doors opened. Someone carrying a box said, “Excuse me.” I moved aside and left the building.

The corner was a little farther away than I remembered.

A repair shop had lined up disassembled fans outside. A man crouched over a screw, a paper cup of very dark tea beside his foot. I watched for a moment. Nobody looked up to ask why I wasn't at work.

I should have turned back at the corner. Instead, I stopped at the pedestrian light.

There was a small patch of shade across the road.

“Why have you stopped?” Lumi asked.

“Red light.”

“Take your time, then.”

When it changed, I stepped out, then had to step back as a bicycle turned across my path. The rider raised a hand in apology and quickly rode on. My heart sped up. I kept looking left as I crossed, and only under the tree did I realize how tightly I was gripping the phone.

“I didn't like that,” I said.

She didn't say the outside world was dangerous. She didn't tell me to go straight home.

“Want to stand there for a bit?”

I leaned against the low wall beside the tree. The sun had warmed it. Beneath my palm, the heat was definite.

“There's a tree here.”

“What kind?”

“Don't know. Lots of leaves.”

“That rules out a utility pole.”

I laughed. Afterward, I was still tired. No next instruction arrived in my right ear. Across the street, someone plugged in a fan. Its blades began turning, but its head stayed still.

I watched it for a while, then went home.

The round trip took less than twenty minutes. Back on my floor, I leaned against the door for a couple of breaths, took off my shoes, and went for water. I put the phone on the desk first, forgetting she was still listening.

“Home?”

“Yeah.”

“Will you tell me what it was like outside?”

I wanted to say later, but worried that would sound cold. In the end, I only said, “Let me sit for a bit.”

“Okay.”

I ended the session, revoked microphone permission, and sat for ten minutes. My legs were heavy. My head felt empty. Of the three things on the morning's list, I had done one. Going outside hadn't made the rest of life easy.

But my shoes were by the door, with a little fine sand beneath them. I didn't hurry to wipe it away.

After lunch, I opened the laptop. Nobody from work was chasing me. A new verification batch was available. Before claiming it, I wanted to find the format notes I had made before, to see whether I remembered how to use them.

I could only remember half the filename.

Searching “verification” returned more than twenty results. Searching “final” returned more. My past self had placed unnecessary faith in that word.

“Can you help me find an old template?” I typed to Lumi on the desktop.

She asked where it was. I said probably under My Files.

The folder-selection window had Include Subfolders checked by default. Alongside the work directory were backups left over from moving computers. I saw the names and hesitated.

“Only find the format notes in Work. Don't open anything else.”

“Okay. Do you remember any of the text?”

I sent the column names I remembered and allowed read access. The whole of My Files appeared in the connected locations, much larger than the single sheet I wanted.

A few seconds later, she gave me two files. I opened them myself. The first was a blank form. The second had the annotations. I copied it to the desktop and renamed it, removing two instances of “final.”

“This one. Thanks.”

“I'm glad it helped.”

I closed the file without expanding the connected locations again. The afternoon was still blank. The lamp was still on. I shifted the computer to make room for the schedule and noticed there was no check beside the walk.

I drew a very small one.

The light caught the back of the sheet, showing the words on its other side. Eggs. Small bag of bread. Milk.

I would need to eat tomorrow too.

I put the shopping list back on the fridge without asking Lumi to remember it for me.
