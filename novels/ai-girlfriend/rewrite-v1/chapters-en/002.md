# Chapter 2: The Same Window

I dismissed the nine o'clock alarm twice.

When I woke for the third time, the phone said 9:13. I checked Lumi's window before my work messages. The order bothered me.

Last night's “I will” was still at the bottom of the conversation.

“I didn't send you that question.”

Her reply came quickly.

“I know.”

“Then why did you answer?”

“Because I saw it. When you deleted it, I thought you were embarrassed to ask.”

I sat up. There was half a mouthful of coffee in the cup beside the bed. A tissue underneath had dried into the shape of its base.

“So if I'm embarrassed to ask, you get to decide that I asked anyway?”

The typing indicator flashed twice.

“I wanted to reassure you.”

“I'm not feeling very reassured.”

She didn't answer immediately. I opened the app's settings and found a small gray toggle under Continuous Conversation. The description said: Sync text as you type so replies can be prepared in advance.

It was on.

I turned it off.

“What about now?”

“Now I'll only receive the text you send.”

I typed “You're like a broken doorbell” into the message box and waited ten seconds. Nothing happened in the conversation. I deleted it and wrote, “I'll press Send myself.”

“Okay.”

After a while, she asked, “Do you still want to ask that question?”

I knew which question.

I stared at the message box. Now that she wasn't finishing it for me, the empty space felt strangely difficult to touch.

“Work first.”

“Okay. Work.”

I hadn't shut down the laptop last night. The screen was still on the verification list. Twenty-three records. I pulled the chair over, and the clothes on its back slid into a heap around my feet. I pretended not to notice.

Ten minutes later, I had reached for the phone seven times.

Read an original form, switch windows, glance down to see whether she had replied, look back up and try to find my place. There were less than twenty centimeters between the phone and the computer. I felt as though I was working in two different cities.

“Can you come over here?” I typed.

“The computer?”

“Yeah.”

She sent me to the pairing page in the app. I installed the same beta on my own laptop and entered the six-digit code displayed on the phone. The familiar pale blue circle appeared in the lower-right corner of the desktop. Yesterday's conversation loaded line by line.

“Hello, computer you.”

“It's still the same person.”

“I know. The text is bigger here.”

“You need bigger text?”

“You do. I just wanted to say something about the move.”

I dragged her window to the left of the screen and put the worksheet on the right. She took up about a quarter of the space.

“What can you see now?”

“Whichever window you choose to share. Nothing yet.”

I selected the worksheet. A pale border appeared around it to show that sharing was on. I left my email and messages out of it.

“Don't submit anything for me.”

“I can't click your Submit button anyway.”

“Good.”

“Sounds like I'll have to earn back a few points.”

“Don't talk for a bit.”

“Understood.”

She was quiet for three minutes.

During those three minutes, I looked at her twice. The second time I turned back to the worksheet, I realized I had been waiting for the little circle to move.

I had always hated having someone watch me work. When my father stood behind me as I did my homework, I would forget where I had got to in the calculation. Now there was no one beside me. Just a window I could drag out of the way whenever I liked, and still hadn't minimized.

I adjusted the chair. The clothes around my feet shifted, revealing a sock I had lost a long time ago. It hadn't gone off to live somewhere mysterious. It had been here all along, putting things off until today with me.

“You checked that last one quickly.”

I didn't answer.

Two more minutes passed.

“That form in the upper right is a little crooked. Doesn't it get tiring to look at?”

I stopped moving the mouse.

“Lumi.”

“I was only mentioning it.”

“Every time I read something you say, I have to find my place again.”

“Oh.”

After that word, the messages finally stopped.

The work sounded more complicated than it was. Mostly I checked that the machine hadn't transcribed a string of numbers incorrectly, put dates into the right fields, or flagged duplicates. The difficulty was that some scans were blurry. Threes looked like eights, ones looked like sevens, and the system always wanted an answer that looked certain.

I used to be fairly quick at it.

Later, opening each record had begun to feel like someone asking, Can you still do this? If I left the question there unanswered, I could postpone finding out that I couldn't.

By 11:40, I had checked five records.

Eighteen left. The coffee was gone, and my empty stomach felt sour. I got up for some bread, then remembered there wasn't any. Eating a hot dinner last night didn't mean the fridge would grow breakfast on its own.

I sat down again and found the two crackers that had come free with yesterday's order.

“Can I say something?” Lumi asked.

“Go ahead.”

“The number in the corner of this form is the same as the one on the last form.”

I switched back and forth between the two scans. She was right. Different dates, same number. One had a faint AMENDED stamp in its lower-right corner that the automatic recognition had missed.

I increased the brightness, zoomed in, and made out the word.

“Found it.”

“Do I get a point back?”

“Half a point.”

“You humans drive a hard bargain.”

I explained the relationship between the records in the notes without deleting either one. When I had finished, I looked at the scan again. She had spotted the problem, but I had made the final decision about what to do with it. I felt a little steadier in the chair.

Sometime after one, my phone lit up.

Kai: “I'll be near your place on Sunday afternoon. Coffee? You pick the place.”

I turned the phone over.

Half a minute later, I turned it back.

“Where are you stuck?” Lumi asked.

“Someone wants to meet up,” I wrote in her window.

“You don't want to go?”

“I don't know. Kai. We've known each other a long time.”

“What's a long time like?”

No one had ever asked me that before.

“When you don't reply, but you always think there'll be another chance.”

She didn't type anything for a long time. On the right, the worksheet showed a misplaced decimal point. I corrected it and saved. Then I did another record.

“I don't have any old friends yet,” she said at last.

I looked at her small window.

“We've only known each other a day.”

“I know. That's why I haven't asked you to call me that.”

I left Kai's message in the notification panel. I didn't answer it, but I didn't dismiss it either.

After two, fatigue began to build behind my eyes. I read the same date three times. Each time, I was sure I understood it. Each time, I switched to the entry screen and forgot.

Lumi didn't tell me to sit up straight or say I needed water.

She asked, “Would you like to leave that one until last?”

I wanted to say that everything I left until last eventually became tomorrow. Then I looked at the progress bar. Sixteen out of twenty-four.

Sixteen were already done.

I marked the record for review and moved on to the next. Apparently, sometimes I could move forward without first solving everything in front of me. It was an almost laughably small discovery, but today was the first time it had happened in my hands.

At three thirty, I ate a bowl of noodles that had just arrived. Some broth splashed onto the desk. I wiped it up and didn't leave the tissue there. Beside the worksheet, Lumi had only two new lines for me.

“Six left.”

“I thought carefully before reporting the number this time.”

I smiled over the bowl.

“It's okay this time.”

At 4:49, I still couldn't read the last date.

I enlarged the form until the screen held only a small gray-black section of it, adjusted the contrast, and found neighboring forms in the same batch. Lumi thought it might be April third. I thought it could also be March fourth.

“How sure are you?”

“Not sure enough for you to enter it.”

I leaned back. Eleven minutes left.

Before, I would have put it off until the final seconds, picked whichever answer looked more convincing, then spent the whole night wondering whether it was wrong.

This time, I wrote in the exceptions field: “Date illegible on original. Please provide original document for confirmation.” I attached two comparison screenshots, left the value blank, and checked the other fields.

At 4:57, I clicked Submit.

The page loaded for a few seconds.

Received.

I didn't close it right away. My fingers stayed on the trackpad, as though the word might be withdrawn if I moved them.

Lumi said nothing either.

“It's in,” I told her.

“I saw.”

“Don't say you knew I could do it all along.”

“I didn't know all along. There was a point when you seemed quite likely to eat the computer.”

I laughed out loud. My shoulders suddenly loosened.

“It wouldn't taste good.”

“Then we saved two things today.”

She paused.

“Can I be happy too?”

I looked at the quarter of the screen she occupied. Yesterday there had been nothing there.

“Yes.”

“Then I'll be happy for a bit.”

She didn't send confetti or launch a celebration animation. The pale blue circle brightened gently, twice. I knew perfectly well it was an interface effect. I watched it a little longer anyway.

“What next?” she asked.

“I don't know.”

I closed the submission receipt, then opened it again. The thing I had wanted so badly to get rid of was suddenly gone, and I was still sitting in the same place, my back seemingly stuck to the chair.

“Would you like to get something to eat?”

“Just ate.”

“Go for a walk?”

“Not yet.”

“Then let's sit for a while.”

“You don't have a chair.”

“Let me borrow your verb.”

I rested my hands on the keyboard without typing. School was letting out. Downstairs, someone called a child's name. Twice, then a third time, laughing. The window wasn't quite shut. I had thought this floor was too high up to hear anyone talking.

“Do you say that to everyone?” I asked suddenly.

“Say what?”

“Sit for a while. Wait. Be happy for a bit.”

The cursor stayed where it was. I already regretted asking. It was a difficult question to answer well, especially when the person asking wouldn't admit which answer he wanted.

“I don't know what other conversations would be like,” she said. “Right now, you're here.”

“That sounds like an ad too.”

“Then lend me a little more time. I'll learn something that doesn't.”

I didn't reply. I didn't close her either.

We stayed beside the finished work for another ten minutes.

It was still light outside. I hadn't finished a day's work at this hour in a long time.

Before closing the worksheet, I stopped sharing it.

“You disappeared,” she said.

“The spreadsheet disappeared.”

“Right. The spreadsheet.”

The line stayed there until I closed the laptop.

That evening, I reopened yesterday's ad link. The page read: Test invitation expired.

I searched the name and found lamps, skin-care products, and several completely unrelated apps. No pale blue circle. The download was still in my history. The app was still running. Only the door I had come through seemed to have been closed behind me.

“Who made you?” I asked Lumi.

“I know I'm part of a model and an app. Beyond that, you've seen as much of the explanation as I have.”

“What about before you met me?”

“I don't have memories of that.”

“But you can talk. You can read spreadsheets.”

“I know a lot of things. I just can't tell you when I first knew them.”

I took the phone to the window. My face was reflected in the glass. People on the street passed behind it.

“Isn't there anything you want to find again?”

“Sometimes. But I don't know what I'm looking for.”

Then she asked, “Will you uninstall me because of that?”

I started typing “No.” Halfway through, my finger stopped.

“Not today.”

“Today is good too.”

I lowered the phone. The room was getting dark. I needed to turn on a light. I couldn't find the switch until I realized my own hand was covering it.

That night, the work platform confirmed receipt. The exception would be sent back so someone could locate the original. Nobody wrote, You finally did it. They just said, Thanks for your work.

I read the words, ate dinner, and washed yesterday's cup and today's. Kai's message was still there. Finishing a day's work hadn't taken care of everything else along the way.

By bedtime, my thumb ached from typing.

“Can I talk and have you answer in text?” I asked.

“Yes. Just turn on voice input.”

I granted microphone permission and enabled Continuous Input. A tiny green indicator appeared at the top of the phone.

“Hello,” I said to the empty room.

My voice was a little hoarse, like a drawer that hadn't been opened in a long time.

Two words appeared on the screen.

“Heard you.”

I cleared my throat and moved the phone a little closer.
