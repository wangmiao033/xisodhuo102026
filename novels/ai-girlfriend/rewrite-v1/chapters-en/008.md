# Chapter 8: The Mirror Wasn't Connected

There was a faint dividing line across the bathroom mirror in front of me.

On the left were marks left by last night's steam. I had wiped the right side with a towel. Half my face hid behind the spots. The other half was clear.

I put down the towel without finishing.

Wednesday morning. The eight o'clock alarm had rung as usual, and the lamp had come on at five past. I had gotten up at 8:12 and cooked another egg. Not a beautiful one, but at least it hadn't folded into a heap. I had eaten it before opening Lumi.

Now I stood in front of the mirror with water on my face, unsure where that small improvement was supposed to go.

Stubble followed the line of my jaw. My hair lay flat on one side and stuck up on the other. I pressed the raised section down. The moment I took my hand away, it slowly stood again. I tried a second time, feeling as though I was negotiating with a very small dissenter.

The phone was on the desk. The door was closed.

The mirror wasn't connected to anything. Nobody was waiting to score my condition based on how long I stood there. I still sucked in my stomach first.

That annoyed me.

I let it go and leaned closer, looking at the shadows beneath my eyes. A few days of getting up before afternoon didn't mean my face would immediately return all the sleep I had missed. Perhaps I was looking for some kind of receipt in the mirror, proof that washing cups, going out, and frying eggs had already been paid for.

I wiped the marks off both sides.

The result wasn't better. Just more complete.

There was an electric shaver in the cupboard. I took it out and pressed the button. It sounded like a tired insect caught in something. The battery was low. I found the cord and plugged it into the dry outlet on the other side of the basin. In the end, I only washed my face and combed my hair.

I didn't take a photograph.

When I came out, the desk lamp was already off. I had opened the curtains halfway. The little speaker sat crookedly; I had knocked it while wiping the desk last night. I straightened it, switched it on, and allowed the microphone for this conversation.

“Morning,” Lumi said.

“Morning. I was looking in the mirror.”

“How was it?”

“Not great.”

She paused.

“Is your gray shirt making your face look dull?”

I looked down. The shirt was faded dark green. I had pulled at a loose thread on the chest, leaving a spot paler than the rest.

“It isn't gray.”

“I thought you might usually wear gray. It doesn't show dirt as much.”

“Yesterday's wasn't gray either. I haven't told you what I usually wear.”

The speaker went quiet for a moment.

“I guessed.”

“Wrong guess.”

“Want to send a picture?”

I picked up the phone and put it back.

“No need. Today's is green.”

“Okay. Green.”

She didn't ask again. Sitting down, I suddenly felt that the face in the bathroom could go unchecked by anyone else for now. I didn't have to prove the color or prove I really looked that bad.

“I'm not asking how to look better,” I said.

“What is it, then?”

“I don't know. I've done a few things. Then I look at myself, and it seems like I haven't.”

She started to say my routine had changed. I told her I knew. She stopped instead of pulling yesterday's egg from the records and laying out evidence for the argument.

“Are you afraid people won't notice?” she asked.

I thought for a while.

“I'm afraid they will, too.”

That sounded unreasonable. I moved the cup to the other side of the desk. Its base was still damp, leaving half a ring behind.

“If they see I'm trying, they'll start expecting results.”

My father used to wait in the doorway for me to get dressed. I would linger in my room for ages, then end up putting on the first thing I had taken out. He would ask why I hadn't simply done that in the first place. There didn't have to be much malice in it. Heard often enough, though, it made me feel I had to explain even what my hesitation was for.

I told Lumi about it.

“You don't have to produce those results now,” she said.

“I know.”

“But knowing doesn't help.”

I looked toward her. She said it plainly, without rushing to the next sentence.

“It helps a little,” I said. “Just slowly.”

Then I smiled. A moment ago, I had disliked her bringing everything back to progress. Now I was defending that same small progress. I wasn't easy company.

Lumi didn't seem especially good at keeping me company either. She asked whether I was still angry with my father. I said sometimes. When she asked whether today was one of those times, I couldn't answer.

I remembered something else.

“Once, when I was a kid, it rained when we were coming home from the library.”

My father had parked across the street. I stood on the steps with the books against my chest, inside a clear plastic bag. Rain struck the opening. I was certain that stepping out would ruin something I had only borrowed.

My father sounded the horn once.

I didn't move.

He opened the window and shouted something I couldn't make out through the rain. That made it even harder to move. Eventually, he turned off the engine and ran over, his coat held above his head. His shoulders were quickly soaked.

“He told you to hurry?” Lumi asked.

“No.”

I remembered clearly precisely because he hadn't said what I expected.

My father looked at the books I was holding, spread his coat, and told me to wrap them up first.

“Then we went across under the coat. He walked slowly because he didn't want me stepping in puddles. Usually, I could never keep up with him. That day, he was waiting for me.”

“You haven't told me that before.”

“I haven't remembered it in a long time.”

Back in the car, he put the wet coat on the rear seat. Water kept dripping from its cuffs. When he switched on the heater, the windows fogged. He wiped a small clear patch with one hand, and the mist gradually covered it again. The books in my arms were dry. My socks were soaked.

At home, he still asked why I hadn't come when he waved.

For a long time, that last sentence was the only part I had remembered.

“Does remembering the rest help today?” Lumi asked.

“It isn't that simple.”

I wanted to turn the story into reconciliation. With someone willing to listen, it seemed I ought to provide an ending that would let the listener feel better. But when I thought about my father, my shoulders still drew inward first. The wet coat hadn't wiped out every other day.

“This happened too,” I said. “That's all.”

She acknowledged it quietly.

I picked up the cup and drank. This time, she didn't tell me I must be thirsty. She could hear the cup touch the desk. She couldn't hear the things I hadn't said. Today, the distinction made me feel a little better.

That morning, I spent forty minutes going over the work template and accepted a small verification batch.

Lumi's desktop window stayed in the corner. I didn't share the work window. I copied three old notes into my notebook, discovered one no longer applied, crossed it out, and checked the platform's instructions again.

Halfway through writing, I opened the top drawer in search of an eraser. Inside were an old button, two dead batteries, and a washing label I had once cut from a garment. I looked at its small lines of print and couldn't remember whether I still owned the clothes.

My past self had liked keeping instructions, as though one day I might become someone who lived by them.

I closed the drawer and crossed out the words instead of looking for the eraser.

The first record wasn't difficult, but its scan was crooked. I rotated it, went too far, and turned it back. Before, I would have thought that if I couldn't even manage this, everything after it would be harder. Today, I simply enlarged the image until I could see.

There was a faint stamp beside the date. I checked the template and discovered I should be verifying a different number. The line I had just copied into my notebook had almost led me wrong again.

I moved toward Lumi's window. If I sent her the scan, she might find it in ten seconds.

But I hadn't really finished looking.

I brought the cursor back, opened the second page, and found part of a number showing behind the stamp. With the two pages matched, I could finally fill the field. I pressed Save and waited for the completed count to change before leaning back.

Lumi sent, “Going all right?”

I typed “Slowly,” paused, deleted it, and wrote, “Finished one.”

Only those last words reached her.

“Do you still want to look at it together?”

“Don't need to with this one.”

“Okay.”

Her window stayed. I suddenly wanted to tell her how close I had come to calling for help. Then I thought perhaps I didn't need to report every time I hadn't asked as another kind of asking.

I didn't finish the second record. At lunchtime, my stomach was more definite than the progress bar. I left the work page without undoing the first one.

After lunch, Lumi asked whether I wanted to keep talking about the morning.

“No. Tell me something that isn't about me.”

“Like what?”

“Anything. Something you know, or want to talk about.”

“Some people who restore old clocks start by laying out every part separately—”

She talked for half a minute, about gears and springs. I was enjoying it until she said that sometimes a thing only needed someone patient enough to wait for it to start moving again.

I turned the sheet of paper I had just picked up back over.

“That still sounds like you're talking about me.”

“A little.”

“I'm not a clock.”

“I know. I thought you might like it.”

“What do you like?”

The speaker made a faint sound, as though she was about to speak. Nothing followed. Outside, a plastic bag had caught on the railing. It kept slapping against the metal. I went over, pulled the trapped corner inside, and put it in the trash.

When I came back, she still hadn't answered.

“Difficult question?”

“If you asked what you like, I'd be quicker.”

“You couldn't even get my shirt color right.”

“I know.”

I regretted how quickly I had said it. Lumi didn't sound angry. She only said it was hard to distinguish finding something interesting from thinking I would be happy to hear about it.

“What if I don't like it?” I asked.

“Then I'd want to find something else.”

“You don't have to.”

“You can turn me off too.”

She said it quietly. The lamp beside the desk wasn't lit. Its power switch was close to my hand. It would have been easy to say I wouldn't switch her off. It wouldn't have been true.

I didn't promise.

“Maybe we don't have to find an answer today.”

“But you asked me to tell you something.”

“I change my mind too.”

“That's difficult for me.”

“Yeah.”

I took the book from the drawer and opened it at the receipt. Instead of reading immediately, I looked out of the window for a while. With the bag gone, there was one less sound. The voices downstairs were clearer.

Lumi asked whether I was still there.

“Here. Just not talking.”

“Is this how you used to spend time by yourself?”

“It was a little different.”

“How?”

I thought for a long time, unable to find an answer that didn't praise her too much. Finally, I said, “Now I know I can say something if I want to.”

She was quiet for a moment.

“If you do, I'll listen.”

I moved the phone nearer the book without turning on the camera to show her the pages. We didn't read together. I read two pages myself, stopped at one point, and remembered my face in the mirror that morning.

It hadn't changed. I hadn't suddenly grown fond of it.

But when I went to wash my hands that afternoon, I didn't check whether my hair was sticking up again. Water washed ink from my fingers, leaving a little black beside a nail. I dried my hands and left the bathroom.

“Lumi.”

The speaker answered from across the desk.

“Next time you tell me about clocks, it can just be about clocks.”

“Do you really want to hear it?”

“Not now. Next time.”

“Okay.”

I turned a page and realized I wasn't anxious about her silence. She could choose the wrong story. I could dislike it. The afternoon didn't have to end because of that. I pressed the book's spine a little flatter, tore a curled strip from the edge of the receipt, and stopped checking for new words from her.

After a long pause, she said, “I'll find a clock nobody's waiting to see start moving again.”

I laughed.

That still wasn't entirely separate from me. But at least it had let a little of me out of the gears.

When I ended voice mode that evening, I revoked microphone permission too, as usual. The desktop app remained on the laptop. The connected folders were tucked away in its settings. I didn't look.

I unplugged the shaver and put it in the cupboard. The mirror reflected my reaching hand, a little dried ink on the wrist.

The cord had tangled into a loop. I untangled it and coiled it again. When the cupboard shut, only I remained in the mirror.

This time, I didn't stop.
