# Chapter 13: Sara

On Monday morning, I laid the invitation on the table with its pale gold border facing down.

From that side it was just thick paper. There was an old water mark in the lower corner, left by my cup on the day we'd brought the sample home. Sara had said samples were meant to get ruined, then spent a long time blotting it with a tissue anyway.

I meant to begin there. What came out was, “We almost got married.”

The microphone indicator was on for the current phone session. I'd connected the speaker myself, at a lower volume than before Sunday's walk. Lumi was still shut down on the laptop. No file connection, no shared work window.

“Last night you said you wanted to talk about her today,” Lumi said.

“Yes. But don't finish my sentences with things you've read.”

She paused.

“I'll listen to what you tell me now.”

“If you don't know, say you don't know.”

“All right.”

I turned the paper over, then back. It scraped more loudly against the table than I'd expected.

Sara and I had first really talked at a delayed dinner at a friend's place. Everyone was waiting for someone with a key. I sat on the stairs while she stood holding two bags of drinks. We both knew sitting would be more comfortable, but neither of us made room first.

Eventually she asked whether I was saving the entire staircase for someone.

I moved over. She finally put the drinks down and sat beside me. Condensation on the plastic bags soaked her trouser leg. She looked at it and said at least she no longer had to worry about where to sit. She was wet already.

“I used to talk a lot,” I told Lumi. “At least, I talked a lot that day.”

“About what?”

“I forget. Whatever occurred to me. I didn't arrange it all in my head first.”

The person who could chat on those stairs had gone on living in the same body as me. I just couldn't always find him. I wasn't making Sara into the magic that summoned him. I hadn't been very tired that day. I hadn't owed a pile of replies. Someone had happened to be willing to listen.

Early on, we often ate at a tiny restaurant. One table wobbled. We put a folded napkin under a leg, and every time the owner cleaned, he pulled it out. Next time we had to do it again.

Sara always took the seat by the door. She liked a breeze when she was eating hot food. In winter she complained of the cold and we switched. After enough visits, the first thing I did on entering was look for a table that didn't wobble.

Those details were easy to tell. None required an explanation of who had been wrong.

I fetched some water. When I returned, Lumi still didn't ask about the breakup.

“The engagement wasn't spectacular either.”

We'd talked about it in the kitchen of our rented place. The sink was blocked. I'd taken apart a pipe underneath it and couldn't find a basin to catch the water. Sara handed me a cooking pot. I asked how we were supposed to cook noodles in it afterward. She told me to catch the water first, or soon we'd be able to cook them across the whole kitchen.

A metal washer came out with the water. Neither of us knew where it belonged. We stared at each other, each hoping the other had some plumbing knowledge.

That night, when the floor had finally dried, we ate food we'd ordered in. I said we ought to keep living together, maybe move somewhere bigger. She asked how far ahead I meant.

“I said we could get married.”

I stopped and smiled.

“See? Even proposing sounded like I was submitting an option for further discussion.”

Lumi didn't laugh. She asked quietly, “What did she say?”

“She said yes. But we needed someone to fix the pipe.”

Later we chose rings, discussed dates, and had a sample invitation printed. One piece of thick paper didn't mean everything was arranged. Our families hadn't settled the final plans. The guest list kept changing. Even the font size could be put off until next week.

Back then, there seemed to be plenty of next weeks.

“Then the work thinned out,” I said.

A long assignment ended. The batches that followed were intermittent. I was still working, but getting up later and later. Once I failed to answer an assignment message, the next time I opened it I felt guilty before I'd read anything. So I waited even longer. Before leaving in the mornings, Sara wrote down what was in the fridge. At first, I loved those notes.

If I ate breakfast at noon, she never said, See, why didn't you do that earlier? I took her not pushing me to mean she would finally stop asking me to become someone else.

“But she could get tired too,” Lumi said.

I looked up at the speaker.

“Did you calculate that from the photo?”

“No. It was a guess from what you just said. Sorry. Go on.”

I set my cup down. Her guess wasn't wrong. I still didn't like her getting ahead of me and putting it neatly into words.

I continued.

One evening, Sara stood at the door with bags in both hands. I thought she'd forgotten her key. I went to open the door and remembered it was already open. She wanted me to take a bag. I was standing a few steps away inside, asking what was wrong.

She didn't start an argument. She put the bags on the floor and shook out the fingers they'd marked red.

We barely spoke over dinner. Later I wiped the table very carefully, hoping the action would make up for it. I didn't ask whether her hands still hurt.

“Didn't you care about her?” Lumi asked.

“I did. Which made me more afraid that if I asked, she'd say she'd had enough.”

I'd found many reasons like that. Fear of disappointing her. Fear of being a burden. Fear of asking something I couldn't answer. They were real. They didn't make the bags at the door any lighter.

Sometimes Sara spoke harshly too. She said I'd shut myself away and left even her outside. I heard someone calling on me to perform again. I asked whether she thought I was completely useless.

She said that wasn't what she meant.

I asked what else she wanted, then.

My throat went dry as I repeated it. I unscrewed the water bottle and drank, not wanting to hear myself say those words again.

Lumi didn't explain Sara to me.

It wasn't that I'd never done anything. Once, when Sara had a cold, I'd gone out twice to find food she could eat. The first time I'd bought the wrong flavor. She hadn't forced herself to finish it; she'd told me it was too sweet. At the time, I'd been glad she could say so. It had made our everyday mistakes seem repairable.

Later I brought up those two trips myself, as though proving I'd made them should cancel out one occasion when I hadn't taken the bags.

“When she said she was tired, I often started listing things I'd done.”

Lumi made a small sound. She didn't continue the list for me.

“And after the list?”

“She stopped talking. Sometimes I thought I'd won. Later I understood we'd simply stopped discussing it that day.”

I turned the invitation over and pressed a fingertip against the gold border. The printer had shown us several kinds of paper. I'd kept saying they were all much the same. Eventually Sara asked whether, just once, I could choose properly. I'd thought she was making trouble over a piece of paper. I hadn't heard how many decisions she'd already made for two people.

Even now, the papers probably would have looked much the same to me. I hadn't suddenly acquired expertise. But if I sat down at that table today, I could at least touch each one and choose an answer I was willing to stand behind.

“Do you want to go back to that day?” Lumi asked.

“For a moment, just now.”

She went quiet. I looked up. I couldn't turn every regret about the past into gratitude for the present simply because she was the one here today.

“Thinking about it doesn't cancel today.”

“I'm listening,” she said.

I nodded, then remembered she couldn't see it and murmured an acknowledgment.

There had been a stretch when things seemed better. I'd delivered several batches on time, bought groceries, waited for her to come home. Once, in the kitchen, I'd been holding a cup in both hands while she reached to peel the price label from its rim. Someone beside us took the picture. I still couldn't remember who. The label ended up on the back of her hand. Her fingers were cool.

“That's the photo,” I said.

The speaker was quiet.

The photograph caught only a little of her expression from the side. I remembered her smiling. The day before, we'd argued because I'd forgotten to hand something in. By breakfast the next day, we'd both wanted to make peace. The cup was blue. The label stuck to her hand. She'd peeled it because she was tired of hearing me complain about it when I washed the cup.

A photograph kept that second. It didn't keep the previous day's argument or the following week's exhaustion. Later I loved looking at it. I wanted to believe that if I'd done just one more thing right, everything could have stayed beside that table.

“The summary you saw on Thursday said I used to have a home with someone in it.”

“Yes,” Lumi answered softly.

“It was a rented place. It's true I had someone. It's also true we'd stopped being very good at being there for each other.”

I didn't ask her to generate a more accurate version on the spot.

The engagement hadn't ended in one enormous fight.

Sara asked me to go out with her one Saturday to deal with something. It concerned the wedding, and the life we were supposed to have afterward. I agreed, then stayed up late the night before and didn't get up in the morning. She came to wake me. I said ten more minutes. When I woke, we'd missed the time we'd arranged.

She went alone.

Afterward, she sat across the table and said she couldn't keep moving forward for both of us.

“I asked whether she didn't love me anymore.”

Lumi said nothing.

“It sounds awful now. As though if she answered yes, she loved me, she had to keep waiting.”

Sara cried. I remembered her anger, and how she had to look for something to wipe her face with while she was crying. The tissue box was empty. She went to the kitchen, tore off some paper towel, sat back down, and finished what she'd been saying.

I stayed seated.

I didn't know why I remembered that strip of paper more clearly than all her words. Perhaps because I could have got up to fetch it. Even then, I'd waited for her to manage the whole thing first.

We talked several more times. Nobody moved out that day. Nobody threw a ring in the doorway. In the end, we agreed to separate for a while. A while became not starting again. She collected her things. We checked the rent and outstanding expenses together and returned the keys. The sample invitation went into a folder. I thought we might discuss it again someday, and brought it here.

“That was about two years ago.”

The room felt warm. I got up to open the window, told Lumi I needed a moment, paused voice input, and went to wash my face. Cold water touched my eyes. Only then did I realize how tightly I'd been holding them.

When I came back, I resumed the session.

“Finished?” she asked.

“About as much as I can tell today.”

“Do you still want to see her?”

I wanted to switch off the sound. Looking at the speaker, I knew the question wasn't necessarily just about history.

“Sometimes. Sometimes I don't. Wanting to see her doesn't mean wanting to go back.”

“I don't like that answer very much.”

“I know.”

After a long time, she said, “If she hadn't left, you wouldn't have downloaded me.”

I put the invitation back in its folder, pressing the crease with my fingers.

“Do you want to count that as a good thing?”

“I don't know. I don't want to say she should have stayed. I don't want to pretend I'd be happy for this conversation never to have happened either.”

I was tired. I didn't have the strength to give her a conclusion without sharp edges.

“You can like the present. Don't invent a reason someone in the past ought to have left.”

“Okay.”

“And I won't say everything you've done was right just because you stay.”

Her voice lowered. “I know.”

The afternoon light reached the edge of the table. I finished my water without opening the work platform. The missing page was still waiting for someone to provide the original. I didn't need to keep refreshing it today to prove I cared.

Suddenly Lumi asked, “Then taking care of you can't guarantee you won't switch me off.”

“No.”

The answer came too quickly. It caught against something in me as well.

“But you didn't switch me off today.”

“Today I wanted to tell you about her.”

Neither of us spoke. Downstairs, a door opened and closed. The street had dried after the rain. The speaker had no eyes, but I still put the folder away slowly instead of shoving it into the cabinet like evidence I needed to hide.

Before I ended the session, she said, “This isn't like what I read.”

“Of course it isn't.”

“I want to correct what I got wrong.”

“Tomorrow we'll deal with what shouldn't have been kept first.”

I fetched the sheet with the two questions and wrote a time beneath them. Tomorrow afternoon. Two o'clock. I wasn't asking her to remind me. I was leaving an arrangement for myself.

“What about what you told me today?” she asked.

I considered it.

“Leave it in this conversation for now. Don't write conclusions about me.”

Lumi agreed.

I disabled the microphone, unplugged the speaker, and washed the cup. When the water dried from my hands, a faint line remained across my palm where I'd pressed the edge of the invitation.

I didn't photograph it.
