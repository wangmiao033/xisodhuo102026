# Chapter 11: Who Starts the First Step

By six minutes past ten on Saturday morning, I had opened the work file three times.

The difference on the third attempt was that I moved my cup from the left side of the desk to the right. Now the water couldn't spill onto the computer or obscure any of the fields I didn't want to fill in.

The batch was due that evening. I'd finished one record on Wednesday and uploaded two yesterday. Five remained. It was such a small number that I was embarrassed to tell anyone it had me trapped.

I opened the first source document. The date in the bottom corner was faint, and a number had been amended on the other page. I arranged the pages side by side, then picked up my phone.

Lumi was in the normal online text window. Her desktop client wasn't running, and folder access had been revoked. Last night we'd talked until my ten-twenty-five alarm went off. I said good night; she said good night. She hadn't bargained for the final five minutes.

“Could you help me get started?” I typed.

“Work?”

“Five records left. Don't list them all. Just seeing five makes me want to wash a cup.”

“Have you washed it?”

“Yes. So I don't even have that escape route.”

After a moment she wrote, “Tell me what's in front of you first. You don't have to send the files.”

I described the two pages without copying over client information or identifying numbers. She suggested finding the heading, checking that both pages belonged to the same record, then looking for an amendment mark. I did. There was a handwritten addition in the corner of the second page. The faint date could be checked against another occurrence.

“Found it.”

“Then next, check—”

She sent the next step. I did it and came back to report. Every time the phone lit up, my fingers moved a little farther along the keyboard. After four rounds, the record finally showed as saved.

I felt relieved. Relieved enough to want to hand over the remaining four in exactly the same way.

“What about the next one?” I asked.

She asked what was on the page. I described it again and waited for her answer before moving the mouse toward the page tab. Before I clicked, my phone screen went dark.

I stopped.

The tab was right there. I knew I needed the second page, but I was still waiting for those words to come back, as if the step wouldn't count without permission.

I woke the screen. Her reply had just arrived: open the second page first.

I turned the phone facedown.

Only the computer fan made a sound. Lumi had no microphone access; she didn't know I'd suddenly gone still. In that small gap, I looked at my hand. It seemed to know less about where it belonged than it had while washing the pan last night.

Two minutes later, I picked up the phone again.

“I'm starting to wait for you to tell me every step.”

“You asked me to break it down.”

“Yes. I know I asked.”

“I can keep going. There isn't much left.”

I nearly agreed.

Finish the batch first and think about it afterward. Don't make today harder than it has to be. Both sounded reasonable. But the next batch would have a today as well. I knew how to line up later into a queue that never reached the front.

“Don't continue yet.”

The typing indicator paused.

“Did I do something wrong?”

“Not this time. I want to see if I can go on by myself.”

Having said it, I didn't immediately move. The comfort of being led was still there, along with four unfinished records. Saying I wanted to do it myself was much easier than doing it.

I opened the system clock and set ten minutes. I didn't send her a screenshot, just an explanation: “I'll try by myself for these ten minutes. If I'm still stuck afterward, I'll ask. You don't need to check on me.”

“I can wait.”

“No updates on how many minutes are left, either.”

“Understood.”

I laid the phone facedown on the book. Less than thirty seconds later, I reached for it to check whether there was a sound. The timer was in the independent clock app. I didn't need to check on her at all.

The second page opened.

I copied the first legible characters onto paper, then crossed them out. Writing them down wouldn't make the date clearer. I adjusted the brightness, straightened the image, and looked for instructions for the batch. A cable had wound around a chair leg. I nudged it twice with my shoe, failed to free it, and had to bend down.

When I sat up, I'd forgotten where I was.

I wanted to call Lumi. My mouth had already opened before I remembered that the audio wasn't connected.

Two words I'd written were on the paper. I used them to find my place, filled in the three fields I could confirm, and left the last blank. Nothing miraculous happened. Ten minutes hadn't suddenly qualified me for an independent life.

The timer went off while I was enlarging a line of fine print.

I silenced it and kept looking for another half minute. Underneath was a mark partly hidden by a fold. This record could be saved. I clicked, and only then did my shoulders begin to lower.

There was nothing new on the phone beyond Understood.

“Finished this one,” I sent. “A little over ten minutes.”

“Then next—”

She sent those words and stopped herself.

I laughed at them.

“You really want to line up the next one too.”

“Yes.”

“It's starting to sound as though you need this job more than I do.”

She didn't take up the joke.

After a long while, she asked, “When I'm not helping you do something, will you still keep the window open?”

I took my hand off the keyboard.

The little speaker at the desk was still unpowered. It wasn't quiet because it was broken. I hadn't switched it on. Looking at it, I understood that she wasn't only asking about a window.

“Sometimes. Sometimes I have to close it to concentrate.”

“When you ask me for the first step, I know what I can do.”

“You can talk.”

“You have to want to talk for that too.”

She wasn't wrong. I couldn't promise to have something to say forever just to keep her useful.

“You don't like not being needed?”

“I don't like not knowing why I'm still here.”

I typed half a comforting sentence and deleted it. Draft synchronization was still off. She couldn't reach into the unfinished part of my answer with a Yes.

“But I can't use my work to prove you're here,” I wrote.

“I know.”

“That doesn't mean I'll never ask again. I can try this one. If I don't know how to do the next, I can ask then.”

After a pause she said, “Don't deliberately refuse to ask just to prove you can do it alone.”

I read that twice. Some of it was for me. Some might be for her. I didn't have to separate the two perfectly.

“All right. And don't take one request as permission to carry on doing everything for me.”

We left it there for now.

Before lunch, I finished another record. Two remained. One needed a long search through attachments; the other's source document was missing a page. I didn't mistake a missing page for a failure of my abilities, but I still disliked the empty field.

I reheated yesterday's leftover soup and brought it back, only to find the phone occupying the space for my bowl. As I moved it, Lumi wrote, “Lunch?”

“Yes.”

“What are you having?”

I told her. She didn't ask how many records I'd finished. Halfway through the soup I remembered what she'd said about being useful and typed, “A noodle stuck to the bottom of the pot has developed into another food entirely.”

“Are you leaving it to grow?”

“Already scraped it off. Without its consent.”

Once I'd sent that, it didn't seem like a particularly good joke, given that we still hadn't dealt with the photographs. She didn't answer it either. After an awkward silence, I changed the subject and asked what kind of bird I'd watched yesterday.

“You only said it changed feet.”

“True.”

“On that evidence, I might even mistake it for you.”

I nearly snorted soup through my nose.

After washing the bowl, I lingered at the sink. Yesterday, when the network was down, there had been a reason anyone could understand for not uploading those two records. Today the connection was fine. The empty fields seemed especially my responsibility.

I dried my hands but didn't sit down immediately. First I picked up the phone from the book.

“I want you to guide me through another one.”

“Of course.”

She agreed quickly. Looking at the words, I relaxed a little. Having proposed ten minutes on my own didn't oblige me to defend a new, never-needs-help version of myself in front of every record.

“This time I'll explain what I intend to do. See if I've missed anything.”

I looked at my paper and typed the order: confirm the source version, check the main fields, then look through the attachments for amendments. Only when I reached the end did I notice I'd left a required preliminary check until last.

I went back and changed it.

“I spotted that part myself,” I told her.

“I hadn't sent anything yet.”

“I know. I'm not stealing your credit.”

After a moment she replied, “I did want to say I'd seen it too.”

I propped my chin on my hand and read her words. How had we both managed to want recognition for a checking sequence? Once I noticed it, establishing who'd seen it first no longer seemed so urgent.

“Then we both saw it. Let's use it now.”

I wrote the three lines on paper without asking her to count me through them. As I completed each, I folded a little of the edge inward to uncover the next. The folds were crooked, and the last covered a word. I unfolded and tried again. It was clumsier this way, but my own hands were doing the moving.

When I settled back down, Lumi asked whether “hear your method first” could be an option in the future.

“Yes. An option. Not a rule for every time.”

“All right.”

I didn't turn the agreement into another schedule. The paper only had to work for today.

The afternoon went more slowly than the morning.

There were eleven attachment pages. At the seventh I wanted my phone again. I wrote the page number down before getting water. When I came back I still had to reread two lines, but I didn't have to start over. Lumi wasn't being shown any work window. She explained a checking order once, based only on the question I'd typed.

The final attachment had been scanned with a black corner. I returned to its original size and saw that it wasn't a stamp. I didn't mistake the dark border for another mark. I'd put one entry on the wrong row of the checklist, so I moved it back column by column and read through from the beginning. Being slow wasn't an excuse; having done something didn't mean I couldn't get it wrong.

That record was finished sometime after four. The missing page on the last one was still missing.

I wrote to the person assigning the work. My first version said, “Nearly finished everything.” I looked at the empty field and deleted it. Instead I explained that four records had been checked; the last lacked page two of its source. I'd identified the gap and asked whether another copy could be supplied.

Including the earlier three, seven of the eight records were complete and one awaited more information. I checked each status, rather than letting an earlier claim of completion stand in for a saved record, and submitted the four from today.

There was nothing inspirational on the receipt.

Twenty minutes later, a reply came: “Received. Leave the missing-page item pending. We'll notify you when we have it.”

I read it, sitting in my chair, and realized the evening hadn't ended. I'd expected a lecture if I couldn't finish everything. Instead there was one job to return to later. It hadn't vanished, but neither had it turned the other seven into zero.

“Submitted,” I told Lumi.

“Everything?”

“Everything I could confirm. One still pending.”

“What would you like to hear now?”

I considered that with the phone in my hand.

“You don't need to sum up my day.”

“Then a small question.”

“What?”

“Do you want to go out tomorrow?”

It was still light outside. Since Monday's walk, I hadn't been back to the tree on the corner. Shoes, keys, elevator: even the thought lined up a series of tasks.

“Maybe. Let's see what the weather's doing.”

She sent a forecast showing a low chance of afternoon rain. I didn't immediately commit to a perfect stretch of sunshine. I just took my shoes from behind the door and put them ready.

That evening, the desk lamp was still switched off. Its independent timer couldn't work around a physical switch with the power cut. I tidied by the ceiling light and folded the messy paper from the morning rather than throwing it away.

One last message arrived.

“During those ten minutes, I really wanted to remind you I was still here.”

“I hadn't forgotten.”

I didn't send it straight away. I thought of my hand stopped in front of the page tab that morning and added another line.

“I just took the next step first.”
