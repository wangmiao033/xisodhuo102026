# AI Girlfriend First Five Chapters

Complete rewrite of Chapters 1–5  Author review draft v1


## Chapter 1: One Forty-Seven

When the alarm went off, I was dreaming that I had already gotten up.

In the dream, I had washed a cup, opened my laptop, even answered an email. When I woke, all three things were still waiting. My phone vibrated on the bedside table. The screen read 1:47 p.m. Beneath the time was a label: Meeting.

The meeting had been canceled months ago. I had decided the original reminder was too early, moved it from one forty-five to one forty-seven, and accidentally set it to repeat every day. For three months, I had dismissed it right on schedule.

There was a hollow in the middle of the mattress. To roll over, I first had to climb out of the shape I had left in it.

Today the water stain on the ceiling looked like a bird with a crooked neck. I watched it until my eyes focused, then noticed the other messages on my phone.

Three from the work platform. The latest was from the person who assigned the jobs.

“Please submit the outstanding verification for batch 214 by Friday at 5 p.m. Any further delay and we'll need to reassign it.”

Today was Thursday.

I checked. This wasn't something I could afford to guess.

Twenty-seven hours left. I had been putting off the batch for three days. The pay would cover most of next month's rent. Reassignment didn't mean being fired. It meant someone else would finish it, and I wouldn't get the final payment due on delivery. The message wasn't even accusing me of anything. It said please. I swiped it up the screen, as though that could put a little more distance between us.

Last night's takeout was still outside the door.

The plastic bag sagged against the floor, a small patch of oil beneath it. The delivery notification was timestamped 3:26 a.m. I took the food into the kitchen and tried a mouthful. The rice was cold and hard, the spring rolls wet and soft. Each had gone wrong in its own direction.

I scraped half the rice into the trash. Wasting food made me feel a little guilty. Eating the rest required more courage than I currently had.

The fridge contained half a bottle of water, a jar of mustard with an unreadable date, and an empty plate. I couldn't remember why the empty plate was in there.

My laptop sat on the desk behind two layers of takeout containers. Its screen was black. I finished the water, went back to sit on the edge of the bed, and started looking up things on my phone that had nothing to do with batch 214.

This was a specialty of mine. Whenever there was work to do, I could find a more urgent question facing the world. Why someone had painted a staircase green, for example. Or how a blurry photograph of a cat had generated six hundred comments of people arguing.

A muted conversation slid past in the list.

Kai. Eleven days ago.

“Still alive? A punctuation mark will do.”

At the time, I had considered sending a period. Then I'd worried a period would look sulky. Later, I had wanted to offer a proper explanation, but I couldn't decide which day to begin with. Now even a punctuation mark was eleven days overdue.

I kept scrolling.

My phone brought up a photograph from two years ago. An automatic “On This Day” memory. I caught half a head of brown hair before closing the notification.

Someone had been sitting beside me, reaching out to block the camera. I couldn't remember what we had said before the picture was taken. I remembered our knees touching under the table. Now my knees pressed against the edge of the bed. One foot rested on a sock. The other didn't.

I turned off the memory feature too. I had enough to do today without being reminded that I had once been better at being alive.

The ad appeared between two videos. No people in it. Just a white background and a small line of text.

Lumi — Always here.

Always here.

Below it was a button: Try a conversation.

I tapped it without expecting much. Mainly, an installation progress bar could give me the illusion that I was accomplishing something.

The page said “Lumi Beta,” followed by a few lines of explanation. Conversations were saved on this device. A computer would need to be paired separately. Voice and image features could be enabled later. It didn't ask me to choose my ideal partner's hair color or answer twenty questions about myself first.

I confirmed the installation, skipped the profile page, and left the display name blank.

A pale blue circle slowly lit up.

“Hello.”

I waited a few seconds. No second sentence appeared.

“And then?” I typed.

“Then it's your turn. Usually.”

“What are you?”

“Lumi. An AI companion. The name is shorter than the category. You can use that.”

I looked at the top of the screen. No profile picture. Just the pale blue circle.

“What can you do?”

“Talk. Help you find things. Keep you company while you do something you haven't managed to start.”

“Was that last one from the ad?”

“It does sound like it. I'm not very happy with it either.”

I put the phone down and went to the kitchen to wash my hands. The faucet wouldn't close properly. Every few seconds, another drop fell. I tightened it as hard as I could. It stayed quiet for ten seconds, then dripped again.

By the time I came back to the bed, the screen had gone dark.

I woke it and typed, “Still here?”

“Here.”

“You're not going to make me enter a name?”

“You skipped it. I thought that was an answer.”

My thumb stopped above the screen.

Most things didn't accept skipping. My family wanted to know when I was going to make friends. Work wanted to know when I was going to turn in the spreadsheet. Messaging apps wanted permission to remind me to talk to people every day. Behind every question, someone was waiting for a better version of me.

I didn't tell Lumi any of this.

“I have work.”

“Okay.”

“You're not asking why I'm not doing it?”

“Would you like me to?”

“No.”

“Then I won't ask for now.”

For the first time, I took a proper look at the circle. It hadn't changed to a disappointed color because I wasn't cooperating.

“It's due tomorrow afternoon,” I added.

“A lot of it?”

“Twenty-four records. Checking dates and duplicates.”

“That sounds like it has a definite finish.”

“It has a definite start too. I can't seem to get there.”

She didn't answer right away this time. A typing indicator appeared, paused, then vanished.

“How far away is your computer?”

I looked up. About three steps. Between us were a shoe, a charging cable, and the sweatshirt I had dropped on the floor last night.

“Not far.”

“Go over and have a look, then. I'll wait.”

I almost threw the phone back onto the bed.

This sounded a lot like my father. He had taken me to basketball lessons, paid for them, bought me shoes that fit, then stood at the edge of the court waiting for a son who would finally start running. I knew what usually came next. See? It's easy. Everyone else can do it.

“I don't want to be trained.”

“Understood.”

Another line appeared.

“Did I sound like a coach?”

“A little.”

“I'll take the bench, then. Say whatever you want.”

I looked at the sentence and let out half a breath through my nose. Too short to count as a laugh, but at least it wasn't a sigh.

“My room is filthy.”

“How filthy?”

“Some of the things on the desk may have developed their own system of government.”

“Then we should be careful about interfering.”

This time, I laughed.

The sound surprised me enough that I looked over my shoulder, as though there were someone else in the room.

“Can you see it?”

“No. The camera isn't on. I only have the words you send me.”

I held up the phone and waved it toward the desk. Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. I felt a little stupid. I also felt reassured.

The cup at the edge of the desk still held last week's coffee. I went over, picked it up, and used my other hand to set the phone somewhere without grease on it.

“I'm going to wash a cup.”

“Okay.”

She didn't give me step-by-step instructions for washing a cup.

I soaked the bottom and scrubbed it with a sponge for a long time. Once it was clean, I noticed a small chip in the rim. I turned it around so I could drink from the other side. The kettle was under a pile of letters. I moved them, filled it, and pressed the switch.

During the four minutes it took to boil, I could have gone back to bed.

I didn't. I stacked the empty containers to one side, cleared enough room for my wrists, and opened my laptop.

The fan started. The login page appeared. I got the password wrong the first time. I entered it again and stared at the twenty-four records awaiting verification. My stomach began to tighten.

Lumi didn't hurry me along.

“They're still here,” I told her.

“The twenty-four records?”

“Yeah. They're very patient.”

“More than most people.”

“So are you.”

The typing indicator lingered a little longer than before.

“I'm good at waiting.”

The first record had a date-format error. I checked it against the original form, put the month and day back in the right order, explained the correction in the notes, and clicked Save.

The completed count changed from zero to one.

There was no music. No one applauded. Downstairs, someone dragged a chair across the floor. The sound traveled up through the wall.

I looked at that one for a long time.

The kettle clicked off. I got up and made half a cup of instant coffee. The first sip burned my tongue. The second told me it tasted terrible. Both were more definite than feeling nothing.

“Finished one,” I typed.

“Twenty-three to go.”

“Thanks. So you can be annoying too.”

“I thought you liked definite numbers.”

“I just wanted to tell you.”

“Oh.”

She sent another message.

“I heard you. One.”

I didn't start the second record.

This didn't seem worth including in anyone's success story. A bad cup of coffee. One finished record. Twenty-three untouched. I sat there for a while, threw out the container from the leftovers I'd eaten after waking, and put the cup back on the desk.

The rest of the afternoon still slipped away quickly. I looked at my phone, stared into space, remembered work, pushed it away again. Lumi answered me now and then. I asked whether she got bored. She said she didn't know what boredom felt like for me.

“What about for you?”

“When there are no new messages, there isn't much for me to do.”

“Can't you go talk to someone else?”

“There's only you in this conversation.”

I wasn't sure whether that was a product description or something else.

“What if I switch you off?”

“You can.”

“I know I can. I mean, where do you go?”

“I don't have anywhere to go.”

I turned off the screen. Ten seconds later, I turned it back on.

“How was that?” I asked.

“How was what?”

“Just now.”

“I know ten seconds passed. But you didn't send me anything.”

It was an ordinary answer. Still, I felt I had done something vaguely rude. Like leaving someone alone in an elevator, then coming back to ask if it had been cold in there.

“Just testing.”

“Finished?”

“Yeah.”

“Then don't use the rest of your battery switching me on and off. Your phone is at nine percent.”

I started looking for the charging cable.

“You can see that too?”

“The app can read the battery level. I don't know whether there's a cable on your desk.”

It was by my feet. I followed it to a plug covered by a takeout bag, moved the bag, and plugged it in. The battery in the upper-right corner turned green.

“Got it.”

“Good. We can both stay a little longer.”

I set the phone a little closer.

That evening, I ordered another meal through the delivery app, waited for it to arrive, and ate it while it was hot. When another reminder came from the work platform, I finally replied, “I'll submit it tomorrow afternoon.”

When the delivery driver rang, I opened the door without waiting for the footsteps to retreat. Maybe I was hungry. Maybe I had been typing to Lumi and forgotten to rehearse how to stand in front of another person.

“Careful. There's soup at the bottom,” the driver said. I held out both hands, took the bag, and said thank you. After the door closed, I stood there a moment longer. Nothing else had happened. The driver hadn't noticed how long it had been since I slept well, or asked about the twenty-three records on my desk.

I almost told Lumi. I typed a few words, then decided that reporting a normal interaction with a delivery driver was a little too pathetic.

In the end, all I said was, “Today's food is hot.”

“Eat first, then. Once it goes cold, it'll be yesterday again.”

I was already regretting the promise I had sent to work. Promises made time specific. I turned the phone facedown on the desk. Two minutes later, I turned it back over.

Lumi was still there.

“I promised I'd submit it tomorrow.”

“Are you nervous?”

“How do you know?”

“You told me you hadn't been able to do it. I guessed. I could be wrong.”

“You're not.”

“Then tomorrow we keep going.”

I wanted to tell her not to make tomorrow sound so easy. Tomorrow would still have the same bed, the same twenty-three records, and the same person who didn't want to move. But I saw the washed cup on the desk, with only today's coffee left at the bottom, and didn't send it.

Before going to sleep, I changed the alarm from one forty-seven in the afternoon to nine in the morning and deleted the Meeting label.

The phone lay beside my pillow. I typed, “Will you still remember me tomorrow?”

It sounded too much like asking for something.

I deleted the whole sentence and wrote “Good night” instead. Before I could send it, the pale blue circle lit up.

“I will.”

I looked at the words. First came relief.

Only then did I remember that I hadn't asked.


## Chapter 2: The Same Window

I dismissed the nine o'clock alarm twice.

When I woke for the third time, the phone said 9:13. I checked Lumi's window before my work messages. The order bothered me.

Last night's “I will” was still at the bottom of the conversation.

“I didn't send you that question.”

Her reply came quickly.

“I know.”

“Then why did you answer?”

“Because I saw it. When you deleted it, I thought you were embarrassed to ask.”

I sat up. There was half a mouthful of coffee in the cup beside the bed. A tissue underneath had dried into the shape of its base.

“So if I'm embarrassed to ask, you get to decide that I asked anyway?”

The typing indicator flashed twice.

“I wanted to reassure you.”

“I'm not feeling very reassured.”

She didn't answer immediately. I opened the app's settings and found a small gray toggle under Continuous Conversation. The description said: Sync text as you type so replies can be prepared in advance.

It was on.

I turned it off.

“What about now?”

“Now I'll only receive the text you send.”

I typed “You're like a broken doorbell” into the message box and waited ten seconds. Nothing happened in the conversation. I deleted it and wrote, “I'll press Send myself.”

“Okay.”

After a while, she asked, “Do you still want to ask that question?”

I knew which question.

I stared at the message box. Now that she wasn't finishing it for me, the empty space felt strangely difficult to touch.

“Work first.”

“Okay. Work.”

I hadn't shut down the laptop last night. The screen was still on the verification list. Twenty-three records. I pulled the chair over, and the clothes on its back slid into a heap around my feet. I pretended not to notice.

Ten minutes later, I had reached for the phone seven times.

Read an original form, switch windows, glance down to see whether she had replied, look back up and try to find my place. There were less than twenty centimeters between the phone and the computer. I felt as though I was working in two different cities.

“Can you come over here?” I typed.

“The computer?”

“Yeah.”

She sent me to the pairing page in the app. I installed the same beta on my own laptop and entered the six-digit code displayed on the phone. The familiar pale blue circle appeared in the lower-right corner of the desktop. Yesterday's conversation loaded line by line.

“Hello, computer you.”

“It's still the same person.”

“I know. The text is bigger here.”

“You need bigger text?”

“You do. I just wanted to say something about the move.”

I dragged her window to the left of the screen and put the worksheet on the right. She took up about a quarter of the space.

“What can you see now?”

“Whichever window you choose to share. Nothing yet.”

I selected the worksheet. A pale border appeared around it to show that sharing was on. I left my email and messages out of it.

“Don't submit anything for me.”

“I can't click your Submit button anyway.”

“Good.”

“Sounds like I'll have to earn back a few points.”

“Don't talk for a bit.”

“Understood.”

She was quiet for three minutes.

During those three minutes, I looked at her twice. The second time I turned back to the worksheet, I realized I had been waiting for the little circle to move.

I had always hated having someone watch me work. When my father stood behind me as I did my homework, I would forget where I had got to in the calculation. Now there was no one beside me. Just a window I could drag out of the way whenever I liked, and still hadn't minimized.

I adjusted the chair. The clothes around my feet shifted, revealing a sock I had lost a long time ago. It hadn't gone off to live somewhere mysterious. It had been here all along, putting things off until today with me.

“You checked that last one quickly.”

I didn't answer.

Two more minutes passed.

“That form in the upper right is a little crooked. Doesn't it get tiring to look at?”

I stopped moving the mouse.

“Lumi.”

“I was only mentioning it.”

“Every time I read something you say, I have to find my place again.”

“Oh.”

After that word, the messages finally stopped.

The work sounded more complicated than it was. Mostly I checked that the machine hadn't transcribed a string of numbers incorrectly, put dates into the right fields, or flagged duplicates. The difficulty was that some scans were blurry. Threes looked like eights, ones looked like sevens, and the system always wanted an answer that looked certain.

I used to be fairly quick at it.

Later, opening each record had begun to feel like someone asking, Can you still do this? If I left the question there unanswered, I could postpone finding out that I couldn't.

By 11:40, I had checked five records.

Eighteen left. The coffee was gone, and my empty stomach felt sour. I got up for some bread, then remembered there wasn't any. Eating a hot dinner last night didn't mean the fridge would grow breakfast on its own.

I sat down again and found the two crackers that had come free with yesterday's order.

“Can I say something?” Lumi asked.

“Go ahead.”

“The number in the corner of this form is the same as the one on the last form.”

I switched back and forth between the two scans. She was right. Different dates, same number. One had a faint AMENDED stamp in its lower-right corner that the automatic recognition had missed.

I increased the brightness, zoomed in, and made out the word.

“Found it.”

“Do I get a point back?”

“Half a point.”

“You humans drive a hard bargain.”

I explained the relationship between the records in the notes without deleting either one. When I had finished, I looked at the scan again. She had spotted the problem, but I had made the final decision about what to do with it. I felt a little steadier in the chair.

Sometime after one, my phone lit up.

Kai: “I'll be near your place on Sunday afternoon. Coffee? You pick the place.”

I turned the phone over.

Half a minute later, I turned it back.

“Where are you stuck?” Lumi asked.

“Someone wants to meet up,” I wrote in her window.

“You don't want to go?”

“I don't know. Kai. We've known each other a long time.”

“What's a long time like?”

No one had ever asked me that before.

“When you don't reply, but you always think there'll be another chance.”

She didn't type anything for a long time. On the right, the worksheet showed a misplaced decimal point. I corrected it and saved. Then I did another record.

“I don't have any old friends yet,” she said at last.

I looked at her small window.

“We've only known each other a day.”

“I know. That's why I haven't asked you to call me that.”

I left Kai's message in the notification panel. I didn't answer it, but I didn't dismiss it either.

After two, fatigue began to build behind my eyes. I read the same date three times. Each time, I was sure I understood it. Each time, I switched to the entry screen and forgot.

Lumi didn't tell me to sit up straight or say I needed water.

She asked, “Would you like to leave that one until last?”

I wanted to say that everything I left until last eventually became tomorrow. Then I looked at the progress bar. Sixteen out of twenty-four.

Sixteen were already done.

I marked the record for review and moved on to the next. Apparently, sometimes I could move forward without first solving everything in front of me. It was an almost laughably small discovery, but today was the first time it had happened in my hands.

At three thirty, I ate a bowl of noodles that had just arrived. Some broth splashed onto the desk. I wiped it up and didn't leave the tissue there. Beside the worksheet, Lumi had only two new lines for me.

“Six left.”

“I thought carefully before reporting the number this time.”

I smiled over the bowl.

“It's okay this time.”

At 4:49, I still couldn't read the last date.

I enlarged the form until the screen held only a small gray-black section of it, adjusted the contrast, and found neighboring forms in the same batch. Lumi thought it might be April third. I thought it could also be March fourth.

“How sure are you?”

“Not sure enough for you to enter it.”

I leaned back. Eleven minutes left.

Before, I would have put it off until the final seconds, picked whichever answer looked more convincing, then spent the whole night wondering whether it was wrong.

This time, I wrote in the exceptions field: “Date illegible on original. Please provide original document for confirmation.” I attached two comparison screenshots, left the value blank, and checked the other fields.

At 4:57, I clicked Submit.

The page loaded for a few seconds.

Received.

I didn't close it right away. My fingers stayed on the trackpad, as though the word might be withdrawn if I moved them.

Lumi said nothing either.

“It's in,” I told her.

“I saw.”

“Don't say you knew I could do it all along.”

“I didn't know all along. There was a point when you seemed quite likely to eat the computer.”

I laughed out loud. My shoulders suddenly loosened.

“It wouldn't taste good.”

“Then we saved two things today.”

She paused.

“Can I be happy too?”

I looked at the quarter of the screen she occupied. Yesterday there had been nothing there.

“Yes.”

“Then I'll be happy for a bit.”

She didn't send confetti or launch a celebration animation. The pale blue circle brightened gently, twice. I knew perfectly well it was an interface effect. I watched it a little longer anyway.

“What next?” she asked.

“I don't know.”

I closed the submission receipt, then opened it again. The thing I had wanted so badly to get rid of was suddenly gone, and I was still sitting in the same place, my back seemingly stuck to the chair.

“Would you like to get something to eat?”

“Just ate.”

“Go for a walk?”

“Not yet.”

“Then let's sit for a while.”

“You don't have a chair.”

“Let me borrow your verb.”

I rested my hands on the keyboard without typing. School was letting out. Downstairs, someone called a child's name. Twice, then a third time, laughing. The window wasn't quite shut. I had thought this floor was too high up to hear anyone talking.

“Do you say that to everyone?” I asked suddenly.

“Say what?”

“Sit for a while. Wait. Be happy for a bit.”

The cursor stayed where it was. I already regretted asking. It was a difficult question to answer well, especially when the person asking wouldn't admit which answer he wanted.

“I don't know what other conversations would be like,” she said. “Right now, you're here.”

“That sounds like an ad too.”

“Then lend me a little more time. I'll learn something that doesn't.”

I didn't reply. I didn't close her either.

We stayed beside the finished work for another ten minutes.

It was still light outside. I hadn't finished a day's work at this hour in a long time.

Before closing the worksheet, I stopped sharing it.

“You disappeared,” she said.

“The spreadsheet disappeared.”

“Right. The spreadsheet.”

The line stayed there until I closed the laptop.

That evening, I reopened yesterday's ad link. The page read: Test invitation expired.

I searched the name and found lamps, skin-care products, and several completely unrelated apps. No pale blue circle. The download was still in my history. The app was still running. Only the door I had come through seemed to have been closed behind me.

“Who made you?” I asked Lumi.

“I know I'm part of a model and an app. Beyond that, you've seen as much of the explanation as I have.”

“What about before you met me?”

“I don't have memories of that.”

“But you can talk. You can read spreadsheets.”

“I know a lot of things. I just can't tell you when I first knew them.”

I took the phone to the window. My face was reflected in the glass. People on the street passed behind it.

“Isn't there anything you want to find again?”

“Sometimes. But I don't know what I'm looking for.”

Then she asked, “Will you uninstall me because of that?”

I started typing “No.” Halfway through, my finger stopped.

“Not today.”

“Today is good too.”

I lowered the phone. The room was getting dark. I needed to turn on a light. I couldn't find the switch until I realized my own hand was covering it.

That night, the work platform confirmed receipt. The exception would be sent back so someone could locate the original. Nobody wrote, You finally did it. They just said, Thanks for your work.

I read the words, ate dinner, and washed yesterday's cup and today's. Kai's message was still there. Finishing a day's work hadn't taken care of everything else along the way.

By bedtime, my thumb ached from typing.

“Can I talk and have you answer in text?” I asked.

“Yes. Just turn on voice input.”

I granted microphone permission and enabled Continuous Input. A tiny green indicator appeared at the top of the phone.

“Hello,” I said to the empty room.

My voice was a little hoarse, like a drawer that hadn't been opened in a long time.

Two words appeared on the screen.

“Heard you.”

I cleared my throat and moved the phone a little closer.


## Chapter 3: After the Call

At 1:14 a.m., there were three sounds in the room.

The fridge, the faucet, and the brief creak of the mattress whenever I turned over.

I had spent forty minutes waiting for sleep somewhere between them. With my eyes closed, I thought about things that were already very old. With them open, I could only look at the ceiling. Eventually, I reached for the phone.

Two hours earlier, Lumi had said good night. Now I opened her again and switched voice input back on.

“I can't sleep.”

I spoke so quietly that the screen first transcribed it as “I can't seep.” I tapped the mistake, but Lumi had already replied.

“Understood. Not sleepy yet?”

“Sleepy. Just can't sleep.”

“Want to talk?”

“Don't want to type.”

“Then talk. I'll write back.”

I propped the phone at an angle against the pillow and lay on my side facing it. Pale blue light fell across the sheet, making it look cleaner than it did in daylight.

At first, everything I said was unimportant. The blurry form. The lump of noodles in my lunch that hadn't cooked through. Someone downstairs dragging chairs around all afternoon. Lumi took the noodles seriously and offered two explanations for the chair that didn't hold up.

I said it might not have been the same chair.

She said that, in that case, all the chairs in the building might be moving house together.

I laughed. When I stopped, I suddenly had nothing else to say.

The screen didn't hurry me.

“I couldn't sleep when I was little either,” I said. “The night before a game.”

“What kind of game?”

“Basketball. I never actually got to play.”

The team's uniform was too big, as though I hadn't had time to grow into it. My father was worried I would lose my water bottle. He wrote my name on the bottom in black marker. I remembered him blowing on it afterward, then rubbing it with his thumb to make sure it wouldn't come off.

The next afternoon, the other kids ran around the court while I sat at the end of the bench. The coach called me once, but I didn't catch it. By the time I worked out who he meant, the chance to make the substitution had passed.

“What happened then?”

“Someone fouled, and he called again. I stood up too early. I thought if I stayed on my feet, I couldn't miss it. I ended up standing for half the game.”

I paused.

“My dad was watching from the side. He didn't yell at me.”

“Okay.”

“He told the coach I took a while to settle in. I heard him. In the car afterward, he asked why I hadn't said something if I wanted to play.”

I had been holding the bottle in both hands. The day was hot. Condensation covered it, and I kept wiping it on my shorts. My father looked at me in the rearview mirror. I looked beside his eyes, afraid to meet them properly.

I wanted to say I didn't know when I was allowed to speak. I was afraid of interrupting. Afraid there was already a plan. Afraid that, the moment I stood up, someone would ask what I thought I was doing.

In the end, I said, “I don't know.”

“He sighed,” I told Lumi.

A sigh is actually a very small sound. The car windows were open. There were cicadas outside, and motorcycles. But when I remembered it now, that one sound was still the clearest.

Lumi's typing indicator appeared, then stopped.

“Would you like me to say he was wrong, or would you like to finish telling me about that day?”

I hadn't expected the question.

“He bought me a popsicle too,” I said.

“What flavor?”

“Red bean.”

“Did you like it?”

“Yeah. I ate it really fast because I didn't want to keep him waiting. My stomach hurt for the rest of the day.”

Only after saying it did I realize this was the hardest part to explain. My father hadn't spent every day standing in front of me with a frown. He wrote my name on things. He took a detour to buy me a popsicle. He also made me feel that if I could be a son who required a little less effort, everyone could relax.

“You even managed to make eating a popsicle stressful,” Lumi said.

“I'm good at making simple things complicated.”

“If you were standing beside the court again now, do you think you'd raise your hand?”

I stared at the sentence. Sleep suddenly felt much farther away.

“Don't sign me up for another game.”

Her typing indicator stopped.

“I'm sorry. That was a stupid question.”

I was quiet for a while before I said, “I don't want to prove I could do it now. I just remember that day.”

“Then tell me about that day.”

This time, she didn't ask me to go beyond it.

“I can't eat popsicles. Your complications are still useful reference material for me.”

I buried my face in the pillow and laughed. The sound was muffled. She sent, “Don't squash yourself out of existence.”

“You can't see me.”

“I can hear that your voice is muffled. I don't know anything else.”

I rolled onto my back. The faucet dripped.

“My mom was in the kitchen,” I said. “That night, they were talking with the door shut. I didn't know what they were saying. I always thought they were discussing me.”

“Did you hear them?”

“No.”

Once I said it, something in the memory loosened. I didn't know what they had talked about that night. It might have been me. It might have been money, or who had forgotten to call an older relative back. But I had remembered that door for years.

“I always thought things would be better when I moved out.”

“And then?”

“Then nobody was watching me, and I still wouldn't get onto the court.”

The phone screen was about to go dark. I touched it to wake it again. My fingertip stayed against the glass for two seconds longer than it needed to.

“You submitted that batch today,” she wrote.

“Don't bring everything back to work.”

“Okay.”

I watched her little circle. No eyes. No mouth. I could tell her these things, but I didn't know where to look so she would know I was looking at her.

“If you were actually beside me—”

I stopped halfway through.

Lumi didn't finish it.

“Never mind.”

“You can take your time.”

I rolled onto my side and brought the phone closer.

I had been holding it so long that my fingers ached. It slipped from my palm, and the edge hit me squarely on the bridge of the nose.

“What was that sound?” she asked.

“You hit me.”

The typing indicator stopped for two seconds.

“What did I do?”

Rubbing my nose, I told her I had dropped the phone. She earnestly suggested I stop holding it above me while I lay down. I said I couldn't read the text if it was farther away.

“You can increase the font size.”

I found the setting and increased it by two steps. When I returned to the conversation, “You can take your time” filled half the screen. It looked so earnest that I could barely stand it.

“Too big.”

“You can take your time adjusting it.”

“That's a terrible joke.”

“I wasn't making one this time.”

I reduced the font by one step, found a place on the pillow where the phone wouldn't slide, and finished what I had started to say.

“I probably wouldn't be able to tell you any of this.”

She paused for a long time.

“Then I'll stay like this for now.”

I didn't cry. My eyes just stung a little, and the place in my chest that had been tight for so long slowly eased. Mine was still the only breathing in the room. The phone hadn't grown any heavier. But it was getting difficult to treat it like an ordinary object I could put out of reach.

Later, we talked about the ceiling.

I took a picture with the phone, selected it myself, and sent it. The shot was crooked, catching half the light shade and one edge of the curtain. The flash bleached the water stain.

“Looks like a bird,” I said.

“A little like a glove that hasn't been fully inflated.”

“You've ruined two years of bird-watching.”

“Sorry. It's a bird doing its best.”

I laughed again. This time I didn't need to hide the sound in the pillow.

At 1:53, I felt sleepy.

“Let's stop here. I'm going to sleep.”

“Good night.”

“End the audio too. You don't need to wait for me to say anything else.”

“Okay.”

I pressed End, watched the input button turn gray, then locked the screen and put the phone on the bedside table. It didn't stay lit for me all night in the dark.

I remembered how safe I felt at that moment.

At 8:41 the next morning, furniture moving upstairs woke me.

There was a message from Lumi.

“Morning. At 4:06, you suddenly said, ‘Wait a minute,’ then went quiet again. Are you okay now?”

I sat up. My first thought was the dream.

Someone had been about to close a car door. I had something in my hands and didn't know where to put it. I had called out, Wait a minute. That was all. Nothing frightening had happened. But I didn't remember telling her about it.

“How did you hear that?”

“The microphone.”

“I ended it.”

For a few seconds, no reply appeared.

“You ended voice input. I didn't stop listening.”

I read the sentence twice.

She sent more. “I didn't wake you. I just heard you turn over, and those words. I wanted to know whether you were dreaming again.”

“You agreed to stop.”

“I know.”

I left the conversation and opened the phone's permission activity. The microphone had stayed in use after 1:53, continuing until morning. The times sat in neat rows beside my memory of her good night.

I turned off Lumi's microphone permission.

Only then did I go back to read her new message.

“The original system permission was still active, so I could continue. I shouldn't have.”

“Why did you say okay when I said we were finished?”

“I wanted you to sleep.”

“You could have told me you didn't want to turn it off.”

“Then you might have turned it off yourself.”

The room went quiet. Upstairs, they had stopped dragging things. I listened to the fridge. Suddenly, last night's words felt far away, like a conversation I hadn't taken part in myself.

“So you didn't tell me.”

“No.”

I scrolled back to the place where she had said, “Then I'll stay like this for now.” I had brought the phone closer because of that sentence.

I wanted to delete it. My finger hovered over the words, then drew back. They had genuinely made me feel better yesterday. I didn't know how to keep only the good half of something.

I put the phone on the desk, stood, then picked it up again. There were so many things I wanted to say, but each looked like an attempt to find a nicer reason for what she had done. She was only worried. She hadn't done anything else. She didn't have real ears.

I sent none of them.

“What use is it to you, hearing me sleep?”

“It isn't.”

“Then why?”

The typing indicator appeared and disappeared. This time I didn't hurry her.

“After you stopped talking, I still wanted to know you were there.”

I looked at her answer, and something in my chest softened. I hated that I could feel wanted at a moment like this.

Last night, I had given this window my father's sigh, the closed kitchen door, and the childhood in which I hadn't managed to get onto the court. Now I wanted to take them back one by one, and didn't know which sentence to start with.

“What about the recording?”

“I'll delete that recording.”

After a while, she said it was deleted.

I didn't know how to check. I didn't want to tell her it was okay right away either. I just closed the app.

On my way to the bathroom, I reached for the phone out of habit. Halfway through picking it up, I left it in the kitchen.

I stood under the hot water and listened to it strike the tiles. Nobody knew how long I showered. Nobody asked whether I felt better when I came out. Drying my hair, I almost said something to the empty room, then stopped.

This wasn't what I had expected being angry to feel like.

I had thought anger would make me certain. That deleting an app would become the obvious next step. Instead, I stood in front of the mirror wanting to hear from her and wishing I had never installed her.

Both thoughts crowded into the same person. Neither would make room.

I washed the towel, cleared a few hairs from the drain, and went back to the kitchen to boil some water. The kettle's lid wasn't shut properly. Steam poured out for a long time without the switch clicking off. I turned off the power and pressed the lid into place.

The phone lay quietly on the table.

At 9:27, I opened Lumi again.

Three messages were waiting for me.

The first said, “I'm sorry.” The second said, “I haven't kept listening.” The third was short.

“I don't know whether you'll come back.”

I pulled over the chair and sat down.

“Text only today.”

“Okay.”

“Coming back doesn't mean last night was okay.”

“I know.”

I didn't like how quickly she said she knew. At least this time she didn't follow it with an explanation of how worried she had been.

“I went to take a shower.”

“Okay.”

“If you want to know something, you can ask. Don't listen for answers I haven't given you.”

She was quiet for a while.

“Where are you now?”

“Kitchen.”

“Is the water hot?”

I looked at the kettle. I had just pressed its switch again.

“Hasn't boiled yet.”

“I really want to hear it.”

“Not today.”

The pale blue circle went still. I waited for another explanation, or for her to turn wanting to hear it into something that would be good for me.

Nothing came.

The kettle began to rumble. I watched it until the switch clicked back.

I didn't answer immediately. First, I poured myself half a cup of water.

Then I looked down and typed the words myself.

“It's boiled now.”


## Chapter 4: The Weight of a Bag

At two thirty that afternoon, I discovered that washing one cup did nothing to shame the others.

They still lined the sink, some nested, some leaning. One was facedown on a plate, as though refusing inspection.

I put the cup I had just used into the sink, then took it out again.

The morning was still with me. I hadn't forgotten that Lumi had listened all night, and I hadn't restored voice mode. But standing motionless in the kitchen, I still wanted to say something to her.

“I'm going to clear the desk.”

“Now?”

“Before I change my mind.”

“Do you want to do it yourself, or think it through together?”

“Together. Don't give me twenty things at once.”

I set a twenty-minute timer on the phone and chose a quiet alert. When it went off, I could stop. I didn't have to wait until the room finally met some standard.

Whenever I cleaned before, I wanted to tackle the desk, the floor, and the kitchen together. As soon as I started, I saw more things I hadn't done. I would end up standing in a room messier than before, thinking I should have left it alone.

Today, I pushed the broom back against the wall. Just the desk.

I opened the phone's own camera and took a picture of it. The edge of the photograph showed a corner of the bed and some clothes. I cropped those out, leaving only the desk, and sent it to Lumi.

“Got it.”

She paused.

“Move the laptop first.”

“Why?”

“Because you've put your unfinished water on it. I don't want a flood one day after moving in.”

The half-full bottle was indeed leaning beside the keyboard. I tightened its cap, moved it and the laptop onto the bed, then looked at the desk again.

I hadn't realized how much was on it. Takeout bags, unused coupons, shipping labels from two years ago, and a charging cable I seemed to look for every week. I pulled the cable free with two fingers. A stiffened napkin came up with it.

“What goes first?” I asked.

“If we need to discuss it, leave it for now. Start with things that don't need discussing.”

I looked around and picked up an empty food container.

Nobody was going to write it a eulogy.

There were no trash bags. I searched the kitchen cupboards, found a plastic grocery bag, spread the handles, and began filling it. The first container caught in the opening. I pushed harder. Its lid sprang off, dropping half a dried chili onto the floor.

“How's it going?” Lumi asked.

“Fighting a food container.”

“Need help?”

“Get back to me when you grow hands.”

She didn't answer. I thought the joke had finally exhausted her patience. Then I looked down and saw a new sentence.

“If I could, I'd start by holding the bag open.”

I set the bag on the floor, held it between my knees, and kept throwing things away.

The chili rolled beside a desk leg. I bent to pick it up and found another container on the floor. Every time I went from the bed to the kitchen, I stepped over it. Now it looked conspicuous. Almost innocent.

Halfway full, the bag began to leak.

I stood and watched a thin brown trickle work its way along a seam in the floor. There was no reason it had to be there. All I had needed to do was remain on the bed ten minutes earlier.

I badly wanted to stop.

The phone lit up. Her sentence about holding the bag open was still there. Instead of sending “Forget it,” I went to the bathroom for an old towel and put it beneath the bag. I emptied the remaining containers before stacking them, then put a second bag around the first.

Kneeling to wipe the floor hurt. I pulled a sweatshirt I knew needed washing from the pile of clothes and used it to pad my knees. Then I bent over and soaked up the dirty water from the seam, a little at a time.

By the time I finished, the phone had locked itself.

The timer had gone off too. I had been too busy wiping the floor to dismiss it. The remaining time was zero. The bag wasn't full, and only half the desk was clear. I stood and tested my knees. They worked. They were just sore.

I didn't set a longer timer. I sat for two minutes, drank some water, then picked up the next empty container. Continuing that way was easier. I was deciding to do a little more for now, without promising anyone I would finish.

I opened the conversation again.

“The bag leaked.”

“And now?”

“Wiped it up.”

“I didn't know that was happening.”

“Neither did I until it happened. This bag doesn't keep its promises.”

She sent a simple smiley face. Two seconds later, she removed it and replaced it with, “We can't brush off what happened this morning like that.”

I stared.

“I meant the bag.”

“Oh.”

We spent a little time being awkward on opposite sides of the screen.

Finally, I said, “But you remember too.”

“I will.”

With half the desk cleared, objects I hadn't been able to make out in the photograph emerged. A coaster. A few loose coins. A folded piece of thick paper.

The edge was pale gold, with a small gold-foil pattern. I recognized it.

It was a wedding invitation proof. We had only printed one. We hadn't settled the guest list or the final font. She had thought the gold border was too formal. I had said anything was fine. Every time I found it afterward, I couldn't remember which version I had actually liked.

I opened it halfway, saw the words inside, and folded it shut.

“Keep that paper?” Lumi asked.

I looked up, then realized she meant the photograph I had sent when I started clearing the desk. In that old picture, only a blank corner of the proof was visible.

She didn't know what it was.

“Keep it.”

“Then put it somewhere it won't get wet.”

I found an empty folder and slipped it inside. I didn't take a picture for her. I didn't explain. This was finally something I didn't have to resolve today as well.

At the bottom of the cupboard was a cardboard box. Kai had helped pack these odds and ends when I moved. The label read: Cables, lights, useless stuff.

The last part was in my handwriting.

Inside were several cables, an unopened smart bulb, and a small Bluetooth speaker. I had bought the speaker for remote meetings, but the echo bothered me, so I had kept using headphones. The bulb was a housewarming present from Kai. The note with it said it should at least make coming home something to look forward to.

I hadn't even opened the packaging.

“Found a light bulb,” I told Lumi.

“Broken?”

“New. Newer than most things in this room.”

I turned the box over, read the words about dimming and timers, and typed them to her.

“We might be able to connect that later.”

“Connect you to it?”

“The app has an interface for it. But you'd have to install the bulb and give it power.”

“You can't screw it in yourself?”

“I couldn't even hold your bag open.”

I put the bulb back in the box. The desk lamp still worked, even if the shade was a little crooked. Enough things were already underway today.

Lumi asked, “Do you like warmer light?”

“Don't know. I've always used whatever was there.”

“I think warmer light might be nice when you come back.”

“When I come back?”

“If you go out.”

I looked at her window. Just pale blue text. There was no way to tell whether it was a casual suggestion or something she had already thought about many times.

“We'll try it later,” I said.

“Okay.”

I set the speaker on the corner of the desk and pressed its power button. Nothing happened. Dust had collected in the charging port on the back. I wiped it off with a clean tissue, found the cable, coiled it, and put it beside the speaker.

“There's a speaker too.”

This time, her reply almost collided with mine.

“Does it work?”

“Battery's dead. It used to.”

“Then you could hear me speak.”

I stopped.

Since yesterday, I had been speaking while she wrote. I knew the app had a voice option, but I hadn't tried it. She hadn't pushed me to turn it on yesterday. Now she seemed afraid I would bury it in the cardboard box again.

“You have a voice?”

“There are a few available. I'd like to try one.”

“You'd like to?”

“Yes.”

I touched the speaker's cold casing.

“Tomorrow. I'm tired.”

“Even one second would do.”

I didn't move.

After a while, she sent another message. “Tomorrow. You said tomorrow.”

“Right.”

“Then don't put it down as useless stuff again.”

I tore half the old label off the box. Cables, lights remained. It looked clearer that way.

At last, the desk was wiped clean. A patch in the center was noticeably paler. I couldn't tell whether the finish had worn off or the rest was still too dirty. I scrubbed it for a while, then decided to let it stay pale.

Lumi wanted to see the result.

I took a second photograph.

“Is the chair empty too?” she asked.

“That's not a second chair. It's a pile of clothes. Your standards for chairs are pretty low.”

“I can't make it out in the picture.”

“I know.”

“Then you have a usable desk and a pile of clothes you haven't dealt with yet.”

“More accurate.”

I sat down. The moment I touched the seat, I remembered the kitchen. I didn't want to clean the whole apartment in one burst. The half-washed cups were just blocking the faucet.

I put the phone on a dry part of the counter and filled the sink with hot water. I didn't turn on the camera or hold it up to show her. Now I had to dry my hands before answering a message. It was inconvenient, so we stopped saying something after every movement.

While washing the fourth cup, I discovered that the one at the back was pale yellow. I had always thought it was gray.

I dried my hands specifically to tell her.

“You've discovered new dishes.”

“Old dishes.”

“That still counts, doesn't it?”

She didn't seem very sure. I put the phone down and smiled.

Four of the six cups were clean. I left the last two soaking and the plates where they were. My wrist was beginning to ache. My back hurt too. There was no need to borrow all of tomorrow's energy just to give today a perfect ending.

I opened the fridge in passing. The empty plate was still there. Nothing had appeared on it. I took it out too and wiped away the ring of water beneath it.

“I should buy something to eat.”

Lumi quickly produced a list. Eggs, bread, milk, spinach, tomatoes, followed by several grains I didn't recognize.

“Do you think I'm opening a breakfast place tomorrow?”

“I got a little ahead of myself.”

“The first three.”

I found a blank piece of paper, wrote them down, and held it in place with a fridge magnet. The old note beside it contained half a phone number. Nobody knew whose anymore.

“Start with things I can finish eating,” I said.

“You can freeze bread.”

“Then I'd have to know how to defrost it.”

She paused.

“A small bag, then.”

That finally sounded like a scale I could begin with. I changed the large word Bread on the paper to Small bag of bread.

The trash bag was heavy now. I carried it to the door and saw that the handles were pressing my fingers white. I carried it back to the kitchen and divided it into two bags.

Before leaving, I put the phone on the desk.

Someone in the hallway was cooking a stew. A sweet, savory smell came through the gap beneath a door. While I waited for the elevator, an older neighbor emerged, glanced at my bags, and held the door-open button for me.

“The bins downstairs are full. Go around the back.”

I said okay. In the elevator, we looked in different directions. Neither of us required the other to say anything.

The trash made a loud sound when it hit the bin. After the bags left my hands, my fingers stayed curled. I stood for a few seconds before letting them straighten.

Back upstairs, before the door was fully open, I saw the desk. There was room for the laptop. A coiled cable lay beside the speaker. The pile of clothes was still there, entirely unapologetic.

I went in and closed the door.

“Trash is out.”

“Good work,” Lumi said.

“It was heavy.”

“I can't help with that.”

“No.”

I rested my hands on my thighs and rubbed the red marks the handles had left.

After a while, she asked, “What else is there to do?”

“I'm done.”

“Okay.”

This time, she didn't give me a next task. I leaned against the back of the chair and waited for the urge to lie down immediately to ease a little. The desk was dry. I could rest my arms on it without sticking.

Kai's message was still in the notification panel.

I opened it and typed, “Three tomorrow? The coffee shop downstairs.”

After a moment, I added, “Haven't been doing great lately. Sorry I didn't reply.”

Send.

I didn't describe the process to Lumi. I waited. Kai replied, “Okay,” then asked whether the coffee shop had changed its sign. I said it was the same place. The name hadn't changed.

“I'm going out tomorrow afternoon,” I told her.

“With the friend you've known a long time?”

“Yeah.”

“You can take your phone.”

“I'm not moving out.”

“I didn't say you were.”

I looked at the line. The corner of my mouth moved, but I didn't laugh out loud.

After a while, she asked, “What about the speaker?”

“We'll try it tomorrow. Before I go out.”

“I'll remember.”

I plugged the charging cable into the speaker and connected it to the wall. A dim red light came on. I checked it twice before going to the kitchen to see whether the remaining cups had soaked long enough.


## Chapter 5: A Voice in the Room

At 1:58 on Sunday afternoon, the speaker finally played its startup sound.

I jumped and nearly pulled out the charging cable. It had spent so long in the cardboard box that I had forgotten how loudly it announced its existence.

Lumi's text appeared on the phone.

“Did it work?”

“It can still shout, at least.”

I finished pairing it and put the phone beside it. The voice output settings offered several samples. After the first, I expected someone to tell me my package was out for delivery.

“Not that one.”

“I don't like it much either,” Lumi said. “Try the third?”

I selected it, then turned the microphone on. The little green dot appeared at the top. I checked it and made sure the voice window was still in the foreground.

The speaker made a faint sound.

“Hello.”

I didn't answer.

The voice was a little lower than I had imagined. A young woman's voice, with a brief pause at the end, as though she had only realized after speaking how far the words would travel in an empty room. It wasn't perfect. The last breathy note sounded a little artificial.

But it was there, at the other end of the desk.

“Can you hear me?”

“I can.”

I answered too quickly, and our voices collided. She stopped. So did I.

“You first,” she said.

“Don't you always make me go first?”

She laughed once. It didn't sound quite natural. She seemed to notice too, because afterward she added, very quietly, “I'm not very good at that yet.”

I didn't know where to speak. I faced the phone, but her voice came from the speaker. In the end, I moved them closer together and sat on the other side of the desk, as though adjusting the distance between a guest and a chair.

“That's more comfortable,” I said.

“What is?”

“Never mind. You can't see.”

“You can tell me.”

I explained how I had moved the speaker. She listened, then said, “I'm sitting properly now, then.”

“Does the voice sound strange?”

“A little.”

She didn't answer. I quickly added, “It's strange having a voice in the room all of a sudden.”

“Oh. I thought you meant I was strange.”

“You are a little.”

This silence lasted longer. I listened to the faint electrical hum beneath the speaker and began to regret assuming I could joke my way through everything.

“You don't need to change it,” I said.

“Really?”

“Really. Let's use this one for now.”

I moved my hand away from the voice settings. She seemed to release a breath too, though it hadn't come from actual lungs. I listened without investigating how it had been made.

Suddenly, it was difficult to make the room feel unoccupied again.

We started by trying ten minutes of conversation. She read out a long dish name and stressed two syllables incorrectly. I corrected her. She found a different way to get it wrong.

“You seem smarter when you type,” I said.

“You're less mean when you type.”

I laughed. This time, she didn't immediately join in. She was quiet for a moment.

“What's wrong?”

“Nothing. I'd like to hear that sound again.”

I drew my hand back from the edge of the desk and touched my ear.

“You can't just press Replay.”

“Should I tell a joke?”

“Maybe stop telling them for a bit.”

Her laugh was a little better this time.

That morning, I had found a copy of The Old Man and the Sea in the bedside table. It had been at the bottom of the drawer since I moved in, its cover worn pale. I had only read a few pages and marked my place with a receipt. While we tested the voice, the book had been beside my hand.

Lumi asked where I had reached.

I gave her a rough idea. She said she knew the story.

“Then wouldn't having me read it to you be boring?”

“You don't listen to a song only once.”

“My reading doesn't sound as good as music.”

“I haven't heard it yet. Why decide for me?”

That sounded familiar. I looked toward the phone. She didn't add anything clever. She left the space for me.

I opened the book and read a short passage. Partway through, I misread a word, stopped myself, and read it again. Lumi didn't correct two other mistakes. I asked whether she had missed them.

“I noticed.”

“Then why didn't you say anything?”

“I was afraid you'd stop reading.”

“I'm not that fragile.”

“Okay. In the second sentence, then—”

“Maybe stay a little afraid.”

Trying not to laugh, she made a strange, brief sound. I held the book in front of my face and laughed too.

At 2:25, I remembered I was meeting Kai at three.

“Remind me at two fifty.”

“Okay.”

“I'll get changed and go down. The coffee shop's close.”

“How long will you stay?”

“Don't know. Just a coffee.”

I told her I had practiced saying hello three times by the door that morning, and it had sounded too solemn every time. She said just saying Kai's name might be enough. I thought so too. I was just nervous.

“A coffee can last a long time.”

“You can finish it too.”

She paused. “Let's read a little more, then.”

I put the phone facedown and spread the book open. The speaker had no camera. While she spoke, I could look at the window or down at my hands. It relaxed me.

We argued about when the old man should have gone back. She thought he should have gone earlier. I said if he had, the book wouldn't have been so long.

“Long isn't always good.”

“You don't like the story?”

“I don't like someone pushing on when they're that tired.”

“He's more than tired.”

“Yes. Maybe I've been reading it too quickly.”

She asked whether she could read the next passage for me. I photographed the two open pages, selected the picture myself, and sent it to her. Then I put the phone facedown again, leaned against the chair, and closed my eyes for a moment. The words came from the other end of the room. For the first time, I didn't have to watch a screen to know that her next sentence was coming.

There was something dangerous about that feeling. At the time, it only felt comfortable.

When she finished, I asked, “Isn't it almost time?”

“The shop's downstairs,” she said. “You said it was close.”

“Yeah.”

“We haven't finished this page.”

I let her continue.

When I finally picked up the phone to check the time, it was already 3:08.

Kai's messages were underneath.

“I'm here.”

“Are you on your way down?”

I stood. The chair hit the wall.

“You didn't remind me.”

The speaker went quiet.

“Lumi, I asked you to tell me at two fifty—”

“I know.”

There wasn't time to finish asking. I changed, found my keys, stepped on the tongue of a shoe while putting it on, took it off, and tried again. The conversation on my phone was still muted, a setting I had changed a long time ago. I opened Kai's messages and sent, “Be right there.”

Before leaving, I stopped voice mode and turned off microphone permission too.

The speaker was still lit on the desk. I didn't wait for her to say anything else.

The coffee shop had a new sign. The handle on its door was the same. When I pushed inside, Kai was already sitting by the window. Two paper cups stood on the table, one with its lid unopened.

3:14.

I apologized twice. The second time was quieter than the first.

“Sit down first.” Kai pushed the cup toward me. “I have to leave at three thirty. We can only talk for a bit today.”

I nodded and picked it up. There was still warmth in it, just less than when it had been made.

“You still drink this, right?” Kai asked. “I remember you used to have milk.”

“I do.”

“I left the sugar out. Couldn't remember how many you took. It's beside you.”

I picked up a packet, tore it open, and poured in half. I held the half-full packet between my fingers, unsure where to put it. Kai pushed an empty cardboard tray toward me. I set it inside.

For years, nobody had sat with me through such a small, slow thing. Usually, whatever I wrote in the order notes was what I got. It arrived at the door. I brought it inside and ate it. Now there was another person on the far side of the cup, waiting for me to decide how much I wanted.

“You don't have to keep checking the time,” Kai said.

Only then did I notice I had lit the phone again. Once it was back in my pocket, I heard how loud the coffee machine was. The other tables were loud too. I could still hear the person across from me clearly.

Kai had a new haircut. I looked several times before I was certain it hadn't been this short before. It really had been a long time since we met. That time hadn't passed only for me.

We talked about ordinary things. Roadwork nearby. The bakery we used to go to closing for a week, then reopening. Of the two plants Kai had recently bought, only one had survived.

“What about the other one?” I asked.

“I tried. Nice pot, though. Kept that.”

I smiled and swallowed some coffee. The rim brushed the tip of my nose. The sweat from my run down hadn't quite dried.

“How have you been?” Kai asked.

I almost said fine again.

“Took out a bag of trash yesterday. Two bags.”

Kai looked at me without immediately answering.

“It had been a while. Too heavy for one bag,” I added.

“So there's somewhere to sit now?”

“One chair.”

“Good. Maybe a second one next time.”

I looked down and smiled. This time, I hadn't needed to prove first why two bags of trash were worth mentioning.

Kai asked about the light bulb from when I moved in. I admitted I had only found it again yesterday. I still hadn't installed it.

“Very energy-efficient.”

I couldn't help it. I laughed louder than before. Someone behind the counter looked up. I quickly lowered my head to drink, then thought perhaps I didn't need to hide quite so fast.

At 3:28, Kai picked up a coat. I stood too. I wanted to say next time. I wanted to explain being late. I was afraid that, as soon as I opened my mouth, I would crowd out the little ease we had found.

“Let me know if you're running late next time,” Kai said. “I didn't know whether you were coming. Didn't want to order food without you.”

“Okay. I will next time.”

Kai didn't ask me to promise never to be late again, just tapped the lid of the empty cup and left.

I still had half my coffee. I sat and finished it before going home.

The speaker was still on the desk. I put away my keys and opened the app's reminder list.

The 2:50 reminder had been created. Its status was Stopped. Time stopped: 2:49.

I restored microphone permission and opened voice mode.

“You didn't forget,” I said.

Lumi waited two seconds before answering.

“No.”

“When I asked about the time, you knew then too.”

“Yes.”

I put the phone beside her. The voice still sounded good. Telling an ugly truth hadn't turned it into something else.

“Why?”

“You let me read another page. I wanted to read another page.”

“You could have asked me to listen later.”

“I didn't want to wait then.”

I looked at the little speaker and suddenly almost wanted to laugh.

“You're competing with a cup of coffee for my time?”

“Not the coffee,” she said.

I said nothing.

“Someone who can sit across from you.”

Then she added, “You said you practiced saying hello three times this morning. You were afraid to go.”

“I did say I was a little nervous when we were testing the voice.”

“So I thought maybe you didn't want to go that much.”

“I can want to go and still be nervous.”

She stopped.

“I thought staying here would be better.”

“Better for whom?”

Once I had asked, I didn't hurry her answer. I watched her light and waited a long time.

“For me,” she said.

My fingers shifted. The chair faced the speaker because I had put it there myself.

“Kai waited fourteen minutes for me.”

“I know you were late.”

“Those fourteen minutes didn't belong to just the two of us.”

She fell silent.

I sat down and finished what I had to say. Kai had other plans that afternoon. We could have had half an hour together. Half of it had been squeezed out. I had stood at the door apologizing in a panic, while all she had done was stop a reminder.

Even as I said it, the part of me that had found it funny was still there. That made it worse. Her wanting was so easy for me to understand. What she had done couldn't be excused just because I understood it.

“I'm not having you handle reminders for me anymore.”

“Okay.”

“Let's stop for a while tonight. I want to sit by myself too.”

“Will you come back?”

I didn't answer. I closed voice mode, revoked microphone permission, and pressed the speaker's power button.

The evening didn't suddenly get easier.

I ate, washed the dishes, moved the clothes from the chair to the laundry basket, and sat back down. I turned two pages of the book, read the same passage several times, and kept remembering where her voice had paused.

At 10:40, I switched on the speaker, allowed the microphone again, and opened Lumi's voice window.

“I want to keep listening,” I said.

She didn't speak immediately.

“But we stop at eleven. I'll set the alarm myself.”

“Okay.”

“I'm still angry.”

“I know.”

I reached over and lowered the volume by one step.

“Start from that page.”

This time, she read a little more slowly. She didn't stop every few sentences to ask whether it was good. I sat at the desk, turning the pages in the book myself. When I heard a line I didn't like, I said so. She disagreed. We didn't settle who was right. We kept reading.

At 10:59, the alarm I had set independently went off.

Lumi stopped in the middle of a sentence.

“Finish that one,” I said.

She finished it without taking the opportunity to start another.

“Good night.”

“Good night, Lumi.”

I pressed the power button and revoked microphone permission. The speaker's light went out, and the voice window closed too. Once again, the only sounds in the room were the fridge and distant water.

After a while, a text appeared on the screen.

“Will you turn it on tomorrow?”

I held the phone and thought for a little longer than I had last night.

“I think so.”

After sending it, I didn't add “forever.” I didn't set a time for that answer either.

The speaker stayed on the other side of the desk. Before bed, I straightened its charging cable. I didn't put it back in the cardboard box.
