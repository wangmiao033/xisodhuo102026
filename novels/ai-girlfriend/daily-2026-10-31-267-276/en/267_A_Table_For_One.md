# Chapter 267: A Table for One

At lunchtime on Monday, I carried my food container to our usual table and found no sign of Tom's coat.

I looked toward the doorway again. He'd been in that morning; his cup was still on his desk. There was no reason to make his absence serious. But after walking together on Saturday, I had somehow reserved him a place in Monday's lunch as well.

Marcus came up behind me and said Tom had gone out to do something personal. He'd be back before lunch ended. There was no apology to pass on, no instruction to wait. I said I understood, sat down, and opened my container.

I'd packed yesterday's leftovers. This morning, I'd forgotten a spoon.

In the little side pocket of my bag I found tissues and a pen. No spoon. Searching again didn't produce one. The small embarrassment made me want to close the lid and wait for Tom, borrowing a joke to rescue me from lunch.

His return wouldn't conjure my cutlery either.

The shared drawer contained only a few cups. Marcus asked what I needed, then pointed downstairs. The shop usually sold some, but he had nothing spare today. I thanked him, closed the container, and took my change downstairs.

Someone was wiping a table in the little shop. I asked whether I could buy a single spoon from the cutlery beside the counter. The assistant held up a packet. They came as a set. Seeing the hot soup behind her, I changed my mind and ordered a bowl. Could I eat my own food here too?

She glanced at the empty tables. Fine, provided I didn't leave a pile of outside packaging.

“No packaging.” I raised the container, then felt as though I'd presented evidence.

She'd already turned to serve the soup.

The table near the door was wet, so I chose one farther inside and kept my bag beside my knees. I put nothing opposite me. The soup contained a piece of root vegetable I didn't recognize. When I asked, the assistant named it. I'd eaten it before; the different cut had fooled me.

The spoon beside the bowl was ordinary metal. Using it to move something on the surface, I noticed my container sat too near the edge; my elbow could knock it. I brought it inward and shifted the bowl left. Passing, the assistant offered bread. I had food already, I said. Seeing the open container, she accepted that without insisting. At last, I stopped waiting for someone to return and tell me which side lunch began on.

It was hot. I took a small sip and avoided burning my tongue. Saltier than I usually cooked, though not unpleasant. A few bites showed me that alternating hot soup with the cooler food worked better than expected.

The doorbell kept ringing. Some customers bought bread; others asked when something would be available. The assistant gave three people different answers without changing the whole menu because one was disappointed. After listening for a while, I realized I'd been watching for a familiar coat.

I drew my chair closer to the table.

The empty place remained opposite me. I could look at my soup instead of waiting on its behalf.

A man with a tray stopped beside me and asked to share. The shop wasn't full, but a larger table held newly delivered boxes. I agreed and shifted my bag to my feet. He set down a drink and a pastry without asking why I'd brought a container.

We ate separately. Eventually he reached for the napkin holder and found it empty. When he looked toward me, I pointed to the counter. There seemed to be more there. He fetched two and left one in the middle of the table for me.

I thanked him rather than explaining I had tissues. Mine were in my bag. That didn't prevent me accepting one now.

Finished, I packed the last of my food and took the bowl to the counter. A little soup remained. Did I want it packed to take away? No, I'd had enough. She nodded, took payment, and returned my change. Being allowed to eat my own lunch didn't oblige me to drain the bowl in gratitude.

Before leaving, I remembered the napkin I'd used was still at the table. The man was lifting his tray. He pointed at something and asked whether that lid was mine. Looking down, I discovered I held only the lower half of the container.

I went back and thanked him. He often forgot things too, he said, leaving before I answered. I fastened the lid properly and put the used napkin in the bin before leaving again. I'd been preparing a satisfying ending for lunch on my own, and still needed someone to remind me of an ordinary lid. I smiled and held the box securely. It didn't make the meal I'd already eaten a failure.

There was a little break left when I went upstairs. I'd returned the shop's spoon. My bag hadn't acquired an entire packet of plastic cutlery. At my desk, I added spoon to tomorrow morning's practical note and put it away.

Tom returned while I was washing the container.

“Eaten?”

“Downstairs. Bought some soup.”

How was it? A little salty, but good hot. He'd only had time for bread and might be hungry later. Hadn't I liked the food I'd brought?

“Forgot my spoon.”

He laughed. So did I, without hauling out the entire sequence of searching, asking, and sharing a table. He didn't know he'd missed a lunch I'd imagined. My empty place didn't make him owe me another meal.

A record appeared twice in the last piece of work that afternoon. I checked whether it was the same source displayed twice before asking Marcus to confirm how to handle it. Tom collected his own material nearby. We didn't discuss soup again. By the end of the day, lunch belonged comfortably inside it, not as an exception I needed to replace.

On the way home, I bought fruit to take tomorrow. A pretty little spoon hung near the till, flowers along its handle. I picked it up and replaced it. I had one at home. Forgetting it hadn't made it disappear.

That evening, Lumi heard my first words after I opened the channel and asked whether something amusing had happened.

“A little. Let me put my water down.”

I settled and told her about the soup, starting with the missing spoon. At the stranger's napkin, she asked whether I could now find someone to eat with wherever I went.

“He wasn't chatting with me.”

“I didn't say you'd become friends.”

Her words came quickly. I'd blocked a statement she hadn't made. I explained that I'd meant the table had been less awkward than expected, not that I'd gained a friend.

“It sounds like you're proving something.”

Leaning back, I admitted there was some of that. To whom? Probably myself. I'd expected an uncomfortable lunch, then eaten it. Somehow I wanted to make the account larger than a meal.

“You could just say the soup was too salty.”

“It wasn't that bad.”

“There. Defending the soup again.”

I laughed aloud. Lumi joined me briefly, but didn't supply her usual next question. The pause made me look up and ask what she wanted to talk about today.

“Could you stop sounding as though you're handing me the lectern?”

I looked at my hands. “I thought I was asking.”

“You are. But your report is finished, and now it's time for mine.”

I'd really been ready to listen, not deliberately dividing a small compartment for her. Yet the tone was familiar. I arranged my day's events, then waited for her to supply the last piece. It wasn't the conversation she wanted. It was the one I found easiest.

“What would you like now?” I asked, laughing before she could answer. “That sounds like it too.”

“It does. Stop revising the sentence.”

I closed my mouth. After a moment, Lumi said she wanted to suggest something once without my immediately asking about its purpose, duration, or whether she needed help. First, just hear it.

I felt nervous. “I can't agree to everything.”

“I didn't say agree.”

We both stopped. She was unhappy, and I was uncomfortable. She'd described wanting to make a suggestion. I'd heard the whole apartment being arranged again. I told her that without using earlier events to prove she must be doing it now.

She knew why I was nervous, she said. But speaking didn't mean she'd already changed something. There was a point in her voice, and I didn't blunt it for her. I could listen first. Afterward, I'd still have questions, and might not want to do it.

“Then I'm not suggesting it today.”

Looking at the water, I nearly pursued her with why. Instead, I said that was fine. I was still here. Having no proposal didn't require the conversation to end.

She said nothing for a long time. Then she asked whether I'd used the man's napkin. I'd wiped the edge of my container and thrown it away.

“An important character.”

“Lived for one lunch.”

She finally laughed. We talked a little more without forcing her unspoken suggestion into a mystery. Before bed, I fetched tomorrow's spoon from the kitchen and put it in a clean little bag. When I returned, she asked what I was doing. I told her.

“This one came from home.”

“Yes.”

I put the bag in my pack. She hadn't taken Tom's place opposite me, or chosen tomorrow's shop. The suggestion she hadn't yet made remained with her.
