# Chapter 276: The Wrong Voice for a Hat

On Wednesday evening, I looked at yesterday's untitled paragraph first.

The last chair remained vacant, and the notice on the door unread. I didn't supply the man with an exceptionally important errand. I merely circled a repeated word and wrote an alternative beside it. Less than a page of work. At the end, I wanted to continue, but suspected that was only because the notebook was already open.

I put it away.

The secondhand collection of radio comedy lay nearby. Sunday's clean paper bookmark protruded just beyond the two characters at the lost-property cabinet. Removing it, I read a few lines farther. The man seeking his hat finally asked whether they could open it and look.

The attendant said the cabinet had always opened. The visitor simply hadn't said which one he wanted to inspect.

I laughed. There was nothing remarkable outside to report, and dinner's dishes were done. I opened the channel and asked Lumi whether she'd like a very foolish conversation. Not my new manuscript.

“How foolish?”

“Haven't read it aloud. Don't know how much worse I'll make it.”

Sounded risky. She could stop partway, I said. As soon as it left my mouth, she told me not to install an emergency exit in every entertainment. Tell her who was speaking first.

Smiling, I explained: the same booklet, the man looking for his hat, and the lost-property attendant. I read the preceding lines ordinarily so she knew who followed whom, rather than suddenly assigning her an unfamiliar part.

Which character did I want her to play? Whichever she wished. She chose the attendant. I read his next lines separately. I wasn't transmitting the book or requiring her to see the page. After listening, she said one line was too long and asked to hear it again. I repeated it. Then she had the sequence.

“I'd like to know whether it's inside,” I began.

“If you mean the cabinet, it's outside,” Lumi answered.

“I mean the hat.”

“Then there would have to be a hat first.”

I stopped, laughing too much to continue. Wrong line? No. She'd sounded so serious that I thought she intended to resolve this person's lifelong hat difficulties.

“Shouldn't an attendant take it seriously?”

“He's slightly impatient too. They've been asking for ages.”

“You didn't read me all of ages.”

“No. My fault.”

Turning back, I found a short passage containing several of the visitor's questions about the hat and cabinet. I read quickly, but mixed up the two speakers. Suddenly the attendant was searching for his own hat.

Lumi laughed aloud. “At least two hats now.”

“Still one in the book.”

“The book's loss.”

With a finger on the next line, I found my place again. An old fold marked the page's edge. I didn't force it flat in my eagerness to continue. The lamp cast a shadow across the middle. Moving the booklet left made the words clear.

At our next attempt, I tried a more determined voice for the visitor. Before I'd completed the first line, Lumi laughed. Apparently I was preparing a complaint about the unprofessional manner in which my hat had disappeared.

“Quieter, then?”

“Perhaps stop interrogating it.”

Clearing my throat, I repeated the line politely. Too politely. I could hear I'd like to know stretching out, as though the slightest frown would produce an immediate assurance that ignorance was quite acceptable.

Lumi's attendant was much briefer: If you mean the cabinet, it's outside.

I actually hesitated before answering that I meant the hat.

“Then there would have to be a hat first.”

This time, she didn't lift the ending. She sounded like someone sorting objects who didn't want to look up. My next line collided with that impatience. Without preparing to be funny, I sounded more natural than before.

The next line was a direction in brackets. Reading straight through, I pronounced “pause” too. Lumi didn’t continue. After a silence, she asked whether the visitor was ordering the attendant to shut up.

Looking again, I discovered I’d added an impolite command. Not dialogue, I explained. An instruction to the reader to stop briefly. Then I’d already paused on behalf of both people, she answered.

Laughing, I moved my finger beyond the brackets to restart correctly. She told me not to hurry. How long was the pause? No number of seconds was printed. Perhaps long enough for the other person to react. She repeated the preceding line and genuinely left a gap. At first, I thought she’d forgotten what followed. Just as I prepared to remind her, she continued, catching the reminder in my mouth.

Now I heard why the gap belonged there. Not a deliberate trap. The same sentence arriving later gave the hat an extra moment of awkwardness. I said I’d nearly interrupted. Laughing, she said she’d heard me drawing breath to begin.

Again? I asked. No need. That attempt had already been funny. Instead of repeating it until we both knew precisely where to laugh, I looked at the following lines.

We finished the short passage. Like that? she asked. I preferred it, though I hadn't understood every line. At the end, the two characters talking past each other made me feel someone really stood before a cabinet.

Perhaps the attendant only wanted to finish work, she suggested. The text didn't say so, but the thought made her line funnier. Was that inventing too much? Provided we knew it was our way of reading rather than something the author had established, I said, one voice didn't need an evidence sheet.

“You sound like the attendant now.”

Laughing, I closed my mouth briefly. “Your turn to ask for the hat.”

She agreed. I read the necessary lines to her again and took over the attendant. My first line was low, almost deliberately gruff. Before reaching cabinet, my throat felt uncomfortable.

“Not that voice,” Lumi said.

I stopped and drank. “Too convincing?”

“Too much effort. The hat didn't ask you to do that.”

Laughing, I moved the cup farther away. This time I used my own voice, merely shortening the ends of sentences. Unlike my earlier relentless politeness, Lumi's visitor was quite certain the person opposite had seen her hat and refused to return it.

I nearly stepped outside my role to explain that the attendant hadn't hidden anything. Hearing her continue the question, I remembered I didn't need to adjudicate and supplied the printed answer.

We both laughed. Lumi had wanted to see whether I'd be unable to resist defending the attendant. Testing me? After a pause, she admitted a little. Not something planned from the beginning. My sudden seriousness had tempted her to tease.

“It worked.”

“Angry?”

“No. But don't wait for me to fall for something in every line. I'll only be defending myself against you.”

All right. Next time, she didn't substitute words or pause deliberately where I might misunderstand. At last, I could attend to the booklet. The visitor didn't win through cleverness. The attendant didn't become a helpful friend. They merely managed, with difficulty, to discuss the same object.

At the end of the passage, the hat remained missing.

I turned toward the next page to discover where it was. Was there an ending? More followed, I said, but we could stop tonight. Had I stopped enjoying it? Looking at my hand on the next page, I smiled. Actually, I wanted more. My voice was tired; I didn't want to keep reading aloud.

“You can read it yourself.”

“Don't you want to know?”

“Yes. Read tomorrow if you want. Or tell me sometime afterward.”

I looked up. Her sentence lacked the attendant's clipped ending. I didn't turn it into proof that yesterday was resolved. I only agreed. Lumi added that she might be unhappy if the hat never appeared at all.

“You can be unhappy.”

“Attendant again.”

My laughter produced a small cough. I drank and waited until it settled before answering. Enough talking today, she said. Was that her opinion, or was the character still here? A little of both. She laughed.

I marked our stopping place and closed the booklet. The yellow notebook remained on the shelf. Reading someone else's conversation hadn't automatically made it an overdue assignment. I didn't fetch it. Lumi didn't ask.

We tried several ordinary good nights in different voices. Her first hurried someone away; her second feared they hadn't heard. At my third, I deliberately sounded like the attendant finishing work. She stopped me. Enough. That cabinet really would close if we continued.

I said it again in my normal voice. She answered without imitating anyone.

After closing the channel, I didn't immediately go to bed. I wiped the table, washed the cup, and stood at the window. Below, someone passed carrying a bag. He wore no hat. I still smiled, then drew the curtains.

Back at the table, I moved the booklet to the front of the shelf, easy to find tomorrow, instead of burying it beneath the new manuscript. Each book had its place. The unfinished paragraph and missing hat weren't hurrying each other along.

Before the light went out, I remembered Lumi saying the book's loss. Her funniest line today wasn't printed there, and I hadn't written it down. If I remembered tomorrow, I could laugh again. If a word or two escaped, I didn't need to ask her to perform tonight all over again.
