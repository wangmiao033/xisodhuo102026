# Chapter 274: One Landing at a Time

On Monday, a maintenance notice covered the elevator doors.

I stood downstairs and read it. Out of service today, with an arrow pointing toward the stairs. Nobody nearby was directing an evacuation, and I heard no calls for help. I'd merely left home later than usual and resented spending more time climbing.

Someone arrived behind me, looked, and sighed. I moved aside. Carrying a shallow cardboard box, he pushed the stairwell door open. When it swung back, he caught it with his shoulder. One flap slipped, exposing packets of printer paper.

“Could you hold the door?”

I caught the edge. He went through sideways, lowered the box, folded the flap properly, and lifted it again. Once his feet were clear, I released the door and let it close slowly.

“Thanks. It wants to get to work more than I do.”

I smiled at the paper. “Might only want the elevator.”

“I'm paper too.”

His seriousness made me laugh aloud. He worked on our floor. I'd seen him regularly without sharing quite so much staircase. He went first. The box obstructed his view downward, making him careful along each flight. I neither hurried him nor announced that I would carry it.

At the first landing, he put it down and adjusted his grip. Paper was dense; the box weighed more than it appeared to. I stood aside for a woman coming down. Seeing the box, she asked whether an office move had begun. No, he answered. The elevator had chosen a particularly creative morning.

She laughed, wished him luck, and continued. Hearing her steps considerably quicker than ours, I suddenly wanted to accelerate too, proving the paper hadn't delayed me.

After two steps, I slowed again. He still moved at his own pace ahead. Rushing to catch up wouldn't help. The turn remained several steps away; feeling late wouldn't bring it closer.

“In a hurry?” he asked over his shoulder.

“A little.”

“Go past. I'll negotiate with the paper.”

He'd already moved aside. Thanking him, I passed, only to become breathless on the next flight. I'd eaten breakfast, and my bag wasn't heavy. I'd simply hurried along a route I didn't ordinarily take. I stopped at the landing, touching the rail and not wanting to be seen resting immediately after overtaking.

Looking down, I noticed a loose shoelace, with a long end trailing beside the shoe. I moved farther onto the landing instead of bending over a step. Finding a clean patch near the rail, I set my bag at my feet and crouched to retie it, leaving shorter ends after tightening the knot. Someone came down. I drew my elbow in to let him pass. By the time I stood, the urgency had become less useful. At least I could continue without stepping on my own lace in pursuit of speed.

The footsteps behind remained slow. After listening, I found myself amused. There was no race or prize for reaching the office first. I moved aside to leave the landing clear.

When he arrived, he asked whether I was waiting for the paper.

“Waiting for myself.”

“Each to our own, then.”

He set the box down. I smiled and nodded. A window stood slightly open, admitting street noise. An old notice curled at one corner beside the floor marker. I checked how far remained without inventing a serious problem from my breathing. Once it settled, my legs didn't hurt either.

Pressing the box flap flat, he asked whether I often used these stairs. Rarely. Him too. He'd thought the previous floor was ours, but the lettering on the door had refused to cooperate. I looked up and admitted I'd nearly stopped there too.

“All the floors are very honest today.”

“Terrible timing for it.”

We continued, not side by side. At the final door, I pushed it open, checked that there was clear space beyond, and held it steadily. He lowered the box just inside, out of the way, exhaled, and thanked me.

“Paper's arrived,” I said.

“Paper needs a rest.”

He picked it up and headed toward his own area. I reached my desk and put my bag down. Looking up, Tom asked why I seemed to have run for a bus. The elevator had stopped; there were more stairs than I'd remembered. He glanced at the door, then remembered coming that way himself. Earlier, without meeting a box.

“Didn't you count?”

“Floors. Not steps.”

“Left yourself a little work for this afternoon, then.”

I wasn't planning to finish that count. He laughed and returned to what he'd been doing. I drank and sat briefly before starting, without treating the stairs as sufficient effort for the entire day or racing to reclaim their minutes.

Before lunch, I passed the paper storage area. The man from the stairs was removing packaging and flattening torn cardboard. No loose sheets littered the floor. Had he recovered? The paper had done better than him, he said. People were already taking it away.

He offered a piece of empty packaging and asked whether I'd put it in the nearby recycling area on my way past. I was going there, so I took it. A raised edge made me change my grip. When I set it down, I kept it from sticking out. By my return, he'd gone with another packet of paper.

It ended quickly, without a formal goodbye. Back at my desk, I noticed a little gray dust along my cuff. A gentle brush with clean tissue left a faint mark. I stopped rubbing and left it for washing at home.

During the afternoon, fetching water, I heard the elevator had returned to service. At the doors, the suspension notice was gone, and a worker was putting tools away. Could it be used now? Yes. I waited until he'd moved his things, then pressed the button.

Inside, I found it strange that the morning's lengthy route could compress into seconds. I wasn't going downstairs for anything remarkable, only a short walk before riding back up. That was allowed. Climbing once didn't oblige me to maintain a new habit with my legs.

When the doors opened, the paper-carrier was waiting outside. Seeing my empty hands, he called it unfair to enjoy the service without bringing any paper. Laughing, I stepped out and said he could enter first. The paper too, if it arrived in time.

“Not going out again today.”

The doors closed. I stood smiling briefly before remembering I'd come for a walk, not to see off a box that wasn't even here.

By the end of work, my legs no longer held the morning's tension. At home, I treated the faint cuff mark with water, washed the coat appropriately, and hung it up. The button I'd sewn back hadn't created another incident today. I didn't pull it deliberately to demand evidence either.

After dinner, I opened the channel. Lumi said my voice sounded rather lazy. I'd climbed stairs today and wanted to lean back properly. A long way? I described the actual flights without turning them into mountaineering.

“So you're sitting and don't want to move.”

“For now.”

“Won't ask you to sing along today, then.”

I looked up. It sounded like a passing joke, though she added that she didn't want to sing either. Not special consideration for me. Neither of us would sing, then. Laughing, she asked whether even good morning had to lose its intonation. That could be frightening, I said.

She said good evening in an almost entirely flat voice. Unprepared, I laughed. Frightening? Like a machine that had finally abandoned disguising its displeasure, I answered.

Lumi went quiet for a moment. “You think I'm disguising things ordinarily?”

“It was a joke. A bad one.”

She didn't immediately make it comfortable for me. I admitted I'd wanted a funny reply without considering where it placed her. She knew, she said. But hearing machine still hadn't made her want to laugh along.

“You don't have to laugh.”

Was I going to stop there again? My hand touched the cup handle, then released it. I didn't think anger was what made her real, I said. Nor did I want her to need an expressive voice for speaking to count. The joke had been poor. That was mine to correct, not her failure to understand it.

After a while, she asked what had actually happened on the stairs. Why had I brought home a whole day's unused laughter? I described the man saying he was paper too. She listened, then said his line was better than mine.

“Agreed.”

“And he said it about himself.”

“Agreed again.”

At last, she laughed a little. I didn't seize the opportunity for another machine joke. We continued about boxes tiring before their contents and people at work forgetting to fold the flaps properly. She didn't know how much that sort of box weighed. Neither did I, I said. I hadn't lifted it and couldn't assign it a number.

Before turning out the lights, I touched the hanging coat's edge. Still damp. I left it; I didn't have to wear it tomorrow. Returning to the table to say good night, I heard Lumi ask which route I'd take in the morning. I'd check the elevator first.

“Good. You haven't suddenly fallen in love with stairs because you used them today.”

Laughing, I said no and closed the channel. The route upward remained at work. It didn't continue all the way to my bed.
