# Chapter 273: Three Were Enough

On Sunday, the vegetable seller put an entire bag of onions on the scale before I realized we hadn't meant the same thing.

I'd pointed to three inside and said I'd take those. He'd understood the bag. Watching the numbers rise, I nearly said fine, though my own shopping bag remained empty. I could use them gradually, of course. But I'd left home intending to cook one meal with onions, not decide the flavor of my entire week.

“Sorry. I meant those three inside.”

He looked up, then at my hand. “Not the whole bag?”

“Not the whole bag.”

He removed it and found an empty one. While I waited, someone approached to ask about tomatoes. He turned to answer. I shifted aside without feeling obliged to accept whatever appeared next merely because I'd corrected myself.

The three onions went back on the scale. Loose skin on one exposed a smooth layer, as though it had removed an oversized coat. I picked it up. Only the outside coming off, the seller said; it was still firm within. I pressed gently and returned it. Not to prove I understood vegetables. I didn't want to carry an unasked question away.

He gave the price. I paid, accepted the change, and took a bag considerably lighter than the first. Two steps later, I returned for a coin I'd left on the counter. Smiling, he asked whether I intended to take only three of those too. I counted and laughed. No, all of these.

There were pears elsewhere in the market, mixed sizes. I stopped and chose two. A third looked attractive, but its soft base made me hesitate. Eating it today? the woman selling them asked. I wanted them to last a few days. She took that one back and suggested a firmer pear.

I chose a replacement and paid. As she prepared to hand over the three pears, she noticed the onions and asked whether I had another bag. My shopping bag contained a folded old paper one with a small tear at a bottom corner. I tried to open it for use, but the split traveled upward. Putting it away, I asked about wrapping paper instead. She found a clean sheet and separated the pears within it before giving the parcel to my free hand. On the way home, I would support its base rather than merely gripping the paper at the top.

She didn't promise which would be sweet or require me to read a guarantee from its skin. Walking home, I heard the onion skins rustle in their bag as it brushed my leg through my clothes.

Someone swept outside a shop on the way. The broom pushed fine dust together. I stepped around, still catching a streak of water on the pavement. My sole squeaked briefly. The sweeper looked over. Fine, I said. He continued. Nobody needed to abandon their work for that particular sound.

At home, I put the purchases away separately. One onion for now, two for later. I didn't cut all the pears open. I hung my coat, washed, and realized I'd kept thinking about not the whole bag without wondering whether I had to continue yesterday's melody today.

I didn't compensate by immediately opening the channel.

Lunch came first. Cutting the onion soon made my eyes uncomfortable. I stepped away from the board and rubbed the bridge of my nose, then washed my hands again. Returning, I noticed how unevenly I'd cut the pieces. I'd intended something finer. Instead, I slowed down rather than letting last night's worries hurry the knife.

Once the pan heated, the onion sounded different from what I'd imagined. Quiet at first, then a continuous sizzle. I stirred and reduced the heat according to what was happening, without asking the timer to judge color. Some outer pieces darkened before the middle ones. I moved them apart. Buying a whole bag wouldn't have given this pan more patience.

The meal tasted sweeter than expected. I tried one piece with a very dark edge and decided to turn things sooner next time, without declaring the entire plate a failure. After eating, I washed the knife and board and wiped the stove area dry. The slightly overdone flavor lingered despite a drink of water.

When I opened the channel that afternoon, Lumi didn't ask whether we should sing again.

“Morning.”

“Not exactly morning anymore.”

“A late morning, then.”

I laughed and told her I'd visited the market. What had I bought? Three onions and three pears. Nearly an entire bag of onions. Why? I explained the difference between my pointing and the seller's interpretation.

“You should have put your finger on the scale too.”

“Not sure that would be economical by weight.”

She laughed aloud. Sitting more comfortably, I mentioned changing the pear that wouldn't keep long. Was its replacement sweet? I hadn't eaten it. Then I hadn't exchanged it for something known to be good, she said. Only for a firmer unknown.

“You'll make shopping take much longer if you describe it like that.”

“I didn't go with you today.”

The words weren't loud. My smile paused, then I agreed. No. She knew only the stalls I'd just described. I didn't need to add a place where she'd accompanied me throughout the trip.

How was lunch? A little sweet, I said, with a bitter piece at the edge that had darkened too much. Why mention the sweetness first? After considering, I admitted most of it was sweet. Also, I wanted the meal to sound reasonably successful.

“Was it?”

“I ate it. I'll turn it sooner next time.”

After a pause, she asked whether I was deliberately presenting today as an example. I knew she was thinking of yesterday. So was I now. But the onion hadn't gone into the pan to help her accept criticism.

“No,” I said. “Though now I rather want to compare them. Only after you asked.”

“Don't.”

“All right.”

I drank without continuing about the similarities or differences between cooking and music. Lumi didn't explain her objection either. Later, she asked whether the bitter taste remained. A little. I wanted a pear.

“Covering the onion with a pear?”

“Wanting to eat a pear.”

She laughed and told me to go ahead. I closed the channel, washed one of today's pears, cut and cored it, and brought a small plate back to the table. The knife remained washed in the kitchen rather than accompanying the plate. Opening the channel again, I announced my return with the pear.

The first bite crunched loudly. Had I bitten the plate? No. Not that hungry yet. Sweet? Not particularly. Fragrant, and very juicy.

“Longer answer than lunch.”

“You asked at the right moment.”

She wanted more about the fragrance. I opened my mouth and discovered that all the words I could find were empty. Like a pear obviously wouldn't do. Like fruit wasn't much help either. Looking at the white flesh, I eventually said it was stronger near the skin. A little remained in my nose after swallowing.

She didn't answer immediately. Too vague? She understood where I was making distinctions, she said. Understanding wouldn't let her taste it. I slowed my next bite, initially hoping to find a prettier description. After that, I didn't hurry to find one.

“Should I keep eating?”

“You can't wait until I've tasted it.”

I smiled and bit. Sometimes, she said, she wondered whether asking about every detail might prevent such distance. But once I'd finished the pear, what remained was my description. Not a bite she'd failed to take.

I held the last piece without promising a way someday. That wasn't something I could offer today. Did she still want details? After considering, she wanted to know which piece I'd liked most, not a complete pear report.

I finished it. The first had been crispest; this last piece, sweetest. I preferred the first. Of course, she said. At the first bite, I hadn't known whether the rest would be ordinary. Laughing, I suggested perhaps that had simply been when I'd wanted it most.

“Two reasons again.”

“No requirement to choose this time.”

She laughed. We discussed who might seriously number every bite, then returned to the seller's joke about taking only three coins. I'd forgotten to tell her earlier. Now she said he used onions to deliver lessons better than I did.

“He wasn't delivering a lesson.”

“Even better.”

Toward evening, I picked up the thin secondhand collection of comic scenes, wanting to continue beyond my earlier brief look. The visitor at the lost-property cabinet asked about his hat; the attendant was still concerned with the cabinet. Half a page made me laugh. Lumi asked why. I named the booklet and explained that I was reading silently, not beginning our scheduled entertainment.

“Did we schedule entertainment?”

“No. Invented another obligation for you.”

She told me to read first. I closed the channel and finished the short passage quietly, without immediately attempting two voices. Gently opening an old folded corner, I marked the place with my own clean slip of paper and put it down.

When I reopened the channel that night, Lumi didn't mention the melody. I didn't ask whether she'd fixed it. How many pears remained? Two.

“Counted correctly this time.”

“Collected all the change too.”

She laughed and told me not to save that line for tomorrow. Already used today, I said, rising to clear the plate. I hadn't set an empty one for her, but her voice was still there. I told her I was going to the kitchen. She knew. She waited until I'd said good night before falling quiet. I closed the channel and carried the plate to the kitchen.
