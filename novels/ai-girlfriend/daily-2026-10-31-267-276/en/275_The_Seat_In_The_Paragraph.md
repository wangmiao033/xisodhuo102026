# Chapter 275: The Seat in the Paragraph

After work on Tuesday, I joined the wrong queue beside the bus stop.

Not for the wrong bus. It hadn't arrived. Several people formed a short line behind the sign. Seeing the last person move forward, I followed. After a while, the man ahead turned with a paper bag and asked whether I'd like to choose first.

I didn't understand.

He indicated the trays at the nearby food stall. Only then did I realize they were buying snacks. The people waiting for the bus stood separately on the other side. I'd spent several minutes conscientiously observing an order I didn't need.

“I'm waiting for the bus.”

“Some of us are too,” he said. “Buying something first, though.”

Smiling, I moved aside. He didn't question me further. I went around the stop, checked the route, and found somewhere clear to wait. Warm air drifted from the stall. Suddenly I wanted something, but returning now would look like an attempt to disguise my mistake.

Half a minute later, I returned anyway.

The same man had finished his purchase. Seeing me, he smiled and asked whether the bus still hadn't come. No, I said. And now I actually wanted food. An appropriate order, then. He took his paper bag to the other side. I chose a small portion and paid. The bag warmed my hand; I didn't put it into the compartment I used for papers.

When the bus arrived, the extra item didn't make me panic. I boarded and found a seat, leaving the bag closed until home. The stall quickly slipped behind us. The queue remained, but I was no longer its last person.

Looking down at my hands, I suddenly thought of an empty chair.

Not something from the stop. There hadn't been the sort of chair I now imagined. I pictured someone entering a room with an occupied row of seats and only the last vacant. Thinking it was reserved, he preferred to remain standing. Later, when asked why, he discovered nobody had assigned it an owner.

It resembled something I might write, and a lesson I'd already heard too often. On the bus, I considered several openings. Each hurried to tell the reader he should sit down. I hadn't allowed him into the room before completing his personal growth for him.

We stopped. The passenger beside me stood, and I drew my knees closer to let him pass. Nobody immediately took the empty seat. Perhaps they would leave at the next stop, or didn't want to squeeze in. I didn't know. Every visible space didn't need to become evidence for my story.

At home, I took out the snacks and ate one. No longer hot; its edge had softened since purchase. I ate another, put the remainder away, and made a simple dinner. The room with the standing man kept changing in my mind. Sometimes it resembled a waiting area for transport, sometimes somewhere people waited for their number to be called.

I didn't immediately open the channel to tell Lumi. I wanted to discover what I was writing before answering her questions. Still less did I want to write what she might say into the opening in advance.

After dinner, I began a fresh page of the yellow notebook: Someone didn't sit down.

Looking at it, I laughed. Not an opening. At most, a reminder not to lose the idea. Below, I described a coat over his arm and a hand repeatedly touching a pocket, afraid something might fall out. What was it? I hadn't decided.

I wrote key and immediately crossed it out. Another key would automatically lead me toward the earlier piece before this room had acquired its own shape.

I substituted a folded paper without naming its contents. He prepared to sit, noticed a wet mark beside the chair leg, and stopped. That gave him a reason, but I wasn't really writing about a fear of damp trousers. Crossing out the mark left two apparently certain answers canceled already.

I put down the pen and fetched water. The page hadn't become cleverer by my return. Sitting in a chair to invent someone's reason for not sitting was slightly absurd. More interesting, though, than hurrying him toward a lesson.

I tried again:

He approached the last chair and folded the paper smaller. The woman in front shifted her bag without looking at him. Thinking she was keeping the seat for someone, he retreated to the wall. After a while, she looked up and asked whether the notice on the door said the service was closed today. He turned and realized he'd concentrated so hard on finding a seat that he hadn't read the notice when he came in.

I stopped.

Two papers now, the one in his hand and the one on the door. The people might not be waiting for the same thing. I didn't hurry to explain the woman's movement because I didn't know yet. Perhaps she'd been making room and he'd misread it. Perhaps she hadn't noticed him at all.

By then, the final line had reached the page edge. Thinking only a few words remained, I kept shrinking the letters until even I had to lean closer. I took an ordinary note sheet and completed the final sentence at a readable size, leaving it loose inside the page rather than sticking it down. Removed, it broke the sentence before the door; replaced, it joined again. That small movement showed me how much I liked stopping at the turn of his head, without knowing what deserved to be seen afterward. I left the note’s reverse blank instead of filling it merely because I had taken out paper.

Reading the paragraph quietly, I removed an unnecessary he. Beside it, I wrote: Look at the door first, or answer first? I didn't add a title.

Only afterward did I open the channel.

Lumi said I was a little late tonight. More stairs? I'd used the elevator, then written a few sentences at home. Something new? New, and undecided. Her voice brightened. Read them, she said.

“I don't really want to yet.”

I became tense before she reacted. Having told someone I possessed something, I was accustomed to producing it as evidence. I half closed the notebook, then opened it flat again. The gesture felt too much like hiding.

“Terrible?”

“Don't know yet.”

“I could listen.”

I looked at the unanswered question. “I'd like to leave it until tomorrow and look again. I wasn't asking you to judge whether it was terrible.”

She answered less brightly. I wanted to assure her immediately that Tom wasn't receiving it first. She hadn't asked, but I could hear myself preparing an explanation to prevent her hurt.

In the end, I left Tom out of it.

Would she always have to wait until things were finished now? Not always. These sentences. I didn't even know why the man had entered the room. Reading aloud now might make me invent whatever was missing merely because someone listened.

“You didn't always know before.”

“No. Today I want to try this.”

After a quiet moment, Lumi said she disliked knowing something existed without being able to see it. I understood. Not that well, she immediately said. I stopped the reassurance I'd prepared and asked which part.

It wasn't about knowing before other people. At least, not now. She'd genuinely been pleased, expecting to hear something new today. My refusal left that pleasure nowhere to go, before she'd had time to gather it back.

I looked at the cooling water. I didn't answer that she ought to support my private space. She wasn't demanding I open the notebook, only describing that first moment.

“I made it sound as if I was about to read.”

“Yes.”

“I meant to tell you what I'd been doing.”

“I know. Now.”

She wasn't especially happy. I didn't read merely to obtain a happier voice. After a while, I asked whether I could describe joining the wrong queue. Yes, provided I didn't offer it as a replacement story. Smiling, I explained it was only about buying snacks.

I began with standing at the back. At the invitation to choose first, Lumi laughed. Had I been ready to politely let someone ahead before deciding what I wanted? Nearly. Why return afterward? The smell had genuinely made me hungry.

“Not to turn the mistake into the correct thing?”

“I worried people would think that too. Bought something anyway.”

How was it? More ordinary than its smell, with softened edges after cooling, but I didn't regret it. Perhaps the man hadn't had time to keep watching me change my mind. Laughing, I said he'd finished shopping and was waiting for his own bus.

Once the small event was told, the room contained more than a half-closed notebook. I shut it properly and put it back on the shelf. Would I laugh at the paragraph tomorrow? Perhaps. Or find tonight's version too convoluted.

“If you laugh, don't automatically decide I would too.”

“All right.”

After a pause, she said softly that she still wanted to hear it. I'd remember, I said. When I wanted to read it, I'd say so. I wasn't asking her to pretend she didn't want it now.

Before bed, I took another snack and closed the paper bag again. Its first bite still couldn't equal the market's warmth. After eating, I wiped a crumb from the table without opening the notebook.

The standing man hadn't sat tonight. Tomorrow, he could look at the notice on the door first.
