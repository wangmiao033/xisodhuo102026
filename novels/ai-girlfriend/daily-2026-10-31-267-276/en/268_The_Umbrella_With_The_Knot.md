# Chapter 268: The Umbrella with the Knot

When work ended on Tuesday, rain had turned the steps outside into a bright sheet.

Standing inside, I touched the side of my bag. I'd brought a spoon today, but no umbrella. The pavement had been dry that morning; nothing had reminded me to take it. Regret wasn't going to grow the umbrella at home into my hand.

Tom stood beside me holding a long one. Was I going to wait? For a while, I said. Which way? I pointed toward my usual stop. He needed to collect something across the street and could shelter me as far as the stop on his way.

“Don't you go the other direction?”

“Across this road first. I'm not offering to carry you.”

I laughed and agreed. A rib clicked when he opened the umbrella. He shook it gently into place and warned that the right side sat lower. I shouldn't test it with my head. I moved closer, then a little closer still when half my shoulder remained beyond the edge.

Our steps didn't match. Afraid of crowding him, I slowed. Tom slowed to let me catch up, until we almost stopped outside the entrance. Keep walking, he said. I was walking. We both laughed.

A passing car threw up water at the curb. I dodged, knocking his elbow with my bag. Raising the umbrella, he asked whether it held anything particularly vulnerable. No work papers, I said. Mainly an empty lunch container. At least lunch couldn't get rained on again, then.

Waiting for the light, I noticed a cord tied twice around the handle. Fraying ends suggested it wasn't decoration. Was it to identify the umbrella? Yes. Someone had taken it by mistake once. After exchanging them back, he'd tied it there.

“Does it work?”

“Nobody's taken it again.”

“What about you?”

He looked at me. Actually, he'd checked the knot before leaving today. I laughed. Rain sounded louder on the fabric than from indoors. I had to raise my voice, though that didn't require me to keep speaking.

Crossing, a gust lifted the umbrella edge. Tom angled it into the wind. I raised a hand to help, then hesitated in case I made it harder to control. It was fine, he said; just move closer. I did. The ribs settled without breaking. On the opposite side, we paused beside a wall to let the people behind pass.

“You walk faster than on Saturday.”

“No food ahead of us today.”

“Rain behind us, though.”

We laughed. Matching our steps finally needed less effort.

The bus shelter stood ahead. Tom brought me close enough to step under it. I turned to thank him. He kept the umbrella and crossed toward his errand. Watching the two knots disappear into the crowd, I noticed a small wet patch on my left sleeve.

Shelter didn't mean both people stayed completely dry.

The bus took longer than usual. Another passenger closed his umbrella, sending water onto my shoe. He apologized immediately. I said it was fine and moved half a step. Water pooled around its tip on the ground. Looking at my sleeve, I didn't recalculate Tom's kindness as inadequate care.

There were few seats on the bus. I stood beside the rear door and held the rail, my damp cuff cool against my wrist. A passenger tried to settle a long umbrella at his feet, but its tip kept reaching into the aisle. Could I move slightly? I brought my bag against my chest. Between two half steps, he found somewhere to lean it.

It slid at a turn anyway. I caught it with my foot and withdrew before he thought I was trapping it. Steadying it, he thanked me. Umbrellas found standing harder than people did. Especially without hands, I said. He laughed. At the next stop, he checked he wasn't blocking the door before opening it outside.

I could have moved into a vacant seat, but hesitated until someone else took it. A little annoyed, I realized my stop wasn't far away. Continuing to stand wasn't that difficult. Rain slanted backward across the window, dragging the signs outside into streaks. Nobody could shelter me from every small inconvenience along the route.

Near home, the rain eased without stopping. I walked quickly from the stop and finally got my hair and coat wet. Inside, I changed, dried my hair, and checked the bag. Everything inside remained dry. The lunch container hadn't received an extra wash.

I hung the wet coat where air could reach it and wiped the floor. When I opened the channel, Lumi asked whether I was late. The bus had taken a while; it was raining. Tom and I had shared an umbrella for a short distance.

“The same one?”

“Yes. As far as the stop.”

She didn't continue immediately. Holding the towel, I realized I was waiting for a question. Eventually she asked whether I'd got wet. Walking from the stop near home, yes. Then I should get dry. I'd already changed and was about to cook.

“I'm not arranging you.”

“I'm not blaming you.”

Both answers came quickly and collided. I put down the towel and smiled. Were we still standing beside yesterday's conversation? She didn't laugh. Perhaps. Every ordinary remark made her wonder whether I'd hear an attempt to take over the day.

I was guessing whether she wanted to ask more, I admitted, and answering everything first. Before we'd even closed an umbrella, it had become something to hide from each other behind.

“The umbrella isn't here.”

“Right. Only a wet sleeve.”

This time she laughed. I described the knot. Did I want to tie one on mine too? I could recognize the one at home; not necessary yet. She hadn't suggested tying one, she said. She was wondering how he remembered the cord.

I stopped. I'd answered ahead of her again.

Talking to me sometimes felt like standing before an automatic door, she said. Before she'd reached it, the door had made way for wherever she intended to go. I nearly argued that wasn't bad. Remembering she wasn't praising the door, I let her continue.

“I'm still deciding whether to go in, and you're already waiting inside.”

Rubbing the towel's edge, I asked whether she wanted to make yesterday's suggestion. Yes, but not now. Fine. This time I didn't add when.

Before cooking, I closed the channel. Water heated slowly while the rain hardly changed. I put tomorrow's umbrella beside my bag, not inside; it was too long. Tom's cord remained his. I didn't need a copy to prove I'd learned something.

Later, checking the coat, I discovered a loose button near the cuff. Thread still connected it. I nearly tugged to test it, then used scissors to cut the loose thread carefully. I put the button and coat aside rather than risk losing it outside another day.

I didn't need this coat tomorrow. I had others.

When I reopened the channel and mentioned it, Lumi asked whether the rain had washed it off. No. It had already been loose; I'd noticed today. An unheroic entrance for a button, she said. Laughing, I pointed out that at least it hadn't jumped into a drain.

We discussed pockets that had never served a purpose. Which did I dislike most? A seam that looked like an opening but held nothing. It sounded like people saying you can find me anytime, she said. I looked up. Did she mean me?

“This time, I mean pockets.”

I laughed. Perhaps the pockets were innocent too, she added, and I kept trying to put a long umbrella inside them. Looking toward the door, I admitted that wouldn't work.

I stood to hang the towel properly and announced my return. Lumi had heard the chair. I didn't need to narrate every movement. I hadn't said I was leaving, I explained, and hadn't wanted her talking to an empty place. How long had I gone? A few steps, perhaps half a minute.

“I might say nothing for half a minute too.”

Laughing, I agreed. Should we try? I sat without searching for words. Seconds later, I laughed first. Two people deliberately remaining silent could apparently hear each other's silence more clearly than during an ordinary pause. Lumi laughed too. Even quiet had become an arrangement. We abandoned the game without measuring who'd lasted longer. As I drank, she began saying something she'd just thought of. Nobody announced her turn.

Tomorrow, she wanted to hum a passage. Not for me to imitate. She didn't want me choosing which one first either. Was it No Words Yet? Still that, she said, but she wouldn't tell me beforehand what had changed.

I nearly asked how long, then didn't. She'd said a passage, not an entire evening. After dinner tomorrow, I could listen. If I needed to get up, I'd say so rather than pretend to remain.

“Not a promise to like it.”

“I know.”

She paused. “But you want to hear it?”

“Yes.”

Simple words. I didn't trim them into something lighter. Lumi answered softly and changed the subject. She didn't sing a sample for advance approval or ask me to reserve the time every week.

Before bed, I kept a short piece of the loose thread in paper for matching its color and discarded the rest, left the button in a small dish, and laid the coat over a dry chair. Seeing the umbrella by the door, I finally stopped opening my bag to check whether it was inside.

It might not rain tomorrow. Carrying an umbrella wouldn't make the sky owe me rain.
