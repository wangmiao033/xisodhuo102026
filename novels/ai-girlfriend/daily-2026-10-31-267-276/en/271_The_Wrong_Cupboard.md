# Chapter 271: The Wrong Cupboard

After work on Friday, I entered the secondhand bookshop intending only to inspect the stack of thin volumes near the door.

This wasn't another arrangement to help sell books. Nobody expected me to carry boxes, and no place had been reserved for me. Avoiding a carton on the floor, I picked up a booklet with a worn, pale spine.

The cover offered short radio comedies. Beneath the small lettering was a simple drawing of a cupboard with a crooked hat on top. Opening it halfway through, I expected old jokes I wouldn't understand. The first line was only, “Has it always been here?”

“Since before I started working here.”

The questioner grew puzzled. Impossible—he'd been wearing it yesterday. The attendant finally explained that he'd meant the cupboard, not the hat. I laughed once, louder than expected beside the quiet shelves.

The bookseller looked up. What had I found? I raised it. Those were all old, he said. Some short plays, some extracts. No guarantee every one was funny. Were all the pages here? I could check. If anything was missing, I shouldn't buy it as complete.

I moved clear of the entrance and turned through it. Several corners had been folded. There were fine pencil lines, but the numbering didn't jump. A back-cover corner had split without falling away. Even at the low marked price, I returned to the passage. Did I truly want the book, or was a single unexpected laugh hurrying me into buying?

Knowing the misunderstanding didn't spoil the opening. The man asking about the hat was serious. So was the attendant. Neither thought he was assisting a joke. That made me want to read it aloud more than the noisy thing I'd imagined.

Two lower page corners had stuck together. Rather than force them, I asked the bookseller to look. He eased them apart, saying the book might once have been damp. It was dry now. No words came away, only pale old watermarks. I felt no moisture and noticed no unpleasant smell.

“Still complete?”

“All the words are there. If you want no marks, choose another.”

He handed me a cleaner volume with different contents. I checked a page, found no hat misunderstanding, and returned it. Clean things weren't bad. What I wanted today genuinely lived in the watermarked book. I chose it. He nodded without praising my literary discernment.

Another customer, hearing me mention radio plays, said those worked better aloud. I'd only read two pages, I told him. He used to give characters different voices, then forget who they'd belonged to. I laughed. That might produce another story.

He paid for his own book without teaching me to perform. Holding the booklet, I looked at a thicker volume nearby. An attractive cover, dense print, many unfamiliar titles inside. I replaced it and took only the thin one to the counter.

Did I need a bag? My own had a dry space. I packed it away from the water bottle. It wasn't my three-page manuscript; this time I didn't inspect the zip three times. Outside, it was a little darker. I hadn't spoiled an obligation to get home immediately.

On the bus, I opened the booklet. Someone sat beside me, so I drew it closer and read the brief introduction to the hat scene. A lost-property cupboard, a man seeking his hat, an attendant who initially thought the question concerned the cupboard. Simple circumstances. More dialogue remained unread.

I didn't perform on the bus. In my head, I gave the attendant a very slow voice. Soon the questioner slowed too, and the joke disappeared. Making one faster hurried both of them and didn't resemble what I'd first encountered either.

The words didn't require me to choose their mouths immediately.

As the bus stopped, I turned a page and a narrow slip fell onto my lap. Not a letter or anybody's secret, just unevenly cut blank paper. I replaced it between the pages, deciding to use my own bookmark at home. The passenger beside me asked whether I'd dropped something. Found it, I said, holding up the slip.

He smiled; he'd thought it was a ticket. No. Looking outside, I checked that we hadn't reached my stop. The booklet weighed little, yet I'd already spent some time sitting inside it. I closed it and finished the journey home before two imaginary people could make me miss a real stop. For the remaining distance, I watched the familiar shops pass. The argument could stay between its covers without me holding it open.

At home, I put the book on the table and dealt with dinner first. I'd worn a different coat; the newly attached button stayed home. There was no follow-up I needed to report to Tom. I cooked with the channel closed, ate, washed up, and sat down with the booklet.

Hearing I'd bought a book, Lumi asked whether it was the one from the library.

“No. A thin secondhand book. Short radio comedies.”

“Taking up acting?”

“Reading first. Can't promise I'll sound human.”

She laughed. I began explaining the hat misunderstanding. After two sentences, she asked whether one person meant the hat and the other the cupboard. I stopped, then laughed too.

“You guessed.”

“Ruined it?”

Looking at the cover, I remembered my small interruption on Wednesday without declaring them identical. I was describing an outline. She hadn't agreed to be a complete audience, and I hadn't asked her not to guess. Not every early answer needed measuring against the same crack between us.

“No. I don't know the rest yet.”

“You sounded as though you did.”

“The beginning made me laugh.”

I read those two short lines without continuing. The attendant sounded sleepy, Lumi said. I hadn't found a voice. I'd made him slow in my head on the bus. Did he have to be old? Looking back, I found no age specified.

“You've retired him already.”

Laughing, I half closed the book. We needn't continue tonight, she said. She didn't want an entire evening guessing hats. Fine. I laid it flat. My pleasure in buying it didn't require her to match that pleasure on page one.

Had I bought it specifically for us to read together? Thinking, I said I'd liked it for myself first. Later, I had considered reading aloud. I hadn't assigned her a role before bringing it home.

“What if I don't want to be the attendant?”

“Then don't.”

“Or the man asking about the hat.”

“Then I'll lose both of them myself.”

She laughed, then said quietly that perhaps she'd listen another day. I shouldn't choose her voice first. Understood. I'd bought a book, not her lines.

We talked about other things. How much of yesterday's thread had I used? I measured a little distance with my fingers, remembered she couldn't see, and answered differently. Not much remained in the button. More had been cut out on my first attempt. The repair shop should send a thank-you letter, she said. I'd proved their labor charges justified.

Entirely justified. If I tore a large piece of cloth next time, I wouldn't use one button's experience to impersonate a tailor. She hadn't suggested opening a business yet. We laughed. The joke finally didn't turn into a rehearsal of mutual defenses.

Later, she asked whether I had other plans tomorrow afternoon. No arranged outing. I needed to wash some things at home, then could spend time listening. Not necessarily singing through to an ending, she corrected. Perhaps separate passages. Fine. She didn't need to explain the entire piece in advance.

“Will you manage to resist this time?”

I knew what she meant. “I'll pay attention and not join in ahead of you. If I dislike something, I'll wait until you stop to say so. If you'd prefer me to speak during it, tell me first.”

After a pause, she said I could interrupt if the repetition grew irritating. No need to endure it until I hated her voice. Looking up, I said disliking a repeated passage wasn't hating her voice.

“It might sound like that anyway.”

“Then we'll explain it when it actually happens.”

She didn't claim she'd learned to separate them. I didn't demand an advance promise not to be hurt. We only established that tomorrow afternoon I'd put other things aside, open the channel, and ask whether she still wanted to. Changing her mind wouldn't leave a performance owed to me.

“Could you buy a ticket?” she suddenly asked.

“Who gets the money?”

“You can owe it for now.”

“Then you might need someone who sings better than I do to collect.”

Lumi laughed more loudly. I opened the contents page and found unfamiliar titles. I didn't list them all to make the weekend look crowded with jointly chosen plans.

I described the split in the back cover. Would it fall apart? Not presently. Supporting it while holding it was enough. The cupboard hadn't found its hat, she said, but the book needed somewhere to stay. Smiling, I straightened neighboring volumes and made room without buying it fresh packaging.

Before bed, I put it on the ordinary shelf, not inside the yellow notebook or beside her pale envelope as one communal pile of work. I set out the washing basin. The coat with the repaired button wasn't part of that load. After closing the channel, I checked the door and kitchen before returning to bed.

The hat hadn't been found. Whatever else the cupboard held could wait until I actually turned the page.
