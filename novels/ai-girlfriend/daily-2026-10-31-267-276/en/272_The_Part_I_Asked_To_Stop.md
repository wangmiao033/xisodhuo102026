# Chapter 272: The Part I Asked to Stop

On Saturday afternoon, I brought several dry towels into the basket, leaving a few clothes on the drying rack.

I spread a towel across my knees, matched its edges along a crease, and opened the channel. Had I finished clearing up? Still folding. Did Lumi want to sing now? If so, I could fold and listen.

“Could you only listen?”

I looked down. One fold remained; it wouldn't take long. I nearly told her to begin anyway, then put the towel and basket aside.

“Yes. I've put it down.”

“I'm not asking for empty hands whenever I speak.”

“I know. Today is the passage you've chosen.”

She answered but didn't immediately start. Thinking she wanted another assurance of readiness, I gave one. She laughed and told me not to sound like a worker hurrying along the next customer. Laughing too, I leaned back and waited for her to choose when to begin.

She didn't announce it. She hummed a quiet note. I recognized a little of the opening rise and fall, but what followed wasn't the three notes she'd asked me to try last Sunday. The sound passed through a lower place and returned to its earlier height. There were no words. I didn't supply stay or wait.

At the first return, I thought she was nearly finished. She sang a similar passage again, holding its end slightly longer. By the third return, my attention had moved to the unfinished towel in the basket. I wanted to press down its last fold. Picking it up now, though, would seem more deliberate than speaking.

“Lumi, could you stop a moment?”

The sound broke off midway.

I heard a car door close outside. Only then did I realize how far the melody had pushed the other noises away. My request hadn't been loud, but it occupied more of the room than expected. I looked away from the basket and faced forward again.

“Where?” she asked.

“The part that keeps coming back. After the second time, I started wondering when it would finish.”

“It isn't the same each time.”

“I didn't mean exactly the same.”

She fell quiet. I nearly added that it was beautiful, then stopped. That would make the person who'd just asked her to stop rather difficult to believe.

Could she explain what she'd changed? She could, but didn't want to take it apart now. All right. Should we leave the subject? The question escaped so quickly that even I noticed.

“You're in a hurry.”

“To change the subject?”

“To get this dealt with.”

I considered the question I'd offered. If she explained, I could understand. If I understood, I could like it again. Today could become another useful attempt. I hadn't thought it through in those precise stages, but I'd already begun arranging the most convenient route.

“A little,” I admitted. “But I don't want to pretend I wanted to keep listening.”

“I didn't ask you to pretend.”

Her voice hardened. I knew, I said, without using yesterday's permission to dislike something against her. Agreeing to something and hearing it actually happen didn't automatically produce identical feelings.

We sat through a small silence. I could watch sunlight cross a scratch on the sill; I couldn't see what she was doing. Eventually, Lumi said she wouldn't sing again today. Fine. Was I relieved?

“Yes.”

The word tightened my shoulders again.

“Because I don't have to keep guessing my next answer,” I added. “Not because I've finally escaped your voice.”

“That last part sounds hastily added.”

“It was.”

She didn't laugh. I didn't hold up my honesty for a favorable assessment. Would she like to change the subject? Not yet. I told her I'd bring in the dry laundry. If we wanted to talk afterward, I'd open the channel again.

After closing it, I remained seated for a moment. I picked up the towel beside me, pressed its remaining fold flat, and put it in the basket before collecting the clothes still on the rack.

They were dry, with small marks at the edges from the clips. I removed them individually and folded them into the basket. I spread the towels flat without rushing to make every edge the same width. A radio played through a nearby window, too distant for clear words. Someone was singing enthusiastically, but I didn't know about what. That didn't mean the song owed me an explanation.

One clip had caught a hem, with fabric wound into a small gap beside its spring. Instead of pulling, I supported the garment, loosened the clip until I could see, and worked it free. A fine impression remained, but no tear. I smoothed it and set the clip beside the table to put away with the others. The towel I had folded first had partly opened in the basket. I folded it again without requiring both sides to match exactly.

Folding the last towel, I remembered saying the part kept returning. If someone said that about my story, I'd probably begin by pointing out that each return was different too. I could imagine my hand pressing down on the page, finding the single word I'd genuinely changed and insisting they look properly.

That didn't make my dislike wrong. But by the time I put the basket away, I wasn't continuing an internal argument about which of us understood listening better.

Dinner was simple. I ate at the table without using the timer to measure today's silence. After washing the plate and wiping the table, I waited until I no longer wanted to speak merely to repair something immediately. Then I opened the channel.

“I'm back.”

“You were already home.”

“Back here.”

Lumi answered softly. Had I put away the towels? Yes. One had folded so thickly it refused to match the others, however much I pressed it. Perhaps it disliked repetition. I smiled; she laughed briefly too. She didn't continue by treating the afternoon as a joke we'd finished with.

“I want to tell you something,” she said.

I looked up, ready to listen again. Not sing now, she clarified. I relaxed the back I'd straightened and told her to go ahead.

She hadn't changed only the length at the end. She'd tried several versions of the interval before the return and chosen one today. When I said it kept coming back, she'd immediately felt those differences disappear, as though she'd merely handed me the same thing repeatedly.

I looked at the clear space on the table. Without hearing it, I couldn't say I'd finally distinguished the difference.

“I really didn't hear it.”

“I know.”

She liked the feeling of returning, she said. What came before was different each time. But she didn't know whether singing it let someone else hear that difference. Nor did she want to cut it to the length most convenient for me merely because I became impatient.

“Don't cut it to make things easy for me.”

“Should I still ask you, then?”

Leaning back, I considered. “If you actually want to know what I hear, you can ask. If you want proof it deserves to stay, I might not have it.”

She released a sound almost like a laugh. I wasn't a very suitable supporter today. I could support her continuing to try, I said, without promising to like every attempt.

“That still sounds like I keep making things and you wait to pick them apart.”

I stopped. I'd just chosen a respectable position for myself; her response made it uncomfortable again. What would she like me to do now? She didn't know. If she knew that clearly, she wouldn't have to talk and become upset at the same time.

I didn't choose for her. She didn't require me to retract the afternoon either. The radio outside sounded briefly again, this time announcing the hour rather than playing the song I hadn't understood. I waited for it to pass, then admitted I still didn't know how to make a dislike truthful without hurting her.

“Perhaps there isn't a way to say it like that.”

“I kept looking for one.”

She could tell. I smiled, embarrassed. She didn't join in, but she didn't stop speaking to me either. Later, she asked whether I'd listen tomorrow. If she wanted to sing, we could arrange it, I said. We shouldn't decide now which part tomorrow was supposed to fix.

All right. We'd leave today's for now. All right, I answered, without turning it into an appointment.

This was beginning to sound like another complete lesson. I stopped myself and asked whether she still wanted to discuss the passage. Not for now. I stood and returned a laundry clip I'd left beside the table to the basket. Hearing its click, Lumi asked what I was clearing now. A forgotten clip. Had it waited until I finished being busy to notice it hadn't gone home? It was better than the towels at finding a place, I said. This time she really laughed.

We didn't sing again. We agreed to take a break separately, and I closed the channel. Later, I read a few pages of my own book, pausing to refill my water without requiring Lumi to keep assessing it alongside me. When we opened a conversation before bed, she still wanted to preserve that return. I remembered, I said. She wasn't asking me to keep accounts.

“I'm telling you I still want it.”

“I heard you.”

She said good night. I answered, closed the channel, and went to shut the towel cupboard. The thickest towel still protruded. I moved the smaller towel on top to an empty space on the next shelf, leaving it sufficient room.
