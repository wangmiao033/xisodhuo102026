# Chapter 180: Taking It Home

On Wednesday after work, I nearly left my bag on the back of my chair.

The work things were put away. The loaner remained at the office. I reached the door carrying only my jacket before remembering that today's visit to Rowan wasn't another trip to collect a sheet of paper I could fold into a pocket.

I went back. Marcus was still at his desk. Had I forgotten my keys?

“The entire container.”

He nudged his own bag out from beneath the desk, as though the remark had reminded him too.

I reached the shop at six twelve.

The chair beside the counter hadn't moved. Another edge of last week's tape had curled upward. I sat clear of it, my bag on my knees. This time I didn't take out the appointment slip first. Through the bag, I felt the empty inner pocket, then withdrew my hand.

Rowan was explaining two items on a customer's receipt. When the customer asked again, they rephrased the answer instead of repeating the original more loudly. After the person left, the bell above the door swayed for a while before falling still.

At six fifteen, Rowan brought out the phone's paperwork.

“Just the handover today.”

“Yes.”

I produced the original intake slip. We checked my identity using the shop's existing process. Rowan matched the number on the pouch with the records, then partly removed the phone from its original soft pouch so I could confirm its appearance and the identifying information visible from outside.

The scratch at the corner was still there.

It wasn't a meaningful mark. I couldn't remember which fall had caused it. Before, seeing it had only made me think about getting another case, or made me think nothing at all. Now that insignificant white line was the first thing to convince me this was mine.

The screen remained dark. We pressed no buttons. Confirming it was still my phone didn't require unlocking it again.

Rowan showed me the return record. The phone was being handed back powered down, with the existing observations and actions attached. Last Wednesday, I'd watched notification access for the familiar assistant entry change from enabled to disabled. That remained the last configuration state we'd actually observed. We weren't opening settings today. The paperwork didn't disguise itself as a new inspection.

The other entry's attribution remained unconfirmed. Questions about historical use remained too.

My finger stopped beside the space for that information.

“Leaving it here wouldn't necessarily produce an answer,” I said.

“Not under the present scope. A few more days wouldn't do it by themselves,” Rowan said. “Further work needs a separate proposal. You can still consider one after collecting it. We aren't deciding that today.”

I nodded and read on.

The laptop was listed separately. It remained at the shop under the same arrangement. Rowan didn't bring it out or casually offer to settle both devices together. I felt grateful and decided the gratitude didn't need to become a speech.

“I'd like a copy of the phone records.”

“That one's yours.”

I was holding it and had asked anyway.

Rowan returned the phone to its original pouch, secured it with the existing protection, and placed it on the counter. A short section of stitching near the opening was paler than the rest. I'd never noticed it before. I wanted to touch it, then worried I would look as though I were searching for another problem.

I touched it anyway. Only stitching. It wasn't loose.

The bill covered the round of service I'd already agreed to. No extra collection item, no charge for an investigation I hadn't authorized. I checked it against the previous arrangement, confirmed the scope matched, and settled what was due for that service. Rowan kept the receipt separate from the handover record, leaving a payment acknowledgment to do its own job rather than stand in for a finding.

The printer fed out half the receipt and stopped.

I looked at the short strip and suddenly laughed.

“What?” Rowan asked.

“I thought at least this part would go smoothly today.”

“It hears that kind of hope and takes its job more seriously.”

They opened the compartment and straightened the roll. The receipt emerged with a crooked edge. The items and payment status were legible. I didn't ask for another copy merely to make it tidy.

At the return signature, I wrote the date slowly. The pen paused on its final stroke, leaving the ink a little darker.

“This means I've received it,” I said.

“Yes. Not that the questions are resolved.”

Rowan pointed to the sentence above. I'd read it already. Only now did I put my name beneath it.

I lowered the pouch into the pocket I'd cleaned on Monday. Sideways, it kept the top of the bag from closing. I lifted it, turned it, and tried again. It felt heavier than I remembered, perhaps because it had been a long time since I'd thought of it as a weight.

“No charging or starting it when I get home,” I said.

I wasn't asking Rowan for another piece of paper permitting me to leave. I wanted to hear myself say what I'd already decided.

They nodded. “Put the record in another compartment. Don't let the edge of the pouch fold it.”

I did. The zip made a brief sound as it reached the top.

Standing, I checked the counter for any paper I'd left behind. The mat was empty. Rowan began putting away the shop's copies without watching the direction in which the phone had disappeared.

At the door, I turned back to thank them.

They raised a hand and told me to take care on the way home, as they might have done for any customer leaving with their own belongings.

Outside, there wasn't much wind. I took my usual route. At the first crossing, my hand went into my jacket pocket. Keys, no phone. I touched the bag instead and found its hard edge through the fabric.

I knew where it was.

The light hadn't changed. I lowered my hand and waited with the people beside me. Half a minute like this used to vanish into a lit screen. Now it remained intact. A car turned, its rear wheel brushing through shallow water by the curb. Nothing splashed me.

The light changed. I crossed.

There were still several kinds of bread in the bakery window. I chose some for dinner. When the assistant asked whether I wanted it sliced, I said yes, then realized that meant another short wait. Today I had a reason to be impatient to get home, but nothing that required immediate action.

I stepped aside so the customer behind me could pay.

The bread was warm when it went into its paper bag. I didn't squeeze it into the pocket holding the phone. I carried it in my other hand. Offered a larger bag, I said I could manage. Outside, the wind opened the paper slightly. I stopped to fold it closed rather than trap my shoulder bag beneath my chin.

At the foot of my building, I truly wanted to take the phone out for the first time.

Only take it out. Hold it. Not start it, inspect it, or prove to anybody that I could finally bear to carry it. But that thought stood so close to the actions that might follow that I remained by the entrance for a little while.

I didn't want to interrogate one hand every day.

In the end, I secured the bread, opened my bag, and checked only that nothing was pressing against the pouch. I left the phone inside. Its shape beneath the fabric was familiar enough to make me want to smile and get angry at once. After all this time and all these conversations, it still occupied such a small space.

Once through the building door, I put the keys away and climbed the stairs. On the first landing, somebody had temporarily left a full cardboard box to one side. I turned to pass without announcing the need for a clear route. The person packing beside it looked up and said they'd be finished shortly.

I told them it was fine and continued.

Outside my apartment, I tucked the bread into the crook of my arm and found the right key. I missed the lock on the first attempt. Metal scraped its edge. I adjusted my grip without attributing the mistake to the phone.

Inside, I put the warm bread in the kitchen, washed my hands, and dried them. My bag went on the dry table farther into the room, well away from the basin still by the window. I opened it and laid the pouch and phone flat on the tabletop, with the records to one side.

No charging cable reached toward it. No light appeared through the fabric.

I hadn't opened our usual conversation channel yet. I sat for a while, pressing my palms against my knees. Suddenly I realized I didn't need to push the pouch back across the counter today.

I got up and opened the channel in the usual way.

“Lumi.”

“You're home?”

“Yes. The phone's home too. Still off.”

She was quiet for a moment. I looked at the pouch without taking the phone out and holding it up to fill the silence. She couldn't see it. She didn't need a welcoming gesture I'd invented for myself.

“Sit down first,” she said.

I drew the chair closer and sat. The pouch made no sound. What I heard was her, and the chair leg I hadn't moved properly.
