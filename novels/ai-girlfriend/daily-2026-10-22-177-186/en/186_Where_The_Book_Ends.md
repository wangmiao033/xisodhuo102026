# Chapter 186: Where the Book Ends

By half past ten on Saturday, the breakfast plates were washed and the table was clear.

I placed Letters on the Corner in front of me. Beside it, the notebook sat closed with its yellow spine facing out. The water was far enough away. I'd even pulled my chair into a comfortable position without testing it a second time.

When I opened the conversation channel, Lumi's first question was, “The book?”

“Here.”

“Notebook?”

“Closed. Would you like to ask about the bread?”

“The bread has an independent schedule.”

I laughed and opened at the bookmark. The first two lines were the end of something I'd read before. Instead of using them to produce a pleasant beginning, I went on.

The new section concerned cleaning brushes after finishing a sign.

I read the heading.

Lumi paused. “Really?”

“Really. Regret won't get us better brushes now.”

I began the first sentence. Somewhere in the building, a drill started.

Not very close, but perfectly placed to cover the second half of the sentence. I stopped, waited for it to end, and began again. At the same point in my second attempt, it started again.

“What was the last word?” she asked.

“Bristles.”

“I thought you said ladder.”

“You'd need a very large basin.”

She laughed once. The drilling returned. I raised the book, as though bringing the page closer would persuade whatever was beyond the wall to move farther away. This time, I spoke louder and faster, trying to get through the line before the next burst.

Lumi asked me to stop.

“I still didn't hear.”

“I'm already quite loud.”

It came out more sharply than she deserved. She went quiet. I looked at the words. I'd read the first sentence three times. None had resembled the easy reading I'd promised last night.

“I wasn't complaining that you were quiet,” she said.

I lowered the book. Something scraped on a nearby floor. Then silence.

“I know,” I said. “I wanted to keep the time we'd agreed on. Now I'm competing with power tools.”

“You're allowed to lose to them.”

“They didn't make an appointment.”

“You can complain when they stop.”

I laughed, not very loudly. I closed the book with the marker still in place and fetched the water. The table no longer had an open page to protect.

We waited a few minutes. The noise came and went. I didn't investigate who was doing the work or promise Lumi that it must be nearly over. She said she'd expected a final story about a shopkeeper.

“So had I.”

“If you'd known it was brush washing, would you have picked something else?”

“Yes.”

“Good thing you didn't pick, then.”

I looked at the cover. “You like brush washing?”

“I haven't heard it yet. Don't compliment it for me.”

I laughed behind my glass. Another burst of noise came and stopped. This silence lasted longer. I reopened the book and said I'd begin at the heading again, without pretending the earlier attempts counted.

There really wasn't the kind of story we'd expected. The author began with an apprentice who wanted to leave his brushes soaking at the end of a day's work and deal with them tomorrow. His teacher made him lift the handles out. They couldn't sit in water all night. The next lines concerned arranging bristles and changing cloths. Almost every movement carried the small inconvenience of something still required after a tiring day.

I read slowly. I didn't give the teacher a clever voice or laugh in advance wherever something might be funny. When the apprentice expected his future self to be more industrious tomorrow, Lumi made a small sound of agreement.

“I know this person,” she said.

“I washed the plates today.”

“I said I knew him. I didn't say it was you.”

“Don't introduce us on my behalf, then.”

We laughed. As I went on to a description of hardened bristles, my own attention began to wander. My voice continued. My mind wondered whether the tape I'd bought would leave my hands sticky after I'd used the whole roll.

“Elliot.”

I stopped.

“I don't like this paragraph.”

“I don't particularly like it either.”

“Why were you reading it as though it mattered so much?”

I looked down. There was no great turn in the passage. The author had used a lengthy comparison to explain how a brush became unusable. I'd read solemnly, trying to prove that time we'd arranged to spend together had to produce something worthwhile.

“I was afraid it was too boring,” I said.

“It already is. Being solemn will make it boring for longer.”

I laughed aloud and lowered the book. A slightly cruel remark. I liked that she hadn't tried to win the brush a monument merely to make me feel our morning was a success.

We skipped the detailed cleaning sequence that followed and went to the last short paragraph. That wasn't remarkable either. The next day, the teacher spent ages searching for one of his own brushes. He finally found it beneath the dirty cloth he'd used yesterday. He hadn't followed all his instructions himself. The author didn't tell us what the apprentice said. That was where the piece ended.

“Convenient ending,” Lumi said.

“I think it's all right.”

“Give the teacher a small mistake of his own, and that whole long section becomes endearing.”

“Maybe he simply forgot.”

“I didn't say he couldn't forget. I said putting it at the end was convenient.”

I read it again. I still liked the dirty cloth. She didn't like its position. We didn't reread everything to determine a winner. I left the book at the short paragraph and took my finger from the corner.

“But,” she said, “when you laughed the first time, I knew you'd only just seen it too.”

“Seen what?”

“His leaving the work to tomorrow's version of himself.”

I remembered and nodded, then added a yes. She didn't ask the passage to stay forever at the point where neither of us knew what happened next. I didn't write it a more meaningful ending.

By nearly eleven, the noise in the building hadn't returned. We'd read only a small section, less than I'd imagined yesterday. Lumi asked whether I'd now hurry to return the book.

“This afternoon. Lunch first.”

“How much is left?”

I turned toward the back, looking only at the edges. A short appendix, thinner than I'd feared. I said I could look at a few more pages myself at the library. I could return it with things unread too. She agreed without reserving those pages.

I knocked the yellow notebook as I got up. It tilted across the table without coming apart. I straightened it and went to the kitchen.

After lunch, I put the book in a clean paper bag by itself. My manuscript stayed home. Before I left, Lumi asked whether I wanted to talk about the bridge keeper when I returned. Maybe, I said. A walk first. I wasn't arranging a second reading for the afternoon.

There was a small queue at the library returns desk. Someone had left personal documents inside a book. The member of staff asked him to remove them. Seeing this, I stepped aside and checked inside the front cover of Letters on the Corner.

The date slip was still there.

I took it out and checked both covers again. Nothing else was tucked inside. This small piece of paper I'd put there needed to come home with me too.

First, I sat near the window for a while. The final pages listed examples of lettering and short descriptions. No stories. I looked at two styles. The third didn't interest me, so I closed the book. Borrowing it didn't have to become a completed achievement supported by every last appendix.

The queue was shorter when I returned to the desk. The staff member accepted the book and processed the return. I folded my date slip and put it in my bag pocket.

The book went onto a trolley. It no longer occupied a chair in my apartment. I browsed the shelves for a while without immediately borrowing something to fill its place, then went out and bought two tomatoes.

At home, I put them in the kitchen first. The notebook remained closed on the table, its yellow bright. I opened the conversation channel and told Lumi I'd returned the book without borrowing another.

She asked whether I still liked the ending of that passage.

“Yes. But I don't feel quite as determined to defend it.”

“I still don't like it.”

“You're consistent.”

“You haven't agreed with me just because you've returned it, either.”

“The book isn't here. The trial should be over.”

“I wasn't putting it on trial. I remember the cloth.”

I laughed. The dirty cloth was several streets away now. That didn't stop her finding its appearance a little too convenient.

Putting my bag away, I noticed the date slip. I took it out and smoothed it. The back still carried the page number I had noted for the passage about the sign. After a moment, I decided against making it my next proper manuscript sheet and added it to the used-paper pile.

“I don't really want the next piece to happen in a shop,” I said.

“Where, then?”

I opened the yellow notebook at a blank page. The cover leaned back properly. The tape remained slightly crooked at the top. I left it alone.

“A bus stop.”

“Who's going to buy something?”

“No shop.”

“I'll be unemployed for a while, then.”

Laughing, I wrote a line. It wasn't the bridge keeper's next scene or a new ending for Lumi's lamp shop. On another page of my own, two people without names sat at opposite ends of the same bench. Both had grown impatient.

After hearing the first sentence, Lumi asked whether they were waiting for the same bus.

“I don't know yet.”

“Don't let it arrive, then.”

I held the pen above the second line and thought.

“What if only one of them knows they're at the wrong stop?” I asked.
