# Chapter 182: The Chair Before Mine

At twenty past four on Thursday, Marcus stood beside my desk holding the portable projector and asked me to untangle a cable from the crook of his elbow.

I freed the cable. He set the equipment down securely before pointing to the process guide in his hand.

“We need a different presenter tomorrow.”

The colleague meant to lead the opening had been pulled away to handle something else. We had the materials and a demonstration sequence, but someone else needed to walk the new colleagues through it. Marcus hadn't come to ask me to fix twenty-four slides. Having heard what he needed, I found it harder to say no.

“Tom can take it. But he won't be finished with what he's doing until half past five. Could you wait and walk him through the opening?”

I was about to say yes.

My bag was beneath my chair, my page lying flat in its folder with the notebook beside it. That morning, I'd removed a staple in case it caught my hand when I turned the paper. An absurdly small preparation. I'd checked it twice.

“I need to leave at half past five,” I said.

Marcus paused. “Something on tonight?”

“I'm reading a little thing I've written at the café. I'm supposed to be there at six twenty.”

Once I'd said it, I wanted to add that it wasn't an important event. He didn't laugh or ask whether publishers would be there.

“Right. Can't keep you until five forty and then say it'll only take five minutes.”

He looked down, a finger resting beside the presenter's name. I didn't pick up my bag just because he'd accepted it quickly. Someone needed to know how to begin tomorrow, and that person might not understand everything we'd left in the files.

We found Tom waiting for an internal reply. He could talk, though he couldn't leave his desk yet. When I explained, he pointed to the opening of the demonstration and asked whether everyone would have the practice materials tomorrow.

“Yes,” Marcus said.

“Then why do they need to watch us for ten minutes first?”

I opened the instructions. The presenter's notes contained an impressive number of requests to look at the screen. Each new step brought another one. Following a route familiar to us, it was easy to forget that the people watching hadn't touched any of it yet.

“They could find their own practice sheet first,” I said. “Then we could show one complete submission at the end.”

Tom looked at me. “Are you about to rewrite the whole session?”

My hands were already on the keyboard.

“Nearly.”

“That would make it much harder for me to take over.”

We pulled our chairs closer and actually tried the opening. Marcus played someone attending for the first time. The materials page offered four links. He followed the first and found completed examples, with nowhere he could begin doing anything himself.

I almost told him the practice version was the third link. Tom raised his hand before I could prompt him.

Marcus went back and read each short label, finally pointing to the third and asking whether that was it. When he found it, he didn't make a joke. If he were in the audience tomorrow, he said, his hand would already have gone up.

I added a clear line explaining how to reach the current practice materials. I left the rest of the files alone. Tom explained how to collect a task in his own words, more briefly than I'd written it. At the submission step, he stopped and asked which materials were only for demonstration and which would enter the actual workflow.

I pointed out the practice version, then let him begin again. On the second attempt, I resisted finishing his sentences twice. He didn't miss anything important. He did remove one of my analogies completely.

“You don't need that?” I asked.

“If I need it, it'll come back to me tomorrow. It hasn't yet.”

Marcus laughed. I laughed too and finally moved my chair back.

Marcus handed Tom the papers for tomorrow. There was only one set. We'd originally expected everyone to look at the screen; now they needed to find their place on paper too. Marcus noted how many copies to prepare and asked whether the final sheet could be double-sided. Tom turned it over, talking and pointing, and discovered that the diagram on the back would make people skip a step.

“Single-sided,” he said. “We've cut a section of the talk. We don't have to make full use of every paper surface as well.”

I arranged the sheets and handed them to him. As soon as he took them, he rearranged them to suit the way he held things.

We explained the presenter change to Derek. He confirmed that Tom would take the opening and Marcus would prepare the practice sheets and equipment. My part was to leave Tom the opening we'd tested. I wasn't required to remain available all evening to guarantee every word he chose differently.

At twelve past five, Marcus came back from testing the equipment. It had a picture but no sound.

I looked up.

“We aren't playing anything with sound,” Tom said.

The three of us were quiet for a moment. Marcus laid a cable on the desk and said he could finally stop fixing a problem we weren't going to use.

I put away the work papers and left the company loaner where it belonged. At five twenty-seven, Tom asked me to look at his revised opening. I read it standing up. In ordinary sentences, he'd explained what to do first, who to go to next, and where people could stop if they got stuck.

“That works,” I said.

“Is that the assessment you're hoping for tonight?”

“If someone actually says that about my story, I might spend all evening feeling terrible.”

“Different kind of document, then.”

He turned back to his screen. I left the office without adding an evening work check to my plans.

The route to the café didn't take me past home. I bought a sandwich with vegetables at a shop still open for the evening and ate on a narrow seat by the window. The paper bag tore easily. I opened it out, but a piece of filling still fell onto my trousers.

I lifted it away with a napkin rather than rubbing it in. Only then did I get out the folder and notebook.

The bridgekeeper was still in the shop. The knot on his shoe was still uncomfortable. Reading the opening, I suddenly found “went inside” unbearably ordinary. I wanted a way for him to enter that seemed more justified and less nervous. I'd already uncapped my pen.

Outside the window, someone carrying two bags tried the door with a shoulder before discovering it opened outward. They set one bag down, freed a hand, and came in.

I put the cap back on.

At least my man had already entered his shop. Ten different versions of his posture weren't going to make me sweat any less tonight.

After finishing my food, I did change one thing. A sentence was long enough to force a breath where it didn't belong. I left the words and darkened the breathing mark already beside them. During last night's practice, Lumi had pointed out that I rushed my breath there. I'd argued that it was one sentence, as though surviving it on one breath proved it deserved to stay.

Today, I had to remember to breathe on my own.

She wasn't in my ear. I didn't say that sentence into an empty pocket, or reach for the phone I'd left at home. A line on a piece of paper didn't need immediate confirmation from another reader. I returned the manuscript to its folder, closed the notebook, cleared the crumbs from the table, and went to the bathroom.

In the mirror, one side of my collar sat higher than the other. I straightened it, went too far, and adjusted it again. None of the rehearsals had covered collar management before reading a page aloud.

There was still a little time before six twenty when I left the shop. I slowed down. At the corner, I waited through two changes of the lights. Not because I needed to. I'd failed to notice the first.

Through the café window, I could see several chairs had been moved. There was no platform, no curtain. Someone carried a cup from the little table at the front to one farther back, clearing a place for the pages.

I pushed the door open just after six twenty by the wall clock.

James was pulling a twice-folded piece of paper from beneath a chair leg. The chair wobbled. He replaced it with a thicker scrap, pressed it in, and tested the seat with his palm.

“Perfect timing. See if that still moves.”

I sat down and shifted my weight. The chair stayed still.

“You can use it,” he said.

“Now?”

“When you read. Or stand, if you prefer. Neither makes you more of a real writer.”

I got up so he could place the chair behind the small table. It wasn't my seat. Not yet. At the counter, I bought water. I'd intended to add a coffee, then reconsidered. I could hear my heartbeat clearly enough already.

James pointed out a place at the end of the second row. There weren't many readers. Before me would be a man carrying several sheets of wide-ruled paper. After me, a woman had her pages in a clear folder. Someone who'd only come for a drink was asked whether he wanted to turn his chair. No need, he said. His ears could turn that far.

I smiled. He didn't notice.

“You're second,” James said quietly. “I'll call you when he's done. Still about five minutes. Don't add sentences just to fill them.”

I nodded and took out my page. One corner of the notebook's cover had split farther. Pressing it with my thumb made the cardboard give a faint crackle. I released it and rested the loose manuscript on my knees.

At half past six, James untied his apron and draped it behind the counter. Beside the chair, he explained that nobody owed anyone a response tonight. Readers could return to their seats when they were finished. Anyone who wanted to offer a thought could offer one.

The first reader went over and arranged his wide-ruled sheets. He moved his cup farther away, just as I did at home, then looked down to find the beginning.

I looked down too. My own first line was plainly there, but my eyes kept sliding over it. I lowered the paper, planted both feet on the floor, and looked up to hear the man begin.
