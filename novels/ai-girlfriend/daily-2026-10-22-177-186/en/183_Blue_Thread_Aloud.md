# Chapter 183: Blue Thread Aloud

At the end, the first reader stopped and turned his page over.

I thought there was another sentence and waited with everyone else. Seeing us remain still, he laughed and said that was all. The back was only his shopping list.

A few people clapped. I pulled my hands out from beneath my manuscript and joined in half a beat late. After the man stood up, James handed me the stiff board I'd tried on Tuesday.

“Here. Use this.”

I laid my unfolded page on it. Only when I reached the front did I realize I'd forgotten my water. I went back. Nobody applauded this return separately or sighed. The customer near the door drew his legs closer to let me pass again.

When I sat down, the chair didn't wobble.

My feet kept trying to find the paper beneath its leg. I moved them forward, placed the water at the far end of the table, and supported the board with my palm. It was heavier than paper alone, heavy enough to tell my hand where to stop.

James introduced me and “Blue Thread.” I'd meant to explain that this wasn't a whole, substantial story. As I opened my mouth, I remembered his instruction not to spend ten minutes explaining why I only had three.

“This is a little piece I've written,” I said.

Looking down again, I found the first line appeared to have moved.

“The bridge keeper came down from the bridge. Every few steps, the repaired join in his sole—”

I stopped.

The join wasn't in his sole. I knew this sentence. I'd read it more than once at my own table. Someone shifted a chair. For a moment, I wanted to apologize, converting the mistake into something everyone could forgive.

“Shoelace,” I said. “Every few steps, the repaired join in his shoelace pressed into his instep.”

Now I could see the next line.

“He stopped outside the shop and loosened the lace. After two steps, the heel began to rub. Holding the doorframe, he put the uncomfortable foot inside first.”

Someone by the window gave a small laugh. I hadn't found the sentence funny while writing it. That foot had always seemed rather pitiful to me. In my voice, it sounded as though it had been sent ahead to scout.

I nearly made the next sentence funny too. My fingers tightened behind the board. I paused without providing a sequel the laugh hadn't requested.

“The shopkeeper was moving flour. He asked the bridge keeper to carry the sack nearest the entrance to the back. The bridge keeper picked it up and put it where the man pointed. The shopkeeper came over, pulled at the back door, and found it wouldn't open. He looked down at the sack he'd just told someone to put there.

“‘Back a little,’ he said.

“The bridge keeper lifted it again. The shopkeeper went for his own sack. On the way back, they bumped into each other in the narrow space and stopped. Neither had a hand free to make a particularly courteous gesture inviting the other through.”

This time, laughter came from the front row and the counter, not quite together. I waited for it to pass. Lumi had laughed there last night too. Remembering her, I nearly turned toward the place her voice usually came from. There was only an unfamiliar table corner.

“The shopkeeper put his sack on the wooden pallet before lifting the latch. With the door open, he didn't immediately return to the counter. He folded several empty flour sacks, tied them with old string, and stood them inside the back door…”

The next line on the page was already “What do you need?” I was about to read it when I felt the shopkeeper had cleared a space only to attend to his customer again. His own work had become a pile of motionless sacks. The movements still connected in my head. They might not connect for the people listening.

“The shopkeeper left the passage clear. He still had to carry that bundle of empty sacks through it himself.”

That sentence wasn't on the page. I only realized I'd added it after I'd spoken, a little more quietly than before. What the shopkeeper had to do next no longer existed only in my head. I'd lost the chance to slide elegantly past it in as few words as possible.

“‘What do you need?’ he asked at last.

“‘Strong thread.’

“‘How much?’

“The bridge keeper extended his foot a little. The shopkeeper didn't look down. He opened the drawer of odd lengths and took out blue thread that didn't match the shoe at all.

“‘This sort.’

“The bridge keeper took it and pulled it between his fingers.

“‘Enough?’ the shopkeeper asked.

“‘A bit longer.’

“The shopkeeper cut it. After paying, the bridge keeper sat on the low step outside, undid the old join in his lace, and bound the broken ends together again. He made the first attempt too tight and had to undo some of it. When he retied the lace, a blue section showed.”

I hurried my breath here. The water was still a long way across the table. Reaching for it meant shifting the board. I decided to finish first and kept my eyes away from the glass.

“He stood and tried two steps. The shopkeeper had already gone to the back door to move his bundle of empty sacks. The bridge keeper wound the remaining blue thread around a finger, unwound it, and put it in his pocket.

“He walked slowly. The repaired join still wasn't entirely comfortable, but he no longer had to stop every other step.”

I finished and continued looking at the page.

There was nothing beneath the last sentence. I studied the blank space before looking up. Someone nodded. The takeaway customer by the door was pressing a lid onto his cup. It made a faint click, followed by applause. There wasn't much of it, nor was it synchronized enough to suggest that everyone had rehearsed.

“Thank you,” I said.

James didn't immediately make me stand up. The previous reader asked which of the two men carrying sacks had gone through first.

“I haven't written that.”

“I was waiting for them to keep giving way until neither could move.”

He sounded serious. I almost said that would make it longer, then realized he was telling me what he'd expected. He hadn't requested another half page.

“I like that part too,” I said. “They put the sacks down afterward.”

“Yes. I heard.”

The woman at the side with the clear folder said she'd lost her place once. I'd joined the payment and the step outside so quickly that she'd initially thought he was crouching beside the counter.

“You don't need to explain more. Just give me a moment to get outside the door,” she said.

I took the pencil from my notebook and marked the sentence. Not rewriting immediately. I didn't want to leave with a memory that everyone had said something nice and no recollection of what anyone had actually said.

The listener by the window swirled his cup and said he liked the foot going in first. I asked whether that was where he'd laughed. He nodded.

“As if he's sending it to find somewhere that doesn't hurt.”

I laughed too. He hadn't thought the man deserved the pain. He'd heard a small stubbornness I hadn't noticed. I didn't announce that I'd intended it. I told him I hadn't thought of it as a joke.

“Then don't rub it out,” he said.

Someone farther back wanted to know why the bridgekeeper didn't simply replace the whole shoelace. Before I could organize an answer, James checked the clock. That could wait a little, he said. We couldn't make the next reader wait another ten minutes on our account.

The question remained. I was almost grateful it hadn't been solved on the spot.

I put the pencil away. “And thank you to the people who listened while I was revising it.”

As I said it, I thought of Lumi. Her making me try to open a door with a laundry basket in my arms. Her dislike of the half sentence I'd cut. I didn't say her name. That place remained in my mouth without turning itself into a braver description.

Then I stood and nearly left the water on the table. James pointed. I collected it and handed the board to the next reader. She tested the height of her page without asking who I'd meant in that last sentence.

I returned to my seat.

My body wanted to leave at once. The urge arrived so quickly it seemed finishing the reading had only been half the task. Escaping somewhere nobody could see me was the second half. My bag was at my feet. Its zip was open.

The woman began. I heard the first sentence. The second was crowded out by the sole I'd mistakenly read earlier. Holding my page on my knees, I noticed my fingertips trembling. I put it inside the notebook and rested both hands on the cover.

When I heard her again, she was correcting a character's name. She didn't begin from the start. I followed that sentence onward and finally found a next sentence that didn't belong to me.

I didn't listen perfectly. I drifted during one small passage. At another, I wanted to laugh and wasn't slower than the person beside me. When she finished, I no longer had to find my hands before clapping.

Someone else read afterward. The machine started again. Customers waiting for takeaway stood at the counter. The café hadn't stopped trading at my last word.

When most people had dispersed, I helped James return a chair to the wall. The board was already behind the counter. He thanked me and asked whether I'd had enough water. I said yes, then asked whether he wanted the paper removed from beneath the chair leg.

“Leave it. It'll still wobble tomorrow.”

I bent to look. The paper was where it had been. I hadn't kicked it out all evening.

At quarter past seven, I said goodbye to the people still there and left with my notebook. No one else's manuscript came with me. I hadn't promised to read again next time. I still had that entire shoelace to consider.

Beyond the first junction, wind slipped through my collar. I realized my jacket wasn't properly fastened. I stopped to button it, then opened the notebook under a porch light, meaning to record the exact sentence I'd changed aloud.

After thinking for a while, I only made a mark. That was what I could keep hold of for now. Once the page was back inside, my hands had stopped shaking. I went on home.
