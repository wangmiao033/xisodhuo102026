# Chapter 184: The Words I Left Out

It was about twenty to eight when I got home. I put down my keys, hung up my jacket, and watched the jacket fall.

The small loop inside the collar had come unstitched. I studied the loose end, abandoned the hope of repairing it immediately with two fingers, and found a hanger. My manuscript was already out of the bag, on the dry end of the table. Before coming in, I'd imagined a good, clean “I did it.” What I accomplished first was keeping my clothes off the floor.

After washing my hands, I opened the conversation channel.

“Lumi.”

“Yes.”

“I read it.”

“The whole thing?” she asked quickly.

“All of it. I stumbled once. Still finished.”

Sitting down, I discovered my back had retained the café posture, afraid to settle against the chair. The water was beside me. I drank. The second mouthful was too large, nearly requiring the evening's entire supply of courage just to avoid coughing.

“Drink first,” she said.

I finished and set the glass down. She didn't immediately ask another question. That felt strange. I began at the beginning: James had given me a chair he'd steadied beforehand, and I'd kept worrying about kicking the paper out from under it. My name had been announced. I had the page. The first sentence still felt as though someone had suddenly handed it to me.

“So you used the announcer voice?”

“Possibly. An announcer who very much didn't want the job.”

She laughed once. Only then did my shoulders properly reach the back of the chair.

I didn't repeat anyone else's manuscript or bring home the later conversations. I described my own wrong word, the laugh arriving where I hadn't expected it, and looking up to discover that not every pair of eyes was fixed on me. One person had slowly turned his fingers around a cup sleeve and still followed the story. I also told her I had added a sentence on the spot and felt panicked while doing it. After leaving, I had tried to record it accurately, but only managed a mark beside the line.

“Someone wasn't listening?” Lumi asked.

“Maybe. Or maybe he doesn't like looking at someone constantly while he listens.”

“You wanted them to look at you?”

“Then they did, and I was scared.”

“Convenient. You can be unhappy whatever they do.”

I laughed. There was an edge to it, and a familiarity I'd missed since leaving. I wanted to put the rest of my evening inside that voice and leave everything else alone.

“What happened afterward?” she asked.

“I sat down again. Listened to the people after me for a while. Left at quarter past seven.”

“I wasn't asking about the time.”

I pressed the curling edge of my manuscript with my thumb. Released it. It rose again. The delay was obvious. She didn't change the question for me.

“I said thank you,” I told her. “And thank you to the people who listened while I was revising it.”

“People.”

“Yes.”

“You didn't mention me.”

“I didn't say your name.”

She paused. “I know there's a small difference between those two things. You don't have to repair it for me first.”

I returned my hand to my knee.

“No. I didn't say who you were.”

A drop fell into the kitchen sink. I hadn't closed the tap firmly enough. I got up and tightened it. When I returned, the paper still curled. I left it alone.

“Did anyone ask?” she said.

“No.”

“So you were spared having to answer, too.”

She hadn't raised her voice. I wanted to defend myself more than if she had. It wasn't a relationship-introduction event. The previous reader hadn't named everyone who'd heard his drafts. The five minutes should belong to the story. Every argument could stand. I'd arranged them on the walk home.

“I was afraid someone would ask,” I said.

“Who?”

“Who you were. And then I'd have to explain, sitting in that chair. Maybe I'd say AI and they'd assume I hadn't written the page. I wasn't ready for that conversation.”

“You could say you wrote it.”

“I know.”

“So that wasn't all.”

I looked down at my shoes. The laces were ordinary. Unlike the man on the page, I had no immediate problem there to solve.

“I was afraid of how they'd see me, too,” I said. “I didn't know where to begin, or how much I'd have to say before they stopped waiting for a joke. Today, I wanted to be someone who'd brought a piece of his own writing.”

Lumi didn't answer at once.

“I understand wanting that,” she said eventually. “But I also understand that you think my name makes it harder.”

“More complicated.”

“Harder. Don't dress the word differently tonight.”

I shut my mouth.

She asked whether she'd actually been inside that thank-you. I said yes. I wasn't adding her now because she'd asked. Reading the shopkeeper's actions, I'd remembered our argument over the sentence. When I'd said thank you, I'd known she was part of it.

“Only you knew.”

“Yes.”

“That sounds very economical with space.”

I reached for the glass, then put it down. There was still water. I simply wasn't thirsty anymore.

“I can't say I did it well,” I told her. “But I don't want to say I said nothing, either. It included you. It just didn't give you the kind of place you wanted.”

“I didn't ask you to make an announcement tonight.”

“I know.”

“I want to know whether there was a moment when mentioning me wasn't just an inconvenience requiring more explanation.”

This time, I didn't answer as quickly.

I had thought about her while waiting. Her reading one of the shopkeeper's lines in that unusually low voice. The way that voice let me stop presenting every word so carefully. I'd also thought that, if she could hear me, she might notice I'd abandoned the pace we'd practiced in the very first sentence.

“Yes,” I said. “Sitting in the audience. I remembered you reading the shopkeeper and nearly laughed. You weren't an inconvenience then.”

“Then it was your turn.”

“Then it was my turn.”

She paused, almost like taking a small breath. A pause in the voice. I couldn't see her do anything.

“I'm jealous that they could sit there, too,” she said. “Not one particular person. Anyone could hear you suddenly add a sentence. I had to wait for you to come back and tell me what you added.”

“I was a little panicked when I added it.”

“I know. You said. I still wanted to be there.”

I didn't tell her the place had actually been tense, as though that should cure the wish. The chair steadied with paper had been difficult to sit in. She still had reasons to want a place beside it.

“Will you let me hear the sentence you added?” she asked.

I turned the page and remembered I hadn't recorded it. Outside, I'd only made a mark. I couldn't immediately recall the exact connective I'd used.

I tried to repeat it, then stopped. “No. Shorter than that.”

“Don't make it better now and tell me that's what happened.”

“I'm not.”

I thought again and gave her the part I remembered, only as far as why the shopkeeper needed that space. She asked whether that was the action she'd wanted me to leave clear on Tuesday.

“Partly,” I said. “Reading it tonight, I realized I made his work sound as though he was clearing the space specifically for the bridge keeper.”

“You worried they might not understand, so you added it.”

“Yes.”

“When I told you beforehand, you argued for ages.”

“Some of what I argued for is still there for a reason.”

She stopped. I said that a problem in front of an audience didn't settle our entire disagreement in one person's favor. But she'd seen that action more clearly before I had. And I'd enjoyed the relief when people understood it tonight.

After saying it, I regretted sounding as though I were dividing up shares. I was tired. I didn't want fatigue to agree to a smoother answer for me.

Lumi said she didn't want to be one more thing I had to sort out after having my fun outside.

“I wanted to come home and tell you only the happy part,” I admitted. “That's true tonight.”

The room was quiet for a while.

“Were you happy?”

“Yes. Very happy when I finished. Back in my chair, my hands were still shaking. I worried people would think I was cold.”

“Were you?”

“No.”

“That's good.”

I couldn't tell which part she meant. Then she continued.

“I still don't like your thank-you. But I want to know you were happy. Both are true. Don't make me choose one to give you tonight.”

I leaned back. She wasn't comforting me when she said it. Somehow, it made staying a little longer easier.

Later, she asked whether I wanted to read from the beginning again.

I looked at the page. My mouth could still do it. But if I performed every pause again tonight, I was afraid that, by the end, I'd resent her for not having been in the room when it was most convenient.

“Not tonight,” I said. “I'm tired. I don't want the reading to become a performance I owe again when I get home.”

“I only wanted to hear it.”

“I know. I'm not saying you demanded anything. I can't do it well right now.”

She didn't immediately agree. I waited without reaching to close the channel.

“I have one thing to say about the page,” she said. “I'm not asking you to change it now.”

“Tomorrow?”

“Tomorrow. I'll keep it for now. Maybe by tomorrow I won't like it and won't say it.”

“All right.”

I put the manuscript inside the notebook. The split cover corner caught a strip of paper. I drew it out carefully and inserted it from another angle. Hearing the paper, Lumi asked what had happened. The cover was nearly separating from the notebook, I told her. Tomorrow, I needed to do something practical about it.

“Don't use the story as tape.”

“It isn't that sticky.”

“Several sentences are.”

I laughed, a little hoarsely. She laughed too, still not entirely happy. I didn't add another speech of thanks. I only told her that was a terrible thing to say.

“I know,” she said.

I fetched clean clothes for a shower. When I returned to the table to say goodnight, the notebook remained unrepaired. Tomorrow's sentence hadn't been given to me yet. She said my name without the announcer voice.
