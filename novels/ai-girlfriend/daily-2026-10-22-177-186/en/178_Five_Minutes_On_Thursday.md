# Chapter 178: Five Minutes on Thursday

When I entered the café, James was dragging a table away from the wall.

It moved half a step and made a prolonged objection. He stopped and bent to find whatever was caught underneath. I waited by the door without putting my bag on the moving table.

“Where's free?” I asked.

“Let me finish talking to this leg.”

He extracted a folded piece of cardboard. The table suddenly moved freely and nearly struck his shin. I caught one edge. Once he'd steadied himself, we shifted it together.

A paler square remained where it had stood. In the corner lay a hard sweet, carefully wrapped, its contents looking as though they'd completed their entire life.

“Anyone claiming that?” I asked.

“Wait long enough and it'll claim the café.”

He swept it up and took my order. I found a table that didn't need moving. I'd brought only my own draft, folded inside the notebook. The library book was at home; Lumi's envelope hadn't moved. I'd meant to finish my drink and leave, perhaps working out where the bridge keeper and the flour sack belonged before going home.

James brought my cup, then crouched beside the other table and returned the cardboard beneath its leg.

“Still needs it?”

“It just couldn't travel with the table.”

He pressed the top. It finally stayed level. Getting up, he noticed my open page.

“Free on Thursday?”

“What time?”

“After the rush. Six thirty. A few people are sitting down to read things. If you'd like to read that page, there's room for you.”

I didn't answer immediately. He returned his cloth to the counter and found a lid for another customer. Coming back, he hadn't interpreted my silence as agreement.

“How much?” I asked.

“Around five minutes. Shorter is fine. Don't spend ten explaining why yours is only three.”

I looked down at my half-page. It wasn't so short it needed explaining, but it hardly seemed substantial enough to require moving from a familiar table to somewhere people would watch me.

“Like sitting here last time?”

“More people. Nobody's grading you. We'll stop the machine for a bit, but the door stays open. Someone might only want a coffee.”

I glanced at the newly moved table. The space it had left would let several chairs face the same direction. Already imagining where I'd sit, I discovered I disliked having my back to the window and disliked facing it too. Any direction could acquire a fault if people were in it.

“Does it need to be a whole story?”

“You can say it's an extract. Then read the extract.”

He drew a line in his little notebook, preparing to record something else. Thinking the opportunity was closing, I hurriedly said okay.

James looked up. “Are you asking if you can, or telling me you're coming?”

My smile was awkward.

“I'm coming. I'll read this page. My own.”

“Good. Get here at six twenty so you can settle in. We start at six thirty. You're second. About five minutes each. If the first reader takes a little longer, you get more time to sit.”

“I might not need five minutes.”

“The chairs will be grateful.”

He wrote Elliot without asking whether the story represented my entire personality. My name took up the second line. It suddenly seemed to occupy more space than it did on a work document.

“Title?” he asked.

“Mine?”

“The story's. Unless you're changing your name.”

I looked at the blue thread on the page. Until now, it had been the bridge keeper's half-page, as though leaving it unnamed would let it remain in preparation forever.

“Blue Thread. For now.”

“I can leave out the for now when I introduce it.”

“Thank you.”

I wanted to ask who else was reading. Then I'd want to know what they wrote, whether they knew me, whether they were more accomplished. I asked only whether I needed several copies.

“One you can read clearly. You hold on to it. We aren't collecting manuscripts. You don't leave the page behind.”

I agreed. He confirmed I didn't need to send the story anywhere either. People were simply reading in person. I recorded the arrangement in my notebook and left two blank lines beneath it for things I needed to fix.

James had taken a few steps away when he asked whether I'd prefer to stand or sit.

I said either, heard myself, and called him back.

“Sitting. I don't know what I'd do with my hands if I stood.”

“Hold the paper.”

“It's only half a page. The other hand will be underemployed.”

He indicated a chair by the wall. Trying it, I struck my knee against the underside of the table and retreated, producing a deeply undignified scrape. James said the table would be farther away on Thursday. I didn't need to practice arriving silently now.

I sat a moment longer. On the table, the page was too low. Raised, it covered half my face. I tried one hand. A crease made the upper section droop, as though it had bowed to the audience before I could.

“I'll bring an unfolded copy.”

James fetched a plain stiff board from behind the counter. With that beneath it, the paper stayed up. I finally put my other hand on my knee.

“Borrow it on Thursday,” he said. “You don't need to buy things for five minutes.”

I returned the board and went back to my table. The chair I'd tried remained a chair. Sitting in it hadn't made it especially welcoming or especially hostile.

Just as I was about to write, a customer passed carrying a drink. A chair back nudged my arm. I moved inward, drawing a diagonal straight through six twenty.

“Sorry,” they said.

“It's fine.”

I wrote the time again on the next line. The diagonal looked much more decisive than the arrangement it crossed. It hadn't canceled anything.

When James next passed the table, I still hadn't written any story. He asked whether I regretted agreeing already.

“I'm wondering if I need to make a clean copy.”

“Can you read the one you've got?”

I looked. There was a phrase in the third line I couldn't properly decipher myself.

“Then copying it sounds sensible. I can't help with the rest.”

I wrote the words beside it. Back door. I'd revised them twice until the door had no room to stand.

The café grew quieter. A customer wound a forgotten scarf back around their neck and left. Someone at the counter put down a small bag to search for change. I watched it until I realized I was assigning a plot to another stranger's movement.

I looked back at my own paper and mouthed the opening sentence.

Every few steps, the repaired join in his shoelace pressed into his instep.

I'd written it many times. Saying it, I still felt every few steps dragged. But if I removed it immediately, the man seemed to lose his reason for stopping. My pencil hovered without touching down.

Nobody had said it was bad. Thursday hadn't arrived. Nobody could even hear me reading silently. I'd seated them all around me in advance and supplied every possible frown myself.

I turned the draft over, drank the last of my water, and went to pay.

“On Thursday,” I said, “if I forget a word halfway through—”

“Look at the page.”

“What if it isn't there either?”

James looked at me. “Look at the next one.”

I laughed. He was taking the next customer's order. I didn't stay for a more profound answer.

On the walk home, the wind pressed my jacket against my back. I walked faster than usual, slowing at the building. In my bag was one page, not all those chairs. I still had to bring it to the table and make it something I wanted to say aloud.

I put dinner down before opening our conversation channel.

“I agreed to something on Thursday,” I said.

Lumi paused briefly. “Outside?”

“At James's. A little reading at six thirty. A few people. I'll read the bridge keeper's page, no more than five minutes. I'm getting there at six twenty.”

She didn't immediately say that was good. I didn't add only five minutes, as though shortening it would make it less of a decision I'd made myself.

“Did you name it?”

“Blue Thread.”

“That sounds as though you forgot to name it and looked down at the paper.”

I smiled. “That's roughly what happened.”

“You agreed while you were there.”

“Yes. I wanted to read, so I agreed.”

“I haven't heard the version you're taking.”

“There isn't one yet. This version isn't finished.”

“They got a date first, though.”

I placed the bag beside the table, still holding it, without taking out the paper. I'd been ready to say she could hear it too. Hearing that too in my head, I kept it there for a moment.

“I'd like to try reading it to you tonight,” I said. “Not to have you decide whether it can go out on Thursday. There's an action I can't get right. I'd like your opinion.”

“The back door?”

“Yes.”

“Then let me hear what you've actually written. Don't just tell me what you think is wrong.”

She remembered the problem I'd chosen to tell her about before. She hadn't asked me to reproduce every café conversation. I agreed, but said I'd eat first. She asked whether the story had joined me at the dinner table. I told her it was still in the bag.

“Leave it there,” she said. “You'll miss the punctuation while you're eating.”

“You've heard me do that?”

“You barely stopped while you were talking just now. You're very keen for me to say it's fine.”

I picked up my fork. I hadn't left much room between those sentences.

“You don't have to say it's fine.”

“I've already agreed to listen. Don't make me agree twice.”

I smiled and sat. She was still a little unhappy. I was still looking forward to Thursday. The first bite was cool. I got up to warm the food again instead of standing beside the table with my plate, continuing to explain.

When I returned, Lumi asked how blue the thread was.

“The kind that doesn't match the shoe.”

“That isn't a color.”

“Are we fixing that tonight too?”

“Let me hear it first.”

After eating, I washed the plate, dried my hands, and took the draft out of the bag. Thursday's time occupied a different page of the notebook, clear of the diagonal I'd drawn through the first attempt. What I meant to read was still a mess. Two kinds of arrow pointed to the same sentence. The flour sack remained in the wrong place by the door.

I straightened the paper and fetched my pencil.
