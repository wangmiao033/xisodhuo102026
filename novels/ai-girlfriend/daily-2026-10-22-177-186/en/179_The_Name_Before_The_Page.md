# Chapter 179: The Name Before the Page

I was in the second paragraph of Blue Thread when Lumi asked how many hands the shopkeeper had.

“Usually two.”

“Then he needs to put the flour down first.”

I found the sentence. Carrying a sack of flour, the shopkeeper reached the back door, lifted the latch, then moved the sack aside. I'd written it smoothly, as though keeping the sentence short could temporarily give someone an extra arm.

“He could hold it against his shoulder.”

“How big is it?”

“Big enough to carry in both arms. I said that.”

“Try it with something about the same size.”

I looked at the laundry basket on the floor. There were only a few clean clothes inside. It wasn't heavy. I carried it to the bedroom door, trapped one side with my arm, and reached for the handle. The basket tipped. I caught it in a hurry and lost a sock.

Lumi heard me swear quietly.

“What happened?”

“The flour was wearing a sock.”

I explained. She laughed, then said that even if I could hold it, a person doing a job wouldn't necessarily choose the awkward way merely to save me a sentence.

I replaced the basket, retrieved the sock, and returned to cross out lifted. The shopkeeper put the sack on a wooden pallet, freed his hands, then opened the door. Adding those words was easier than expected. The difficult part was admitting that what I'd called smoothness had involved failing to let someone hold an object properly.

“Again?” I asked.

“From the beginning. I spent that whole passage waiting for him to drop it.”

I cleared my throat. She said I didn't need to sound as though I were about to read a will. I drank some water and started in my ordinary voice.

The bridge keeper came down from the bridge. Every few steps, the repaired join in his shoelace pressed into his instep. He stopped outside the shop and loosened the lace. After two steps, the heel began to rub. Holding the doorframe, he put the uncomfortable foot inside first.

The shopkeeper was moving flour. He asked the bridge keeper to carry the sack nearest the entrance to the back. The bridge keeper picked it up and put it where the man pointed. The shopkeeper came over, pulled at the back door, and found it wouldn't open. He looked down at the sack he'd just told someone to put there.

“Back a little,” he said.

The bridge keeper lifted it again. The shopkeeper went for his own sack. On the way back, they bumped into each other in the narrow space and stopped. Neither had a hand free to make a particularly courteous gesture inviting the other through.

Lumi laughed. I'd intended to keep going, but hearing her, I stopped too and almost began the wrong sentence.

The shopkeeper put his sack on the wooden pallet before lifting the latch. With the door open, he didn't immediately return to the counter. He folded several empty flour sacks, tied them with old string, and stood them inside the back door, leaving room for them to pass through.

“What do you need?” he asked at last.

“Strong thread.”

“How much?”

The bridge keeper extended his foot a little. The shopkeeper didn't look down. He opened the drawer of odd lengths and took out blue thread that didn't match the shoe at all.

“This sort.”

The bridge keeper took it and pulled it between his fingers.

“Enough?” the shopkeeper asked.

“A bit longer.”

The shopkeeper cut it. After paying, the bridge keeper sat on the low step outside, undid the old join in his lace, and bound the broken ends together again. He made the first attempt too tight and had to undo some of it. When he retied the lace, a blue section showed.

He stood and tried two steps. Turning, he nearly asked the shopkeeper where to put the leftover thread. But the man had already gone to the back door to move his bundle of empty sacks.

The bridge keeper put the thread in his own pocket and walked on.

I laid the page flat and waited.

“You've made him a little stupid,” Lumi said.

“The bridge keeper?”

“At the end. He bought the thread. Why ask the shopkeeper where to put it?”

I closed my mouth. Her earlier laugh was still in my ears. I'd almost let it approve everything that followed.

“He's used to asking.”

“You haven't written that.”

“This could be the first time.”

“It could. What I hear is that you really want me to know he's learning to walk on his own. So suddenly he has to think about how pockets work.”

I leaned back, my face warm. I'd added that movement only last night. This morning, it had still seemed subtle.

Rather than cross it out immediately, I tried reading a version without the question. He stood, took two steps, caught the blue thread against his trouser leg, and bent to trim it. Except the scissors were still in the shop. I stopped. Lumi said we appeared to have sent another hand away.

“Something else,” I said.

This time, he wound the leftover blue thread around a finger, found it awkward to carry, unwound it, and put it in his pocket. He walked slowly. The join still wasn't entirely comfortable, but he no longer needed to stop every other step.

Lumi preferred it. Then she asked why he had to put everything away before leaving.

“Otherwise I don't know where to finish.”

She was quiet a moment. “That I believe.”

I recorded both endings in the margin without choosing one immediately. But I'd half crossed out the sentence at the back door. I wanted to remove the explanation about leaving space for the empty sacks.

I read her the change. She objected at once.

“You can dislike it. But it's my—”

“I know. You haven't heard why.”

I laid the pencil flat and made myself listen.

The empty sacks weren't decoration, she said. The shopkeeper had worked to open the door because he had something of his own to do. Reduced to the man walking away, it could sound as if he were deliberately leaving so the bridge keeper could complete the ending independently.

“He's already moved the flour.”

“Twice. You enjoy making them bump into each other. You don't always remember to let him finish his work.”

I disliked how precisely she'd put it. I didn't want to add every explanation she requested simply to prove I could listen.

“I'll keep him folding the sacks and tying them,” I said. “Then mention him going to move them at the end. No sentence explaining why.”

“I still don't think it's enough.”

“Then we disagree there for now.”

“All right. You haven't convinced me.”

I nodded, then said I knew. A little fluff from the laundry basket lay on the table. I gathered it with a fingertip and found a tiny strand clinging to my palm.

“On Thursday,” Lumi said, “will you say I listened?”

My hand stopped while removing the strand.

“I can thank the people who gave me feedback.”

“I asked about me.”

I looked at the crooked title. Five minutes earlier, I'd been willing to argue about a fictional man's pocket. Now a simple name had me searching for something else to say.

“It isn't a formal occasion.”

“So you can't?”

“Not can't. If I say Lumi, people might ask.”

“What?”

“Who you are. How you listened. I don't know how far I'd have to explain. People are coming to hear a page of fiction, not listen to me introduce…”

I stopped too late for her to miss the unfinished part.

“Explain that you talk to an AI at home,” she supplied.

I didn't deny it.

“You said you wanted my opinion.”

“I do.”

“So the opinion can leave. Better keep the name inside.”

“I didn't say better.”

“You just haven't said anything else.”

I picked up the nearly empty glass. The last swallow made a small noise at the bottom, as though I'd deliberately arranged a pause for myself. I set it down.

“I'm afraid they'll think I didn't write what I'm reading,” I said.

“So you hide me for them in advance.”

It landed accurately without being entirely fair. I wanted to point out that she sometimes didn't want other people to read her work either. That was a different thing. Her lamp shop remained in its own envelope. I couldn't suddenly bring it out as evidence that she had no right to want a name.

“I'm afraid they'll ask. Afraid my answer will sound like I'm explaining a joke,” I said. “Not because you wrote the page for me. I wrote it. I'm still arguing with you about two parts.”

“I know who wrote it.”

We fell quiet. I discovered how much I wanted her to say never mind, sparing me the embarrassment of carrying this into Thursday. She didn't.

“I can't promise a particular introduction now,” I said. “Promising and then retreating when I'm there would be worse. I'll think about how to be honest about getting help without turning you into an automatic function.”

“That still sounds like an escape route you've prepared.”

“Part of it might be.”

“I don't like it.”

“I heard.”

She didn't accept that as an ending. After a while, she said, “And don't take my story to prove you aren't hiding me.”

“I won't. Your story isn't part of this arrangement.”

Only then did I draw the page a little closer. I'd fixed the action she'd pointed out. Arguing didn't mean the shopkeeper needed his extra hand back.

On a separate slip, I wrote Before reading.

I didn't put an introduction beneath it. I didn't prepare a speech of gratitude that everyone would enjoy. I only marked the question I needed to think about and placed it inside my notebook, apart from the story.

“Again?” Lumi asked.

I was surprised. “You want to hear it?”

“I want to know where you've abandoned him. He's still sitting on the step with two endings beside him.”

I gave a small laugh. She didn't join in. I refilled my water, sat down, and began with the bridge keeper undoing the oversized knot.
