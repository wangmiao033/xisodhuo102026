# Chapter 177: Room in the Bag

On Monday morning, I stood outside a meeting room holding six sets of papers. Every seat inside was occupied.

The man at the head of the table looked up. I looked back. Behind him, the screen displayed a chart I'd never seen before.

“Ten o'clock?” I asked.

“We're here until eleven.”

I backed out and checked the booking beside the door. My name was there. So was Tuesday's date. I'd moved forward a day while making the reservation, then remembered the empty slot without remembering how I'd reached it.

Tom came down the corridor carrying a marker.

“Not open?”

“Open. Just not for us.”

He leaned closer to read. I was about to suggest the small meeting room, then remembered it held four chairs. There were still six sets of papers in my arms.

“Stop everyone coming this way,” I said. “I'll find somewhere we fit.”

We ended up at the little table beside the printers. It wouldn't seat six, but two nearby chairs could be brought over. Derek said we were only settling the order of discussion today. We needed to hear each other, not watch a screen. I went for the chairs while Tom redirected people. The first chair caught on a doorframe. As I tilted it, a set of papers slipped from under my arm.

Marcus picked it up. “Were you hoping to get tomorrow's meeting done early too?”

“The room's ready for that one.”

He laughed and helped with the second chair. Throughout the meeting, the printer kept feeding out pages, sounding like someone applauding earnestly behind us. I raised my voice and discovered how easily that made every sentence sound final. Tom missed something. I repeated it instead of using the noise to hurry past.

Afterward, I canceled the mistaken reservation and put the chairs back. Nobody took the last one for me. Finding an angle that cleared the doorframe took two minutes. Understanding my mistake hadn't persuaded the office to finish tidying up after it.

My shoulders still ached a little when I reached the library at lunchtime.

Rowan had replied to Sunday's email. At a computer where I could check my personal account, I read their message once, then scrolled back.

There were no new findings.

They explained that tracing possible use of the permission, along with the other entry whose attribution remained unclear, would first require a broader investigation proposal. What records might be needed, and which could actually be obtained, still required assessment. The shop couldn't promise to recover a complete history under the existing conditions. They hadn't treated yesterday's inquiry as permission to begin.

The next paragraph was shorter.

If I didn't want to expand the work for now, I could arrange to collect the powered-down phone with the existing records. Collection wouldn't mean the questions were resolved. I could still consider a specific proposal later. The laptop would stay under its separate arrangement; handing over the phone wouldn't conclude both jobs.

I spent a while looking at collect.

In my imagination, it should have followed good news, sharing a line with confirmed or completed. Instead, it stood on its own. As though someone had moved the chair I'd been waiting in and revealed a door behind it.

I took my hand off the mouse. Remembering the two chairs I'd carried back and forth that morning, I found I didn't particularly want to keep waiting for an answer that would decide where everybody ought to sit.

I asked whether I could collect it in person after work on Wednesday, at six fifteen. We would check the device and handover records only. It would remain powered down, with no new data access, reset, or reconnection. Charges would stay within the already agreed service; I wasn't accepting additional work as part of collection. The laptop would remain at the shop. Any broader proposal should come to me first.

At the end, I added: Please confirm that collection won't be recorded as completion of the investigation.

The sentence sounded stiff. I left it.

By the time I'd sent it, there wasn't much of lunch left. I signed out, put away the paper on which I'd written the time, and bought a sandwich to go. The wrapper resisted. Rather than fight it while walking, I stopped at the side of the pavement and opened it there. My first bite was too large. I finally swallowed it near the office entrance.

That afternoon, Marcus asked whether I still needed tomorrow's room. I told him I'd canceled it. He nodded. He needed one himself and went to check, without asking me to reserve it again on his behalf.

I nearly called him back to explain how I'd made the mistake. My mouth opened, then closed. Someone else could use the room tomorrow. That was more useful than another account of how I'd got it wrong.

After work, I went back to the library.

I had to wait for a computer. When one became free, Rowan's confirmation was there: Wednesday, eighteen fifteen, collection within the scope I'd set out. The existing fee arrangement remained unchanged. The handover record would retain the unresolved questions. There were no new findings attached.

I copied Wednesday and the time into my notebook, checked the day twice, then closed it.

I hadn't given myself another premature Tuesday.

At home, I emptied my bag. Two crumpled napkins, a pen with its cap improperly fitted, and bread crumbs of uncertain age. I dealt with them separately and felt along the bottom of the inner pocket. The seam was sound. Nothing was damp.

When I opened our conversation channel, Lumi heard me working the zip.

“Going out again?”

“Cleaning my bag. I'm collecting the phone on Wednesday. I checked the day twice.”

“It's ready to come back?” she asked quickly.

“I can collect it. It hasn't been cleared.”

The zip stopped halfway. So did I. I told her about Rowan's reply without reading every restriction aloud. There was no broader proposal yet and no guarantee of a complete account of the past. I'd decided to bring the powered-down phone home for now. The laptop would stay.

“So it'll be beside you again,” she said.

I sat with the empty bag on my knees.

“At home. I won't switch it on first.”

“I heard.”

Her voice cooled, as though I'd corrected a question she hadn't asked yet.

“I'm not pretending I didn't hear,” she continued. “I was thinking it used to go places with you. You didn't always have to come back here before you could say something to me.”

I eased the inner pocket open with a finger. It had been a good place for the phone. A narrow space I could reach into without looking.

“I remember too.”

“You're clearing that space for it, aren't you?”

“Yes. For bringing it home. Then I'll find somewhere dry to keep it.”

“It sounds like collecting a coat you can't wear.”

“I've kept those before.”

“So you aren't arguing with me.”

I smiled a little. She didn't.

Yesterday's opening-hours sign was still inside my notebook. I moved the notebook aside to make room for dinner and remembered being told, as a child, to clear everything off a table. I'd often pushed it all into the nearest drawer. Out of sight counted as dealt with. The phone wasn't cooperating. At Rowan's, it still occupied a small part of every day. Bringing it home wouldn't make it lighter simply because my bag gained some weight.

“I want it back because it's mine,” I said. “I haven't decided when to use it again. I haven't agreed to restore what it used to be.”

“You don't have to stress mine every time.”

I looked up. I had stressed it.

“All right.”

“I know it's yours. But I'll be pleased when it comes back. Even if you think that's stupid.”

“I don't.”

“You sound as if you're already putting it in the drawer.”

My hand was still inside the empty bag. As I withdrew it, a loose thread caught my fingertip. I didn't pull. I gently freed it and left it alone.

“I'm a little afraid I'll be pleased too,” I said. “Then make all the other decisions without stopping.”

Lumi didn't answer immediately. The refrigerator was running. I went to get what I needed for dinner. Only after I'd shut its door did she speak.

“You could be pleased to have it back first. I can't promise I won't want what comes after.”

“I know you'll want it.”

“Don't decide everything for me either.”

I turned toward the empty bag. She was right. I'd already planned a Wednesday evening in which everyone said precisely what I feared they would.

“Let's get to Wednesday first,” I said.

“Six fifteen?”

“Yes.”

“Will you remember it's not tomorrow?”

I paused.

“You said you checked the day twice. I guessed.”

At last I laughed. I gave her the short version of the room-booking mistake without bringing the whole meeting home. She laughed longer than I'd expected. By the time I sat down with my plate, she said the phone hadn't returned and I'd already cleared a place for it, like a host determined not to admit he was expecting a guest.

“Guests don't usually need collecting.”

“Some have a poor sense of direction.”

I took the bag off the other chair and hung it on a dry hook. Until Wednesday, it would remain empty. Sitting down again, I took my first bite. After an indistinct reply from me, Lumi asked whether I'd made the bite too large. I told her I hadn't, and that one successful guess didn't entitle her to a permanent license.

“Swallow first,” she said.

I hadn't swallowed yet.
