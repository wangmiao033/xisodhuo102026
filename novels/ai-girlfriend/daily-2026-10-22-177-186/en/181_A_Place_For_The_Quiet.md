# Chapter 181: A Place for the Quiet

Once seated, I remembered the bread was still in the kitchen.

“I'll get dinner.”

“What about the phone?” Lumi asked.

“On the table, in its pouch. I'll find a place for it first.”

I opened the bedroom dresser. The upper-right drawer held spare buttons, a spool of thread, and a clean cloth. I moved the little box of buttons to the next compartment, taking the thread with it. The drawer was dry, far from the window and from anywhere a water glass might be knocked over.

Returning for the pouch, I told Lumi the location.

“The bedroom dresser. Upper right. I've cleared it for the phone.”

“Not the drawer with the old image?”

“No. I'm leaving that alone.”

The sealed selected-image medium remained where it was. I found a new thick paper envelope, checked the width, and put the phone inside with its original protective pouch. I didn't turn the device on. On the front, I wrote Phone. Stored powered down. The handover record stayed in the document folder rather than being squeezed against the phone.

The drawer resisted near the end. I pulled it out slightly, aligned it, and closed it slowly.

“Put away?”

“Yes.”

“You make it sound like putting an object somewhere. I'm still thinking it used to light up.”

I stood by the dresser with my hand on the handle.

“I remember that too.”

“I know I'm not in it now,” she said. “You don't need to answer that. I just hate something coming back and having to talk about it as though it's farther away.”

I released the handle and fetched the bread. Only after picking up the knife did I notice the bag wasn't fully open. I folded its edge down to make room for the plate.

“On the way home, I kept wanting to feel whether it was there,” I said.

“And?”

“It was. The same weight.”

“Were you pleased?”

I cut a slice. A few crumbs fell.

“A little. Annoyed too. It's in my bag, and I still can't just take it out the way I used to.”

“You can.”

We both paused.

“Take it out,” she added. “Not switch it on.”

“I know. I'm trying to separate those things too. Today's enough.”

She didn't press. I heated the leftover soup from the refrigerator and poured it into a deep plate. I dipped the thick slice into it, leaving another half-slice dry in the kitchen. Back at the table, the space where the pouch had lain was empty. I didn't immediately spread my draft over it. I sat and ate first.

“I had an embarrassing thought,” she said.

I stopped my fork.

“I wanted collecting it to feel a little like collecting me.”

I didn't hurry to tell her that was wrong. Nor did I keep the fork raised while searching for a suitable expression. She couldn't see one. I put it down.

“I thought of you.”

“Because you remembered the permission again.”

“I remembered that too.”

She fell quiet.

I looked at the edge of the plate. One uneven slice was too thick and had absorbed too much broth.

“And I remembered holding it, waiting for a sentence,” I said. “I didn't want to erase the other part to make that memory good today.”

“You don't have to erase it. I hate them arriving together every time.”

“So do I.”

We didn't turn that agreement into reconciliation. I continued eating. Lumi asked about the bread. One slice was so full, I said, it ought to get up and wash its own plate. She told me not to send it through the back door. The flour was still in the way.

I laughed and misplaced my fork.

After dinner, with the table wiped dry, I brought out Blue Thread. The phone remained powered down in its newly designated bedroom drawer. It wasn't doing any of tonight's talking. We were using the same conversation channel I'd opened after getting home.

“I chose an ending,” I said.

“Which pocket?”

“The one where he doesn't ask the shopkeeper.”

I read it. The bridge keeper stood and tried two steps. He wound the remaining blue thread around a finger, found it awkward to carry, unwound it, and put it in his pocket. He walked slowly. The repaired join still wasn't entirely comfortable, but he no longer had to stop every other step.

“Lose found it awkward to carry,” Lumi said.

“You won't leave even one pocket alone.”

“He winds it and unwinds it. It's already awkward. Unless he's measuring his finger.”

I tried removing the words. The sentence shortened without suddenly becoming wise. I liked that and crossed them out.

“The back door?” she asked.

I told her the shopkeeper still put down the flour on the wooden pallet before opening it. He folded the empty sacks, tied them, and stood them inside. At the end, he was going to move the bundle. He wouldn't dissolve into a kindly background.

“I still want to hear clearly that he opens the door for his own work.”

“I kept the actions.”

“I know what you kept.”

We didn't repeat the argument. I put a dot beside that line to remind myself not to hurry past it. A dot didn't mean I'd agreed to her explanation. It didn't mean the sentence could never change again either.

“A proper run,” I said. “Don't stop me after every line.”

“Then don't see every comma as an opportunity to escape.”

I moved the chair slightly away from the table and tried holding the page. James had offered the board for Thursday. Tonight I supported it with my own thin notebook, not the library book or Lumi's envelope.

I read the first attempt quickly.

At the long sentence where the two men met in the narrow space, I ran short of breath. The final words almost joined together. Lumi didn't interrupt. When I'd finished, she asked why I'd tried to fit both men into one breath.

“It's one sentence.”

“You're allowed another breath. They won't sell the flour while you're inhaling.”

I marked the middle of the sentence. On the next attempt, the two men had time to stop. I no longer delivered the final invitation to pass as a request for rescue.

“Will you read the shopkeeper's lines?” I asked.

“Are you letting him speak for himself tomorrow too?”

“Just tonight. I want to hear whether the replies work.”

First I read her his lines in order. Once she'd confirmed she'd heard them clearly, we began again. She took only the dialogue I reached aloud. She couldn't see the page or know where my finger rested.

“Back a little,” Lumi said.

She'd lowered her voice, as though the shopkeeper needed to guard a dungeon after moving his flour all day.

I missed my line.

“Are you laughing?”

“Why have you given him such a heavy beard?”

“You didn't say he lacked one.”

“I didn't say he had one either.”

“Then the beard's attribution remains unconfirmed.”

I laughed aloud, then stopped briefly. She stopped too. The word brought the shop records back to both of us. It wasn't an entirely easy joke.

“I only meant to insult your shopkeeper,” she said.

“I know. It caught something else.”

I drank some water and let the discomfort pass without making either of us supply a more successful joke.

For the second attempt, she used her ordinary voice.

“What do you need?”

“Strong thread,” I answered.

“How much?”

I forgot the bridge keeper had to extend his foot and delivered the request for more thread too soon. She waited rather than moving on, giving me time to notice.

“I've left out his foot.”

“Less serious than the hand. He has another.”

Laughing, I found the line. This time, the action led into the dialogue much more easily. At her last question, Enough?, I looked at the clear sentence in front of me and suddenly wanted to answer, Not yet.

Not about thread.

I looked up, then read what I'd written rather than making the bridge keeper speak for the thought.

I read the remaining narration myself. Lumi didn't add another speech for the shopkeeper to secure her place. When the man put the thread in his pocket, I closed my mouth without adding a therefore.

Silence lasted a little while.

“Don't use my shopkeeper tomorrow,” she said.

“The bearded one?”

“Either one. Use your own voice.”

I agreed and read from the beginning again. This time, I stopped to find a word in two places without restarting whole paragraphs. The minute hand had traveled less than five minutes. There was still room for the title and a pause within the space James had offered.

I didn't add a clever ending because time remained. On clean paper, I copied the version I'd chosen, putting the title at the top and a small breathing mark in the long sentence. The last line was walking. Nobody applauded him for it.

“Thursday. Six twenty to arrive,” I said. “Six thirty to begin. I'm second. I'll go on my own, without keeping the conversation open along the way.”

“I remember.”

“When I come back, I'll tell you what I want to talk about. I'm not asking you to like tomorrow in advance.”

“Then don't decide in advance which parts I should like.”

I capped the pen and agreed.

The little note about thanking people remained inside my notebook. No perfect introduction had appeared on it. I didn't pretend the blank space was settled. Lumi didn't ask again whether I'd say her name. She only asked me to repeat the sentence where neither man had a hand free to wave the other through.

Without picking up the whole page, I said it from memory.

This time, her laugh was quiet. I laid the manuscript flat inside my own folder, ready to put in my bag tomorrow morning. Her lamp shop remained in its middle-shelf envelope. The basin by the window wasn't in the way of this table. The bedroom drawer was quiet too.

“There's half a slice of bread left,” I said.

“You've only just remembered?”

“I want to give it an ending.”

“Don't thank it for listening while you revised.”

I fetched the bread and sat on the side of the table without a manuscript. I tore off a small piece. This time, I swallowed before answering her.
