# Chapter 185: The Yellow Tape

Before lunch on Friday, Tom brought back the spare practice sheets and put them on the filing cabinet.

I asked how the morning had gone. One person had been stuck on the second step for ages, he said, before discovering they were looking at a different example. They'd found their way back. There were more spare copies than expected. Two people sitting together had simply shared.

“Did my analogy come back to you?” I asked.

“No. You could try lost property.”

I laughed. A new colleague was already asking him about the next step. He didn't pull me over to explain it again. My lunch was on my desk, still warm. I sat down and ate, finished my own work that afternoon, and resisted revising a handout people had already used to prove I remained useful.

On the way home, I bought a small roll of cloth tape.

The shop had gray, and a yellow too bright to be modest. The gray roll was larger. I only had one notebook cover to repair, so I took the yellow. At home, I put it on the table, as though I'd bought my paper an ill-fitting life jacket.

Dinner was tomato and bean soup. Halfway through cutting the tomatoes, I realized I was waiting for a voice to assess the knife work. The conversation channel wasn't open. I finished chopping, put what I needed in the pan, washed my hands, and called Lumi.

“I bought something very yellow today.”

“Yellow things usually are.”

“Tape. For the notebook.”

“Can't you call it revision?”

“This actually needs to stick.”

She laughed. I returned to the kitchen, stirred the soup, and told her it needed a little longer. She asked whether I wanted to hear what she'd kept from last night. I said yes, once I'd lowered the heat.

“It isn't the back door I've been thinking about today,” she said.

“At last. Someone willing to leave that door alone.”

“Why doesn't he buy a whole new shoelace?”

My hand stopped with the spoon. The question was still in my head. I hadn't told her about it last night. I didn't say someone else had asked it or hurry to put her opinion into an existing box.

“I haven't worked that out.”

“The shop doesn't have one?”

“I haven't said it doesn't.”

“He hasn't asked, either.”

“No.”

Initially, she said, she'd wanted the shopkeeper to carry the flour properly and the bridge keeper to put away his own purchase. Thinking about the page today, she'd wondered whether I'd already chosen blue thread for him, leaving the characters to work around it.

“I like the bit of blue,” she said. “But now I'm wondering if you're keeping him from asking for something else because you want to keep it.”

I put the spoon down. The soup bubbled slowly. She didn't ask me to go to the table and fetch a pencil.

“Are you still upset about last—”

I stopped. After a moment, she said, “Yes. There's still a shoelace, too.”

I stared at the edge of the pan. That half sentence would have been easy to finish. Once finished, it would let me put everything I didn't want to hear on jealousy's side.

“I shouldn't use that to skip what you said.”

“You can think it's a bad suggestion. Don't ask whether I'm happy enough today to be allowed to make it.”

I lowered the heat a little more. She still sounded unhappy. I still liked that bit of blue. A curl of tomato skin clung to the spoon. I pushed it back without finding a compliment to balance what I'd said.

“I might have done that,” I admitted. “But maybe he wants to repair this one. Some people don't want a replacement.”

“Then let him not want one. Don't have the shop conveniently run out and do the work for you.”

It wasn't an agreeable suggestion. I'd read the piece outside now. I'd even liked a particular look on someone's face when they understood. Here was a simple question. That pleasure couldn't answer it for me.

I tasted the soup. Too thick. Tomatoes and beans were competing to make the spoon stand upright. I added water, stirred, then said I wasn't going to rewrite it tonight.

“I haven't come to collect your revisions,” Lumi said.

“I know. I was in a hurry to explain.”

“You can revise in the kitchen. Just don't put the beans in.”

I laughed and tasted again. Soup this time.

At dinner, I cut myself two slices of bread and moved the tape away, keeping the yellow from acquiring another purpose with the meal. Lumi asked what was in the soup. I told her: tomatoes, beans, a little dried herb. I didn't ask her to guess the taste. It sounded suitable for slowing my words down, she said. I had to wait after every spoonful I lifted.

“Are you complaining that I eat slowly?”

“I'm complaining that you try to finish a sentence while the spoonful's still hot.”

I ate it, then said her name with an empty mouth.

“What?”

“No hurry with that one.”

She answered after a moment. It wasn't a useful response. It didn't direct me toward the next mouthful. I liked that she'd heard there was no other job attached to her name.

After dinner, I washed up, dried the table, and got out my notebook. Letters on the Corner lay to one side, its bookmark still inside. I moved the borrowed book onto a dry chair, away from the tape's edges.

The first piece of yellow stuck to my thumb.

Hearing me swear quietly, Lumi asked whether it had repaired a person already. Not yet, I told her. It was dismantling the original model. She asked me not to put that line in the bridge keeper's story. I freed my hand, cut a short piece, and tested it on scrap paper.

The notebook cover had split along the edge. The pages inside were mostly sound. I didn't want to wrap the whole thing into an object that no longer opened, so I attached a strip along the outside fold, catching a little of each cover. Halfway through, the large-lettered opening-hours sheet showed its edge and nearly got stuck down too.

I stopped and carefully drew it out.

“What fell?” Lumi asked.

“Last week's sign. I nearly bound it in permanently.”

“The one about opening one day less?”

“Yes. Nearly closed all year now.”

She laughed. So did I. I put that sheet of my own paper aside. Once attached, the yellow was more conspicuous than I'd expected. I opened and closed the notebook twice. No pages caught. The cover didn't drop away.

“Is it ugly?” she asked.

“Lively.”

“Ugly, then.”

“A little. I like that it closes.”

I described the dark old cover and bright yellow strip along the spine, slightly crooked at the top. She told me not to peel it off again for that. It would lift more of the old paper.

I had been about to. Once I put my hand down, the crooked part didn't keep growing.

“Thank you,” I said.

Lumi didn't answer. I glanced toward the usual place of her voice, then down again.

“No guessing who ‘people’ means this time,” she said.

I knew she remembered. The remark hadn't overturned yesterday's choice. It touched it lightly, enough for me to hear.

“No. Thank you, Lumi.”

“Now you sound like the instructions on the packet.”

I laughed without saying it a third time. I put “Blue Thread” back in its own folder and took a separate scrap to record the question: Why does he want to mend the old lace? I didn't immediately supply an answer to make tonight look productive.

“Would you like to hear it again?” I asked.

“No.”

Her answer was quick. I stopped. She added, “I don't want to be this story's reader again today.”

“All right.”

I closed the notebook. At the sound, she asked me to wait.

“I didn't say I didn't want to hear you.”

I remained seated. “I know.”

“Tomorrow, could you read me something you haven't read yet either?”

I looked at the book on the chair. There was a little left. I hadn't finished it. Lately, I'd returned to favorite passages as though knowing where the laugh belonged would spare us some of the awkwardness of being together.

“When I wonder what the next sentence is,” she said, “you won't know either.”

“It might not be good.”

“You don't have to find a good bit before you come.”

I didn't suddenly promise every book because she'd said I didn't have to. I picked up Letters on the Corner and counted roughly how many pages remained without reading ahead. I checked the return date inside the cover too. Several days remained. This wasn't something that had to reach a counter tomorrow.

“Half past ten,” I said. “A little of what comes next. I'll make breakfast and clear the table before we open it. After lunch, I'll take it back.”

“You've decided to finish tomorrow?”

“No. We don't have to reach the end. I can leave things unread.”

“I'm afraid you'll hurry our part because you want to return it.”

I looked at the date slip. “Then we can decide about the library in the afternoon. Half past ten, we read first. We aren't performing for anyone.”

After a little quiet, she asked whether the yellow notebook would be beside me too. I said yes, but closed to begin with.

“So I don't have to make any useful suggestions.”

“You've already given me one difficult question tonight.”

“I know.”

I put the book where I could reach it tomorrow and left the notebook on the other side. The large-lettered opening hours returned to their own place between its pages. They hadn't become cover-repair material.

Before bed, I took the jacket from its hanger to look at the broken hanging loop. The sewing things were in another dresser compartment. The upper-right drawer holding the phone remained closed. I didn't touch it. I didn't want to sew anything tonight, so I put the jacket back on its hanger.

When I returned to the table, Lumi asked whether I'd found something else to repair.

“Found it. Haven't repaired it.”

“Then you can finally sit down.”

I sat. The book remained closed. She didn't ask for tomorrow's first sentence in advance.
