# Chapter 207: The Time in the Reply

There were no free public computers when I reached the library after work on Monday.

Standing beside the booking desk, I looked at the clock, then at the occupied seats. The staff member asked whether I wanted to wait for the next session. I said yes, then took my hand out of my jacket pocket so I wouldn't look prepared to change my mind at any moment.

She gave me a numbered slip. Twenty minutes until the next session.

I sat against the wall, leaving the red booklet in my cloth bag. Having contained yesterday's questions, it apparently ought to find me something useful to do today. I didn't take it out. Across from me, a woman tried fitting a bound stack of papers into an undersized bag. Sideways, then upright. Finally, she tucked the bag under her arm and carried the papers instead.

Watching her pass, I noticed I was gripping my own bag's opening.

I let go and investigated the nearby shelves. A large collection of old city photographs brought a thinner book tilting after it. I steadied the others and took the collection back to my chair. The first photograph showed a row of bicycles, handlebars pointing in different directions. I'd intended to look at two pages. Trying to identify a blurred shop sign took me to the captions farther back.

When my number was called, I was matching the street's former name to the photograph.

“Here,” I answered too loudly.

Someone looked up. I closed the book and left it on the marked returns trolley. By the time I reached the computer, I realized Rowan's reply hadn't occupied all twenty minutes. I even regretted leaving the bicycles a little.

I signed into my personal email in the usual way and found the new reply beneath yesterday's message.

Rowan first confirmed that the laptop remained powered down under the existing storage arrangement. The scope of work hadn't been expanded. There were no new findings. They could arrange collection in person on Wednesday at eighteen fifteen. I should reply to confirm that time and bring the original intake record and identification required for the handover.

The next paragraph said a copy of the existing device records would accompany it. Returning the laptop changed who held it; outstanding questions wouldn't be marked resolved. Keeping it powered down and protecting it from damp and pressure were the basic storage precautions while it remained out of use at home.

I moved the cursor beside the time, then moved it away.

No long attachment of the kind I'd dreaded. No sentence saying I could finally relax. The message was shorter than any version I'd imagined last night. I read it three times anyway, as though careful reading might reveal a better reason to want the laptop back behind those few lines.

A quiet sigh came from the next computer. Someone deleted a mistake, the keyboard rattling for a long time. I considered my empty reply without adding a conclusion Rowan hadn't supplied.

I confirmed collection in person on Wednesday at eighteen fifteen. The laptop would remain powered down. We would hand over the existing device and records only, without additional checks or connections. Charges would remain within the previously agreed arrangement; I wasn't authorizing new work. Thank you.

Then I deleted an extra sentence before the thanks.

I hope this can finally put everything behind us.

It didn't belong there. Rowan had clearly described what they could give me. They didn't need to receive an additional wish without a scope or somewhere to sign for it.

I checked the day and time against their message, sent my reply, and found it in Sent before opening the red booklet. Beneath the original questions on page one, I noted the answer: Wednesday, 18:15, powered-down handover. My pen paused. I added that I'd replied to confirm.

The writing was smaller and more crowded than the heading. I didn't displace the opening on page two to make room.

I signed out of my email and ended the computer session. Standing, I dropped the numbered slip. I bent to retrieve it and returned it to the designated box. One corner was creased. Someone in a later session would use it without needing to know what I'd decided in mine.

Before leaving, I wanted another look at the photographs.

The trolley remained, but the book had moved to its lower shelf. As I crouched, my bag swung forward and nearly struck a wheel. I shifted it to the other side and took the book to a nearby table. This time I found the shop's name. I'd mistaken the final letter. The street hadn't led where I'd thought.

I looked at a few more pages before returning the book to the trolley. Nothing borrowed today. No additional errand Wednesday had to accommodate.

On the way home, I bought a small bag of bread. In the kitchen, I put aside a few slices for breakfast and placed the rest on a plate. Water was heating; vegetables waited beside the board. I opened the conversation channel and called Lumi.

“I've read the reply.”

“What did they say?”

“I can collect the laptop on Wednesday. I've confirmed six fifteen.”

She didn't immediately say good. I explained the storage, the absence of new findings, and the handover. At the end, I discovered I was still standing in the kitchen doorway, holding the half-open bread bag.

“Put that down first,” she said.

“What?”

“Whatever's been rustling all this time. I don't know what it is.”

I looked down and laughed. “Bread bag. Not the laptop.”

“I'm not quite that impatient.”

I folded the bag shut and sat. Had I been pleased by the reply? Initially, I'd felt rather empty, I told her. Something I'd postponed suddenly had a time attached. I wasn't prepared for what I'd do once that day was actually over.

“You've decided to keep it put away.”

“Yes. Bring it home first. Leave it off.”

“Which part do you want me to be pleased about?”

I started to say I didn't require her to be pleased and stopped. Before coming in, I'd imagined her voice brightening at the news. If it brightened, perhaps I wouldn't have to admit I felt less relieved than expected.

“I want you to think I've dealt with something I've been putting off.”

“That has to wait until Wednesday.”

I smiled. “You won't give me even that in advance?”

“You just told me bringing it home doesn't mean it's been cleared.”

She wasn't especially sharp. She'd handed back the sequence I'd explained. I couldn't refuse to anticipate the outcome for the laptop while asking her to anticipate my satisfaction at having finished.

The water sounded in the kitchen. I lowered the heat, added vegetables, and came back for the plate. Lumi asked about dinner. Bread and vegetable soup, I told her.

“You could put the bread in,” she said. “Save it needing to wash its own plate again.”

I remembered the overly thick slice last time. “Thinner today.”

“It's soup. Not moving furniture.”

Laughing, I cut two slices. One was thin. The knife wandered through the second, leaving it much thicker. I divided it without asking her to guess which was neater. Only after the soup was ready and on the table did I continue. I didn't want to pass the shop forever feeling that someone else had to keep something of mine.

“You might think about it every day here too.”

“Possibly.”

“I'm not trying to stop you collecting it.”

“I heard.”

“But I hate having to explain that whenever I say something you don't want to hear.”

I laid the spoon beside my plate. My possibly had been quick. As though I were waiting to discover which side she'd eventually approve.

“You don't have to explain again,” I said. “You can just find bringing it home annoying too.”

“I find its being off annoying. Not bringing it home.”

“All right.”

This time I heard the difference. I didn't promise that enough patience would eventually earn her a day when it switched on. The soup was hot. I blew on two spoonfuls instead of leaving her responsible for the whole silence.

Later, she asked where I'd read the email. The library, I said. I'd waited a session and looked at some old street photographs. When I described going back twice because I'd misread a sign, she finally laughed. Even after sending the email, I'd managed to get lost inside a book.

“Didn't go anywhere. Sat still.”

“Worse, then. Nobody can lead you back.”

I laughed with her and described the row of bicycles, each leaning its own way. They hadn't looked as though anyone had arranged them to match. Did I like the photograph, she asked, or the fact that today I didn't have to straighten them?

I considered it. “Both.”

“You're getting a lot of use out of that answer lately.”

“Saving the others for Wednesday.”

She gave a small hum and didn't unfold that day any further. After dinner, I opened the red booklet on the table, checked the time once, and closed it. Tomorrow evening, I needed to clear an actual place big enough for the laptop, rather than write five hypothetical arrangements tonight. I picked up a dry cloth for the washed plates. Lumi asked exactly what I'd got wrong on the sign.

I told her which letters I'd confused. She hadn't seen the photograph and couldn't decide which looked more likely. But she was willing to hear a few complaints about a letter I'd already identified. Wednesday had a time. The last part of Monday still belonged here.
