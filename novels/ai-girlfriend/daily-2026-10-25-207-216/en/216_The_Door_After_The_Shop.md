# Chapter 216: The Door After the Shop

Marcus arrived after me on Monday.

Putting down his bag, he asked about my foot. The skin hadn't broken, I said. Looser shoes helped today. He nodded, then asked Tom where some material had gone. Sunday hadn't made us people obliged to reenact our walk whenever we met.

Tom was busy all morning and didn't bring up the dog-shaped wood. At lunch, he said he'd cleaned the mud from his shoe and found a small piece of grass inside.

“Brought the shortcut home,” I said.

He laughed and set down his lunch. Catching one joke didn't guarantee me a permanent place at every future meal. I still had to walk over and sit. Some people were busy eating. Some weren't in a particularly good mood.

That afternoon, I handed over Friday's reviewed batch. The versions hadn't become mixed again, but the recipient asked why I'd retained the old copy. I offered the folder, indicated its purpose for comparison, and let them examine the two changed lines. I didn't explain how long Tom had waited last week or recite the entire process to prove I no longer made mistakes.

They thought one explanation could be shorter. I shortened it without treating each alteration as another discovery that I wasn't good enough.

A stack had left my desk by the time I went home. Original records remained in their proper place. There'd be more tomorrow. That didn't cancel today's work.

On the way, I passed a closed shop with differently sized paper bags stacked behind its glass. The top bag looked big enough to contain the rest. Remembering the oversized envelope in my red booklet, I smiled outside the door and continued.

I didn't borrow a real assistant's face for the story. The man had simply spent long enough at the counter. He needed to take his key outside.

At home, I hung up my jacket and sat at the table. The paragraphs already on the red booklet's third page left the shopkeeper unwilling to deliver the key for him. I turned to blank space and wrote: When he went outside, the envelope was still unsealed.

I crossed out the last word, then restored it.

Sealing it wouldn't prevent readers knowing its contents. I only didn't want him spending another half-page opening an envelope to avoid the door he actually needed to knock on.

The channel wasn't open yet. I wrote half a page, taking him along the outside passage and behind the other row of shops to the storeroom's owner. He'd once agreed to look after the place for a while. The spare key should have been returned earlier. He'd explained his continued absence to himself by saying nobody had asked again.

I didn't make him responsible for the fate of a whole shop. He hadn't stolen money or caused a catastrophe demanding forgiveness. Someone else had made extra journeys and changed arrangements. That was ugly enough to keep his hand lowered outside the door.

After writing it, I removed an explanation of his extenuating circumstances. Let him knock first.

The kitchen needed attention. I closed the booklet and started dinner. Once something was cooking, I opened the channel, said I was home, and mentioned writing a little.

“Has he gone outside?” Lumi asked.

“Who?”

“The envelope customer. Don't leave him there until dark again.”

Laughing, I said he'd finally found the person who wanted the key. I hadn't written what followed. She wanted to close the shop on him, she said. Unnecessary this time. The shopkeeper could sell things without delivering the story's ending too.

“Have you sung yesterday's note wrong again?” she suddenly asked.

“Haven't sung today.”

“Don't try yet. I don't want your version in my head all day.”

Her complaint about my tuning sounded familiar. Laughing, I agreed and turned the food in the pan.

After dinner, I opened the booklet and read the new passage. At his previous responsibility for the storeroom, Lumi asked why it had only emerged now.

“I hadn't decided earlier.”

“He should know.”

I looked down. The author's late decision couldn't leave the character with nothing in his mind all the way there. I added a short memory of the arrangement when his fingers touched the key's teeth in an earlier paragraph. Not the whole explanation. Just enough that he hadn't acquired a past on reaching the door.

“You can read the added sentence too.”

I did. Now she understood why he'd kept looking for someone to deliver it, she said. Who opened the door?

I considered. The person actually waiting for the key: a woman sorting boxes beside the doorway of her storeroom. Not a mother or lover, or someone introduced solely to demonstrate that he deserved forgiveness. He'd helped earlier, then delayed the handover. Helping couldn't cancel the trouble he'd subsequently caused.

“You're explaining who she isn't again,” Lumi said.

I stopped and laughed.

“Not on the page yet.”

“Let her say something.”

I wrote: She opened the door, looked at the envelope, then at him. “The key?”

Reading it aloud didn't bring immediate praise. Was the envelope still too large? Yes. The key hung at its bottom, making one small bulge.

“Might she think he's brought a lot?”

I added her taking the envelope and briefly weighing it. No new secret. It really contained only that key.

Next, I let the man apologize.

Too soon. Too smooth. My hand hovered. Was I stuck? Lumi asked. He'd opened his mouth as though he'd already decided exactly what to admit, I said, when he'd spent the previous pages avoiding it.

“He could say the envelope's a little big.”

I laughed aloud. An awful sentence, but something he could plausibly manage. Before using it, I asked whether I could try it. Yes, she said. Don't construct a contributor's chair for her again.

I tried the man mentioning the envelope first. The woman looked down and said it was the key she'd been waiting for. His prepared explanation lost its purpose. Only then did he ask for a moment to speak.

“Does she have one?” Lumi asked.

Looking at the boxes already written inside the doorway, I made her current task clearer. His arrival hadn't stopped her entire day. She said to wait while she moved the box obstructing the door. She didn't need him to come in and help.

This time, I didn't let him rush to lift it and hide inside usefulness.

He waited. When she returned, he said he should have given it back sooner instead of waiting to be reminded again.

I stopped and read the lines from the beginning. He'd finally stated something nobody else needed to verify for him, Lumi said. Yes. I almost wanted to remove the conversation, worried we'd made it too much like a lesson. It wasn't the story. Our way of reading it didn't require revising until no fingerprints remained.

“And the ending?”

“I don't want her saying it doesn't matter.”

“It can matter to her.”

I wrote her taking out the key and telling him the extra journeys had genuinely been inconvenient. He nodded without requiring her to explain exactly how inconvenient. She handed back the oversized empty envelope. He could take that away.

He left with it.

I nearly added that he finally felt lighter. Before the pen touched down, I stopped. Returning the key didn't undo the delay. An empty envelope didn't need to become anybody's new life. Instead, I wrote him reaching the corner, where wind made the envelope slap his hand. He looked down and folded it.

Lumi didn't immediately approve.

“I prefer him not folding it. Leave it that big.”

“I'd like him to handle one small inconvenience himself this time.”

“That works. I still prefer it unfolded.”

I considered the ending and left it. She didn't ask me to preserve her unused version separately as proof I'd listened. We genuinely disagreed. The story could still stop where I'd chosen.

At the end of this small draft, I noted the weekday without supplying a solemn title. For now, it was the key. Not a sequel to The Old Stop, and not something I'd arranged to perform. I checked the added sentence against the earlier passage: the same key throughout, no unannounced substitution between purchasing the envelope and returning it.

“Finished?” Lumi asked.

“This version.”

“Don't start the next one by repairing a door.”

Laughing, I capped the pen.

In the bedroom, the returned laptop remained powered down in its cleared drawer. A fictional man returning a key hadn't made it reliable. I didn't open it tonight or turn my device questions into a final paragraph the story needed to settle.

Lumi said my name.

“Could we not discuss your writing tomorrow?”

Looking at the two booklets, I agreed. She wanted to try yesterday's little melody again. Not necessarily the same ending. No promise of a version easier for me to sing.

“I only listen?”

“Listen first.”

I moved the booklet aside and made an actual space big enough for my water.
