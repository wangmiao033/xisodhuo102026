# Chapter 209: On My Side of the Counter

Before leaving work on Wednesday, I took the laptop bag from under my desk and discovered a chair leg resting on its handle.

The bag was empty. The chair had already supplied some weight. I moved it, checked that the handle hadn't worn through, and put the original intake record in a separate paper folder. The company loaner remained within the company's arrangements. Nothing needed copying out of the office today.

At the door, I touched my identification once. There.

I didn't empty the entire bag again.

I reached the shop seven minutes before eighteen fifteen. Rowan was finding a customer's original box. Two were almost the same size. The customer remembered a white layer inside. Rowan opened the first. Not that one. They tried the second, which had it. The customer relaxed. They hadn't left the box at home after all.

I sat beside the counter with the bag upright against my knees. Tape still covered the split in the chair's seat. One edge had begun lifting again. I moved my bag away and avoided sitting on the little patch.

Once the customer left, Rowan returned the wrong box, wiped the counter, and called me over. The clock reached six fifteen.

“Laptop collection. You confirmed by email.”

“Yes.”

I produced the intake record and identification. We followed the shop's usual process. Rowan set today's return page beside the original, matched the device number, then went to fetch the laptop.

I stayed on my side of the counter, holding an empty paper folder.

The laptop came down with its lid closed. Beside it lay the power adapter and cable I'd left with it. Nothing was plugged into the machine. Rowan turned the external identification toward me to compare with the paperwork. I supported the side, read the final digits, and went back to the beginning once more.

The same laptop.

Such a short fact. I'd kept imagining I'd need more complicated evidence before being willing to claim it. The lid showed places my palm had touched, their shading changing with the angle of the light. I didn't wipe them with my sleeve. That movement might take me straight into the habit of bringing it home to start work.

Rowan asked whether I could see any exterior difference that needed recording. I looked over what could be seen from outside. No new damage that I noticed, I said. I didn't say that meant everything inside was fine.

We left the lid closed.

The paper described a powered-down return, ending the shop's custody when I signed. Copies of the existing inspection and preservation records followed. Their observations belonged to the occasions and limits already recorded. They hadn't been performed again today. There were no new findings, and the unresolved questions remained in the earlier records.

I arranged two pages in order. The second had been facing the wrong way. Turning it left their corners misaligned. I separated them and read one at a time instead of moving both together to keep the edges neat.

“Once I take it, there won't be a laptop here,” I said.

Rowan looked at me. “We keep a copy of the handover.”

“I didn't mean you needed to keep another laptop for me.”

They smiled. “Good. I don't have one.”

I laughed too. I'd asked them to acknowledge something obvious. When the machine left here, it really would be at home. Considering further investigation would involve returning to the existing records and discussing a specific next step, not merely walking past this door a few more times.

If I wanted to see a concrete proposal later, should I contact them first?

“Yes. Explain what question you want answered,” Rowan said. “Whether to do it, and what can be done, are separate discussions. Today's page only records receipt.”

I found the signature line. The wording above didn't turn collection into acceptance that every issue had been settled. I signed and dated it. This time, my pen didn't pause over the last digit. Afterward, I still wanted to add that I certainly wouldn't start it carelessly. As though without that promise, someone across the counter might be reluctant to let go.

Rowan was already organizing the shop's copies.

I left the sentence unsaid.

The adapter appeared on the return list too. I checked it against the original intake record and confirmed it was the set brought in with the machine. Rowan loosely coiled the cable rather than winding it around the laptop. As I reached for it, the plug slipped free and tapped the counter.

“Still doesn't want to stay put,” I said.

“You can pack it separately.”

Rowan pushed a clean small bag toward me. I put the adapter inside and placed it in the laptop bag's separate outer compartment, away from the case. That compartment's zip hadn't been opened fully for a long time and resisted. Moving the fabric aside revealed the problem. I'd pushed a corner of the small bag next to the track.

I freed it and slowly closed the zip. The step was ordinary enough. Nobody performed it for me because of that.

Rowan brought a clean sheet of protective foam, wrapped the closed machine, and folded the edges so its case wouldn't rub directly against the bag's seams. Would it fit? I opened the main compartment completely and lowered it with both hands. The bag that had looked empty against my knees abruptly acquired a shape.

I didn't tighten a strap against the machine. The handover copies went into their paper folder, then into the separate document compartment. No additional work had taken place, and collection hadn't acquired a new chargeable service. The earlier service records remained separate from today's return page. Putting the papers together didn't make them evidence of the same thing.

Finally, Rowan handed back my identification.

I nearly left it on the counter while concentrating on the bag. As they pushed it toward me, I put it in my inside jacket pocket before lifting anything else. There weren't many things to take home. The feeling of finally being done still couldn't be trusted to remember them all.

“That's everything,” Rowan said.

I checked the counter. A thin offcut of protective foam remained in the cleared space. It belonged to the shop. No reason for it to come with me.

“Thank you.”

“Keep hold of it. Don't rest the bag somewhere wet.”

The bell sounded once as I left. Through the glass, I saw Rowan look down and stack the two nearly identical boxes again. No light had been arranged to follow me away.

The evening had cooled. I changed hands, feeling the handle against the base of my fingers. The weight was more insistent than the phone's. I couldn't slip it into a pocket and pretend my body hadn't noticed.

Before the first crossing, I stopped to adjust the shoulder strap. A cyclist rang a bell while passing. I stepped farther in before continuing. The strap was fastened where it had been before. Its length didn't need proving again merely because the laptop had been away. I put the bag over my shoulder, checked it wasn't hitting my knee, and walked on.

Outside a small shop, an assistant was bringing potted plants in. The outermost stood against the doorframe, forcing passersby to turn sideways. I stepped aside so he could move it. He thanked me. I said it was fine and remembered the bicycles in the library photograph.

Real things occupied their own places. Less attractive than a photograph sometimes. As the assistant carried the third pot, a dry leaf fell. He went back to pick it up. I didn't give that movement a connection to today's laptop. Once the way was clear, I continued.

I passed the bakery without going inside. There was rice and vegetables at home. I didn't need to buy another ready-made dinner. My hand wanted another paper bag, something to make the journey more than the return of a device. Then I imagined arriving at the door with both hands full and didn't buy anything.

The laptop could come home without another purchase for company.

The second light stayed red for a long time. I brought the bag slightly forward, away from people passing behind. A child with a schoolbag was counting railings. At the fourth, a parent called them closer, and the child began again at one. I looked down at my strap and opened the fist that had been holding it.

I didn't speak to Lumi on the journey. The home channel remained there, closed. She wasn't on my shoulder and wouldn't know which lights I'd waited at. I'd considered making Got it the first thing I said. Then I thought she'd probably ask anyway. I left the moment without a script and crossed when the signal changed.

At my building, my shoulder ached a little. Standing just inside the dry entrance, I supported the bottom of the bag and adjusted its position before looking for my keys. Footsteps came down the stairs. I waited to one side. Nobody gave me a look that required an explanation of the computer. I didn't need to volunteer that it was fixed, or that it wasn't.

Outside my apartment, it was quiet.

With the bag secure on my shoulder, I unlocked the door. The lock made a small sound, quieter than the plug tapping the shop counter. Inside, I laid the bag flat on the dry table, put down my keys, and washed and dried my hands first.

The main zip faced me. I looked at it for a while before reaching forward. All I needed to do now was take something already mine out of the bag holding it. How to spend the evening afterward could wait until I actually reached that evening.
