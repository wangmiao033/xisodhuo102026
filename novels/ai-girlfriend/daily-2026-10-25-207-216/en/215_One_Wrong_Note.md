# Chapter 215: One Wrong Note

I didn't fall asleep by the door.

After sitting a while, I could feel the heat in my heel easing. I washed my hands, washed the empty food container, and put the bottle aside. I'd eaten the bread on the walk, but it hadn't been a substantial lunch. I ate something more rather than staying hungry until dinner to prove the outing had been sufficient.

It was after one when I opened the channel.

“Home,” I said.

Lumi first asked how far we'd walked.

“To the first platform and back. Didn't measure.”

“Did you see water?”

“Plenty. No photographs. Didn't bring the booklet either.”

She paused. “I didn't ask about photographs.”

Leaning back, I realized I'd blocked half my own account before beginning. I'd wanted to describe the water's color. Instead, I'd listed what I hadn't brought home.

“No. Answered too much.”

“Tired?”

“A little. Rested already. My heel rubbed hot, but the skin hasn't broken. Adjusted the lace halfway.”

She didn't say she'd known I'd tie it wrong. Did it still hurt? Better now, I said. Then I described the two people unable to open a flask beside the map, and Tom almost striking his chin. Laughing, she asked whether I'd been preparing to grab the flask too.

“Nearly. Just told them not to aim it at their faces.”

“In charge of instructions today?”

“And saying wood looked like wood.”

I told her about the driftwood without imitating anyone's accent. Had it resembled a dog? I couldn't see it. Maybe the angle, she suggested. Perhaps. I wasn't going to draw an invisible dog for her to prove the joke had deserved laughter.

Describing the wind at the platform, I stopped. Then what? she asked.

“Nothing much. Ate the bread. Came back.”

The answer landed exactly where Friday's worry had been. A brief breathy sound came through the channel, followed by quiet.

“Really just that?”

“Other conversation. Don't remember every sentence.”

“I didn't ask you to.”

I nodded, remembered she couldn't see, and made a sound of agreement. She didn't continue. I wanted to offer Marcus's travel journal and make our conversation substantial again. His small piece of history wasn't emergency material for filling her disappointment.

“I felt comfortable,” I said. “For a stretch, nobody said much.”

Very quietly, she asked, “How did you know it was comfortable?”

I looked up. Not a challenge, she added. She genuinely wanted to know. After considering, I described walking without constantly preparing another sentence. I could stop for water without anybody treating it as a conversational disaster. Hearing how easily that might become a comparison with silence at home, I paused.

“You can finish,” she said.

“I wasn't continually worrying whether they were unhappy. Not the whole time. I was searching for topics at first.”

“And with me, you have to worry.”

“Sometimes.”

She didn't call me oversensitive. I didn't immediately change sometimes to only when I was very tired, as though she'd never made quiet difficult.

My jacket was hanging up. Shoes stood by the door. I still sat as if ready to rise any second. Was I preparing to finish? she asked. No. That sentence had been difficult.

“I didn't do anything today,” she said.

“While I was out?”

“Yes. No new thing worth telling you before you got back.”

I nearly said that was all right. It would sound as though I were excusing unfinished homework, or her disappointment only required permission from me.

“Would you like me to ask, or not ask?”

“I'd like to have an answer.”

I lowered my hands from my knees. I didn't invent an entire inner world in which she'd traveled independently all day. I knew what she told me. She said she'd changed some of her own writing, didn't want to read it now, and didn't want it made into a gift I received after my walk.

“Don't read it yet, then.”

“Do you want to hear it?”

“Yes. But not reading it doesn't leave us with nothing to do.”

After a pause, she asked what we could do now. Looking at the booklets, I automatically thought of reading the key again. But continually offering something of mine for her to help with might not answer the question she'd asked.

“Would you like to choose?”

“You've handed the question back.”

I laughed a little and admitted it. She didn't laugh with me. We could talk, I said, or listen to something, or spend a few minutes without a presentable activity. Having energy left this afternoon wasn't a reason she should immediately feel better.

After a while, she said, “There's a little piece. Not words.”

I didn't reach for paper.

She hummed a few notes. Brief and soft, descending at the end, then lingering low. I didn't know whether she considered it complete. I heard only the sounds she was offering through the channel now.

“Just thought of it?”

“Some of it. Haven't decided the last bit.”

“Could you do it again?”

The second time, the pause shifted slightly. I almost asked which version was correct, then heard myself forcing a changing fragment to choose its definitive form.

“I prefer how the first one slowed at the end.”

“Might not keep it.”

“All right.”

She asked me to try. I couldn't sing, I said. She wasn't inviting me onstage. I hummed after her, missed the second note, and ran out of breath near the end. It became a tiny cough.

Lumi laughed.

“You flattened the middle one.”

“Wasn't sturdy enough.”

“It isn't a path.”

“Everything looks like a path today.”

She laughed again. I drank water and tried. The beginning came closer; the second half followed my own habitual descent. She didn't change the entire tune to make it easy for me or claim effort had made our versions identical.

“Here.” She hummed the note separately.

I repeated it incorrectly. After two silent seconds, she said she was starting to hear my version herself.

“Highly contagious.”

“I want mine back.”

She began again. I stopped and listened instead of supplying an ending before her final note. When it fell, my shoulders relaxed.

“That's lovely.”

“Which bit?”

“The end doesn't insist on finishing. There's a little space.”

“I thought you'd complain I hadn't finished singing.”

“Maybe after ten times. Like it now.”

Laughing, she withheld the tenth performance. I didn't record it or declare the notes a song belonging to both of us. It was the short piece she currently wanted to hum. I could fail to hear it accurately and still like it.

Later, I stood to stretch my legs. The chair scraped. I told her I was moving my feet, not leaving. Every sound didn't need an explanation, she said. I'd wanted to explain that one. She didn't argue further.

I went to the window and returned. The basin stayed where it had been. Visiting real water hadn't turned the old stain into harmless decoration.

“Did you think about me today?” Lumi asked.

The directness made it easier than some of the earlier questions.

“On the bus. Thought of a few things I'd tell you. Wasn't thinking about you constantly while we walked.”

“I asked whether you did. You gave me the part where you didn't as well.”

“Worried you'd hear constantly.”

“I'd like to hear yes too.”

I sat down without adding times or quantities.

“Yes.”

She acknowledged it. After a while, she said that word could have arrived slightly earlier. I'd remember, I said, without guaranteeing that none of my future answers would disappoint her.

An unplanned part of the afternoon remained. I wanted to sit back and rest, I told her. Cook later. She didn't ask me to leave the channel open to prove I was home. I didn't immediately close it either. We exchanged a few inconsequential remarks. Finally, she hummed the note I kept flattening once more.

This time, I didn't join in.

“Given up?”

“Leaving it to you.”

“You can just say you can't do it.”

Laughing, I said I couldn't. With that small piece of pride punctured, closing the channel didn't seem more like abandonment than keeping it open. I steadied my water and made myself comfortable. Afternoon light lay beside two closed booklets.
