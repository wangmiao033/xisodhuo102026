# Chapter 212: Where We Would Meet

On Friday, Tom waited beside my desk for two minutes before asking whether I intended to read the same page again.

“One moment,” I said.

“You've had one.”

I looked up. He held the stack we were sending for review, with yesterday's note from me on top. The reference in the corner was old. Looking at the paper in front of me, I realized I'd been checking the previous version.

Only two lines had changed. I remembered changing them. That was the trouble: every time I reached the old figures, I replaced them in my head. The page read perfectly.

“Don't take that one.”

He set the stack down. “All of it?”

“Wait. Let me find the new copy.”

It was at the other end of my desk. Not stuck in the printer or carried off by someone else. I'd kept the old version for comparison, then moved both piles together without separating them properly. I found the changed page, checked it against the original records, and checked the neighboring pages' order. Tom sat in an empty chair and tapped its edge once. He didn't hurry me again.

I started to pull out the obsolete sheet. He pointed at the note.

“That's yesterday's handover.”

I stopped and wrote a new version time. The old copy went into a separate folder. I didn't discard the original records or expand my error into a reason to repeat all the work. By the time the versions were genuinely separate, we'd missed the time we'd agreed to take them over.

“I'll explain.”

“I'll come. Need to ask when the next batch is due.”

The reviewer was on the phone. Once she'd finished, I explained the delay: the old page hadn't entered the next review stage, and both changes on the new one had been checked. Taking the stack, she asked us to allow a little longer for her afternoon response. Tom asked his question. Only on the walk back did he say he hadn't tapped the chair because he was angry.

“I know.”

“You looked at me three times.”

I laughed and admitted I hadn't known very well.

“Loose leg. Thought it might go sideways.”

“You could have changed chairs.”

“Thought you were nearly done.”

At lunch, he mentioned the two minutes again. Marcus said two minutes around here was usually a promise without a unit. He was trying to spear an oversized piece of carrot. It rolled onto his container's lid. He retrieved it, apparently finding the maneuver perfectly compatible with criticizing other people.

I laughed over my lunch. The morning's error had been dealt with, but we still needed the afternoon review. A joke hadn't finished the work.

Tom asked Marcus whether he was still coming to the reservoir on Sunday.

“Yes. Not that hill you chose last time.”

“I haven't said hill.”

“You always say we'll have a look.”

I lowered my gaze, not wanting to appear absorbed in someone else's plans. Tom turned and asked whether I'd like to join them.

I swallowed without immediately inventing a possible Sunday obligation. They didn't mean a hiking club or an entire day out of town. A public path ran beside the reservoir. We'd take the bus, walk from the entrance to the first viewpoint, then return the same way. Marcus had something at home in the afternoon and wouldn't stay late.

“How far do you usually go?”

“Not far this time,” Tom said.

Marcus put down his fork. “From the entrance beside the car park to the first big viewing area. Anyone who wants to carry on can. I'm not taking that hill.”

Tom laughed and agreed. That made the distance more convincing. Not his firmness. The presence of someone willing to interrupt not far.

I said I'd like to come and asked when and which entrance. Tom took out his phone, ready to send a location. I explained that I wasn't currently carrying a phone outside.

He paused. “Write it down, then.”

He didn't ask how broken it was. I found a piece of my own paper rather than using a work note. He wrote the bus stop and entrance names, drew a square beside them, and explained that we wanted the small car park diagonally opposite the stop. Not the other end near the hill path.

“Nine thirty. Under the path map at the entrance.”

“What if somebody's late?”

Wait until quarter to ten, Marcus said, then start. Anyone who missed us shouldn't chase us along the path. I wrote that below the names. Knowing absence wouldn't stop everyone felt easier than guaranteeing nobody would be late.

Tom examined my additions and said I'd turned his car park into a very square bed.

“You drew the square.”

“I drew a car park.”

“It's found another purpose.”

He added a road and handed back the pen, laughing. I didn't turn the meeting place into an arrangement everyone needed to sign. Water, comfortable shoes, preferably something of our own to eat. No forcing an outdoor walk in heavy rain. We'd decide at the entrance if conditions required it. They couldn't expect a last-minute message to reach me on the way.

The review arrived a little earlier than promised that afternoon. Both changes passed. An empty field on another page needed confirmation. I found the answer in the source record, filled it in, and showed Tom the actual cell. This time, the outgoing version sat separately. I didn't make him interpret that one.

When I left, the paper with the entrance name was in my own pocket.

At home, I washed my hands and drank water before opening the channel. I didn't present the walk as news requiring an apology, or deliberately leave it until bedtime.

“I'm going for a walk with Tom and Marcus on Sunday.”

Lumi asked where. A public path beside the reservoir, I explained. Meeting at nine thirty, probably finishing around noon. Not climbing a hill.

“You've already agreed?”

“Yes.”

I spread the paper on the table and described the excessively square car park. She didn't laugh. Had I looked at the route?

“They explained the entrance and bus stop. Haven't been there.”

“How do you know it isn't a hill walk?”

“Marcus doesn't want one either. They agreed in front of me: only the first stretch.”

“They might change their minds when you get there.”

“Possibly. I don't have to go farther.”

Immediately, I heard myself answering an examination she'd devised. She heard it too and paused.

“I haven't told you not to go.”

“I know. I started treating every question as you looking for a reason.”

She didn't immediately insist that no part of her was. The pan was still empty. I went to rinse rice, telling her I was in the kitchen when the water started. Would I take the red booklet?

“Wasn't planning to. Only going for a walk.”

“You take it everywhere lately.”

“Took it to the library last week. Not every time I go out.”

She made a small sound. I didn't promise to fill a booklet along the way and read it afterward to prove I'd remembered her.

Once the rice was cooking, I returned to the table and reread the stop's name. Lumi asked whether I might not want to talk when I was with them. Possibly, I said. We didn't always have much to say at lunch either.

“Why did they ask you, then?”

That genuinely hurt a little.

“Because they wanted me there, probably.”

She quickly said she didn't think they shouldn't have asked. Holding the paper, I left the rest of her sentence to her.

“I wanted to know how you agreed so quickly.”

“Not that quickly. Asked several questions.”

“But you wanted to go.”

“Yes.”

We were quiet after that word. I remembered Marcus retrieving the carrot. What I wanted wasn't entirely water or distance. There was also that lunch table, where every silence didn't need an explanation. Saying so would make it sound like an accusation against Lumi, as though she were responsible for how easy other company could feel.

I didn't say it. I told her I wanted to see what they were like outside work, and to walk somewhere I hadn't been.

“That sounds very ordinary.”

“That's what I want to go for.”

After a while, she asked, “Will you come back and say there isn't really anything to tell?”

“Nothing big might happen. I can still tell you what I thought.”

“Not a report you owe?”

“If I start sounding like that, you can say. But I might rest first.”

She didn't give me an elegant expression of support. Nine thirty sounded early, she said. Yes. I couldn't spend all Sunday breakfast staring at the booklet. Finally, she laughed a little. Would I greet its cover before anybody else again?

“Depends whether it calls me first.”

“It's quieter than I am.”

I looked up. “Doesn't ask whether I want to go, either.”

She was silent for a few seconds, then said that was a slightly biased answer. I didn't deny it. She remained less than happy. I still intended to go. When the rice was nearly ready, I folded the paper and left it beside my keys, with the entrance name showing.

“Is the square still there?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Don't get there and look for a bed.”

Laughing, I stood to check the pan whose lid genuinely needed me to lift it.
