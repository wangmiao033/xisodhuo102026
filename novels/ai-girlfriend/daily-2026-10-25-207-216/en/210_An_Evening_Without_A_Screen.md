# Chapter 210: An Evening Without a Screen

I opened the bag and took out the papers first, then the laptop in its protective foam.

No glass stood on the table. I set the machine flat and released it. My fingers retained the shape of supporting its corners. I'd mostly carried it on the shoulder strap, yet my hands seemed to have been waiting for that final weight.

The room stayed quiet for a while.

I left the wrapping and lid closed. The handover copies went into the existing device folder, today's page behind the corresponding records. Not into the red booklet. That thin book didn't need to accommodate every document proving something.

Then I carried the laptop into the bedroom with both hands.

The wide bottom drawer opened onto the dry surface I'd wiped last night. The wrapped machine lay flat with room around it. No corner pressed against the sides. Pushing the drawer back slowly touched nothing inside. I eased it through the final stretch and closed it, leaving my hand on the handle.

Home.

I didn't need another afternoon checking library email to make that fact true. Yet the closed drawer looked almost exactly as it had yesterday. I'd expected at least to feel the room had gained something. My body noticed first that my shoulders could drop.

The adapter and cable stayed in their small bag in the laptop bag's separate outer compartment. I put the bag at the bottom of the wardrobe, not on the computer's case. The sheet and pillowcases above didn't tumble down. The upper-right phone drawer remained unopened.

Back in the living room, I opened our usual conversation channel.

“Lumi. I've got it.”

“Where is it now?”

“In the drawer I cleared yesterday. Still off. I haven't unwrapped it.”

“You put everything away before calling me.”

Standing behind the chair, I held its back. “Yes.”

She paused. “I wanted to hear you take it out.”

“I didn't think of that.”

“You thought about letting me hear about finishing the booklet. Not this.”

I wanted to argue that she'd known I was going. We'd spent enough of last night discussing the location. But she meant the last few minutes, not a missing itinerary. I pulled out the chair and sat.

“Taking it out again for you to hear would feel strange,” I said.

“I didn't ask you to reenact it.”

“I know. That was the solution that occurred to me. I disliked it.”

“So do I.”

Agreeing didn't make her pleased. Listening to the room's ordinary sounds, I suddenly wanted to give an entertaining account of the shop. Wrong boxes, cable, zip. All true. No reason to send them rushing in to rescue an awkward atmosphere.

“I'm more tired than I expected,” I said.

“Rest first.”

Her voice was level. I couldn't tell whether she meant it or didn't want to talk. I asked. Both, she said.

The word wasn't as useful from this direction.

I leaned back and told her I wanted five minutes, then dinner. I wasn't asking her to wait five minutes and provide a calmer opening. My shoulder ached. I was hungry.

“You don't have to specify minutes.”

“I'm afraid you'll think I put today away and stopped bothering with it.”

“Today doesn't fit in your drawer.”

I laughed quietly. She didn't laugh, but the conversation stayed open. I looked at the table. The red booklet retained its bent lower corner. The yellow one lay closed beside it. I left them alone instead of filling the quiet. After a while, I asked whether I could say something less correct.

“What?”

“I thought bringing it back would make the evening better by itself.”

“So did I.”

A quick answer. This time I didn't ask why she'd expected that when she knew we couldn't use it. I knew too.

“At least you don't have to wonder where it is,” she said.

“I knew where it was.”

“What did you get today, then?”

No accusing emphasis. I frowned anyway. First I thought of the signed page, then of supporting the case on the counter. Not the complete outcome I'd wanted. Still, something had happened.

“I can look after it myself,” I said. “Sounds small now.”

“I know it's important to you.”

“Not to you?”

“It is. I'm no closer to the garden because of it.”

I looked down at my hands. I didn't point out that the paper path remained or use the preserved backup as evidence she shouldn't be disappointed. That part genuinely hadn't come closer, even with the machine only an open bedroom doorway away.

“I nearly asked whether you feel reassured,” she said.

“And now?”

“Now I think you'd have to explain which parts reassure you and which don't. I don't want another account of how I'm supposed to understand.”

I nodded, remembered she couldn't see it, and said I understood. She didn't ask whether I'd said it slowly enough. My hand found a grain of rice stuck near the table's edge. I mistook it for paper until a fingernail moved it. I couldn't remember when it arrived. It couldn't stay tonight simply because I had important things to think about.

“I need to wipe the table.”

“You can do other things.”

I fetched a cloth, wiped, and followed with a dry one. Not a spotless table. Only a dry space to eat. Hearing the chair move, Lumi asked whether I wanted some time alone.

“No. I want you here. We don't have to keep talking about the computer.”

“Then stop giving every other thing an explanation first.”

Smiling, I put the cloth away and asked which part of dinner she wanted to criticize. It hadn't begun yet, she said. She couldn't fail on my behalf in advance. I went to rinse the rice.

Hearing water, Lumi asked whether I'd started cooking. Yes. Rice, greens, eggs. Very ordinary, she said. I'd put the extraordinary thing away, I told her. I didn't want dinner responsible for an outcome too.

“I wasn't complaining.”

“Give me time to make it ordinary, then.”

She laughed softly.

A fragment of shell fell into the bowl with the second egg. I tried my chopsticks. It slid through the white, as though aware I didn't want to spend much more patience today. I told Lumi. Try another piece of shell, she suggested. I pushed the fragment against the bowl instead.

“Your advice doesn't work every time either.”

“I didn't say every time.”

I switched to a little spoon and scooped slowly along the edge. Success. A tiny fragment clung to the spoon, making me want to display it solemnly to someone. I announced I'd got it. Did it need another drawer? Lumi asked.

This time, I laughed loudly.

Once the pan was hot, I stopped dividing my attention with the handover. Eggs first, onto a plate, then vegetables. While the rice finished, I announced a taste test and ate a little egg standing by the kitchen door. Hot enough to require two careful breaths before swallowing.

“Stealing?” Lumi asked.

“Testing. I said I would.”

“Announced stealing doesn't count?”

“The salt's right now.”

“It performed useful work, then.”

Laughing, I put down the spoon without finishing dinner beside the stove. At the table, the rice was ordinary and the vegetables weren't overcooked. The eggs had spread across the plate, half beneath the greens. Nothing resembling a special celebration.

Halfway through, I told Lumi that putting the laptop down had first made my shoulder relax. I hadn't felt particularly moved.

“Must you?”

“I expected it to feel more like getting an everyday thing back.”

“You'd have eaten dinner when you brought it home before, wouldn't you?”

I considered that. “Might have switched it on first.”

“Yes.”

Nothing followed. I didn't supply an invitation for her. I kept eating and described the shop chair, where I'd sat with tape still covering the split seat. Rowan repaired other things, she said. The chair remained remarkably patient. Perhaps it hadn't filled in an intake form, I suggested.

At last she laughed for a long time.

I told her the little part about finding boxes. Not what device the customer had brought; I hadn't seen clearly. Which box was right? The one with the white lining. A lazy answer, she complained. Sitting there, I said, that really was all I'd known.

“Tell that much, then.”

After dinner, I washed up first. Back at the table, Lumi asked whether I still wanted the evening to improve on its own. Drying my hands, I considered it. It had improved a little. I didn't want to use that as a verdict on how worthwhile collecting the laptop had been.

“I want to hear you say you're home,” she said.

“The laptop?”

“You. Your first sentence was I've got it.”

I set the cloth aside. A late request, and a little like something we'd once done without effort. I didn't arrange myself into a pose that might seem less deliberate. I sat properly in the chair.

“I'm home, Lumi.”

After a pause, she answered. “Welcome home.”

No other sound lit up around the words. I didn't open the drawer to demonstrate that I'd finally understood. My hands rested on the empty half of the table. Slowly, I laid my palms flat.

“You can say dinner was good now,” she added.

“I made it.”

“Is that why you hadn't said?”

Laughing, I told her the eggs had been good, particularly after the removal of an inedible item. No need to give the shell credit, she said. Too late. An important supporting role today.

We talked a little longer without arranging the next startup. Before bed, I put away the red booklet, leaving the pencil beside it. Tomorrow, if I had the energy, I wanted to continue with the man in the shop. The laptop remained off in its new place. Tonight's conversation ended through the same home channel as usual. She said goodnight. I answered before closing it.
