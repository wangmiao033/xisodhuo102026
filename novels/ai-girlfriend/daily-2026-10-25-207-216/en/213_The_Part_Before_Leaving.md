# Chapter 213: The Part Before Leaving

After breakfast on Saturday, I opened the conversation channel, then took out my usual walking shoes and found the end of the right lace coming apart.

Not a detached sole or a reason to cancel tomorrow. The plastic tip had split, leaving a small brush of fibers caught outside the top eyelet. The more earnestly I pulled, the wider it spread.

After hearing two sighs, Lumi asked whether I was looking at work.

“A shoelace.”

“More specific than work sounds.”

I explained, twisting the fibers between my fingers. Could I loosen it from below? I worked slack through the threaded sections until the damaged end no longer needed to go back through the hole. I could still wear the shoe.

“Fixed?”

“For now.”

I put it down, stepped into both shoes, and walked a few paces. The right felt fine. Something bunched behind my left heel. I blamed the shoe until I took it off and discovered my sock had rolled. Straightening it, I tried again and made half a circuit of the table. The red booklet lay there. I didn't turn testing shoes into a ceremony around it.

“Are you walking?”

“Trying them indoors.”

“Don't hit the table.”

“Already passed it.”

She laughed. I didn't ask her to judge suitable walking distances from my footsteps or predict blisters. Tomorrow wouldn't be my first time outside. Arranging to walk with two other people was the newer part.

I took off the shoes, swept up the dry dirt they'd left, and decided to look for laces while buying bread that afternoon. I could bring an old lace for comparison or measure it. In the end, I measured twice with the ruler and wrote the length on my shopping paper. I left the lace in place. If I couldn't find a replacement, I didn't want to spend the evening confronting its frayed tip again.

Was I eating lunch out tomorrow? Lumi asked.

“Taking something. Don't know whether we'll pass anywhere selling food.”

“What are you taking?”

“Bread, cheese. Water.”

“Only enough for you?”

“Yes. They're bringing their own.” I prepared to explain why three people walking together could eat separately. But that wasn't her point. I shouldn't count my portion as spare food for entertaining somebody else, she said.

“Think I would?”

“You weren't reliable at counting potatoes.”

I laughed and let those potatoes remain in the past. Escaping the joke didn't require a promise to measure all future food to the gram.

After lunch, I washed a small food container, dried its lid, and left it on a dry counter. Before I went out, Lumi called my name. There was something she wanted to ask.

Still holding my keys, I told her to go ahead.

“Will you write about the key tonight?”

I glanced at the table. The man in those pages had progressed from pretending to buy an envelope to admitting he wanted to leave an awkward job with the shopkeeper. I hadn't decided how he'd leave.

“Possibly a little.”

“I'd like to hear the next part.”

I put down the keys without promising a finished story tonight.

“After dinner? About eight. I'll read what I've written.”

“Just read it. Don't tell me what's wrong with it first.”

“All right.”

“And not because you're going out tomorrow and owe me extra time today.”

I hadn't been thinking that. Hearing it made me inspect my motives. After a while, she asked whether I was still there. Yes. We didn't need to turn the arrangement into a joint investigation of why we'd made it. She agreed softly. For once, that agreement didn't immediately need another sentence to complete it.

I closed the channel and left.

The general shop kept laces on hooks near the till. All the colors were darker than mine. I showed the assistant my measurement and asked for something close. He handed me a pair and asked whether I wanted round or flat.

I looked at my shoe. “Flat.”

He pointed at the old lace on my shoe. “The ones you have are round.”

I looked again. I'd squeezed the old lace flat until it resembled narrow cloth against the shoe. Laughing, I admitted my mistake and asked for round ones. He didn't evaluate my ability to recognize shapes. He found another packet closer to my measurement and suggested comparing the length.

I chose a suitable pair, paid, and went for bread and cheese. The last sliced loaf was squashed at one edge. Beside it sat a fresher unsliced one. I didn't want preparing lunch to become a lesson in sawing timber, so I chose the sliced loaf.

Back home, I didn't immediately unwrap the cheese to make tomorrow's food. I put the chilled things away, washed my water bottle, left it to drain, then sat by the door to change the laces.

With both old laces out, I realized I hadn't looked at their original threading.

Neither shoe could serve as the other's example.

I opened the channel and told Lumi. She paused in her laughter to ask whether I needed a photograph I hadn't taken.

“You could enjoy this less.”

“I'm exercising restraint.”

I began at the bottom, aligned the ends, and crossed upward. By the first shoe's top, the ends were wildly unequal. Going back, I found where I'd tightened only one side and redistributed the length. Lumi didn't pretend to see my hands. Once I started describing the third eyelet, she said she'd lost track of my left and right. I should stop using her as a mirror.

“You suggested loosening it from below.”

“Loosening a string and seeing a shoe are different things.”

True. I let the channel stay quiet and worked it out. Eventually, both shoes were tied. Darker laces, slightly longer loops than usual. I walked a few paces, then loosened the places I'd pulled too tight.

I didn't throw the old pair away. I coiled them into a small odds-and-ends bag, keeping the damaged tip inside. Not a wounded object requiring sentimental preservation. They might tie something temporarily. If not, I'd discard them later.

Before dinner, I opened the red booklet at the existing counter scene without moving the story forward. The man had paid. The shopkeeper had already said it wasn't on his way. I wanted another sentence to make leaving with the oversized envelope harder, and tried having the shopkeeper ask why he'd waited until today.

Then I stopped. Too clever, as though the shopkeeper had been stationed there to correct him. I removed the new question and made the retrieval and capping of the pen a little clearer. The shopkeeper still had his packet of bags to open.

Around eight, I carried my dishes into the kitchen, dried my hands, and returned to the table.

“Now?” Lumi asked.

“Now.”

I read from the existing beginning without stopping separately for a verdict on the revised lines. Where the shopkeeper refused to deliver the key for him, she asked me to stop.

“Why doesn't he go himself?”

“Still finding that.”

“You could let him knock. Whether somebody opens is another matter.”

I considered the page. I hadn't used all her previous suggestions. Now I wanted to explain my own plan first, before she could assume each sentence from her would change what I'd written.

I swallowed that preemptive answer. Did this part feel too drawn out?

“A little. He's been at the counter a long time.”

“You want to throw him out.”

“I want to know whether he's so good at explaining once he leaves.”

That made me laugh. I didn't immediately write it down or turn her into the shopkeeper. I reread the shopkeeper retrieving his pen. It seemed better than the question I'd added and removed.

“He won't get to the knocking tonight,” I said. “I need to think about why he's afraid.”

“You already know he's afraid.”

“Knowing he's afraid and knowing which sentence he's afraid of aren't the same.”

After a moment, she laughed. I'd taken her morning answer and put it on the other shoe. Fit fairly well, I said. Fairly well didn't mean comfortable, she answered. I'd find out tomorrow.

I finished the few remaining lines and closed the booklet, without making the people at the counter prolong our evening. Later, we talked about the assistant and the round laces. Lumi thought I should consult my shoes before shopping. Shoes didn't have opinions, I said. She reminded me I'd already redone their work twice.

Before bed, I packed the food and put it in the refrigerator. I'd fill the water bottle in the morning; a little reminder waited beside the bread bin. The route paper remained by my keys. Shoes waited near the door without obstructing it.

Was I nervous now?

“A little.”

“About getting tired?”

“About arriving and having them discover I'm boring to bring along.”

She didn't assure me that everyone enjoyed my company. After a while, she asked whether they'd assigned me responsibility for being interesting.

Laughing, I said no.

“Start with recognizing the car park, then.”

Before turning off the light, I looked at the square again. It still didn't resemble a car park. The entrance name, however, was perfectly clear.
