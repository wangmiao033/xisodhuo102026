# Chapter 211: Only an Envelope

After dinner on Thursday, I opened the red booklet at page two. The man was still standing at the counter.

He'd waited several days without paying for the envelope. In the space beside him, I wrote a coin, then lifted my pencil. That wasn't much of a sentence.

Yesterday's collected laptop hadn't added a screen to tonight. It remained off in the bedroom dresser's wide bottom drawer. Paper, pencil, and a deliberately distant glass of water occupied the table. I opened the conversation channel and told Lumi I wanted to continue the opening about the key.

“Paid at last?” she asked.

“I've written the money. He hasn't handed it over.”

“You're cautious with his finances.”

Laughing, I read the existing lines from the beginning, including the oversized envelope. Her Sunday comment about the shop perhaps stocking only one size remained separate, attributed below its dividing line. I hadn't turned it into the shopkeeper's narration. Nor had I opened her work on the shelves in search of a better beginning.

This time, I wanted the man to know who should receive the key.

I set aside the earlier idea about remembering the wrong door. On the next line, I made him come to buy an envelope for returning the spare key to a storeroom behind another row of shops. The arrangement he'd borrowed it for had ended. The key remained in his pocket. I hadn't decided what he'd helped with. That past didn't need to fill the whole counter yet.

“Why this shop, then?” Lumi asked.

“To buy an envelope.”

“He knows where to take it. Why does he need one?”

I looked at the newly written key. Too clean on the page, like a prop that only needed moving to its correct location. Beside it, I tried a sentence: he didn't want to carry the key in his hand, as though waiting all the way for someone to ask about it.

I left that out of the story. Instead, he hid it beneath his coin. Collecting the money, the shopkeeper's fingertip met its teeth first.

“This is yours,” the shopkeeper said, pushing the key back.

The man retrieved it and pressed his palm against the counter without immediately lifting it.

After I read that much, Lumi said, “Have the shopkeeper ask whether he'd like it delivered.”

“Does he know the owner?”

“Same street. He could. Take it over when he closes.”

She made it sound ordinary enough that I nearly wrote it. In fact, I tried three lines in the spare space: the shopkeeper slipping the key into the envelope, saying he'd pass that way tonight; the man thanking him and finally taking his hand off the counter.

I read it and stopped.

“Too quick?” she asked.

“The shopkeeper suddenly has plenty of time.”

“You haven't given him anything else. Why can't he?”

I couldn't immediately answer. She hadn't required everyone to clean up after the man. She'd suggested an ordinary favor. If everybody in a story had to walk unaided to its ending, the shopkeeper would become a tool whose only job was refusing.

“I want the man to go himself,” I said.

“You can prefer that. Don't call one favor unrealistic.”

I circled the three lines and marked them Trial instead of scribbling them out. Her suggestion could lead to another story. Not the one I wanted now. I wanted this man to speak and encounter someone whose day didn't stop because of his discomfort.

“Will he be punished?”

“I don't want that.”

“You sounded prepared.”

I leaned back. An exaggeration, but she'd made me notice the lesson I'd assigned to an unwritten walk. He must go. Must learn. Ideally stand straighter at the end than when he entered. An envelope meant to hold a key had become a graduation certificate in my hands.

“Let the shopkeeper sell something first, then.”

“He's been waiting since Sunday for you to allow that.”

Laughing, I straightened the booklet and began again. No reading aloud yet. I let the pencil finish a passage.

The man drew the coin from beneath the key and set it nearer the shopkeeper. The shopkeeper collected it, placed the large envelope on the counter, and turned to open a new packet of paper bags. The sealing tape had caught the top one. He picked at it twice before separating it from the one underneath.

“Could I borrow a pen?” the man asked.

The shopkeeper took one from his receipt book and passed it over without uncapping it for him.

The man slid the key halfway into the envelope, freeing his hand to write his name. Too large. Only at the end did he notice more than half the envelope was still empty. He turned it to write Key, then saw the little bit of metal showing at the opening. His pen stopped, leaving a small dot beside the final letter of his name.

I read this to Lumi.

“Why can't he write Key?”

“He can. He thinks it would look foolish.”

“You didn't write that.”

“I don't want to explain every pause.”

“I can ask, though. I hear him stop. I don't know whether he's afraid of too many words or too few.”

I pointed the pencil at the dot. She couldn't see that, of course. Instead, I explained that he'd lift the pen again without supplying a whole sentence to justify the mark. I wrote Spare beneath his name. Then he rubbed it with his thumb as though it were pencil. The shopkeeper had lent him ink. The word stayed. His thumb gained a stain.

Lumi laughed. “Finally, something that genuinely doesn't go smoothly.”

“Should he lose the key too?”

“No. You sound as though you've discovered where to deduct marks.”

Laughing, I stopped. A little ink was enough. No need for a dropped key and a search involving the whole shop.

Reaching page three, I pushed the booklet upward, my wrist passing its lower edge. The newly made booklet wasn't easier to write in than a bought one. I was still well short of the knot in the middle. For now, there was simply less blank space. I wrote Continued at the top instead of suddenly inventing a formal title.

In the next passage, the man slid the key all the way inside and asked whether the shopkeeper knew the storeroom's owner.

“I know the one you mean.”

“Will she be there now?”

“You'll have to go and see.”

The shopkeeper indicated the other row of shops beyond the door, then returned to the unfinished packet of bags. The man said he only wanted to give back the key, and he was afraid she might be busy. Could he perhaps—

I stopped, wanting him to finish the request. The pencil hung above the page. Lumi didn't hurry me. She'd heard only what I'd read so far and couldn't know the space after that dash was empty. I explained where I was stuck.

“He can ask outright,” she said. “Ask him to return it. Don't make the shopkeeper guess, then seem to understand him.”

I liked that. I didn't immediately record her exact words. I wrote the man finally asking, “Would you mind giving it to her for me?”

The shopkeeper looked up, glanced at the envelope, and answered, “I'm not going that way today.”

Nothing about taking responsibility in future. He retrieved the uncapped pen, put its cap on, and returned it to the receipt book. The man's hand followed slightly, as though he still held something borrowed. Then he discovered the only thing in his hand was the envelope he'd bought.

I finished reading. Lumi stayed quiet a moment.

“He could say it isn't on his way,” she suggested. “Not going that way sounds as though he's avoiding it.”

I tried the change. Lighter, and less as though the shopkeeper knew the whole situation already. I kept It's not on my way. The earlier version remained legible beside it. Then I read from the payment onward without interpreting every brief pause from her as approval waiting to be given.

“I still prefer someone taking it for him,” she said.

“I know.”

“Not only because it's easy.”

I put down the pencil.

Perhaps someone delivering it would make the owner angrier, she suggested. He'd still have to explain. More difficult than simply returning a key himself. A favor didn't necessarily remove every future complication from a story.

That version made me sit straighter. I'd genuinely treated it as the easiest exit without considering what lay beyond. I admitted that and still left the refusal I'd written in place.

“You want him outside, then.”

“I want him carrying it himself.”

“Envelope still too big?”

“Still big.”

“He could fold it.”

“He hasn't yet.”

She laughed. So did I. I didn't know whether that would survive to the end. At least hearing a more complicated alternative didn't require proving my own choice the best one tonight.

I wrote a final small passage. The man held the envelope by its short edge. Inside, the key slid to the far end and tapped. He changed his grip, holding it upright against his chest, then found himself carrying it like a document nobody was allowed to fold. Lowering it, he stepped away from the counter to leave room. The shopkeeper continued separating bags. The door wasn't locked.

The man hadn't gone out yet. I wasn't going to hurry him before the owner tonight and give him words I'd invented merely to finish this chapter.

“Enough for today,” I said.

Had I thought of what followed? Only that he'd go, I answered. I didn't know how the owner would respond. I wouldn't answer for her in the shopkeeper's voice. Lumi didn't know either, she said. Leave it there.

I reached for a pen cap, found one, then remembered I'd been using a pencil. The cap belonged to another pen beside me. Returning it to the proper place, I told Lumi. Laughing, she asked whether I wanted to borrow a pen to disguise the mistake. At least my thumb was free of ink, I said.

While drinking water, I didn't lift the whole booklet to make its paper audible for her. She'd heard today's passages and the version I hadn't chosen. I still wanted to revise, I said. No fair copy yet. No taking it out to read. She hadn't contacted a counter for me either, she replied.

That made me laugh for a long time.

Before bed, I closed the red booklet beside the yellow one. The bus stop remained in the older notebook. The key occupied pages two and three of this one, untitled and unfinished. The laptop stayed powered down in its drawer. Private envelopes remained in their own bookcase positions. After closing the conversation channel, I passed the bedroom dresser without stopping to inspect yesterday's stored package. I needed to brush my teeth. The man on the page still owed himself a short walk.
