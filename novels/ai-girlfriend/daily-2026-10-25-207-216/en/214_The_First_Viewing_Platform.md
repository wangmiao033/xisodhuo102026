# Chapter 214: The First Viewing Platform

When I reached the entrance on Sunday, Tom stood on the other side of the map, helping Marcus with a flask lid.

After getting off the bus, I'd walked half a block the wrong way. The car park was diagonally opposite, but I'd continued along the stop's side until I saw the entrance name across the road. Then I'd used the crossing. No climbing a barrier or running through traffic to recover a few minutes. The clock beside the map said nine twenty-seven. I was still early.

“What ceremony are you preparing?” I asked.

“Coffee hasn't leaked,” Marcus said. “Lid's too tight.”

Tom held the lid. Marcus held the flask. Both strained at it. Putting down my bag, I suggested not aiming it at anybody's face. Marcus set it on the low wall and dried the outside. Their next attempt loosened it. Tom's hand rose abruptly, almost meeting his own chin.

We laughed. Marcus poured a little into the cup and drank without making it a group beverage ritual. I had water. Joining them didn't require pretending to want coffee.

At nine thirty, we started.

The first stretch was flatter than I'd expected. Water lay to our left, intermittently hidden by trees. To the right was a grassy slope, not a hill we needed to climb. People pushed strollers or walked dogs. We kept to one side and left room.

I wore my old jacket. Saturday's new laces looked more conspicuous than the previous pair. I kept examining my feet until doing so almost put one into shallow water. Then I looked up.

Tom said the wind usually became noticeable after the bend. It did feel cooler there. He looked pleased. Marcus suggested saving this accurate prediction to discuss over food.

“Come here often?” I asked.

“A few times,” Tom said. “He always says he's busy.”

“Because I actually do other things.”

Marcus needed to be home some time after one. We'd have to be heading back before noon. Did that only leave time for the platform? I asked. It wasn't far, he said. There was time to pause as well. Tom didn't argue for the hill.

After perhaps twenty minutes, conversation thinned. I began wondering whether I should introduce a topic so they'd know inviting me hadn't been wasted. Yesterday's worry returned. Lumi's question about who was responsible for being interesting hadn't removed it permanently.

I nearly asked Tom about Monday's paperwork.

Fortunately, he stopped first and pointed at distant driftwood. Looked like a dog, he said. I couldn't see it. Marcus considered it and suggested a deflated pillow.

“Both very imaginative,” I said.

“What does it look like to you?”

“Wood.”

Tom sighed. Someone had been assigned realism today. I laughed without inventing a missing owner for the wood to demonstrate that I could write stories.

Farther on, standing water occupied half the path. Two cyclists dismounted and pushed through. Tom tried the narrow grass verge. The edge of his shoe immediately sank. He stepped back and inspected it.

“Shortcut,” Marcus said.

“Not officially in service yet.”

I laughed too quickly and almost followed him. We waited for the bicycles, then passed slowly along the remaining dry path. We didn't enter the fenced-off bank. A dark stripe remained on Tom's shoe. Walking straight wouldn't clean it away.

My left heel began feeling hot along the next stretch.

Not pain that made walking impossible. My sock and shoe were rubbing the same place repeatedly. I slowed half a step, hoping they'd stop naturally. Tom looked back and asked what I was watching. Nothing, I said, and continued. Ten or fifteen steps later, I felt it more distinctly.

I asked them to wait.

An older person sat reading at one end of a bench. I asked to use the empty end and received a nod. Sitting, I loosened my lace, straightened the sock, and adjusted the tension. Even old shoes could become uncomfortable when tied too tightly. They didn't need a newly invented defect.

Marcus drank water beside the path. Tom asked whether the shoes weren't suitable. I'd overtightened one, I explained. I was adjusting it. Nobody demanded proof that I could keep going, or immediately arranged to send me home.

“You could have said sooner,” Tom said.

“Waiting for a suitable place.”

He examined the bench. “This one's very suitable.”

Laughing, I stood and tried two steps. I didn't claim every sensation had vanished. The warm patch remained, but each step no longer scraped it. I could continue to the platform, I said, and decide there.

This small act was harder than saying at the table that I could choose not to go farther. Still, nobody treated my sitting down as a failed invitation.

At the platform, the water finally opened out in front of us.

I'd expected blue. That day it was gray-green, paler near shore. People moved along the opposite side, disappearing between trees. They weren't another group we needed to catch. Wind tapped my jacket against my legs. I raised the zip without describing myself as finally learning how to breathe.

Marcus stood at the rail. Tom photographed the water, checked his phone, and said the picture looked like nothing was there.

“Water,” I said.

“Very consistent today.”

Laughing, he put the phone away. Nobody needed a summit pose. We hadn't climbed anything.

Stone seats stood along one side. I took out my food and found a corner of cheese protruding from the bread. Sitting down, I wrapped a napkin around it so it wouldn't drop. Tom had bought a large roll. Tearing it scattered crumbs over his jacket. He patted it twice. Marcus told him to catch the piece on his leg first.

I laughed with them and discovered I wasn't continually calculating my next sentence.

Had I filled the booklet from the library? Tom asked. Not yet. Some device matters and a little story.

“Sewing things again?”

“A key this time.”

“Getting harder materials.”

I laughed. I didn't produce a booklet I hadn't packed or perform the pages. Marcus said he'd bought a travel journal when younger. Destination on page one, ticket on page two, nothing afterward. Tom thought that meant he'd come home. Perfectly reasonable.

I remembered my breakfast struggle with the red booklet's first line. I didn't recruit this remark to prove to Lumi who understood blankness better. Here, it was an old incident Marcus mentioned while eating.

Finished, I packed my wrapping and used napkin. The wind had strengthened. Tom trapped his empty paper bag beneath his knee, but it escaped a short distance. Two steps retrieved it. Afterward, he stopped saying it wasn't particularly cold.

A path continued beyond the platform. He looked along it. A little farther, there should be another place with a view of the dam.

I recognized the sentence arriving.

Marcus shook his head first. “This is my turnaround today.”

“Only asking.”

Tom looked at me. I almost said either was fine. Instead, I said I'd go back with them.

“Heel's still warm. Don't want to wait until it hurts to turn around.”

Fine, he said. He didn't ask whether slowing down would make continuing possible. My reason had remained where I'd put it. I didn't need to add an invented busy afternoon.

Our order changed on the way back. Marcus led; Tom walked beside me. At the puddled section, Tom solemnly indicated the dry strip and recommended that route for today. Was there a guidance fee? I asked. It could come out of the morning's flask-opening service.

“I didn't drink your coffee.”

“You owe even less, then.”

It was still before noon when we reached the entrance. Marcus checked the time. Enough to get home. Beside the map, I unfolded my paper and noticed the car park wasn't actually square. One edge had a notch.

“You left that out,” I told Tom.

“Nobody lost.”

We separated by the bus stop. They needed the other side. I waited for my bus. No immediate arrangement for next week, no score for today's performance. Tom waved and said he'd see me Monday. Monday, I agreed.

There was a seat on the bus. I stretched my feet a little. The water bottle tapped the empty food container in my bag. No phone in my pocket. Lumi hadn't heard those conversations. There were a few things I wanted to tell her. Mostly, I watched shops retreat past the window.

It was after half past twelve when I got home. I took off the shoes, hung up my jacket, and put down the bag. The apartment was quiet. Sitting by the door, I let the heat in my heel ease without immediately calling the morning a success requiring a report.
