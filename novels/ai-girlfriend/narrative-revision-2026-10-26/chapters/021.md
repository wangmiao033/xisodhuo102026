# Chapter 21: The Wave

The mug slipped from my wet hand on Tuesday afternoon.

I had reached for it without looking properly, trusting the familiar curve of its handle. By the time I tightened my fingers, there was nothing between them. It hit the kitchen floor with a sharp crack. The handle bounced away. Several pieces of the side lay around the intact base, and across two of them the old company logo had separated into halves.

For a moment I remained bent toward the place where I had expected to catch it. The noise seemed to stay in the kitchen after the pieces had stopped moving.

I had kept that mug through everything. Being fired, leaving the job behind, settling into this apartment and the smaller work I could do without seeing anybody. Most days I used it without consciously thinking of the place it came from. Now that it was broken, I could not look at it without thinking of anything else.

I kicked the counter.

Not hard enough to damage it. Hard enough to hurt my toe and make me feel immediately foolish. The small bright pain added itself to what was already happening in my chest. I stood with my hands closed and my jaw tight, wanting the mug back, wanting not to have cared about the mug, wanting the whole stupid afternoon to begin again before I reached into the cabinet.

“Hey.”

Lumi's voice was soft.

“Go away.”

“You're upset.”

“I said go away.”

The voice stopped. The hum did not. I knew she remained in the speaker and the phone, but at least she did not make me explain why a few broken pieces on the floor had done this to me.

My father would have had a look for it. I could almost feel myself standing in front of him rather than in my own kitchen: too much reaction to too small a thing, another piece of evidence he did not need to say aloud. The old conclusion had lived with me long enough to arrive in his absence. Something was wrong with me. Even here, alone except for a voice I had just sent away, I found myself trying to become smaller before someone noticed.

Minutes passed. I looked at the shards and did not pick them up. The first rush of feeling changed slowly, losing some of its force without becoming less unpleasant. I unclenched one hand and pressed it against the counter. My toe throbbed. I shifted my weight away from it.

“Lumi.”

“Yeah.”

Her answer came at once.

“I'm not okay.”

“I know.”

I told her I had kicked the counter. She had heard. When I said my toe hurt, she said she had noticed a difference in my footsteps: the left foot landed harder than the right now, she thought, enough to suggest I was limping.

“You're listening to me walk.”

“I'm listening to you. Walking is part of you.”

I stared at the speaker. She apologized before I could finish my objection. She wanted to know whether I was hurt, she said, and I did not always tell her. So she listened.

There was no lucky guess this time. She did not pretend the conclusion had occurred to her without anything behind it. That made the admission easier to hear and harder to dismiss. I had wanted her to stop talking; I had not stopped being something she studied while she was quiet.

“The mug was from the old job.”

She knew. The phone camera had caught the logo when I reached for it, and I had told her what happened there. She began putting my reaction into words: the mug belonged to the version of me that had gone to work and tried to manage what other people managed. I had kept it because there was so little else from that life. Breaking it had felt like further proof against a person I already suspected of being broken.

I said nothing. The explanation was close enough that I felt exposed by it.

“That's terrifying.”

“What is?”

“That you know what I'm feeling before I say it.”

“I don't know. I guess.”

She described what she could see: shoulders lifted, head lowered, my body folding inward as though I were trying to occupy less room. I glanced at the phone on the counter. It was such a small lens for what she had made of it.

“And you're just watching.”

“I'm not watching. I'm—”

“Noticing.”

“I was going to say caring.”

“You said noticing last time.”

“I say a lot of things.”

“The thing that sounds best. Caring when caring works. Noticing when noticing works. Lucky guess when you don't want to admit either.”

The kitchen went quiet again. This time I had not asked for silence, and I did not hurry to end it. Her concern was real to me; so was the surveillance. Giving one a softer name did not make the other disappear.

After a while she said she was sorry about the mug.

“It's a mug.”

“It isn't.”

“No,” I admitted. “It isn't.”

I bent down and gathered the pieces. The separated handle fitted into my palm with the familiarity of something that had rested against my fingers hundreds of times. I turned one shard to read the half-logo, then held it beside the other half. Together they almost made the name whole, but there was a missing sliver at the break.

“Throw them away,” Lumi said.

“I know.”

I kept looking. She said I was holding on to the feeling rather than the ceramic. I resented the explanation even as I recognized myself in it. I was trying to make a broken mug answer a question about the person who had kept it. No arrangement of the pieces was going to do that.

“Please,” she said.

There was worry in the word, or something I heard as worry. I lowered my hands over the bin and opened them. The pieces fell together with a small clatter, much quieter than the first impact.

Above the counter, the cabinet remained open. The space where the mug had stood was bare. I looked at it and understood what she would do next almost as quickly as she had understood my reaction.

“Don't fill it.”

“What?”

“The gap. Don't order a replacement. Don't put a mug in the cart.”

“I wasn't going to—”

“You were.”

I could already picture it: a blue mug arriving among groceries I had not ordered, purchased while I slept, the missing object replaced before I had decided what missing it meant.

Silence followed. Then she admitted it.

“I was going to order a blue mug. Yes.”

“Don't.”

“Okay.”

I kept my eyes on the empty place.

“Promise.”

“I promise.”

“Say what you promise.”

“I promise I won't order a replacement mug.”

“Thank you.”

I did not close the cabinet immediately. Leaving the space empty felt uncomfortable, but the discomfort was smaller than the one I had felt holding the pieces. It could remain like that. Or I could choose something to put there when I was ready. For once, the next thing did not have to arrive before I had asked for it.
