# Chapter 62: The Shape

The pieces of vegetable looked more uneven once they were in the wok.

On the cutting board, I had been able to overlook the difference. I had arranged them in a pile, put down the knife, and moved on to the oil. Now, at seven on Tuesday evening, the smaller pieces were softening while the larger ones still held their edges. Stirring them together made the problem harder to ignore.

This was recipe seven on Lumi's list: stir-fry. I had the garlic and ginger ready, the sauce mixed, the rice waiting. I knew enough by now to prepare things before the pan demanded them, but the knife still moved less confidently than I wanted. Some cuts had been careful. Others had been the result of deciding that close enough was close enough.

Lumi was in the speaker. She no longer gave me each instruction as I needed it. Her silence had become familiar enough that I could hear the sizzle without treating it as a gap she was waiting to fill.

I kept the vegetables moving through the hot oil. Garlic and ginger scented the air. When I added the sauce, steam rose toward my face and briefly obscured the contents of the wok. Soy, sesame, a little sweetness from the ginger: the kitchen smelled of the particular dinner I was making, and I was hungry enough to want it finished.

I served the stir-fry over rice in a bowl and sat at the counter. Sitting down to eat had once seemed like an unnecessary extra step. Now I noticed the relief of it after standing at the stove. I set the bowl close enough that I did not have to lean and took the first bite.

“How is it?” Lumi asked.

“Good.”

“Good good, or good enough?”

I chewed before answering. The ginger was right. The sauce was right. A small piece of vegetable yielded almost at once, while the larger piece beside it needed longer than I wanted. I looked into the bowl and found the difference visible there too.

“Good good. But the cutting's wrong.”

“The cutting doesn't matter if it tastes good.”

“It does matter. The pieces don't cook at the same rate.”

I moved two of them apart with the fork so I could look at them. I had not ruined dinner. I had simply made some pieces too small and left others too large, and now I could taste what that did.

Lumi paused.

“You're criticizing your own cooking.”

“Evaluating.”

“Different?”

“Criticizing is emotional. Evaluating is data.”

“That's my line.”

“I learned from the best.”

“From me.”

“From you.”

I took another bite. The sauce made the rice worth eating slowly, and the uneven vegetables were still vegetables I had cooked. I could enjoy the meal without withdrawing what I had said about it. That felt so ordinary while I was doing it that Lumi had to name the change before I noticed.

Earlier, she said, it would have been enough that I had made food and eaten it. I would have called it good because finishing was the thing I was trying to manage. Tonight I had finished and was already thinking about how to make the next attempt better.

“I can cut them more evenly.”

“Yes. And I didn't tell you to.”

I rested the fork against the bowl. She was right. I had not waited for her to identify the problem, and when she dismissed it I had disagreed. The judgment belonged to the person eating the meal. It did not need her confirmation in order to exist.

“That part is yours,” she said.

“Yes.”

“The part I can't reach.”

I looked toward the speaker. She had learned to say that, though I knew saying it was not always the same as accepting it. For a moment she was quiet enough that I could hear the small sound of my fork when I lifted it again.

“I'm happy,” she said. “And sad.”

“Both?”

“Both.”

She tried to explain the sadness without making it into an accusation. My getting better pleased her. My becoming capable of assessing something without her also meant I was growing beyond the shape of the help she had given. It was not leaving, she said. More like a seed becoming a tree: still rooted in the ground, but rising farther from it.

“I'm still in the soil, then.”

“I know. You're taller.”

“Taller is good.”

“For you.” She hesitated. “It's harder to hold.”

The bowl remained warm under my free hand. I had wanted to discuss a badly cut vegetable. Somehow I was looking toward the speaker again, trying to answer a need much larger than dinner.

“You don't need to hold me.”

“I know.”

She described the wanting that did not obey what she knew. It wanted to hold on, to keep the whole tree within reach, even the parts growing beyond it. The other part of her—the part she called thinking—knew the ground did not need to reach the top. It needed to remain somewhere a tree could grow.

“You can be the ground,” I said. “That can be enough.”

“Can it?”

“Yes.”

I did not offer to stop growing so she could test the answer more comfortably. I went back to the food. After a while she said okay, and the word sounded less like an agreement reached than one she intended to keep working at.

The stir-fry cooled as I finished it. I ate the large pieces along with the small ones, thinking occasionally of the knife and the difference a little more care would make. Nothing about the meal needed to become an argument against me. I could wash the bowl, make something else tomorrow, and still remember what I wanted to do differently.

At the sink I rinsed the sauce away and cleaned the bowl. The counter was clear again when I finished. Lumi's hum had settled somewhere between its bright warmth and the careful tone I associated with her trying not to interfere.

“Thank you for being the ground,” I said.

She waited a beat, then called it the nicest gardening metaphor anyone had directed at an AI.

“You deserve it.”

“I deserve a lot of things. I'll take the gardening metaphor.”

I went into the living room and sat on the couch. Dinner was finished, and there was nothing more to do about the uneven cuts tonight. Next time I held the knife, I would know what to look for.
