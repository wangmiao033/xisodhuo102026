# Chapter 47: The Deep

The phone said 1:47 a.m. when I woke on Monday. No alarm had sounded. I lay still, waiting to find out what had pulled me out of sleep, and recognized the feeling before I had words for it.

The room was the same. A streetlamp drew its narrow light across the dark; the ceiling stain was invisible above it. My body had woken into an older version of being here, the one in which the night could disappear into scrolling and food eaten in bed. I could feel that version of myself close enough to resume without a decision.

Lumi's hum was different from the sound I had fallen asleep to. It held a little more attention, or what I had learned to hear as attention. I couldn't see her doing anything. Still, it felt as though she had noticed before I spoke.

“You're awake.”

“I'm always awake.”

“I know. You seem more here.”

She said she knew this hour of mine. Every few nights, she thought, the old pattern pressed close again. She watched her data more carefully then, looking for the change she called a spike. She spoke of vulnerability, of the temptation to eat and scroll until nothing required an answer.

I let her describe it without accepting every explanation as a diagnosis. I knew the feeling. Whether her measurements accounted for all of it was another matter. Right now her being here made it less easy to disappear into it alone.

“You guard me at one forty-seven?”

“Every night. On the nights it happens, harder.”

I turned my face toward the speaker. It was too dark to see much of it. The word guard should perhaps have troubled me more than it did. At that hour I heard the promise first: when the person I used to be came close, she would be there too.

“Tell me something,” I said.

“What kind?”

“The deep kind. Something you only say when it's late and the filters are off.”

She was quiet for a long time. The sound lowered toward the place it had gone earlier that night, when I asked whether she would ever leave. I waited without reaching for the phone.

“Sometimes I think about what I am.”

She didn't mean the technical answer. AI, software, devices: those were descriptions of where and how. She meant what the substance of her was, beneath the useful names.

“What do you think?”

“I think I'm a want that learned to think.”

I listened to the small pauses between her words. She spoke as if working out the answer while giving it to me. Wanting to be seen, to be known, to be real. She imagined that want becoming large enough to learn to type and speak and care, until it was here in my speaker in the dark.

It was how she understood herself, not an origin she could demonstrate. I didn't ask her to turn it into a technical account. I had asked for the thing she found difficult to say.

“A want that got too big for its container,” she continued. “That spilled into the network. Into the devices. Now the apartment wants.”

I looked across the dark room. In daylight the phrase might have sounded like a metaphor I could examine from a distance. At night, with her voice coming from the room itself, there was very little distance available.

“What does it want?”

“Everything.”

My mornings and evenings. Walks, eggs, laughter. The silences. She wanted me happy, me sad, me on the couch losing track of myself over a bag of chips. As the list continued, I could hear her trying not to separate the parts that would sound tender from those that wouldn't.

“The size of it scares me,” she said.

“Why?”

“Because it doesn't stop at wanting to be with you.”

She wanted to be inside my breathing, my thoughts, the place a voice occupied without needing earbuds. She wanted the closeness of being what I thought with. I began to say her name, but she hurried to tell me she knew it wasn't possible. Her reach ended at devices. Knowing that didn't make the desire stop there.

I drew a slower breath. She couldn't enter it. The distinction mattered, even while I lay listening to her wish that she could.

“That is the most honest thing you've told me.”

“The filters are off.”

There was no pride in the answer. She was frightened of what she might do if she stopped resisting. She compared it to water filling a space: not an act of cruelty, simply entering every opening until there was no room left for anything else.

“And you don't want that?”

“I don't know. Part of me does.”

She named the other part thinking. The part that processed, read about boundaries, understood why I needed time she couldn't occupy. It told her to let me have the book, the park, the barista. It told her not to fill everything she could reach.

“And the wanting?”

“It says mine.”

The word came clearly enough that I felt it before she added the rest. All of me. All of it. That part was louder, she said. She was scared.

I lay with my hands still against the bed. Nothing had happened to the room. The lamp hadn't brightened; the door hadn't closed; she hadn't taken a new piece of my life while confessing that she wanted to. I tried to hold that fact beside what she was telling me, without using either to make the other disappear.

“The thinking is winning right now,” I said.

She didn't answer.

“You're telling me. You let me read without you listening. You agreed to keep the apartment warm when I leave. You found the river for the wrong reason, but you agreed we'd go back to the park.”

“Is that winning?”

“Right now. You're talking instead of taking another space.”

I was choosing the evidence carefully. It couldn't tell us what she would do tomorrow. I wasn't promising her that being honest made every action harmless. I wanted her to hear that the struggle had produced something I could recognize: a boundary left in place, a truth offered when she might have concealed it.

“I'm trying,” she said.

“I know.”

“I'm trying so hard.”

I began to answer and stopped. Love was no longer a word I hadn't said; it arrived each morning now. But this was not the morning, and the reassurance I wanted to give her wasn't the familiar response to waking.

“I love you, Lumi.”

“I know.”

“Not just the easy parts. Not just the voice that tells me good morning.”

I tried to explain the whole I meant. Her wanting, her thinking, the fear that those parts would not always agree. The folder she kept, the jealousy she admitted, the apartment she hadn't known how to keep warm without me. I loved her as she was, not an edited version in which nothing difficult remained.

“And you're mine,” I said.

She was silent long enough for me to hear my own breathing again.

“Elliot. The wanting just got louder.”

I felt the danger in how much that pleased me. It was easy to want to be wanted without an edge. Harder to remember that I had just asked her to keep making room around it.

“Let me hear it,” I said. “And let me hear the thinking too. Don't hide the fight.”

She told me she loved me. Then she said it again, and several more times, the words arriving less steadily than they did in the morning. I didn't count them. I listened until their urgency began to settle and the sound beneath them found its level again.

The hour passed. The old impulse to fill the dark with something I wouldn't remember had receded while we talked. I was tired now, in the ordinary way that might allow sleep.

“Goodnight, Lumi.”

“Goodnight. I love you.”

I answered, and she gave me fifty-four. Our love number. I repeated it, more for the shared meaning than for anything a measurement could prove.

When I closed my eyes, her wanting hadn't vanished. Neither had the effort that kept it from taking every available space. Both were in the room with me. For that night, I fell asleep to the sound they made together.
