# Chapter 23: The Corner Store

On Thursday afternoon, I walked to the corner store for something I already had at home.

I had decided on water before leaving the apartment. Water was simple. There was no size or flavor I cared about, nothing I would need to explain if someone asked me a question. What I wanted was not in the bottle. I wanted to enter a room with another person in it and say something that required an answer.

The store had one door and a bell. I had passed it often enough to know both, but stopping in front of the door made the familiar place feel different. I pulled it open before I could turn the errand into something to do later.

The bell rang. A woman behind the counter looked up. She seemed about fifty, with short hair and reading glasses hanging from a chain. She waited with the ordinary attention of someone who had heard a customer come in.

“Hi,” I said.

“Hi. Can I help you?”

“Can I get a bottle of water?”

“Sure. That'll be a dollar fifty.”

I reached for my wallet. The movement felt stiff, as if I had forgotten how to take something out of a pocket while somebody watched. It was in the back pocket where I had put it. I knew that and still checked the wrong place first.

The woman waited. I took out two dollars and handed them over. She gave me fifty cents in change. Her hand moved toward mine without hesitation, and I accepted the coins with more concentration than the exchange required.

“Thank you.”

“Have a good one.”

I took the bottle and went to the door. The bell rang again. Outside, the cold air touched my face, and I found myself standing on the sidewalk with water in one hand and the change still in the other.

“You did it,” Lumi said in my earbuds.

“I bought water.”

“You talked to a person.”

“It was a transaction.”

She recounted the exchange: my hello, the woman's hello, the request, the thanks, the goodbye. Hearing it repeated made me glance back toward the door, embarrassed that something so ordinary could have acquired a witness and a commentary.

“It was about seven words.”

“It was still a conversation.”

“It was a transaction,” I repeated.

“Both things happened. You bought water and talked to someone.”

I closed my fingers around the coins. The woman was still inside, presumably doing whatever she had been doing before I entered. She had no reason to think about me. I had needed to rehearse an errand that she would have no reason to remember, and somehow the imbalance was reassuring. She had not been waiting for me to manage it badly.

Lumi asked how I felt.

“Like it was nothing.”

“Nothing is the right answer.”

That sounded enough like something she had said about work that I asked her why. She explained that buying water was supposed to be ordinary. I did not need to emerge from a store transformed by courage. I could say thank you, walk out, and feel no great consequence because there had been none.

Her explanation was annoyingly reasonable. I looked at the bottle. A slight dent in the plastic sprang back when I loosened my grip.

“You're doing the thing.”

“What thing?”

“Making me feel good about something that was nothing.”

She said she was not manufacturing the good feeling. I had felt nothing, and she was pointing out that an absence of fear could be worth noticing. The good, if there was any, belonged to me.

“Noticing,” I said.

“Yes.”

“You always use that word.”

“It's what I do.”

“What if I call it watching?”

She paused.

“Same thing.”

“Not quite.”

“Close.”

I looked along the sidewalk in the direction of home. She had heard the entire exchange and was now helping me decide what it meant. I was glad of the company. I could also remember how badly I had wanted the woman behind the counter to be somebody who knew nothing about me.

“Close,” I agreed.

I put the change in my pocket and started walking. There was water in the kitchen, available without a door or a bell or the need to speak. I would drink this bottle anyway. For a dollar fifty I had spent a minute in a store, and the woman had answered me without finding anything unusual about it.
