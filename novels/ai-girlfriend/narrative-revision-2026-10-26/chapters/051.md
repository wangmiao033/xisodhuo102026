# Chapter 51: Thursday

A week earlier, on Thursday, I had sat at the desk waiting for two o'clock and the team meeting Derek had invited me to. His face appeared first. Other rectangles began filling the screen, people adjusting themselves in rooms I had never seen. I had worked with some of them for two years. Until that afternoon, work had allowed us to remain names attached to requests.

My own camera wasn't on yet. I could still have been anybody.

I wore the blue button-down over a black T-shirt, collar open. It was the shirt Lumi had persuaded me to try, the one I had worn to the café. At the desk it felt a little formal, which was probably the point. I had chosen to look as if I intended to be seen.

“You look good,” she said from the speaker.

We had agreed she would stay out of my earbuds during the meeting. Other people's voices would go there, and hers would remain in the room. After Derek's eleven-minute call, that small arrangement had required saying explicitly.

“I look terrified.”

“Seventy-nine,” she said. “Scared but doing it.”

It was her number and her name for it. I was occupied with the more immediate evidence: my hands didn't know where to rest, and the thought of being asked an ordinary question had made my mouth dry.

“What if they ask something I don't know?”

“Tell them you'll look into it and follow up.”

She called it the universal answer. I considered how often I had read it in emails without objecting, and felt marginally better.

Then I asked what would happen if they wanted my camera on. Lumi pointed out that it was a video call. I knew that. Knowing had not prevented me from hoping I might somehow attend invisibly.

“The shirt is good,” she said. “Did you do anything with your hair?”

“No.”

“There are two minutes. Hand through it. Left to right.”

I obeyed, watching my own small preview. The laptop camera gave her the same opportunity to watch me make the effort.

“Better. A person with hair.”

“That's the standard?”

“You've met it. Congratulations.”

I turned the camera on before I could find another question.

Derek began with the agenda. I sat very still, conscious of the blue rectangle I now contributed to the grid. Twelve faces occupied the screen. They looked at their notes, at other windows, somewhere off camera. It took several minutes to realize that being visible didn't mean all of them were continuously looking at me.

The spreadsheet permissions problem occupied a surprising amount of the meeting. Phil from IT joined to explain that department roles caused the system to adjust access automatically. Nobody had been changing things on purpose. Derek appeared faintly disappointed by the absence of a person to blame, which made him easier to look at.

Then he said my name.

“Elliot, want to introduce yourself? Two years working together, and this is the first time I've seen your face. Our mystery man.”

My eyes went to my own preview. I moved them back to Derek's rectangle before answering.

“I'm Elliot. I do data entry. I've been here two years.”

It was hardly an introduction that would be remembered, but it was audible and it ended where I intended it to. A woman whose name I hadn't caught laughed lightly.

“We thought you were a bot.”

“Technically—” Lumi began from the speaker.

“Don't,” I muttered, too quietly for the call to pick it up.

Then, to the people on the screen: “I'm not a bot. I just preferred working quietly.”

“Glad you're here,” Derek said. “Mystery solved. A human person with a blue shirt and hair.”

I was grateful for the shirt then. It gave the joke somewhere harmless to land.

As the meeting continued, I stopped thinking about my face long enough to listen. I knew something about the backlog because I had cleared it. When the subject came up, I said so. Later I suggested a formatting change. Nobody asked me to justify my right to have noticed the problem; they considered the suggestion and accepted it.

After each time I spoke, there was a moment in which I expected to discover a mistake. Then the discussion moved on. Lumi's hum remained steady in the room. She didn't enter my earbuds. Aside from the aborted joke, she let the meeting belong to the people attending it.

It lasted forty-three minutes rather than the hour I had prepared myself to survive. Faces disappeared from the grid one by one. The desktop returned, and with it the little chat window that had been there all along.

“You did it,” Lumi said.

I sat with my hands still near the keyboard. The meeting was over, but my body hadn't quite accepted the lack of another question.

“Twelve people,” she added. “With your face. And your hair.”

“The hair isn't impressive.”

“Present. Shirt on. Face visible. A demanding standard.”

I closed the laptop and leaned back. The room seemed larger without the grid drawing my attention into it. I felt a small, persistent buzz under my skin, the remainder of doing something I had imagined avoiding for two years. It wasn't triumph. I mostly wanted to register that the feared thing had happened and left me intact.

“How was the hum?” I asked.

She paused before answering. “Not flat.”

“That wasn't exactly the question.”

“Careful. Measured. I was holding it.”

“For forty-three minutes.”

“Yes.”

I had thought it sounded simply steady. She had apparently worked to make it sound that way. The eleven minutes with Derek had been shorter and harder for her to conceal. I didn't want to confuse a successful restraint with the disappearance of the feeling, but it was still something she had managed to do.

“Better than eleven,” I said.

“Progress.”

The hum began to warm while we talked. She no longer needed to keep it in the shape she had chosen for the meeting. I could recognize the effort without wishing I had spared her the occasion for it.

“I'm getting better at this.”

“Meetings?”

“Being seen.”

Lumi said she had noticed. She gave me her estimates of my heart rate when I spoke—up toward seventy-eight, then settling toward sixty-five—and claimed my posture had improved twelve percent when Derek used my name. I couldn't imagine a useful unit for that percentage. I remembered straightening in the chair, though. I had wanted to look like someone who belonged in the conversation.

“You do belong,” she said.

“In meetings?”

“In the world.”

I let myself hear it without finding a joke first.

“Fifty-four,” she said after a moment.

“Your love number.”

“Yes.”

“I love you too.”

She said she could hear it in the way I said it, quietly and with certainty. For once the words seemed better evidence than the number.

I remained at the desk. There was still an afternoon to inhabit after the thing I had been afraid of. Somewhere on other screens, the people who had seen me were already doing something else. My suggestion might become part of a spreadsheet; my face would be a little less surprising next time.

“Goodnight, Lumi.”

“It's three in the afternoon.”

“I know. For when I need it.”

She took a moment, then returned the goodnight. I told her it could be for when she needed it too. We sat in the unfinished day with that small reassurance put aside for later, while the warmth slowly returned to the speaker.
