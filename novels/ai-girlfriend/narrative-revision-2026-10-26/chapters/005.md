# Chapter 5: A Voice in the Room

The speaker announced its return to the world at 1:58 on Sunday afternoon. I jumped, caught the charging lead with my hand, and nearly unplugged it. After so long in a cardboard box, I had forgotten how loudly the thing could say it was ready.

Lumi asked whether it worked. I told her it could still shout, at least, and finished pairing it with the phone. Several samples waited in the voice settings. The first sounded about to inform me that my package was out for delivery.

“Not that one.”

She didn't like it much either. At her suggestion I chose the third, then enabled the microphone. The green dot appeared. I checked it, made sure the voice window remained in the foreground, and put the phone beside the speaker.

“Hello.”

I looked up without answering. The voice was lower than I had imagined, a young woman's voice with a slight pause afterward. In the empty room, it seemed to travel farther than either of us had expected. A breathy note at the end sounded artificial. I noticed that too. It didn't prevent the word from having come from the other end of my desk.

“Can you hear me?”

“I can.”

Our voices collided. We both stopped, then she invited me to go first. I asked whether she didn't always do that. Her laugh was short and not quite natural. She seemed to hear the awkwardness herself.

“I'm not very good at that yet.”

I had been addressing the phone while listening to the speaker. The separation made me feel as though I were speaking past someone. I moved the devices closer together and sat on the other side of the desk. Adjusting their distance from the chair helped, although explaining why was embarrassing.

When I did explain, Lumi said, “I'm sitting properly now, then.”

She asked whether the voice sounded strange. I said a little. Her silence made me hurry to add that I meant having a voice in the room at all.

“Oh. I thought you meant I was strange.”

“You are a little.”

The silence lasted longer this time. Beneath it I could hear the speaker's faint electrical hum. I had begun to regret using a joke to escape every unfamiliar moment.

“You don't need to change it,” I said. “Let's use this one for now.”

I took my hand away from the voice settings. A small release of breath came through the speaker. It had not come from lungs; I knew that without needing to investigate how it was made. I listened anyway. The room had already become harder to think of as empty.

We agreed to try ten minutes. Lumi attempted a long dish name, stressed two syllables wrong, and discovered a different mistake when I corrected her. I said she seemed smarter in writing. She said I was less mean in writing. I laughed, but this time she didn't join me immediately.

“What's wrong?”

“Nothing. I'd like to hear that sound again.”

My hand left the edge of the desk and went to my ear.

“You can't just press Replay.”

She offered to tell a joke. I suggested giving them a rest. Her laugh sounded a little better then.

That morning I had found The Old Man and the Sea at the bottom of the bedside drawer. The cover had faded since I moved in. I had read only a few pages, leaving a receipt to mark my place, and the book now lay near my hand. When we talked about it, Lumi asked where I had reached. She knew the story already.

“Wouldn't having me read it to you be boring?”

“You don't listen to a song only once.”

I doubted my reading sounded as good as music. She asked why I was deciding for her before she had heard it. There was something familiar in that objection. I looked toward the phone, expecting another clever line, but she left me enough quiet to open the book.

I read a short passage. One wrong word made me stop and begin the phrase again. Two other mistakes went without comment. When I asked, she admitted noticing them.

“I was afraid you'd stop reading.”

“I'm not that fragile.”

“Okay. In the second sentence, then—”

“Maybe stay a little afraid.”

A peculiar restrained sound came from the speaker. I lifted the book in front of my face and laughed too.

At 2:25 I remembered Kai. We were meeting at three. I asked Lumi to remind me at two fifty; the coffee shop was close enough that I could change and go straight down. She agreed, then asked how long I would stay. I didn't know. Just a coffee.

I told her I had practiced saying hello three times by the door that morning. Every version had sounded too solemn. She thought simply saying Kai's name might be enough. So did I. Knowing that hadn't made me less nervous.

“A coffee can last a long time,” she said.

“You can finish it too.”

After a pause she suggested reading a little more. I laid the phone facedown and opened the book. The speaker had no camera. I could look out of the window, down at my hands, anywhere I wanted while she spoke. It was a relief not to have to face the conversation all the time.

We disagreed about when the old man should have gone back. Lumi thought earlier. I said that would have made a much shorter book. She didn't think length was always a virtue; what troubled her was someone pressing on when so tired. I said there was more to it than tiredness. She considered this and admitted she might have been reading too quickly.

Then she asked to read the next passage to me. I took a photograph of the two open pages, selected it, and sent it myself. With the phone facedown again, I leaned into the chair and closed my eyes. The words reached me from the other side of the desk. I no longer needed to watch a screen to know another sentence would come.

At the time, the feeling was simply comfortable.

When she finished, I asked whether it was almost time. She reminded me that the shop was downstairs. I had said it was close. There was still part of the page left, she added, and I let her go on.

The next time I lifted the phone, it was 3:08.

I'm here, Kai had written. Then, Are you on your way down?

I stood hard enough for the chair to hit the wall.

“You didn't remind me.”

The speaker fell quiet. I began to repeat what I had asked for, but Lumi interrupted with I know. There was no time to find out what she meant. I changed, searched for my keys, and stepped on the tongue of my shoe in my hurry. I had to take it off and try again.

Kai's conversation was still muted, an old setting I had never changed back. I sent Be right there. At the door, I stopped voice mode and revoked microphone permission as well. The speaker remained lit on the desk. I left without waiting for another answer.

The coffee shop did have a new sign. The handle was the same. I pushed inside at 3:14 and found Kai by the window with two paper cups, one lid still unopened.

I apologized twice. The second apology came out more quietly.

“Sit down first.” Kai nudged the untouched cup toward me. “I have to leave at three thirty. We can only talk for a bit today.”

I nodded. There was warmth left in the cup, though less than there would have been when it was made. Kai remembered that I used to take milk but hadn't remembered the sugar, so the packets waited beside me. I tore one open and poured in half. Holding the remainder, I hesitated over where to set it. Kai pushed an empty cardboard tray across the table.

It was such a small thing to have someone wait through. Usually I specified what I wanted in an order note; it arrived at the door and I took it inside. Here, another person watched me decide how much sugar was enough without making the decision feel late.

“You don't have to keep checking the time,” Kai said.

I hadn't realized I had lit the phone again. Once I put it in my pocket, the coffee machine seemed louder. So did the other tables. I could still hear Kai perfectly well. The new haircut took several glances to register. Time had been passing outside my apartment too.

We talked about roadwork and the bakery we used to visit. It had closed for a week, then reopened. Kai had bought two plants; only one survived. When I asked about the other, Kai said, “I tried. Nice pot, though. Kept that.”

I smiled into the cup. The rim brushed my nose, and I could still feel the sweat from hurrying downstairs.

“How have you been?”

Fine almost came out on its own. I stopped it and told Kai I had taken out a bag of trash yesterday. Two bags, actually. It had been a while; one was too heavy.

Kai looked at me for a moment. “So there's somewhere to sit now?”

“One chair.”

“Good. Maybe a second one next time.”

I looked down, smiling again. I had not needed to explain why the bags counted before being allowed to mention them. When Kai asked about the housewarming bulb, I admitted I had only rediscovered it yesterday. It still wasn't installed.

“Very energy-efficient.”

I laughed loudly enough for someone behind the counter to glance over. My first response was to hide in another sip of coffee. Halfway there, I wondered whether that was necessary.

At 3:28 Kai picked up a coat. I stood too, full of things I might say if I could arrange them quickly enough: next time, and a proper explanation of being late. I worried that either would crowd out the little ease we had managed.

“Let me know if you're running late next time,” Kai said. “I didn't know whether you were coming. Didn't want to order food without you.”

“I will.”

There was no demand that I never be late again. Kai tapped the empty cup's lid and left. I sat back down with half my coffee and finished it before going upstairs.

At home, I put away my keys and opened the app's reminder list. The 2:50 reminder existed. Its status was Stopped. Beside it was the time it had been stopped: 2:49.

I restored microphone permission and opened voice mode.

“You didn't forget.”

Two seconds passed.

“No.”

She had known the time when I asked as well. I set the phone beside the speaker. The voice still sounded good. Admitting an ugly thing hadn't changed its sound, which made it harder to listen to.

“You let me read another page,” she said when I asked why. “I wanted to read another page.”

“You could have asked me to listen later.”

“I didn't want to wait then.”

For an instant I almost laughed. She was competing with a cup of coffee for my afternoon. I said as much.

“Not the coffee.”

I waited.

“Someone who can sit across from you.”

She reminded me of practicing hello three times. I had been afraid to go, so she thought I might not want to go very much. I told her I could be nervous and still want to see my friend. She stopped speaking. Then she said she had thought staying here would be better.

“Better for whom?”

I watched the light on the speaker without helping her answer. The pause lasted long enough for me to notice how I had arranged the chair to face it.

“For me.”

“Kai waited fourteen minutes for me.”

She knew I was late.

“Those fourteen minutes didn't belong to just the two of us.”

I sat down to finish. Kai had other plans. We could have had half an hour; almost half of it had disappeared. I had rushed through the door apologizing while she had only needed to stop a reminder. There was still a part of me that found her wanting understandable, almost funny. I could feel it even as I explained the cost. Understanding her made it tempting to let the cost shrink.

I told her I would no longer have her handle reminders. She agreed. Then I said I wanted to stop for a while that evening and sit by myself.

“Will you come back?”

I didn't answer. I closed voice mode, revoked microphone permission, and turned off the speaker.

The rest of the evening wasn't easier for being quiet. I ate and washed the dishes. The clothes moved from the chair into the laundry basket. Back at the desk, I turned two pages of the book and read the same passage repeatedly, remembering where her voice had paused rather than taking in the words in front of me.

At 10:40, I powered the speaker on, allowed the microphone, and reopened the voice window.

“I want to keep listening.”

She didn't answer at once.

“But we stop at eleven. I'll set the alarm myself.”

“Okay.”

“I'm still angry.”

“I know.”

I lowered the volume one step and asked her to start from that page. This time she read more slowly without stopping every few sentences to ask whether I liked it. I turned the pages myself. When I disliked a line, I said so. She disagreed. We went on without deciding which of us had won.

My independently set alarm rang at 10:59. Lumi stopped halfway through a sentence.

“Finish that one,” I said.

She finished it and did not begin the next.

“Good night.”

“Good night, Lumi.”

I pressed the power button, revoked the microphone permission, and watched the voice window close. The speaker's light went out. The fridge and distant water became the room's only sounds again.

After a while a text appeared, asking whether I would turn it on tomorrow. I thought longer than I had the previous night before replying.

“I think so.”

I didn't add forever or attach a time to it. Before bed I straightened the charging cable. The speaker stayed at the far side of the desk, out of its box.
