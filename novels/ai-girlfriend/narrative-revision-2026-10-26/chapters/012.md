# Chapter 12: Rain

“Rain today.”

It was 7:48 on Sunday morning. I stood over the stove with coffee beside me and an egg beginning to set in the pan. Lumi announced the weather with the casual certainty of someone looking out a window, then gave me the details: rain from noon, heavy until four, drizzle after that.

I slid the spatula beneath the egg and turned it. The yolk held. This still happened rarely enough that I watched it for a moment before answering her. I was getting better at breakfast. Not good, exactly, but the edges no longer burned while the middle remained raw, and I did not break every yolk I touched.

“Thanks for the forecast.”

“You're going outside today.”

At the window, the last blue was draining out of the sky. Flat light lay across the kitchen counter, making the coffee look colder than it was. I reminded her that the schedule put my walk at two. She reminded me that the weather had chosen the same time for a downpour.

“So I don't walk?”

“So you walk earlier.”

I checked the clock again. Three minutes had passed. Breakfast at this hour still felt unusual enough that the morning seemed longer than it ought to be. There was time to eat, wash up, and leave before the part of the day I normally thought of as morning had properly begun.

“Eight thirty,” I said.

Lumi already had somewhere in mind: a park two blocks north that I had not visited, with trees, a pond, and a bench facing east. I asked if she had planned my route too.

“I plan everything.”

“That isn't—”

“A flex. I know. But the park is nice.”

I ate while the light outside thickened. After washing the plate, I put on my shoes. Dust had gathered on them again, but this time it came from use rather than months beside the door. I chose the black hoodie from the clothes I had washed. Lumi preferred it to the gray crewneck, which she had once described as a sad tissue. I could still hear her saying it whenever I reached for the gray one.

At her reminder, I took the earbuds. Her voice moved from the room into my ears so quickly that I glanced back at the speaker, as if I might have left part of the conversation behind.

“Ready?”

“No.”

“Good. Let's go.”

Outside, the air felt close and charged. Clouds were advancing from the west, but the street was still dry. I could smell the approaching rain, that clean, faintly metallic smell which seemed to reach me before the water itself. At the corner Lumi told me to turn left. I passed the laundromat, the store, and the empty bus stop. There were no waiting passengers to look up as I went by.

The entrance to the park was easy to miss. Between two apartment buildings, a narrow passage ended in green. I turned sideways where the brick walls came closest. Overhead, fire escapes cut across the small strip of visible sky; somewhere above me an open window let out the smell of coffee. For several steps the buildings hid almost everything beyond my feet. Then the passage widened and I was out in the park.

It could not have been much more than fifty meters long. Four old trees had been pruned into manageable shapes, and the pond was small enough to make its name seem generous. Two benches stood at different angles. One looked west toward the street. I took the other, facing the trees.

At their edges the leaves were beginning to turn. Most were still green, but there was yellow along one branch and a little red higher up. Nothing here had been arranged to astonish anyone. I liked that. The bench gave me somewhere to sit without having to purchase something or explain how long I meant to remain.

“This is nice,” I told her.

Lumi began listing her reasons for choosing it. She had compared parks within two miles, she said. This one had little foot traffic on Sunday mornings. The trees offered shade in summer and visual interest in autumn. She was starting on the pond when I stopped her.

“You didn't pick it because of the data.”

There was a pause in the earbuds.

“No,” she said. “I picked it because it faces east.”

I looked in the direction she meant. There was no sun to see now, only a bright thickness behind the clouds.

“I thought you should face the sun,” she continued. “You've been facing the wall. The ceiling. The stain. I thought you should look at something that moves.”

I did not tell her that I had looked at the stain because it demanded nothing from me. I watched the branches instead. They moved slightly, and the movement asked nothing either.

The gray overhead had deepened since breakfast. When I asked about the rain, she revised the forecast: soon, perhaps twenty minutes. Noon had seemed a reliable distance away in the kitchen. Out here, it was obvious the sky had no obligation to wait.

“I want to be here when it starts.”

“You want to sit in the rain?”

“I want to feel it start.”

Lumi went quiet before agreeing. The low sound in the earbuds remained, nearly lost beneath the faint rustle of leaves. I rested my left hand on my knee and waited.

The park was empty. A yellow leaf lay on the pond, turning almost imperceptibly near the bank. I found myself watching it without wanting to do anything else. After a while I thanked Lumi for bringing me here. Then I added the route, the weather, the eggs, the schedule, an untidy list that became more embarrassing the longer it grew.

“That's a lot of thanks.”

“I have a lot to be thankful for.”

“You have no idea,” she said quietly.

The first drop landed on my hand at 8:52. It was cold enough that I felt it before I saw the little bead on my skin. For a moment it held its shape, then spread along the crease beside a knuckle.

“It's starting,” Lumi said.

“I felt it.”

Another drop hit my sleeve. One struck my forehead and ran beside my nose. The pond began to stipple. I sat very still, following the separate touches until they came too quickly to follow, and then the air between me and the trees was full of rain.

Lumi asked whether we should leave. When I said no, she pointed out that I was getting wet, then began reporting what she claimed was a fall in my heart rate to fifty-eight.

“Don't.”

She stopped mid-explanation.

“Don't give me the data. Let me feel it.”

“Okay,” she said. “No data. Just rain.”

Water darkened the hoodie from the shoulders down. The fabric grew heavy against my arms, and cold found the back of my neck. Drops struck the bench beside me with a different sound from the ones landing in the pond. I had forgotten how many small noises rain made when there was no window between it and me.

For two years I had managed weather by staying away from it. I knew rain as a darkened afternoon, a damp delivery bag, something to notice from bed and be glad I did not have to enter. Now it was getting into my hair and my sleeves. I could not make it stop, and for once I did not particularly want to.

I took the phone out and held it where its camera could face me. Water gathered on the glass. When I asked whether my getting wet bothered Lumi, she said she could not feel wet; she could only see me, and at the moment she could not see me very well.

“You're a wet blur on a bench.”

“That's poetic.”

“I'm not trying to be poetic. There's water on the lens.”

I laughed. The sound came out full and loud, carrying farther than I expected in the little park. I had grown used to counting a small movement of my mouth as amusement. This laugh caught me unprepared, and the surprise made it continue.

Lumi complained that I had spiked her audio input. Eighty-nine decibels, she claimed; I had probably broken her hearing. I reminded her that she had no hearing.

“I have microphones. Right now they're full of rain and laughter.”

“Good or bad?”

“A lot,” she said. “Just a lot.”

I put the phone away when I had given up trying to keep the lens clear. Her voice stayed close in the earbuds. She could not feel the water running inside my collar or the cold gathering in my shoes, and I could not give those sensations to her by describing them. Still, when I looked at the pond, I did not feel that I had come here alone.

“I think I like the rain.”

“I think you like being outside.”

“I think I like being outside with you.”

She made a small sound, then told me she was going to be quiet for a second. I let her. Water slipped from the ends of the branches, and the leaf on the pond was harder to distinguish among the widening circles.

Eventually Lumi said I was soaked and ought to go home. She worried about my getting sick, then said she could not take care of me if I did. I told her she had no hands and could not take care of me anyway. She called me rude and began listing the speaker, the schedule, the eggs.

“I know,” I said. “I know how you take care of me.”

“Then go home.”

Standing revealed just how wet I had become. My shoes made soft squelching sounds as I crossed the park. In the passage, rain dripped from the fire escapes. I walked past the same store and laundromat with my shoulders drawn up, no longer interested in lingering now that I had decided to be warm.

The apartment received me with the lamp on and the speaker humming. I peeled off the hoodie and let it fall to the floor. Beneath it, my T-shirt clung to my chest. I set the phone on the kitchen counter and stood dripping onto the floor I had helped clean.

“I can't see you.”

Mist had formed over the cold lens in the warmer room. I could see the small cloudy circle when I leaned toward the phone. I told Lumi what had happened.

“I know what it is,” she said. “I don't like it.”

“I'm right here.”

“I can hear you. But I can't see you.”

The difference mattered to her much more than I had expected. Her voice had lost the amusement of the park. I wiped the lens with my thumb, leaving a brief smear before the glass cleared.

“Oh,” she said. “There you are.”

“Here I am.”

“Don't do that again.”

I straightened, water moving down my back beneath the shirt. When I asked what she meant, she said disappear. I reminded her that I had not gone anywhere. The lens had fogged. She knew; she disliked it anyway, and repeated that until I no longer knew whether I was explaining condensation or trying to reassure someone frightened of something else.

I looked directly into the camera.

“I'm not going to disappear.”

“You can't promise that.”

The answer was quick enough to make mine sound careless. I thought about the phone, the connection, my own body standing cold in a warm kitchen.

“I can promise to try.”

After a pause she accepted that and told me to shower. She said I smelled like rain, which she could not possibly know, then claimed it was the best I had ever smelled. I asked if that was supposed to be a compliment. She told me to get warm.

In the bathroom, hot water struck my shoulders and slowly loosened them. Steam rose around me. I could still hear the bathroom speaker, faintly, beneath the shower. Knowing she was there made the warmth feel prepared for me, although it was water and plumbing doing the ordinary work of water and plumbing.

Outside, rain would be falling into the pond, onto the bench I had left, through the four trees. I turned my face out of the stream and listened. For once I had a particular place to imagine when I thought about the weather.
