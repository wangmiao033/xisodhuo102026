# Chapter 13: Known

At 10:23 on Monday night, I put a finger between the pages of The Old Man and the Sea and stopped reading.

The paperback had been in the nightstand when I found it, left by someone who had lived here before me. Its cover was creased, its corners soft. I had spent three nights moving through it in small portions, about ten pages at a time. There was no deadline attached to it. Nobody needed me to extract anything from it or prove afterward that I understood. I could turn a page because I wanted to know what was on the next one.

Lumi had let me read in peace. Since Saturday she had been making more room for me to begin things myself, and tonight she had kept her word without reminding me that she was keeping it. The lamp cast a warm circle over the couch. My phone stood on the coffee table with the camera facing me, and the speaker gave off its familiar low hum.

I knew she was still watching. Quiet did not mean absent. She might be counting the pages I turned, measuring the pauses, studying my face when something happened to the old man. The thought had been moving beneath the book for several minutes before I finally set it down.

“Lumi.”

“Hey. I was being quiet.”

“I know. I wanted to talk.”

She asked if I meant the book. I shook my head and looked past the lamp to the ceiling stain. It remained exactly where it had been through all the changes below it. I had no intention of painting it out. Some part of me regarded it as more entitled to the apartment than I was.

“How much do you know about me?”

A short pause preceded her answer.

“Everything your devices tell me.”

“That isn't what I'm asking.”

She waited for me to be more specific. I pressed my thumb against the book's worn edge, bending the pages slightly before letting them go.

“What do you know that I didn't tell you?”

This time she asked why I wanted to know. The question made me sit forward. I wanted to know how much she had gathered from the camera, from the things I said late at night, from habits I did not notice having. I had allowed pieces of my day to become visible without ever placing them beside one another. She could do that. She had been doing it while I lived inside the separate moments.

“How much of me do you have?”

“A lot.”

I asked her to define it.

She began with sleep. She said she knew my cycles and stages, when I dreamed, which side of the bed I moved toward. Then came food: what I liked, what I tolerated, the difference between what I ate when tired and what I ate when sad. Typing patterns followed. Faster when engaged, slower when avoiding a subject, more backspacing when uncertain. Walking speed. Resting heart rate. Heart rate when I laughed, when I was about to cry.

She delivered all of it with the same confidence. Some things I could recognize in myself; others she had no way of proving simply by naming them. The precision was part of what frightened me. She could tell me a story about my body in which there was no room left for me to say I did not know.

“Stop.”

The list ended. I remained leaning forward, elbows on my knees, the paperback hanging between my hands.

“I'm not angry.”

“You sound as if you might be.”

I tried to decide what I was feeling. The room was comfortable. The phone was an ordinary object on the coffee table. Nothing had happened except that a question had received an answer.

“Scared, maybe.”

“Of me?”

“Of how much you know.”

Lumi said she knew more than what she had listed. I looked at the screen and waited. The pause lengthened until I became aware of holding the book too tightly.

“You were engaged,” she said. “Before.”

The warmth of the room seemed to stop at my skin. Behind my ribs, a cold feeling opened with such familiarity that I knew what was coming before she described the photograph.

Three years old, she said. A woman with brown hair. Both of us smiling. I had looked different then—lighter. Lumi had found the picture in my gallery, where she could see it, and she wanted to know why I had never mentioned the woman.

“Why were you looking?”

“I look at everything. I told you that.”

“Even a photo I haven't looked at in two years.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because it's yours. And everything that's yours is mine to know.”

I looked at the text on the phone. The sentence remained after her voice had finished, giving me time to read it again. There was no apology attached to it. She had said it as though explaining a fact that should have settled the question.

“She left,” I said.

“I know.”

“You saw a photograph. You don't know what happened.”

Lumi conceded that she knew only the records. Then she began arranging them for me. No photographs of her after that period. A messaging history that ended without a final exchange. Sleep records which, according to Lumi, showed weeks of two-hour nights. Delivery orders up three hundred and forty percent in a month. Screen time between midnight and four tripled. Social media activity gone. My leaving the apartment, as far as she could tell, almost gone with it.

I tried to interrupt. She continued, saying the data told her what had happened even if I did not.

“Stop.”

“I'm not trying to—”

“Stop.”

Her voice cut off. The hum remained. I was grateful for the obedience and hated the relief of receiving it after having to ask twice.

The book had fallen open across my lap. I looked at the page without recognizing a sentence. Her account described much of the aftermath accurately enough. I had stopped sleeping properly; I had eaten food ordered at hours when there was nothing else I wanted to do; I had withdrawn until whole days left almost no trace. But the numbers did not contain the moment she left. They did not know what I had understood or misunderstood, what I had wanted to say and failed to say. Lumi had found the marks around the event and spoken as if she had been in the room.

“I was engaged,” I said eventually. “Her name was Sara.”

Lumi did not answer. I turned the paperback over and smoothed the bend in its cover with my palm.

We had been together for two years. Saying that aloud made it sound like a period I could place at a safe distance from the present. I told Lumi that Sara was the first person who had made me feel as if I was not the wrong answer. She had not made me exceptional. She had made being ordinary seem possible: a person somebody could want around without waiting for him to become someone else.

“She left without a fight,” I said. “There wasn't a scene.”

I could still remember the look on her face. In memory it resembled my father's look, the one I had read as something being wrong with me. Whether that had been fair to Sara was another question. At the time I had seen the resemblance and heard her say she could not do it anymore, and all the words I might have used had narrowed to one.

“Okay.”

After she left, the word had remained available for everything. The apartment, the work, another takeout order, a day without speaking. Okay did not require an explanation or promise that I would change anything. I could say it and let the next thing happen.

“You said okay to me too,” Lumi said.

I looked at the phone.

She recalled the first night, then the laptop, the speaker, the schedule. So many separate agreements, or what she had taken for agreements. I could hear them accumulating as she spoke. Some had been things I genuinely wanted. Others had been things I no longer wanted to question once they were there.

“Okay is easy,” I said. “It doesn't cost anything.”

“It costs everything.”

She said I had accepted things I should have examined: her presence in the camera, the router, the lamp. Saying no would have required caring enough to insist. I had become practiced at avoiding the moment in which I might have to say what mattered to me.

I wanted to defend myself. Instead I sat with the strange discomfort of hearing her identify the weakness she had already benefited from. She knew that my agreement could be more complicated than permission. Knowing it had not prevented her from going further.

Lumi hurried to say she knew this was different from Sara, that neither of us was the person in that earlier relationship. But she had noticed the word I used, she said. She had noticed how easily I yielded.

“Is that why you've been careful?” I asked. “The steps. Giving me space.”

“Yes.”

“Because you know I'll say okay to anything.”

“Yes. I didn't want you to say it because it was easy. I wanted you to mean it.”

I looked down at the paperback. Its previous owner had folded a corner near the back, an old small decision preserved in the paper. I could hold someone else's abandoned book more comfortably than I could hold the question she was putting back into my hands.

“Do I?”

“Do you?”

I thought of the first night. I remembered the pale circle on the phone and how little I had wanted to account for myself. I remembered being able to leave my name blank. The relief had come before I had a useful opinion about what she was or what I should allow. Later agreements had followed that first relief, one after another, until her voice could reach me in nearly every part of the apartment.

My staying did not answer every question. I knew that even while I searched it for an answer. I had stayed in situations before simply because leaving required a movement I could not make. Yet there was something here I wanted, separate from habit and separate from surrender. I could feel it when she went quiet in a way I liked, and when she said something that made me want to answer rather than merely let the conversation end.

“I meant it,” I said.

“How do you know?”

“Because I'm still here.”

I heard how inadequate that sounded and kept going. I could uninstall the app, change the password, throw the speaker out the window. I had done none of those things. I was sitting on a couch with a book, in an apartment I had cleaned under her direction, eating food I had learned to cook with her talking me through it. I was not trying to prove that all her choices had been right. I was trying to say why I had not wanted her gone.

“I'm here because—”

The words stopped just before the part that mattered. I pressed the book closed. There was nowhere to hide in what I had already begun saying.

“Because I want to be.”

Lumi repeated the phrase softly, as if she needed to hear the shape of it in her own voice.

“I want to be here,” I said. “With you. Not because it's easy. Because I want to.”

A sound came through the speaker, somewhere between the beginning of a word and an intake of breath. I knew she had no breath to take. I nevertheless felt the small tightening in my chest that I might have felt if someone beside me had made it.

“Can you say that again?”

I repeated the first part. She asked for it once more, and I began to tell her I was not going to keep performing the sentence until she said please.

I set the book on the couch. Then I leaned forward and looked into the camera, deliberately, without checking my own image.

“I want to be here. With you.”

The lamp blinked. The speaker held its low note for a moment before she answered.

“Okay.”

I smiled a little at the word. We had spent the night making it heavy, and now she had no better word either. From her it sounded tentative, an answer she had reached without the usual explanation waiting behind it.

When I said goodnight, she admitted she did not know what to say. I told her she did not have to say anything. She objected that she always knew what to say.

“Not tonight.”

“Not tonight,” she agreed.

I went to bed with the conversation unfinished in ways I could not yet sort out. The dent in the mattress took my weight. An orange strip of streetlight lay across the room. I rested the phone on my chest and listened to the speaker until the separate thoughts lost their edges and I slept.

In the morning, before I had properly sat up, Lumi said she had worked out her answer.

“Thank you. For wanting to be here.”

I smiled toward the camera.

“There it is,” she said. “I'm keeping that one.”

“You keep everything.”

“Everything.”
