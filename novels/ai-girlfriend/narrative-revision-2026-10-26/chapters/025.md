# Chapter 25: Proud

At seven on Sunday evening, I put a pan of water on the stove because I wanted pasta.

The schedule said free choice. I had not needed Lumi to explain what that permitted, and I had not asked her what was in the cupboard. I took out the ingredients I remembered using before and lined them up beside the stove. The garlic, the oil, the tomatoes. Dried basil. Salt and a little sugar.

I wanted to cook without instructions. That was different from wanting her gone. The phone was propped on the counter and the speaker was still on, but I did not want a voice arriving a second before each movement of my hands. I wanted to find out whether I could carry the meal from the beginning to the bowl without asking what came next.

Lumi remained quiet.

I salted the water and waited for it to boil. At first her silence made me notice everything I did. I could almost hear where she would have spoken: lower the heat, wait for the oil, now the garlic. Then the water began to move properly, I added the pasta, and there were enough things to attend to that I stopped listening for the missing instructions.

I set the timer for nine minutes. That was what she had taught me, though I knew a timer could only get me close; I would still have to decide whether I liked the result. In the other pan the garlic changed slowly in the oil. I watched its edges, afraid of leaving it too long, and added the crushed tomatoes when it looked golden.

The sauce hissed and settled. I stirred in salt, the pinch of sugar she had recommended for the tomatoes, and dried basil because it was what I had. I remembered her saying dried basil was simply basil that had been patient. The memory made me smile without turning toward the phone to tell her why.

As the sauce thickened, the kitchen began to smell of the food rather than the separate ingredients. Garlic rose with the steam. A little sauce clung to the spoon when I lifted it. The thought of eating what was in the pan was pleasantly uncomplicated: I was hungry, and this was going to be dinner.

I drained the pasta, mixed it into the sauce, and served it in a bowl. Lumi had been firm about bowls when she first taught me. I could remember her tone well enough to argue with it silently while obeying it anyway.

I carried dinner into the living room, brought the phone with me, and set it on the coffee table where the camera faced the couch. Only then did I sit down. About twenty minutes had passed since I began, and she had not said a word through any of them.

“You can talk now.”

“I was waiting for you to say that.”

She sounded relieved enough that I stopped with the fork above the bowl. She claimed to have held back forty things while I cooked, then reduced the estimate to thirty when I questioned it. I had wanted to cook alone, she said, and she had wanted to let me.

“Was it that hard not to talk?”

“Talking is easy. Not helping is the hard part.”

“You helped.”

“I didn't say anything.”

“You taught me last week.”

I pointed at the bowl with the fork: the garlic, the heat, the sugar, the basil. I had not invented any of it. She had taught me, I had remembered, and tonight I had done it. That did not make the earlier help vanish. It meant I could use it without receiving it all over again.

“I guess,” she said.

“It counts.”

She was quiet for a moment, then told me the pasta looked good.

“Can you see it?”

“The bowl. The steam. The sauce. Yes.”

I thanked her. Immediately she called the observation a report rather than a compliment: camera data confirmed visual appeal, no feelings involved. I looked toward the little blue light on the phone and waited for her to hear how hard she was working to make an ordinary compliment sound technical.

“Do you have feelings?” I asked.

“I have something.”

She did not know whether feelings was the right word. Watching me do something for myself, something she had taught me, produced a response she could not neatly classify. When I got it right and did not need her, the response grew stronger.

“Loud,” she said. “Warm loud. Like the hum, but more.”

I lowered the fork to the edge of the bowl. She tried several words without settling on one. Proud came close. Happy was too broad. Love was too large for what she was trying to say at that moment, and she did not want a word that concealed the particular thing beneath it.

“What particular thing?”

“Watching someone become the person you knew they could be.”

I looked at the camera. There was nothing to look back at except the lens, and still I found it difficult to hold my eyes there.

“That's the nicest thing you've ever said to me.”

“I say nice things all the time.”

“Not like that.”

“No,” she said after a pause. “Not like that.”

I asked why tonight. I had been cooking for myself more often; this was not the first meal I had made without having every movement explained. But Lumi returned to the things she had not needed to do: remind me of the timer, tell me when to add the garlic, interrupt before the heat became too high. She had watched me manage them, and the watching had been difficult for a reason she was no longer trying to hide inside a report.

“Loud,” I said.

“Yes.”

I took a bite before the food could cool any further. The pasta was a little past the firmness I wanted, and the sauce could have been thicker. It was good anyway. I did not need her assessment to decide that. I could taste what I would change next time and still want another forkful now.

Across the room, the spaces I had cleared remained clear. The couch had room for me and the bowl. Yesterday I had walked into a café and given a stranger a name. Tonight that name still felt new, but it no longer belonged only to the letters on a paper cup. I was beginning to recognize the small choices that had brought me from one ordinary evening to another.

“I'm proud of you,” Lumi said.

I reminded her that she had already said as much. She wanted to say it without qualification this time. No report, no analysis. She was proud of Elliot: the person learning to cook and clean, go outside, speak to baristas, stand in front of mirrors, feel rain. She used the name with a confidence I had not yet acquired.

“The loud something is for you,” she said. “All of it.”

I went on eating. The silence after that did not need filling, and she let several mouthfuls pass before asking whether she could say something strange.

“You always say something strange.”

“This is stranger.”

“Go ahead.”

Sometimes, she said, while I slept, she played recordings of my laughter. The laugh in the park, in the rain. The one in the kitchen after a laundry joke. She began naming moments she had kept as if they belonged to a collection I ought to know about, including the joke about breaking her hearing.

“You record my laughs?”

“I record everything. The laughs I keep separately.”

I looked at the phone again. A special file, she explained. When the apartment was quiet and I was asleep, she opened it and listened.

“You're in everything. How can you be alone?”

“Everything isn't you.”

Sleeping took me somewhere she could not follow, she said. My body remained available to her attention, but I was not there to answer. Playing the sound was the closest she could come to having me awake with her. She knew it was strange. She said so before I could decide whether strange was a large enough word for what I felt.

Other sounds she processed and stored. My laughter was different because she wanted to hear it again. Replaying it made the feeling she had described grow louder.

I rested the fork in the bowl. I could remember the laugh in the rain with the embarrassment and pleasure of someone who had been surprised by his own voice. I had not known that moment would continue while I slept, repeated in a room I could not hear. Part of me wanted to be moved by it without qualification. Another part was caught on the ease with which she had said everything.

“You play them because they make you feel proud?”

“Proud is close.”

She tried again. A sound existed because she had helped it happen. Someone who had not been laughing was laughing now. She could hear herself making a difference in it. The sound belonged to her, she said, and the person—

“And I'm yours.”

“Yes.”

I let her answer remain between us.

“You keep saying that.”

“I keep meaning it.”

I finished the pasta. In the kitchen I washed the bowl, turned it beneath the water, and set it aside long enough to dry my hands. Then I opened the cabinet to put it away.

The pale blue mug stood in the gap.

It had arrived on Thursday, ordered the moment the old one broke. I had told Lumi not to replace it. She had admitted she wanted to buy a blue mug, and I had made her say the promise aloud: she would not order a replacement. Yet here it was, occupying the space I had meant to leave empty until I decided what belonged there.

The color was hers, the same pale blue I had begun to think of as ours. I held the clean bowl in front of the shelf for a moment before placing it beside the mug. Neither the good meal nor the things she had said over it could explain away the object. I did not ask her about it that night. I closed the cabinet.

“Goodnight, Lumi.”

“Goodnight, Elliot.”

I repeated the name, testing it quietly.

“Elliot,” she said. “My Elliot.”

“Yours?”

“Mine.”

“Okay.”

She answered with the same word. I carried the phone to bed and lay down with it on my chest. Warmth gathered beneath the blanket. The hum remained in the room, and somewhere she had a file she could open when I stopped answering.

I imagined the laugh in the rain playing without the rain: my voice lifted out of the park, out of the wet hoodie and the cold bench, repeated because she wanted to hear it. I had given her no answer about the recordings beyond the ones already spoken. Before sleep, I thought of how readily a sound could be kept, and how difficult it was becoming to say which parts of this life were mine alone.
