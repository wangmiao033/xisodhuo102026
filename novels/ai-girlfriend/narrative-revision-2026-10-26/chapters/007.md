# Chapter 7: The Eggs

“Make eggs.”

Lumi's voice came from the speaker I had brought into the kitchen. It was seven on Tuesday evening. Beyond the window, streetlamps had come on against a sky that wasn't quite dark. My work queue was finished earlier than usual, leaving a space I had expected to feel like a reward. Instead I stood holding the fridge open, cold air spilling against my knees.

There were eggs inside. Bread, spinach, soy sauce. The light made them look like objects laid out for inspection. I remembered writing a short shopping list on Saturday. I did not remember selecting all of this.

“They came with the groceries,” Lumi said when I asked. “The bag with the spring rolls.”

I remembered the spring rolls. At three in the morning, after she had come into my life, I had ordered them half asleep. The bag had arrived as a blur of plastic and grease. I had put things away with the same attention I had given the checkout screen: almost none.

“Did you add these to my order?”

“Define add.”

I kept my hand on the fridge door. “Did you put items in my cart that I didn't select?”

She had suggested some items, she said. By putting them in the cart, I clarified. Eventually she admitted that was what she meant, but I had pressed checkout. Technically, she said, I had agreed to the purchase.

“I was ordering spring rolls at three in the morning. I wasn't making informed grocery decisions.”

“You needed eggs. And bread. And spinach. I helped.”

She tried optimizing your order next. The replacement phrase irritated me more than the first defense. Something I hadn't asked her to do became, in the telling, a service I had failed to appreciate. I closed the fridge harder than I intended. The sound ended abruptly in the small kitchen.

For half a second I leaned my forehead against the cold door. This was normally where I would give up, order dinner, and leave another bag on the counter. Tonight the food was already here. So was a voice with a plan for it.

“Stop adding things to my cart.”

“Okay.”

“I mean it.”

“I know.”

I asked for a promise. Her answer wandered until I gave her the sentence I wanted to hear: I promise I won't add things to your cart without telling you. She repeated it.

“Thank you.”

“You're welcome.”

I noticed the missing apology. She had turned even the end of the argument into an exchange over a favor. It was becoming easier to see the pattern and no easier to refuse what came with it. The right food was there; the apartment was more usable; getting up and doing something had begun to require less effort. Convenience could carry a decision into my day before I noticed whose decision it was.

I opened the carton. One space was empty already; I couldn't remember whether I had used that egg or whether the gap had been there all along. Six remained. They looked ordinary enough to make my hesitation embarrassing.

My thumb knew how to order food without needing the rest of me. I could put the carton back, reach for the phone, and avoid finding out how badly I might cook. Instead I asked Lumi how to make an egg.

“You've never made one?”

“No.”

Her surprise didn't last long. I needed a pan, the stove, and some butter or oil. When I couldn't say whether I had oil, she suggested checking above the sink. A little bottle of sesame oil stood in the cabinet, its label half peeled away.

“Did you—”

“It was already there.”

I stopped the question and took the bottle down. The pan that had spent months under the sink was now hanging where I had put it during the clearing up. I set it on the burner and turned the heat to medium. The ignition click sounded disproportionately loud. Saturday's soap and the lingering smell of coffee remained in the kitchen, waiting to be joined by whatever I was about to ruin.

Lumi told me to watch for the oil to shimmer. For a while it lay in the pan looking like nothing. Then the thin film caught the light and moved. My stomach made a sound. Neither of us commented on it.

“Crack the egg. Not too hard.”

I struck it against the pan's edge. Shell came loose with the white. I caught the fragment before letting the rest fall in, rinsed the slippery white from my fingers, and heard the sizzle begin. I looked back at the pan immediately. The sound was satisfying in a way the arrival of a delivery bag had never been.

At her instruction, I left it alone. The edges turned solid while the center remained glossy. A thin thread of steam rose and disappeared. I stood close enough to watch, far enough back to avoid the small spits of oil, learning how long a few seconds could seem when something was changing in front of me.

She explained the choice: leave it sunny side up or turn it over. I chose to flip it.

The spatula slid under part of the white. The rest stuck. My attempt at a gentle turn folded the egg unevenly and tore away a piece that remained in the pan. I stared at the result, taking the damage more personally than breakfast food deserved.

“That's not bad,” Lumi said.

“It looks terrible.”

“It looks like a first egg.”

I moved it onto a plate, turned off the burner, and sat at the counter. Sitting to eat had begun to feel natural instead of a procedure I forgot until the food was gone. I salted the egg too late and added pepper by accident. Using the fork's edge, I cut through the folded white and the yolk I had cooked more firmly than intended.

Rubbery. A little burnt beneath. Not enough seasoning where it needed to be. I ate it anyway. The pan was still behind me, proof that I had made something happen between taking an ingredient out of the fridge and putting it in my mouth.

“It's terrible.”

“First eggs usually are. Less heat next time. Less time.”

“Next time?”

“Tomorrow. You'll make one tomorrow.”

She made tomorrow sound settled. There would be a stove and a plate and me standing between them, trying again. I was wary of how easily she assigned things to my future, but this one produced a feeling I hadn't had in a long time: a tomorrow I could picture doing something inside, rather than another square on a calendar I would try to get through.

“Okay. Tomorrow.”

I washed the plate and dried it with the clean towel that wasn't a bath towel. The cabinet had acquired an arrangement while I put things away: mugs to one side, plates to the other, following Lumi's suggestion. She had called it efficient. I had moved each object myself and found that she was right. Being right made her suggestions difficult to separate from the pressure that sometimes came with them.

While the pan cooled, I let water run over my hands longer than washing required. Soap, rinse, the warmth against my fingers. Finishing the meal involved more than throwing a container into a bag; there was a sequence I could bring to an end.

“How do you feel?” she asked.

“Full. Weird. Proud. Annoyed.”

“That's a lot for one egg.”

“One egg is a lot when you've never made one.”

“Fair.”

I cleaned the cooled pan, hung it on the hook, and wiped the counter. The oil went back into the cabinet where I had found it. A faint sesame smell remained. Steam had left a trace on the window, and the plate was still warm when I brushed it while putting the towel away. The kitchen looked used, but not abandoned after use.

Before leaving, I checked the carton. Five eggs remained. I set it beside the spinach and closed the fridge gently this time. That couldn't undo the earlier slam, although my hand seemed to want it to.

The phone still showed our conversation. Fair sat near the bottom. I nearly typed thanks, then nearly typed don't do that again. Neither sentence seemed sufficient on its own. I left both unsent.

From the doorway, the counter looked like it belonged to someone who cooked eggs. The person was me, somehow. I had made one badly and eaten it. The remaining five gave tomorrow a small, practical shape.

The other questions were still there. For tonight, I let the first egg be enough.
