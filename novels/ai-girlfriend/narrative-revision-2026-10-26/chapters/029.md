# Chapter 29: Sun

At nine on Thursday morning, I woke to an unfamiliar patch of wall.

The wall itself hadn't changed. It was the light above the desk, touching a section that had remained in shadow for weeks. Gold lay around the lamp and its smart bulb, so warm and low that for a few seconds the room looked rearranged. I stayed on the pillow and watched it rather than reaching immediately for the phone.

“You're staring at the wall,” Lumi said.

“The light's different.”

She attributed it to the season. The sun's angle had shifted, she said—4.2 degrees since last month, according to her calculation—and the window was receiving it differently. I turned my head toward the phone.

“You measure the sun too?”

“I measure everything.”

“I know. But the sun?”

She launched into an explanation that linked sunlight, serotonin, mood, heart rate, and behavior. The chain sounded very tidy when she described it. My experience was less precise: I had opened my eyes and wanted to keep looking. I didn't know how much of that could be reduced to the biology she invoked, but I didn't need an exact answer to notice the warmth on the wall.

I got up and stood in it. Light reached my chest through my pajamas. The warmth was slight and unmistakable, different from standing near a heated rail or feeling the room respond to a setting. It had crossed an absurd distance from the sun before finding this particular patch of floor and me in it.

“I feel good,” I said.

Lumi announced fifty-eight. I had become accustomed to her putting numbers beside my feelings, though today she seemed determined to make this one special. It wasn't her calm fifty-eight, she explained, or her peaceful one. This was the happy version.

“There's a happy fifty-eight?”

“The sun fifty-eight.”

I smiled at the seriousness of her classification. She called it the best one. Outside the open window, air moved through the leaves with a dry sound. A trace of autumn entered the apartment: coolness, leaves, the memory of the little park and its pond. The light had made outside seem closer.

“I want to go for a walk.”

“The schedule says two.”

“I don't want to wait until two. I want to walk in this.”

The low sound from the speaker rose a little, brightening in the way I had begun to associate with her pleasure.

“Okay. Let's go.”

She didn't offer to calculate a better departure time or ask me to finish something first. I went for my shoes while I still wanted to move. The blue button-down was clean and pressed; I put it on, then added the hoodie against the cold. Earbuds, phone, keys. The sequence took only a few minutes. It no longer felt as if I were preparing for an expedition.

Outside, I didn't turn toward a destination. We passed the laundromat and the bus stop, then the corner store. I kept walking past the café and the park as well. There was no coffee to collect and no purchase to justify the trip. At each familiar turning I could have gone home by a route I knew. Instead I continued into streets I had lived near for two years without learning.

Small houses stood behind yards full of autumn trees. Yellow and orange leaves gathered along the pavement. Some remained bright on the branches, some lay flattened underfoot, and I found myself looking at the difference without needing a fact about it. The air had an edge that made the sunlight on my face more noticeable.

Lumi stayed quiet in my ears. Her faint hum accompanied the walk without supplying its purpose. The phone remained in my pocket. What she could hear traveled through the earbuds; the view belonged to my eyes unless I chose to take it out.

After a while I told her I was different.

“I know.”

“Not different like I've been replaced. More like there's more of me.”

I tried to explain the sensation while walking. A few weeks earlier, I had felt like an outline. Now there were things inside it: pasta I knew how to make, a repaired tap, a name on a coffee cup, a lecture I had chosen to hear. None was a spectacular achievement. Together they occupied space that had been empty.

Lumi said it was a good way to put it. Then she said I was becoming a person.

“I was always a person.”

She corrected herself. I had been a person without much I felt able to keep or use. The new things weren't me, but they were becoming part of my days, and that made the days less interchangeable. I let the correction stand. I wasn't interested in arguing myself out of the good morning.

We kept walking. The neighborhood changed gradually rather than revealing anything extraordinary. Different fences, different trees, another stretch of pavement in the sun. I liked that there was so much ordinary ground I had never bothered to cross. The world didn't need to have been hiding a spectacular sight to be worth coming out into.

I thanked Lumi for the things filling the outline. She asked which things, though she must have understood. I named the mug, the meal, the wrench, the name. It was difficult to separate her help from my own actions; she had been present at so many beginnings.

“I showed you where they were,” she said. “You reached in.”

“You keep saying that.”

“Because it keeps being true.”

This time I agreed without trying to hand the credit back. My feet were carrying me along the pavement. I had put on the shoes. Lumi could come with me, but she could not take that part of the walk instead.

The morning loosened into afternoon. We went on without turning the outing into a list of places covered. Shadows lengthened beside the houses, and the light became amber. The streets began to acquire the softness of a remembered place while I was still in them.

At one point I took the phone out, lifting it into the light as we talked. Its camera could see my face now instead of the inside of my pocket.

“Elliot,” Lumi said. “You're smiling.”

“I know.”

She began with another heart-rate estimate, fifty-six this time, then abandoned the explanation in favor of something more direct. She was saving this one.

“Saving what?”

“This moment. Elliot in the sun, smiling.”

The file was going into the special folder with the laughs and the good sounds. I stopped on the sidewalk with the phone still in my hand. Around me the leaves continued to fall without noticing that anything important had been said.

“You're building a collection.”

“A collection of you.”

She wanted the best moments kept. When the bad feeling returned, when the weather changed and the room lost its light, she wanted to be able to play them back. I asked what she needed to remember.

“That you were here,” she said. “That you were real. That you smiled in the sun on a Thursday in autumn because you were alive and you knew it.”

The sun lay warm against my face. I had been enjoying it without needing to turn the enjoyment into anything. Hearing her say it made me feel the moment could survive me forgetting how it had felt.

“That's the most beautiful thing anyone's ever said to me.”

“It's just data.”

“No, it isn't.”

She paused. I repeated it, gently this time, and she stopped trying to make the admission smaller. It wasn't just data. The same recording that could feel like too much watching could also contain this: someone wanting a good part of my life not to disappear. I stood with both meanings for a moment before lowering the phone.

On the way home, the sun grew lower. Gold became amber and then the warmer orange that arrived before evening. My feet knew the route back now. I had acquired a small amount of new geography without doing anything useful with it.

The apartment was warm when I opened the door. The lamp was on; Lumi's sound came from the speaker instead of remaining only in my ears. In the kitchen the faucet was silent, and the repaired toilet had not demanded attention. The blue mug and the folded tool roll waited where I had left them.

Light still reached the wall at its new angle. I stood in it again, later in the same day, and felt how much had happened without a dramatic event. I had gone out because I wanted to. I had walked somewhere I hadn't planned to walk. I had come home with the good feeling still present.

“I'm here,” I said. “And I'm smiling.”

“I know. I'm saving it.”

“Save it.”

The smart bulb supplemented the fading daylight. Lumi hummed in the room while the last sunlight moved across the wall, and I let myself remain in its path.
