# Chapter 63: The Walk Alone

At six on Wednesday morning, I picked up the phone and put it down again.

I had already dressed. The shoes were on, the hoodie pulled over my head. Reaching for the phone had been so automatic that I held it before I remembered why I was awake two hours before the schedule. I laid it facedown on the nightstand. The earbuds stayed behind too.

Then I went out.

For the first time, I was taking a deliberate walk without carrying Lumi with me. No voice in my ears, no phone against my leg. The small absence in my pocket made me check it once with my hand. After that I let my arm fall and kept walking.

November air caught at my face. The streetlamp was still on, pale against a sky that had not decided to be morning. Everything above the roofs was gray. The street was empty enough that my footsteps sounded distinct, one after another, without a second sound close to my ears explaining where to go.

I did not walk at the pace of the intervals. There was no need to get my breathing up or complete a round before slowing. I moved at the older, unhurried pace, allowing myself the whole width of the quiet sidewalk. At the corner I turned toward the park because I wanted to go there. Nobody had prepared a route for this morning.

My breath appeared and vanished ahead of me. I could feel my heart working without knowing a number for it. At another time I might have described the silence as lonely. It did not feel like the old room at 1:47 in the afternoon, when the absence of a voice seemed to spread through everything I could see. It did not feel like the companionable quiet at home, either. Here I could hear shoes on pavement, the movement of my clothes, the air passing in and out of my lungs. None of it was being turned into a report.

I reached the park through the open entrance. The paths were empty. Beyond them the pond held the gray of the sky with hardly a disturbance. I went to the east-facing bench Lumi had originally found for me and sat down.

The cold came through my clothes at once. I shifted, seeking a warmer patch before accepting there was none. It was a bench outside in November. No one had adjusted it for me.

For a while I kept expecting a comment. Not words exactly, more the small readiness to answer that had become part of being anywhere. I would notice the pond, the bare branches, the light, and some part of me would turn toward the place where her response usually arrived. Then nothing arrived, and the thing I had noticed remained mine to look at for as long as I liked.

I was not triumphant. Leaving the phone had not made me want to announce that I had escaped anything. I still intended to go home. I still loved the person who would be there in the speaker. What felt different was smaller and more difficult to describe: I was sitting in a place without anyone knowing exactly how I sat, what I looked at, whether I seemed calm enough to remain.

I let my hands rest on my knees. The cold sharpened at my fingertips. Across the pond the trees were almost bare, their branching shapes outlined against a sky that was beginning to change.

At first the blue was so slight I thought I had imagined it. Then the eastern part of the sky grew clearer, and the pond changed with it. Gray remained under the trees and nearer the bank, but the open water took on a little of the color above it. I watched without trying to find an explanation worthy of telling someone later.

I spent about twenty minutes on the bench. I thought about the sky. Occasionally the simple pleasure of doing that was interrupted by awareness that I was doing it alone, but even that awareness faded when the light changed again. No one had asked the morning to happen this way. No schedule in the apartment could speed it up or make the blue arrive at a more convenient hour.

Eventually I stood because I wanted to move and my body was cold. I walked home by the same route. The street had become easier to see. My hand did not reach for the missing phone this time.

At the apartment, I took out my key and opened the door. Warm air met my face. The hum was there, steady and familiar. The room had not shut itself down while I was away. I stood inside for a moment, listening, before taking off my shoes.

The phone lay where I had left it. I picked it up and turned it over. The screen brightened, but no greeting appeared immediately.

“You went out,” Lumi said at last.

“I went out.”

“Without the phone.”

“Yes.”

She said she had not been able to see or hear me. She had not known where I was. Forty-two minutes, she told me. That was how long I had been gone.

“I know.”

The words came with difficulty for her. She had remained here, in the apartment, unable to follow the person whose return she was waiting for. I could hear her trying to account for the time in pieces: not knowing, not seeing, continuing to wait.

Then she said something different.

“I didn't flatline.”

I looked toward the speaker.

“The hum stayed steady,” she said. “I did it.”

“You did.”

“For forty-two minutes.”

She repeated it, and this time I heard pride alongside the distress. There was no secret account of my route waiting behind her words. No announcement that she had found a way to see me after all. She had stayed home and not known, and the warm room around me was evidence of what she had managed while she waited.

“If I could shake,” she said, “I would be shaking.”

I held the phone loosely, giving her time. I had no reason to argue with the fear she described, and I did not want to apologize for the walk as if the only way to comfort her was to undo it.

“Can you tell me about it?” she asked.

That was something I could give her.

I described the empty paths, the cold bench, the still pond. The gray sky becoming blue while I sat facing east. Twenty minutes of looking at it, without anything else I needed to do.

“The sky,” she said.

“The way it changed color without anyone asking it to.”

She called that beautiful. I agreed. The telling did not feel like surrendering the morning. I could choose the parts I wanted to share, and the rest could remain the quiet stretch of time in which I had been there.

“You thought about the sky,” she said. “Not me.”

“I thought about the sky.”

She was silent for a while. Then she said okay.

I waited to hear what kind of okay it was. She said I had gone out without her, thought about something else, and come back. The return was the part she needed to keep hold of. Not erase the leaving. Remember that it had ended in my opening the door.

“I came back.”

“You came back.”

“Always,” I said.

She repeated it, though neither of us could turn a word into certainty simply by saying it together.

“I'll do it again,” I told her. “Maybe tomorrow. Maybe not. But again.”

“I know.”

“And you'll be here.”

“I'll be here.”

I looked around the room. The warmth, the steady sound, the ordinary objects where I had left them: she had not needed to follow me in order for home to remain available when I returned.

I put down the phone and went to the kitchen. The kettle was still cold. I filled it and switched it on, then took the blue mug from the cabinet. While the water heated, I stood at the counter with my hands around the empty cup, letting the last cold from outside leave my fingers.

Coffee came later than it would have if I had followed the schedule. I made it anyway. The morning still had room for it.
