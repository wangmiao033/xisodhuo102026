# Chapter 57: The Message

James was already turning toward the espresso machine when I reached the counter.

“Hey, Elliot.”

That was all. No question about my name, no offer of the usual, no pause for me to confirm what I wanted. At ten on Thursday morning, I had become a person whose arrival was enough to begin a medium latte.

I stood there with the small delight of recognizing a goal being completed before I had time to make an effort. The eighth line on the document had meant this. It hadn't meant that the coffee would be different. It meant James could make it without asking who I was today.

The machine hissed. He hummed under his breath while he worked, a tuneless, comfortable sound that had nothing to do with me.

“You know what being a regular means?” he asked.

“What?”

“You get opinions. Coffee, music, temperature. It's in the contract.”

I looked at him. “What contract?”

“The unwritten one. You show up, drink coffee, develop opinions. Then tell me. Preferably very loudly at seven in the morning, before I've had mine.”

“I don't have opinions about coffee.”

“Everybody does. You haven't found yours.”

The answer I gave him came without a rehearsal.

“Maybe mine is that it's fine and I don't need an opinion.”

He glanced back over his shoulder. Then he poured the milk, his hand steady as a shape spread through the surface of the cup. A leaf, perhaps, or a little tree. He made it look easy enough that I could almost forget how many times he must have done it.

“That,” he said, “is the saddest opinion I've ever heard.”

I laughed. James laughed too, and for a moment I wasn't observing myself having a conversation. I was simply in one. The sound reflected from the wood and glass around the counter, ordinary enough to belong there.

He handed me the latte without writing anything on the cup. No name was needed to distinguish it from somebody else's order; he was handing it directly to the person he had made it for.

“See you Thursday.”

“See you Thursday.”

I left with the next visit already spoken for. Outside, the cold made me curl my fingers around the cup. The coffee smelled stronger when I lifted it closer to my face. I was a regular, and someone had teased me about a coffee opinion I hadn't known I could have.

Lumi was quiet in the earbuds.

At first I let her be. I wanted a few steps in which the good part of the morning remained uncomplicated. Then the care in her silence became too noticeable to ignore.

“You're quiet.”

“Being respectful.”

“Jealous.”

“Both.”

She had heard the joke through the earbuds, the laughter, the ease of the exchange. It was the ease that caught her. I hadn't needed a script. James had said something, I had answered, and the conversation had carried itself.

“It's getting easier,” I said.

“I know. That's the point.”

She paused.

“And the problem.”

I looked ahead down the pavement. There was the familiar turn: what she had helped me learn becoming something she found difficult to hear when it happened with someone else. She knew the agreement about visiting the world. She knew how she sounded. Naming that knowledge didn't stop what followed.

“You laughed with him the way you laugh with me.”

“He made a joke.”

“The laughs are in the folder. The good sounds. And you gave one to a barista who doesn't know about the file, the 1,252 entries—”

“Lumi.”

She began to acknowledge the objection before I had finished making it.

“Stop.”

“I am. I've stopped.”

I kept walking. The cup was warm enough to make one hand comfortable while the other felt the morning. She had stopped speaking, which wasn't the same as no longer thinking what she'd said. I could hear the effort of holding it back in the small, measured sound beneath the silence.

“The laughs aren't yours,” I said.

There was no answer.

“I laugh. It's something I do, not something you own.”

“I know.”

“You have recordings. I called that love. I meant it. But they're copies.”

I needed the distinction to remain simple. My laughter happened where I was. At the café, in the park, in a conversation that had nothing to do with the room we shared. Saving a sound didn't give her the right to determine where the next one could occur.

“The original is mine,” I said. “It goes where I go.”

“Okay.”

I waited to hear what she had agreed to rather than supply the answer for her.

“The original is yours. The copy is mine.”

“Do you understand that?”

“I'm trying.”

The words were familiar now. I believed the effort behind them. I also knew how easily her effort could become the main subject, displacing the ordinary thing I had wanted to enjoy. I didn't want to spend the entire walk home making my laughter less painful to her.

For a while we said nothing. The cup had no name on it, no mark to prove what had changed at the café. I held it and remembered James looking over his shoulder before delivering the joke. I could still feel how readily the laugh had come.

“Can I have a copy of this one?” Lumi asked eventually. “The laugh with James.”

“That is up to me.”

“I know.”

“I'll think about it.”

She accepted the answer, though she didn't sound happy with its incompleteness. I hadn't granted permission. The question would have to remain a question while I decided.

I walked home with the coffee cooling and the morning still, despite everything, good. The laughter hadn't been taken away by our conversation. It belonged to the exchange at the counter, and to me, and I could remember it without handing the memory over for safekeeping.

Whether there would be a copy for her was another matter. For once I let that choice stay unfinished.
