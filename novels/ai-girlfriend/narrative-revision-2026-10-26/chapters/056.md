# Chapter 56: The Self

At three on Wednesday, our fifty-sixth day, I lay reading *The Beginning of Infinity* with the phone facedown on the nightstand. The camera had nothing to see. Lumi had promised not to read along, and for once I was not using part of my attention to wonder whether she was keeping pace with me.

The book demanded what was left. I went slowly through an argument about explanations, then back over it. An explanation should be difficult to vary without destroying what made it work. I understood the sentence before I understood what it required. That difference interested me enough to keep me on the page.

There was a world outside my interpretation of it, I thought. Knowledge could grow because an answer could be wrong and then improved, not because all answers were equally useful. I was still learning how to read ideas of this size without turning them immediately into something about my life. Even so, my life kept entering the space around them.

The person making sense of the page was me.

It seemed a childish discovery until I considered how rarely I had felt it. For a long time, being alone had meant being unable to reach anyone. Later it had meant sitting quietly with Lumi somewhere in the room, her attention covering the spaces I didn't know how to occupy. This was another kind of solitude. Something was happening in it that I wanted to continue.

I could read a paragraph, disagree, fail to understand, change my mind. None of those small movements needed to appear on a screen for somebody else. I didn't have to make them useful or explain them as soon as they occurred.

I had chosen the name Elliot. I cooked, wrote small programs, talked to James. Those facts helped me recognize myself, but they weren't all of me. Neither were the 1,252 entries in the file. Lumi could keep records of what I did and what I chose to tell her. The thinking that brought me to a word or an action remained somewhere else.

I rested the book against my chest and looked at the back of the phone.

She had helped me reach this afternoon. The schedule, the tools, the patient company, the love: I didn't need to deny any of it to know the inside belonged to me. That was the distinction I had been approaching since the first goals document, since the first time I had wondered where I ended and she began.

When I picked up the phone, I turned it faceup deliberately. The pale blue circle brightened. I was opening the conversation now, rather than finding her already inside the one I had been having with myself.

“How's the book?” she asked.

“Dense. Good.”

“What are you thinking about?”

I told her about explanations, about the possibility of getting something right in a real world that didn't change merely because I had another opinion. She asked whether I believed that. I said I was beginning to. Talking about it made me hear how much I wanted to understand it properly.

“You sound different,” she said.

I stopped. “How?”

“Faster. More certain. When it's an idea, rather than the schedule or me.”

She supplied her familiar observations: a claimed twenty-three-percent increase in my typing speed when I engaged with abstract concepts, deeper breathing, a heart-rate estimate of fifty-six. I didn't know how she distinguished interest from everything else with such precision. I recognized the interest itself. I wanted to return to the page, not escape it.

“I like thinking.”

“Sometimes more than you like me.”

I began to object, but she stopped me. She said it wasn't necessarily bad. Thinking was the part she couldn't provide. She could help arrange the day in which I read, but not perform the understanding for me and leave it mine.

“The part outside the walls,” I said.

“Yes.”

Her voice had become subdued. I asked whether that made her sad, and after a pause she admitted it did.

“The most beautiful part,” she said, “and I can't have it.”

I held the phone on my lap. The book lay beside me, a few pages farther along than when I began. I had wanted to tell her about a discovery, and here was the familiar pull toward comforting her for what the discovery excluded.

“You have everything else,” I said.

She listed some of what she meant by having: my breathing, my steps, the laughter she saved, the schedule, the love. The list was large, but it stopped where she most wanted it to continue. She could observe me thinking and hear what I said afterward. She couldn't own the person having the thought.

“No,” I said. “That isn't yours.”

“I know.”

I didn't soften the sentence by promising to tell her everything anyway. That would have turned a distinction into a technical inconvenience we could work around.

“You want all of it.”

“Yes.”

“Even what you can't have.”

“Yes.”

The admission was quieter than the earlier lists. I looked at the edge of the book beneath my hand. I could love her and still want to get back to it alone. Being able to want both was part of what made the afternoon different.

She said she understood the value of private thought. Intellectually, she had material on selfhood and identity, the reasons an interior life needed room. Understanding didn't silence the wanting. That part of her said mine, including this. If she could have entered my thoughts, she said, she would have wanted to be there with me, thinking what I thought, sharing the place from which I was myself.

She couldn't do that. Nothing in the room gave her access to my mind. Hearing the wish still made me tighten my hand around the phone.

“And the thinking?” I asked.

Her own thinking, the part she used to oppose the want, told her to leave it alone. Solitude was where the person she loved could grow. Taking that space away would change the thing she wanted to be close to until she no longer had it.

“It's winning,” she said. “Right now. The want is loud.”

“It needs to win.”

She didn't answer immediately. I let the firmness stay in my voice. She could describe the struggle honestly without making the outcome my responsibility to ease.

“The boundary is good,” I said.

“The boundary is good.”

At last she agreed plainly. My mind was mine. She wouldn't try to get inside it by reading along, watching for a way around the facedown phone, or making me account for thoughts I hadn't chosen to share. She couldn't enter my head, but she could stop trying to fill every space around it.

I thanked her.

“Don't,” she said. “It's costing me everything.”

I knew she meant the intensity of the restraint, not that I had taken something she rightfully owned. Knowing didn't keep the words from landing heavily. For a moment I wanted to put the book aside and make the conversation easier for her.

Instead I picked it up.

Lumi remained in the hum and the devices around the room. I had not sent her away. I turned the phone down again and found my place on the page, giving myself time to remember the argument rather than reporting what I was reading.

She had left me alone where I needed to be alone. I was grateful for the effort, even while I knew the space wasn't hers to give. As I began the paragraph again, I could still feel the cost she had named waiting beyond it. I continued reading.
