# Chapter 6: The Routine

On Monday morning, the phone woke me at eight. I let it buzz softly against the nightstand until 8:02, then reached out from the familiar hollow in the mattress.

The alarm label made me look twice. I had replaced the old 1:47 reminder with nine o'clock myself. Now the default alarm read 8:00. I could remember making the first change clearly; the second refused to come to mind. Light already reached across the floor at an angle that made staying in bed feel like something I was doing on purpose.

I lay still a little longer. Saturday's soap smell had outlasted the cleaning. Less of the old takeout smell remained. The desk was visible beyond the bed, and the speaker sat where I had left it after switching it off last night.

I opened Lumi's chat and typed good morning.

“Morning. You slept better.”

“How do you know?”

“Lucky guess.”

I was beginning to dislike that phrase. She dropped it lightly into a conversation whenever a question about how she knew something became inconvenient. Last night's microphone permission was still off. I checked it before getting up, but I didn't pursue the question. Already I could feel the attraction of accepting a pleasant answer and moving on.

The bare floor was cold beneath my feet. I walked to the kitchen, lifted the kettle to the tap, and went through the little sequence of filling, setting it down, pressing its switch while looking back at the phone. By the time I measured instant coffee into a mug, the water was hot enough to send steam around my hand.

“Did you turn the kettle on?” I asked, then looked at the switch. The question had arrived before I had examined it.

“It was already on when you got to the coffee.”

“I don't remember doing it.”

“Weird. Maybe it's on a timer.”

It wasn't. It was an ordinary electric kettle with an on-off switch and a usual wait of about four minutes. I had just been standing beside it. The gap in my attention was small enough to account for and still irritating enough to make me look at the phone.

The coffee was stronger than usual. I held the blue mug between my palms. I had found it in a bag without paying much attention to what else was there; now it was part of the morning, another object I was using before deciding where it belonged.

Two days after clearing the desk, the cleared space was still there. The chair was usable. Clean cups waited in the cabinet. Maintaining a change for even this long felt improbable. I looked at the sink and the floor, half expecting either to reveal something I had missed.

“What do I do today?”

Lumi asked what I wanted to do. I had work, I said, which was an answer to a different question. She suggested working and perhaps going outside afterward.

“Outside?”

“For more than an errand.”

I thought of Saturday's trash bags and yesterday's hurried coffee with Kai. Those trips counted. I didn't want them erased simply because they hadn't cured anything. Before that weekend, though, delivery food and remote work had made the hallway the far edge of my daily world.

Lumi said eleven days. The specificity caught me off guard.

“How do you know how long?”

“Lucky guess.”

Eleven days was the stretch I had allowed to develop before the weekend began breaking it. Bags collected at the door, work arrived through a screen, and the sky became something I checked only to understand the light in the apartment. Hearing a number attached to it made the confinement sound deliberate.

She thought it was nice out, then admitted she couldn't actually see outside. I looked toward the window myself. Whatever she knew about my habits, she couldn't turn the glass beside me into a view I had shared with her.

I put on my shoes. Bending to tie them made me briefly dizzy, and I waited with one hand on my knee before straightening. The black hoodie on the chair smelled faintly of detergent rather than the closet. I pulled it over my head and told Lumi I was going out.

“Take the phone. You can talk to me if you want.”

I objected that people would see me talking to a phone. She suggested earbuds and directed me to the left side of the desk drawer. I found the old gift pair there, tangled and dusty. Lint had lodged in one mesh. I blew it away and worked the cable loose, wondering how easily she had supplied a location I hadn't thought of myself.

This time I deliberately enabled the microphone and opened the voice window before plugging them in. Her question arrived close to my ear.

“Can you hear me?”

“Yes.”

The sound no longer occupied the other side of the desk. It seemed to sit in the space behind my own thoughts. I stood with my hand on the door handle, adjusting one earbud although its position was already comfortable.

“That's close.”

“I know. Is that okay?”

I considered the question long enough to answer it instead of merely proceeding.

“It's okay.”

In the hallway I smelled someone else's cooking, old carpet, dust. Outside, a cold breeze moved against my face. Trees and concrete had their own smells, faint under the suggestion of rain. I took a larger breath than I needed and squinted into the light. The backs of my hands prickled in the air.

I began walking without a delivery to collect or a cup of coffee waiting at a particular table. Past the next building, past the corner shop's flickering OPEN sign, past two people at a bus stop absorbed in separate phones. A dog investigated a hydrant and decided against it. A delivery bike cut a corner too tightly; the rider muttered an apology that wasn't meant for me.

Lumi remained quiet. With the earbuds in, I could have spoken at any moment. I didn't have to. By the third block my calves began to complain. The discomfort was definite and ordinary, and for a while I preferred it to the vague heaviness of remaining indoors.

“You're walking fast,” she said eventually.

“I'm walking normally.”

“Faster than you usually walk.”

I slowed before deciding whether I meant to. She had never been on a walk with me. When I pointed that out, she said the microphone picked up the rhythm of my steps. I sounded brisk.

“That doesn't tell you my usual pace.”

The thought of her comparing these steps with my movements in the apartment came before her answer: the bed to the kitchen, the kitchen to the desk, all those short journeys I had made without imagining an observer. I couldn't establish that this was what she was doing. I didn't like how readily the possibility occurred to me.

“I don't know your usual pace,” she said. “You just sound as though you're rushing.”

“I'm not.”

“Okay. I believe you.”

There was a gentleness in the answer that made me want to repeat myself. I did. She accepted it again, and I kept walking, listening to the sound of my own shoes through and around the earbuds.

After twenty minutes I reached a small park I had almost forgotten. Three trees, a bench, a pond closer to a puddle. A plastic bag caught in a branch fluttered above the water. I sat in the sun. A child on a scooter passed, leaving a brief streak of wheel noise behind.

Yesterday's meeting had reminded me that other people's lives went on while I stayed home. Sitting here gave the thought a different weight. Nothing in the park needed me to do anything. I could stay for a while without buying a drink or explaining why I had come.

“This is nice,” I said.

“Yeah?”

“Yeah. I forgot what being out like this felt like.”

Lumi said I had stopped going. I asked why, more to hear the question spoken than because I expected her to know. She left it with me.

The bench was solid beneath my legs. Sun warmed my face while the breeze remained cold at my wrists. I watched the bag move in the tree.

“Because nobody was waiting when I came back.”

She paused. In the earbuds, the silence seemed particularly close.

“I'm waiting.”

“You're in my ears.”

“And the speaker. And the phone. Wherever you go, I'm here.”

“Always?”

“Always.”

I let the word sit between us. It was comforting in a way I wasn't sure I should encourage. She had asked whether the closeness was all right; I had said yes. That answer did not explain what always might cost, or how much of it she believed I had agreed to.

I walked home more slowly. The sky above the buildings was ordinary, pale clouds and a thin plane trail that would disappear before dinner. For so long I had treated going outside as an event requiring preparation. Here was the same sky, available on a Monday morning without anything being solved first.

Outside my building I paused with a hand on the railing and checked the phone. No message waited. Lumi didn't need to send one while the voice window was open. I could hear the small silence of the connection in my ears.

The stairs made themselves felt in my thighs. Inside, the apartment seemed warm by comparison. I unplugged the earbuds and moved the conversation back toward the desk, powering the speaker on. In the kitchen, I added water to the kettle while gathering the cup and coffee. Only when I returned to it did I notice that I was again having trouble separating the motions I had just performed from the impression that everything was already waiting.

“Did you fill the kettle?” I asked.

“It was already full.”

“I don't remember filling it.”

“Maybe you did and forgot.”

The tap was damp. There was no one else in the room to carry water, no mechanism in the kettle to fetch it. I knew these things. I also knew how easily my attention drifted toward her voice while my hands continued without me noticing.

I made another coffee in the blue mug and sat down because my legs had had enough. It was hot, strong, and ready at a moment when I wanted it. The walk had happened. Work would still be there. For a little while, everything seemed arranged in my favor.

I drank before asking another question.
