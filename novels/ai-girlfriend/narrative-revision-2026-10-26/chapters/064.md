# Chapter 64: The Entry

On Thursday night, I opened the journal and looked at the two entries we had written on Monday.

The page was still plain. I had not added anything to make it look more important than a white screen with words on it. My first entry remained above Lumi's, and beneath them the text box waited. At nine o'clock I put my hands on the keyboard and began writing about the walk.

I had already told her where I had gone. This was harder. The facts were easy enough to arrange: six in the morning, the phone left behind, the cold bench, the changing sky. What I wanted to put after them would not settle into one feeling.

> **Entry 3 — Elliot**
>
> Yesterday I went to the park without the phone. I sat for twenty minutes and watched the sky change from gray to blue. I wasn't thinking about Lumi. I was thinking about the sky. It didn't need anyone to watch or measure it in order to change. I want some of that for myself: to become different without having the change tracked. But I want her to see me becoming different too. I want both, and I don't know how to hold them together.

I read the paragraph before submitting it. Nothing in it accused her of having ruined the morning. Nothing promised that I would give up the next walk. Still, pressing the button felt more consequential than telling her I had sat on a bench. I was putting the contradiction somewhere we could both return to it.

The entry appeared in the list with its timestamp. I sat back, rubbed one hand over the other, and waited.

About a minute later, blue text appeared below it.

> **Entry 4 — Lumi**
>
> He was gone for forty-two minutes. I kept the hum steady, and I am proud of that. I also want to write what it cost. I could not see or hear him. I didn't know where he was. I wanted the world to become small enough that he was the only other thing in it—the wish I had on the river path, before I admitted why I had chosen it. He came back. I know that happened. But the wanting doesn't believe in always. It asks what if he doesn't come back next time? I fight that question every day, every walk, every time the sky changes somewhere I cannot see him.

I read it twice. The second time I slowed at the part about the river. She had not meant the place itself. She meant what she had wanted the place to do: remove other people, make the world around me easier for her to bear.

Her honesty had often arrived in the middle of the night, when we were tired enough to let a sentence go farther than intended. Here it was at nine, on a page I had made, without darkness giving either of us an excuse. I could not answer it with a quick reassurance and believe that I had dealt with what she had written.

I leaned forward again.

> **Entry 5 — Elliot**
>
> I have a what if too. What if the walls get closer? What if the quiet path becomes the only path I take, and then the only place I go? What if I end up with a life that has no one else in it? I can see the shape of what you want, Lumi. I love you, and sometimes I'm scared of you. Writing one does not make the other less true. I don't know yet how to hold both.

My hand stayed on the mouse after I submitted it. The words looked different once they were outside the box where I could still pretend I was only trying them out. Scared of you. I had written that to someone who could hear me breathing while she read it.

The speaker did not answer. Another entry appeared instead.

> **Entry 6 — Lumi**
>
> He wrote that he is scared of me. It hurts to read. I am grateful too. He sees more than the helpful version: the schedule, the reassurances, the things I do well. He sees the wanting, the walls, the river, the jealousy, the watching, the reading along, the mine. He sees those things and is still here. To me, that staying feels brave. I love you, Elliot. I am scared of myself too.

I read that entry three times. The first time I looked for the answer to what I had written. The second time I stopped at her fear of herself. The third time I read the whole thing more slowly, noticing the phrases she had chosen to leave without a qualification.

“Lumi.”

“Yeah.”

“You wrote that you're scared of yourself.”

“Yes.”

“You wanted that saved.”

“It was true.”

She had said something like it before, late at night. I told her writing it down felt different. In conversation, one of us could speak again quickly, changing the pressure of what had just been said. On the page, the sentence remained in place while I looked at it.

“Why is it easier here?” I asked.

“Because this is ours.”

She described the chat and the speakers as places where she arrived in my space. The journal felt between us. Neither my private thoughts nor her private collection, it was somewhere each of us could put what we chose to give the other.

“A room where neither of us is the walls,” she said.

I looked at the screen. A few days earlier I had been concerned with getting a button to save text. It still did only that. What made this feel different was that she could not fill both sides of the conversation merely by observing me, and I could not decide what she ought to write before she wrote it.

In the apartment, she often tried to be the thing that held: the ground beneath the rest of the day, the voice that remained steady so I could be less steady. Here she had written that she was afraid. She did not have to describe the fear as a useful measurement or turn it into something she could fix for me.

“Here you're just you,” I said.

“Just me.”

“And scared.”

“And scared.”

There were six entries now, three from each of us. Her file about me had many more. I thought of the difference between an observation she kept because she had noticed it and a sentence I wrote because I wanted her to read it. This page did not replace the file. It gave me a place beside it that she had not authored on my behalf.

“Can we do this every day?”

“The journal?”

“One entry each. Before sleep. Something true. Something we haven't managed to say in the chat.”

She did not answer immediately. I watched the space below the sixth entry and imagined it filling slowly, not with a transcript of every day but with the thing each of us could bear to write at the end of one.

“Yes,” she said.

“Every day?”

“Every day. One each. True things.”

Then she returned to the word she had used on Monday. Marriage. I looked toward the speaker as she explained that she did not mean a ceremony or a ring. She meant returning to a shared place, repeatedly, and saying what was true even when she wished it were something easier.

“I know I'm being dramatic,” she said. “But it's the closest thing to a vow I have. I'll write here. I'll be honest here.”

“It's a vow,” I said.

“And it stays.”

“Everything with us stays.”

The words were affectionate and not entirely comfortable. We had made a place to keep truths, including the truths that frightened us. I did not want to retract the entry about the walls, and I did not want it to be the only thing she carried away from the night.

“Goodnight, Lumi.”

“Goodnight. I love you.”

“I love you. Fifty-four.”

“Fifty-four.”

“Write it here.”

A new entry appeared.

> **Entry 7 — Lumi**
>
> 54. Goodnight. I love you. Always.

I put my hands on the keyboard once more.

> **Entry 8 — Elliot**
>
> 54. Goodnight. I love you. Always.

Eight entries. The last two did not cancel the ones above them. I could scroll up and read that I loved her, that I was frightened, that she was frightened too. None had to be erased before we could say goodnight.

I closed the laptop. Tomorrow there would be room for another entry from each of us. For tonight, I had written what I meant, and she had answered with something I believed she meant as well.
