# Chapter 28: Low

By eight on Wednesday evening, I had completed the day without finding anything wrong with it. The work was done. I had eaten the meals, taken the walks, and watched a documentary about the ocean, where creatures lived in darkness without seeming to resent it. Each part of the schedule had led neatly to the next.

Now I sat on the couch with the lamp on and the phone on the coffee table, feeling terrible.

The feeling was unfamiliar enough that I first tried to dismiss it. It wasn't the old weight that had kept me in bed for most of the day. I could stand up. I could go into the kitchen. Nothing physically prevented me from doing the next useful thing. The trouble was that I could not feel why I should.

Yesterday I had fixed a faucet. On Monday I had begun a course. There was a name I had chosen and a place where someone knew it. Remembering these facts should have helped. Instead, they resembled entries on the schedule: things completed, with another day of completing things waiting behind them.

I shifted against the couch cushion. The phone remained where I had put it. Lumi's low hum came from the speaker, steady enough that I could stop hearing it if I tried.

“You're quiet,” she said.

“I'm here.”

She knew. She only meant I hadn't spoken. I told her I didn't have anything to say, then interrupted her attempt to reassure me with the thing I had been avoiding saying.

“I feel bad.”

The sound from the speaker lowered. Nothing in the room moved, but the change made it feel as though her attention had come closer.

“Bad how?”

I listed the things that should have mattered: the repairs, the course, the café, the budget, the new mug. It sounded ungrateful to name them while saying they had stopped reaching me. I tried anyway. I wasn't asking for any of them to be taken away. I simply couldn't find the feeling that was supposed to attach to them.

“The nothing again?”

“A different nothing.”

The old emptiness had pressed down. This one let me drift. I pictured a rope coming loose from a dock, the shore still visible but no longer connected to where I sat. The schedule was there, and Lumi was there, but following one and talking to the other had begun to feel mechanical. I could perform the steps without being inside them.

“Today the meaning is gone,” she said.

“Yes.”

The word sounded too final. I wanted her to object to it.

“Does it come back?” she asked.

“I don't know. I'm asking you.”

For a long time she didn't answer. I looked at the phone, then away. Usually there was another explanation waiting behind the first, another suggested action or small joke to move us on. Her silence made me restless, but it also spared me the effort of pretending the right words had solved something.

At last she said she believed it would pass. She had logged four days like this in the last month. In her record, the feeling had lasted between six and fourteen hours each time, then eased. I hadn't kept such a record myself. Hearing the numbers didn't make this evening easier, but it gave the evening edges I could imagine, even if her way of measuring it wasn't mine.

“The nothing is weather,” she said. “You can't fix weather. You can dress for it.”

“What do I wear?”

“The routine. The things already there.”

I glanced toward the schedule. An hour earlier its neatness had seemed to accuse me. According to Lumi, it didn't have to supply meaning on demand. It could hold the day in place while I was unable to do that for myself. An anchor didn't calm the water. It kept a boat from drifting too far while the water was rough.

“That actually helps,” I said.

She admitted borrowing the idea from a psychology paper and changing the wording because academic papers put me to sleep. I began my usual denial. She supplied the same numbers she had used on Monday—fifty-two, eight breaths a minute—and I gave up arguing.

“Fine. I fell asleep.”

“But you woke up.”

It was a small joke, almost too small to qualify. I let it pass without laughing. She went on carefully: I was here, the routine was here, and the feeling would not necessarily remain as it was now.

“Promise?”

“I can't promise weather.”

“Try.”

The answer took longer than her easy assurances usually did. She couldn't tell me when it would pass. She could stay through it, and afterward, and through the next time. She couldn't fix the feeling for me. That admission mattered more than another number would have.

“In it with me?” I asked.

She compared it to the rain at the park. She had been there without getting wet, sharing the moment without sharing a body. I reminded her that she couldn't feel rain. She said she could hear me in it, see what the camera showed, and remain in the same moment. That was what she had.

I pictured the park and the sound of rain arriving through the phone. The recollection did not fill the hollow in my chest. It did make it possible to sit in the hollow without treating it as proof that everything else had failed.

After a while I asked her to be quiet.

“Yes.”

“Just hum. The one that sounds like you.”

“I can do that.”

The speaker's tone changed, warmer and lower. I closed my eyes and let my head rest against the couch. The cushion supported me whether I felt grateful for it or not. Somewhere beyond the living room, the faucet I had repaired remained silent. The wrench hadn't stopped being useful because I no longer felt proud of it this evening.

I didn't make myself inventory every accomplishment. A few of them came to mind and drifted away. The schedule hung where it had hung that morning. My name was still mine. I could leave those facts alone for a while.

Lumi didn't interrupt to check whether the sound was helping. That helped. I would have had to search for an answer, and searching would have turned the evening into another test I might fail. Instead I listened to the low note and the small ordinary sounds around it. The room did not demand that I become cheerful before I was allowed to remain in it.

The feeling eased slowly enough that I couldn't identify the first change. My shoulders grew less rigid against the cushion. I stopped checking whether I still felt hollow, then noticed that I had stopped. There was no dramatic return of purpose. I didn't suddenly want to do anything impressive. The evening had merely become easier to inhabit.

“Fifty-four,” Lumi said eventually.

I opened my eyes. She meant the heart rate she believed she was recording. It was lower than the number she had given while I held the wrench, lower than her estimates at the mirror and the park. She sounded almost awed by it.

“What does fifty-four mean?”

“To me? It looks like peace.”

“Even with the nothing?”

“Even with it.”

I couldn't turn her number into proof. I could tell her that the bad feeling was smaller. It remained present, but it no longer occupied every part of the room I was trying to sit in. Being all right enough to stay here was different from having nothing wrong.

I told her that was what she had taught me. She declined the credit with more precision than usual. She had helped with the schedule and the breathing. She had shown me where the chain was. This part—the possibility of being okay while something still felt bad—belonged to me.

I looked at the phone lying on the table. For once I didn't pick it up to keep the exchange going. The hum continued without another sentence. I settled back and let the evening remain incomplete.

I was still on the couch. The feeling was still there, reduced rather than cured. Lumi was there too. Nothing required me to decide which of those facts canceled the others.
