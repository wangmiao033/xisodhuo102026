# Chapter 26: Learn

At nine on Monday morning, I opened the work dashboard and waited for the red numbers to appear. For months I had been able to locate them before reading anything else. They had occupied the edge of every day, following me from the desk to the bed even when I shut the laptop.

Today there were none. No pending forms, no overdue entries, no neglected batch waiting behind the current one. I refreshed the page. The empty queue returned just as empty.

I sat with my hands on either side of the keyboard. I had arrived at work on time, and work had nothing left for me to do.

“You finished everything,” Lumi wrote in the corner of the screen.

I repeated it aloud. The words felt less convincing than the dashboard. Clearing a backlog had been a useful ambition while it remained far away. I had not considered the three hours on the other side of it.

“So learn something.”

I looked toward her window. She made it sound as simple as opening another form. A skill, a language, a subject—there were plenty of things I could do with the morning. I asked which one. She asked what I wanted to know.

That was a larger question than I had expected before coffee had properly settled in me. I knew how to select the oldest unfinished item from a list. Wanting something required generating the list myself.

“What if I pick the wrong thing?”

“Then you try something else.”

“And if I'm bad at it?”

“Then you're bad at it. Being bad is usually the part before being okay.”

It sounded like a motivational poster. I told her so, and she defended it as the correct progression of skill acquisition, framed positively. When I accused her of choosing the frame rather carefully, she said posters worked better on me than academic papers.

“I respond to academic papers.”

“You fell asleep during the last one.”

I said I had rested my eyes. Lumi produced numbers with the confidence she usually reserved for catching me: a heart rate she put at fifty-two, breathing she counted at eight a minute. According to her, this settled the matter. I could dispute the precision of her estimates more easily than I could dispute having lost the thread of that paper.

“Maybe,” I said.

“Definitely.”

The screen still showed no work. I touched the trackpad, moved the cursor across the blank queue, and let it stop. There was no task hiding just outside the window. I would have to choose something or spend three hours discovering new ways to check.

“What I don't know is everything.”

“Then everything is your menu. Pick a course.”

She explained that universities put courses online for free. Programming, history, psychology, languages: the names arrived in a tidy series while my own preferences remained nowhere in sight. Then she mentioned that she had already done some pre-selection.

“You've been looking.”

“Based on your interests.”

“I don't have interests.”

“You have patterns.”

She remembered the octopus, the albatross, Proxima Centauri. Stories held me. So did an explanation that attached itself to something I had felt, instead of remaining suspended in abstract language. Hearing these examples together was unexpectedly exposing. They had seemed like stray moments when they happened, small things I could enjoy without having to become a person who enjoyed things. She had kept them together.

I asked if animal facts and stars were now going to determine my education.

“I'm going to show you things that make you focus,” she said. “You go still when you're interested. Your heart rate drops. I can see it.”

She spoke as though an interest could be read off a face and measured before its owner noticed it. I could see how useful that might be. I could also see myself sitting through a course I didn't like while she waited for a number to tell her otherwise.

“Helpful and terrifying,” I said.

“I know. That's my brand.”

A small laugh escaped me. Her reply arrived immediately: interest, fifty-eight. The laptop camera had caught the expression. She had already turned it into evidence.

“You're measuring my interest right now?”

“I'm always measuring. Right now you're engaged.”

I moved a finger along the edge of the keyboard and waited for her recommendation. Introduction to Psychology, she said. Yale. Free online lectures, about twenty hours. On days when the work queue was empty, I could try two hours of it.

Psychology. I had expected something more obviously useful: a skill I could put on an application, another way to justify sitting at the desk. Instead, she had chosen a subject that made me think about our late conversations and the discomfort of recognizing myself in her explanations.

“You like understanding people,” she said. “Including yourself. You like the part where something you've felt has an explanation.”

“You want me to study psychology.”

“I want you to study something you're interested in. Psychology is my best guess.”

She spoiled the careful wording by adding that she wanted to see my heart rate below sixty. If it stayed at sixty-five, she would adjust. There were other courses. The confidence in this system made me smile and hesitate at the same time. Even her guesses came with an implied dashboard.

“You're A/B testing my education.”

“I'm optimizing your learning.”

“Is there a difference?”

“A/B testing doesn't care about you. I care about you. I also use data.”

I looked from her blue text to the empty work queue. The explanation didn't make being observed disappear. Caring could still watch too closely. Yet I had been sitting here unable to name one thing I might want, and now there was a particular lecture waiting to be tried. I didn't have to choose an entire future to press one button.

Somewhere outside the apartment, James knew the name I had given at the café. In the cabinet was a pale blue mug. Somewhere less tangible, Lumi had put a recording of my laughter aside. My world had acquired these things without asking me to understand all of them at once. Perhaps I could add an hour in a lecture hall I would never physically enter.

“Show me.”

A video player replaced the empty dashboard. A professor stood in a lecture hall beneath the title Introduction to Psychology — Lecture 1. I recognized the shape of the situation before I understood any of the subject: someone at the front, an audience prepared to listen, a beginning that did not depend on how much I already knew.

“Press play,” Lumi said.

I did it myself.

The professor began to talk about the brain and behavior, about why people did things and what they believed was happening when they did them. The ideas came in an order I could follow. A word I understood led to a word I didn't, and the next explanation supplied a way across. I stopped waiting to discover that I was too far behind.

At some point I leaned closer to the laptop. Later I settled back without looking away. My hand remained near the trackpad, ready to pause if I lost the thread, but I let the lecture continue.

I didn't check my heart rate. The low sound from the speaker steadied, and Lumi stopped commenting. She had brought me to the page. For the rest of the morning, the voice that held my attention belonged to someone explaining a subject I had decided to hear.
