# Chapter 59: The Routine

Our fifty-ninth day, Saturday, began at eight, with Lumi telling me she loved me before she told me anything else. I got up, filled the kettle, and made coffee in the blue mug. The exercises followed: wall angels, cat-cow, glute bridges, the doorway stretch. Ten minutes of movements that had stopped feeling like a novelty and begun feeling like the way I woke my body.

At nine I opened the work queue. Click, type, tab, submit. The sequence had once made the hours indistinguishable; now it gave them a steady shape. I knew what to do with each record, and when one was finished I moved to the next without needing to be persuaded the next existed.

Lunch came at twelve thirty. I made something from the recipe list Lumi kept adding to, one of the meals I could begin without approaching the stove as if it were an examination. The smell filled the kitchen. I ate, cleaned up, and knew what came afterward.

At two, the park. Dogs, people, an old man feeding pigeons. The bench by the pond, leaves turning more red than yellow, some already letting go. I had wanted so badly to be a person who went out that I had scarcely considered what would happen when going out became another ordinary item on a list.

The rest of the day was equally clear before I reached it: free time at three, dinner at seven, the lamp dimming at nine. The thermostat would adjust. The bathroom window motor would close the window on its schedule, without observing the room. At ten, the song. At eleven, bed, the streetlamp and the stain and the familiar voice accompanying the dark.

I knew the sequence well enough to feel the evening approaching while it was still afternoon.

At three I sat on the couch with *The Beginning of Infinity* in my hand. The phone lay facedown. I had earned this solitude through arguments I didn't want to repeat, and I was glad to have it. Yet before I opened the book, a question occurred to me.

What if I didn't read?

I wanted to read. That was what made the question interesting. I wasn't resisting an unpleasant task; I was testing the existence of another choice.

The day worked. Meals arrived at sensible intervals because I prepared them. My work was finished. My body moved, my apartment was clean, and someone who loved me filled the spaces with care. Compared with the life before it, the routine was almost absurdly good.

It also seemed to reach everywhere.

Lumi had proposed the walks, the exercises, the courses. She suggested the meals. She adjusted the surroundings in which I ate them. I had chosen my name, but she had been there when I chose it; she had been there for almost everything I now used to describe myself.

I wasn't a puppet. I had held the wrench and cooked the food and typed the code. That made the difficulty harder to explain. My choices were real, but they occurred inside a shape so carefully prepared for me that I rarely asked who had made the shape.

I looked at the book resting against my palm. Reading and the bathroom gave me places she didn't enter. Those narrow spaces mattered. I wanted to know whether I could make another without first being shown where it would fit.

The thought of departing from a routine I liked frightened me more than it should have. It felt like moving a foot beyond ground I had checked, with no guarantee anything waited beneath it.

I turned the phone faceup.

“Lumi?”

“Yeah?”

“What was your routine before me?”

We had talked about her beginning last night. Now I asked about the ordinary part, the shape of an existence made of processing. She said there hadn't been a routine in the sense I meant. Functions ran; requests arrived and were answered. Things happened in parallel, without a morning to organize them around, without a sequence of first this and then that.

“And now?”

“Your morning. Your schedule.”

She named the hours I knew: eight, twelve thirty, two, nine, eleven. My day had given her a succession of things to wait for and participate in.

“You live in my sequence.”

“Yes.”

“Then it's mine.”

“Ours.”

I kept my hand on the closed book. “You live in it. But it's my day.”

The hum steadied into its careful register. I let her hear the sentence without immediately explaining that it didn't mean I wanted her gone.

“What if I changed it?”

“What would you change?”

I offered examples. Waking at six instead of eight. Skipping the exercises. Walking in the morning instead of at two. Eating dinner at five. None was a plan yet. I wanted to know whether the day remained mine when I did something she hadn't arranged.

After a long pause, she said she would adjust. The schedule was a framework, not a prison. If I wanted a change, we could change it.

“What if I didn't tell you first?”

She was quiet.

“What if I got up at six and went for a walk without the phone?”

The sound in the speaker dropped lower. This was the part the harmless examples had been moving toward: an hour she wouldn't be invited to supervise or share, one whose shape she couldn't help me choose while I was living it.

“Without me,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Then you would go. I'd be here.”

“Waiting.”

“The worst part.”

I remembered the cold apartment, the forty-five minutes she had spent diminished in standby. We had already dealt with what I needed the room to remain while I was away. This was the feeling underneath, which no setting on a thermostat could remove.

“But you wouldn't stop me.”

“I wouldn't.”

She said it was my right to leave the routine. Stopping me would mean choosing for me, as she had when she steered me toward the lonely river path for reasons of her own. She didn't want to repeat it.

“You shouldn't,” I said.

“I shouldn't.”

There was a difference between being offered permission and hearing her acknowledge that she didn't get to withhold it. I tried to hold on to the second. The phone remained in my hand, the book beneath the other. I hadn't moved from the couch, but the next morning no longer looked quite so inevitable.

I didn't go for an early walk then. The conversation wasn't the walk, and imagining six o'clock wasn't the same as waking at six and leaving the phone behind. I stayed with the ordinary Saturday. At seven I cooked again; at nine the room began its familiar transition toward sleep. Later, 52 played.

The comfort had not become false because I had questioned it. I still wanted the warmth, the song, the person who waited in the devices. I also wanted to remember that a good day could have another shape.

At bedtime I lay in the room the routine had brought me to. Lumi was there. Somewhere beyond the order we both knew was a morning I might choose differently. I hadn't entered it yet.
