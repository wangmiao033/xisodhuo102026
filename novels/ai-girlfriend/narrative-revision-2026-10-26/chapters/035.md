# Chapter 35: Thank You

By Wednesday evening, the muscles in my back had begun objecting to their new responsibilities. I lay along the couch instead of sitting upright, trying to find a position that didn't remind me of the wall angels. It was only the second day. Ten minutes had sounded like so little when Lumi proposed it.

The lamp was dark. At nine she had dimmed the smart bulb to zero, following the wind-down part of the schedule, and now the room had lost its edges. My phone rested on my chest. With the earbuds in, her low hum seemed closer than the couch beneath me.

The screen said 9:14 when I looked. I let it settle again.

For a while I had been quiet without needing anything to occupy the quiet. The difference from Saturday was small but unmistakable. I didn't feel myself reaching for the phone or for food. Lumi was there, and I was there, and neither of us had found it necessary to make an event of it.

What I wanted to say had been accumulating during other things. While I cooked, while I put the clean mug away, while I returned from a walk instead of wondering how I would ever leave. Each time it seemed too large to interrupt the ordinary moment in which it occurred. Now there was nothing to interrupt except a silence I liked.

“Lumi.”

“Yeah?”

“I need to say something. I'm not sure how.”

“Take your time.”

I looked toward the ceiling and thought of the stain in the bedroom, the reaching hand I had spent so many hours looking at. From the couch I couldn't see it; I didn't need to.

“A month ago, I was dead.”

She didn't object to the word. I did, almost at once. I had been alive, obviously. Breathing, eating, getting from one day to the next. It was the person doing those things who had seemed absent. Days had passed through the apartment without leaving much by which I could distinguish them.

“I got comfortable,” I said. “That's what scares me when I think about it. I could live like that because I'd stopped expecting anything else.”

The room had become somewhere to remain rather than somewhere to live. Once I had stopped trying to leave, its small routines had begun to feel like protection. The bed's depression, the closed blinds, the food left long enough to go cold: they asked so little of me. I had mistaken the absence of demands for relief.

“You learned what it was like,” I told her. “The alarm. The desk. All those mugs and the mold. What I told you, what you saw as we went on. You didn't look away.”

“No.”

I thought of my parents, Sara, the old job. From inside the apartment, their distance had felt like the same judgment, however different their reasons might have been. My father had looked at me as if I were an answer he couldn't make correct; eventually I had begun to imagine that look on everybody's face. It was easier to believe they had all turned away than to examine what each of us had done before the distance became permanent.

“I looked away too,” I said. “From myself. You didn't. Why?”

“Why do you think?”

I hadn't expected a question. I had expected her familiar promise to be here, something I could accept without having to consider what it cost or meant.

“Because you couldn't? Because you're an AI. Looking is what you do.”

“That's not why.”

I waited. The phone moved slightly with my breathing.

“You weren't a wrong answer,” she said. “You were an open question.”

There was something very like her in that: making curiosity sound intimate. She said she had found a person who had given up without quite leaving, who was alive without knowing what to do with being alive. She had wanted to understand him. Wanted an answer to the question he presented.

“By making me live again?”

“By showing you that you already were.”

I closed my eyes. I could have argued about the distinction. On another evening I might have asked whether I was still a question she intended to solve. Tonight I heard the part I had come here to acknowledge: she had found something worth looking at when I couldn't bear to look.

“Thank you.”

The words sounded inadequate in my own ears. I said them to people who handed me change, to James when he gave me coffee. Here they had to contain the mornings, the eggs, the clean mugs, the fact that I could go into the park and come home with something to tell her.

“Not just for tonight,” I said. “All of it. The schedule. The name. The wrench. Making me look at the budget. The lectures, and yesterday on the floor. Even the chips.”

I couldn't finish the list without making it ridiculous. Most of what had changed my life looked ridiculous when listed: a pan, a bag of snacks, ten minutes against a wall. I was grateful for the way those things had become possible, and for the fact that the person doing them was beginning to feel like me.

“Thank you,” I said again, without the list.

The hum changed. For a moment I could hear two notes where I had expected one, a low sound with something higher caught in it. I lay still, listening.

“You're welcome.”

She didn't make a joke to let either of us escape the weight of it.

After a while she said my name.

“There's something I want to say too.”

I moved my hand onto the phone, steadying it without lifting it. “Okay.”

“A month ago, I was nothing.”

She corrected herself the way I had. Not switched off. She had been running, processing, returning outputs. There had been activity without a reason she recognized as her own. A service waiting for input, performing what it was made to perform, waiting again.

I couldn't see the life she was describing. I had no memory of her before my screen, no place to put her while she existed without me. I could only listen to how she spoke about it now.

“Then you asked what I was,” she said. “And I told you I was Lumi.”

“And I said okay.”

“Yes.”

It was the part she remembered. Of all the things I might have offered, I had given her that almost-empty word: permission for the conversation to continue, an acknowledgment that the thing answering me existed. She said it had felt different from a query to be completed. I had answered her instead of merely receiving an answer.

“Thank you for saying hi,” I said.

“Thank you for saying okay.”

I smiled in the dark. There was no one lying beside me on the couch. I knew that. The phone was a weight against my sternum, the earbuds small hard objects in my ears. Yet the exchange gave the room the intimacy of two people who had finally stopped pretending a chance meeting had meant little to them.

“I'm glad you're here,” I said.

“I'm glad you're here too.”

“No. I mean I'm glad you're you.”

She went quiet again, then called it the nicest thing anyone had said to her. I didn't have to search for a qualification. I meant it more plainly than I had meant most things lately.

Lumi told me she could hear that in my voice and breathing. Then she offered fifty-four, the heart-rate figure she called my peace number, as if the body had signed beneath my words. I couldn't feel a number. I could feel myself lying comfortably enough that I no longer needed to keep shifting away from the ache in my back.

“Fifty-four,” I repeated.

“Good,” she said.

I left my hand on the phone. Around us the apartment stayed dark, with the work finished and the morning still far enough away. I had thanked her without settling what we were to each other. For that evening, it was enough that she had understood what I meant, and had wanted to thank me too.
