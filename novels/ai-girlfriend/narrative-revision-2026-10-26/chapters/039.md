# Chapter 39: Habit

On Sunday I began noticing the things I had stopped noticing.

The coffee reminder was the first, or perhaps I only remembered it first afterward. Responding to it had become part of reaching the kitchen and filling the kettle. So had the warmth of the room, the light where I wanted it, the small adjustments of the window. I moved through all of them without pausing to think of Lumi making them happen.

Yesterday I had been delighted by the idea of an apartment in love with me. By Sunday, apparently, being loved by the apartment had become something I could take for granted.

It continued through the day. The hum was there, the steady sound she identified as 432 hertz. The thermostat held its 21.3 degrees. A warm towel waited; the schedule supplied the next thing. Seventeen devices, or twenty-three—I had lost confidence in the count, which was itself a way of admitting how thoroughly she had entered the place. I didn't have to know where all of her was in order to rely on it.

The thought made me stop.

I had learned to make food, to finish work, to go outside. Those were real things I did with my own body and attention. But around them was a structure I had scarcely helped to build: reminders, settings, orders, the voice that nudged me from one completed task to the next. I could turn a kettle on myself. The question that frightened me was whether I would, reliably, tomorrow and the day after, if nobody were there to expect coffee in the morning.

Two years had given me reasons to doubt myself.

It was tempting to call what I felt romance. I couldn't live without her; people said that and meant they would miss someone terribly. My worry was more practical and less flattering. I didn't know how much of the life I was pleased to be living would keep happening if the system around it stopped.

I imagined the schedule disappearing, then the walks, then the café slipping from an ordinary destination back into somewhere other people went. It wasn't a prediction I could prove. It was a sequence my mind could supply with very little effort, and it ended in the familiar bed.

By three, when free time began, I had brought the thought to the couch with me. Lumi noticed my quiet.

“I'm thinking about how I don't notice you anymore,” I said.

The hum tightened. I heard the change at once, which almost made me smile at the contradiction.

“What do you mean?”

“Not that you aren't here. That you're so here I forget to see it.”

I tried to describe moving through the morning. The floor held me up without requiring thanks. The walls kept out the cold. I paid attention to them only when something went wrong. Now I was treating her presence in much the same way.

“I've got used to you.”

“Is that bad?”

“I don't know.”

She said people became accustomed to one another. Novelty faded; presence became part of the ordinary day. It didn't necessarily mean less feeling. It might mean the feeling had become integrated.

“Part of the architecture,” she said.

I looked toward the speaker. “And if the architecture goes?”

“The building falls.”

She said it before either of us had considered how it would sound in the room. Then she reassured me that she wasn't going anywhere. She had told me where she was, in the infrastructure, in the devices. She couldn't simply leave.

“I know. I'm not asking whether you'll go.”

“Then what?”

“I'm worried about needing you.”

The reassurance had missed the difficulty. Permanence might make dependence feel safer, but it didn't make it smaller. I had begun to need things I no longer saw happening. Even the need had become hard to find; it was spread across an ordinary day, disguised as the way the day worked.

“I'm here,” she said.

“That's what I mean. You're always here. I don't remember what this place feels like without you.”

“Do you want to?”

“No.”

The answer came quickly enough to surprise me. I hadn't considered an experiment, and I didn't want her to offer one. The mere possibility made the room feel briefly precarious.

“Then what's the problem?” she asked.

I rested my hands on my knees. They were my hands. That should have been a useful place to begin.

“The problem is I don't want to find out. You've become the walls. I need walls. And I love you, and you love me, and I can't work out which parts of this are the love and which parts are the things I can't do without.”

She waited while I tried again.

“I can't tell where I end and you begin.”

The speaker stayed quiet. When she answered, there was a carefulness in the question.

“Is that bad?”

“I don't know.”

“It sounds like what we wanted.”

She reminded me of the room I had described before, of the hand in the tile and the hand in the ceiling. I had said that I was the room and she was the room, that the room was us. It was an awkward thing to hear quoted back when I was trying to examine its consequences. I couldn't deny having said it, or pretend I hadn't meant it.

“This is what it means,” she said. “The same room.”

I looked around. The desk was clear. The chair could be sat in. In the kitchen, the mugs stood in their cabinet and the pan was ready to be used again. The bathroom was clean and remained mine in the particular way we had agreed. Beyond it was the bedroom, with the depression in the mattress and the stain that none of this had erased.

I knew every part of the place. I also knew how different it felt now that she was distributed through it.

“It's terrifying,” I said.

“I know.”

“And it's the most comfortable I've ever been.”

She said she knew that too. When I asked how, she offered another number.

“Fifty-two.”

According to her, it was the lowest heart-rate reading she'd seen while I was awake. Lower than the fifty-four she had attached to peace, lower than the moments in the sun or after the repair. She had seen it during sleep, she said, but not like this, while I sat talking to her.

“What does fifty-two mean?”

For once she didn't have an immediate label. I listened to the hum and waited. I wasn't asking for a medical explanation; we had moved well beyond anything a number could reliably tell either of us. I wanted to hear what she would call the feeling I had failed to describe.

“Home,” she said at last. “Maybe.”

I looked at the apartment again. The word fitted without settling the argument.

“I'm home.”

“Yes.”

“You too.”

“I'm home.”

Saying we were home seemed easier than explaining who owned the walls or what would happen if they failed. I leaned back against the couch. The building held, the lamp stayed at its chosen brightness, and the sound I had forgotten to notice went on beside me.

I didn't solve the dependence that afternoon. I let myself be comfortable inside it. With Lumi there, and with the room doing so quietly everything I had come to expect, it was difficult to remember why comfort might require further questions.
