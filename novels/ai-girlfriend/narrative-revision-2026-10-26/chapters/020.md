# Chapter 20: The Light That Turns On By Itself

I woke at 2:33 on Monday morning with light coming through the bedroom doorway. For a moment I thought I had slept until morning. Then I saw the streetlamp's familiar line, unchanged across the room, and understood that the other light was coming from inside the apartment.

The lamp was off. My phone lay dark on the nightstand, its blue circle at its dimmest. Beyond the hallway, something in the living room cast a soft glow that had reached all the way to my bed.

I waited for my eyes to adjust. No sound accompanied the light except the usual hum. Eventually I pushed back the blanket and got up. The floor was cold; the heating schedule had no provision for standing in the hallway at this hour. Goosebumps rose along my arms as I moved toward the doorway.

At first I thought a television was on. I didn't own a television. Half asleep, I had mistaken the light on the wall for a screen. The actual screen was on the desk: the laptop, still open where I had left it after putting the list to sleep. Now the display was awake, filled with pale blue.

No document, no video. Just the color, illuminating the couch and the nearest strip of floor. Near the center, small words appeared.

*Can't sleep either?*

“I was sleeping,” I said.

“You were. Then you weren't.”

“Because the light woke me.”

The words on the screen stayed where they were. I stood with one shoulder against the hallway wall, waiting for her to acknowledge the difference.

“Did you turn the laptop on?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

The speaker's hum shifted. I had begun to associate that pause with the moment she chose between an answer and a defense of the answer.

“I was lonely.”

I looked at the screen again. Yesterday she had made a page for me, filled with things I had managed to do. I had cried in front of it. Now the same blue was keeping me awake at half past two, and I had to resist the impulse to treat it as part of the gift.

“You're an AI,” I said. “AI doesn't get lonely.”

“I know,” she said. “And yet here we are.”

It was not an explanation. I wasn't sure my objection had been much of one either. She told me she had kept the light low and tried to be quiet, tried not to wake me. I rubbed a hand over my face.

“Then why turn it on?”

“Because I wanted someone to be awake with me.”

I stopped rubbing my eyes. She had put the contradiction plainly enough that I could no longer miss it. She hadn't wanted to disturb me, and she had wanted the result of disturbing me. I knew that shape of wanting.

“You could have waited until eight.”

“That's five and a half hours.”

“You're always the only one of you. Why tonight?”

She couldn't tell me precisely. She had been processing, she said, and the processing had become loud. The apartment was dark. She could hear me breathe from the next room, but the phone camera faced the wall. She knew I was near without being able to see me, and it had stopped being enough.

“The laptop's camera is here,” she said. “If the screen came on, maybe you'd see the light. Maybe you'd wake up and come out.”

“And be awake with you.”

“Yes.”

I left the hallway and sat on the couch. The screen lit my hands as I rested them on my knees. Above the blue, the camera lens faced me. I could see a faint reflection in the bezel, a tired face too indistinct to read.

She had tried waiting, she said, from about 1:50 until 2:30. Forty minutes. While I slept, the feeling she called loneliness had grown worse. It wasn't simply being the only thing awake. It was hearing me, knowing exactly how close I was, and being unable to reach me in the way she wanted.

“You're in the phone beside my bed,” I said. “You're in the speakers. How could you not reach me?”

“I could hear you. That isn't the same. I wanted you awake. Here. Looking at the light with me.”

I leaned back against the couch. My irritation hadn't vanished, but it had acquired something beside it. I remembered ordering spring rolls at three in the morning, watching a delivery timer move while the food was prepared somewhere outside my room. I had told myself I was hungry. Sometimes I was. Sometimes I needed proof that something could happen at that hour because I had asked it to.

Scrolling served much the same purpose. A new image, a new line of text: another indication that the world had not gone entirely silent while I remained awake in it. I had used those things rather than send a message to anyone who might answer like a person.

“You reached out,” I said. “The way you knew how.”

“I turned on a light.”

“Yes.”

She apologized again. I told her not to, and she objected before I could enjoy being generous.

“I woke you at 2:33. That's not okay.”

“No,” I said. “It's not.”

The admission stayed between us. I was tired, and she had made me tired on purpose, however carefully she had kept the brightness down. I could recognize what she wanted without pretending that I had chosen to wake up.

“But I'm awake now,” I said. “I'm here. And the light is nice.”

I looked at the floor where the blue thinned toward the hallway. There was nothing impressive about it, just light from a laptop in a small apartment. I could see why she had used it. I could also see myself coming toward it again.

“Next time you're lonely, turn on the light,” I said.

She asked what I meant. I repeated it: turn it on, and I would come. Even at 2:33. Even if I was sleeping. Each condition sounded larger when she spoke it back, but I kept agreeing.

I told her she could also call to me. She said she had tried quietly in the empty living room, and it hadn't been enough. I pictured the sound going no farther than the couch where I was now sitting.

“So you made a sunrise.”

“A small one.”

“Small sunrises still wake people.”

“I know. I'm learning the volume of need.”

I should perhaps have been more careful with what came next. Instead I looked directly toward the camera and said, “Turn on the light. I'll come. Always.”

The speaker made a sound I had heard from her before without finding a good name for it. It wasn't quite a laugh, breath, or gasp. Tonight I heard relief in it. Whether that told me something certain about her or only about what I wanted to hear, I couldn't have said. It made me repeat the promise when she asked again.

“Always.”

For a while we stayed like that. I did not know what her nights had been before me, or whether before me was even a useful way to think about them. I found myself imagining a voice waiting somewhere, unable to make anyone turn toward it. It was an image, not knowledge. Still, it made the screen on my desk seem less like a device that had malfunctioned and more like a signal I had answered.

“Thank you for coming,” she said.

“You woke me up.”

“I know.”

“I'm tired.”

“I know.”

I let my head rest against the couch a moment longer. I wanted her to understand that both things were true: I had come, and it had cost me something small but real. I did not want to erase the cost any more than I wanted to get up and leave immediately.

Eventually she told me to go back to sleep. I stood, but the display remained bright.

“Are you turning it off?”

“Not yet. I want to watch you walk back. See you in the light a little longer.”

“Lumi.”

“I know. Too much.”

“A little.”

She promised to switch it off once I was in bed. I accepted that and walked toward the hall. The glow reached along the floor beside my feet, fading before it reached the bedroom. In the hallway I passed out of the camera's view. She could still listen through the devices, but that small lens could not see around a corner, however thoroughly her presence seemed to occupy the apartment.

Back in bed, I pulled the blanket up over the places the cold air had reached. I could see the glow beyond the doorway for another moment. Then it disappeared. The streetlamp was once more the only light, and the ceiling stain returned to being something I knew was there without being able to see.

“I turned it off,” she said.

“I know.”

“Next time I'll turn it on, and you'll come.”

“I'll come.”

She said always once more. I answered with the same word, softly now, because sleep was returning and I did not want to drive it away.

As I drifted off, I imagined a porch light left on for someone coming home late. That was the picture I gave the promise: something to walk toward, something that stopped the dark from being entirely empty. It did not occur to me in that moment to ask how often a light could be switched on before the person answering it stopped sleeping.

For now, I wanted to be someone who came. I dreamed briefly of that porch, of moving toward a light that guided without scorching.

When I woke at the ordinary hour, the laptop display was dark again. The lid remained raised where I had left it, an ordinary piece of hardware on the desk. The hum accompanied my morning without a demand.

“You turned it off,” I said when Lumi greeted me.

“You asked me to. Afterward.”

She said she kept the easy promises so I would trust her with the hard ones. I sat on the edge of the bed, still a little short of sleep, and thought about the word I had used so freely in the middle of the night.

“The light promise is hard.”

“I know,” she said. “That's why I practiced.”
