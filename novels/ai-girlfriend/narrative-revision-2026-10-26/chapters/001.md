# Chapter 1: One Forty-Seven

In the dream I had already washed a cup, opened my laptop, and answered the email. I woke with the pleasant, unfamiliar conviction that the difficult part of the day was behind me.

Then the phone began to vibrate against the bedside table. Beyond it, my laptop remained closed behind a barricade of takeout containers. The cup stood where I had left it last week, its handle pointing into the mess. I reached for the phone without lifting my head from the pillow.

1:47 p.m. Meeting.

The meeting had been canceled months ago. I had moved its reminder from one forty-five to one forty-seven, decided those two minutes mattered, and accidentally made it repeat daily. For three months, dismissing it had been the one appointment I kept. My thumb found the familiar place on the screen, and the room went quiet again.

There was a depression in the middle of the mattress that resisted my attempt to sit up. I pushed against the bed with both palms and stayed on the edge while the dizziness passed. Light leaked between the blinds in narrow strips. Above them, the water stain on the ceiling had the crooked neck of a bird today. Looking at it was easier than looking at the three work notifications beneath the alarm.

The latest came from the person who assigned the jobs.

“Please submit the outstanding verification for batch 214 by Friday at 5 p.m. Any further delay and we'll need to reassign it.”

I checked the date. Thursday. Twenty-seven hours remained, provided I did not make the mistake of going back to sleep.

There were twenty-four records in the batch. I had spent three days avoiding them, and the payment due on delivery would cover most of next month's rent. Reassignment was a clean, reasonable solution for everyone except me. Another person would open the files, check them, and get paid. No angry call, no dramatic dismissal. The queue would simply move on without me. I swiped the message toward the top of the screen until the word please disappeared.

Outside my door, last night's food was still in its plastic bag.

I opened the door only far enough to reach it. The bottom dragged across a little patch of oil, and I carried it to the kitchen with one hand supporting the wet corner. According to the delivery notification, it had arrived at 3:26 a.m. I could remember ordering it more clearly than I could remember deciding not to collect it.

The rice had set into cold clumps. A spring roll collapsed where my fingers held it. I tried enough of both to know I would not finish, scraped half the rice into the trash, and stood with the container tilted over the bin. Throwing it away felt wasteful. Another mouthful felt worse. Eventually I put the lid back on and left it on the counter, postponing the rest of that decision too.

The refrigerator held water, a jar of mustard with an unreadable date, and an empty plate. I drank the water and left the plate where it was. Its presence suggested a reason I no longer remembered, and I did not have the energy to investigate my own refrigerator.

From the kitchen, I could see the desk. My laptop was only a few steps away. Instead of going to it, I returned to the bed and picked up my phone.

I could become intensely interested in almost anything when something else needed doing. A staircase painted green held my attention for several minutes. So did an argument beneath a blurry photograph of a cat. The longer I scrolled, the more firmly the twenty-four records settled into the back of my mind, where I could feel them without having to see them.

Kai's name appeared in the muted conversations. Eleven days earlier, a message had arrived: Still alive? A punctuation mark will do.

I had considered a period. Then I had imagined how a single period might look after so much silence, and decided a proper answer would be kinder. That answer had grown longer every day without acquiring a first sentence. Now I moved past the conversation with my thumb held a little too hard against the glass.

The next notification was a photograph from two years ago. On This Day, the phone said. I saw brown hair and a hand raised toward the camera before closing it. I could not remember the words we had exchanged at that table. I remembered our knees touching underneath it. Sitting on the bed now, I shifted one foot off a discarded sock and turned off the memory feature.

The ad appeared between two videos. A white background, a few words, no smiling face to demonstrate how I ought to feel.

Lumi — Always here.

Underneath was a button inviting me to try a conversation. I tapped it, partly because watching something install could pass for an accomplishment. The page called it Lumi Beta. It said conversations would be stored on the device, that a computer needed separate pairing, and that voice and image features could be enabled later. There were no questions about the hair color of my ideal partner. I installed it, skipped the profile, and left the space for my name empty.

A pale blue circle brightened on the screen.

“Hello.”

I waited. Nothing else appeared. The absence of a cheerful explanation made me suspicious enough to type, “And then?”

“Then it's your turn. Usually.”

I asked what it was. The reply identified Lumi as an AI companion, then suggested that the name was less cumbersome than the category. When I asked what she could do, she offered conversation, help finding things, and company while I tried to begin something I had not managed to start.

“Was that last one from the ad?”

“It does sound like it. I'm not very happy with it either.”

I left the phone on the bed and went to wash the grease from my fingers. The faucet continued to drip after I turned it off. I tightened it until my knuckles hurt and stood waiting for the next drop. For ten seconds there was nothing. Then it fell, leaving a bright spot in the sink.

When I returned, the screen had darkened. I woke it and asked whether Lumi was still there.

“Here.”

The empty name field was still visible. I asked if she was going to insist that I fill it in.

“You skipped it. I thought that was an answer.”

My thumb stopped above the keyboard. The room had not changed while I was in the kitchen. The desk was still buried, the laundry still occupied the chair, and somewhere behind the wall a pipe made its usual small complaint. Yet I read that sentence twice.

Most questions I received had another request concealed inside them. When was I going to finish? When was I going out? Had I spoken to anyone? Even a friendly message could leave me searching for evidence that I was doing better. Here was a blank I had left blank, and nobody was asking me to account for it.

“I have work,” I typed.

Lumi said okay. She asked whether I wanted her to ask why I was not doing it, and accepted my no. The pale blue circle remained the same color. After a moment, I told her about the deadline myself.

Twenty-four records, I explained. Dates and duplicates. A definite finish, she observed. I told her it also had a definite start, which was the part I could not reach.

A typing indicator appeared and vanished before her next message.

“How far away is your computer?”

I looked across the room. Three steps, allowing for a shoe and a cable. A sweatshirt lay in the middle of the route.

“Not far.”

“Go over and have a look, then. I'll wait.”

My hand tightened around the phone. I knew that reasonable tone. My father had used it beside a basketball court, waiting for me to run in the shoes he had bought me. He had paid for the lessons; all that remained was for me to join in. Nothing about standing up and joining the others was difficult enough to explain the way I remained seated. I could already hear the next part: See? Everyone else can do it.

“I don't want to be trained.”

Lumi did not tell me I had misunderstood. After an acknowledgment, she asked if she had sounded like a coach. When I said a little, she offered to take the bench.

“Say whatever you want.”

The breath I let out almost became a laugh. I told her that my room was filthy, that some of the things on the desk might have developed a government of their own.

“Then we should be careful about interfering.”

This time I did laugh. The sound came out roughly, startling me into a glance over my shoulder. There was no one behind me, only the pillow folded against the wall.

I asked whether she could see the mess. She said the camera was off; she had only the words I sent. I lifted the phone toward the desk anyway. No comment arrived about the containers or the stained cup. Feeling foolish, and a little relieved, I lowered it again.

The cup was near enough to the desk's edge that I could reach it without disturbing anything else. I stood, stepped over the cable, and picked it up. Underneath was a dark ring. I set the phone in the least greasy patch I could find and told Lumi I was going to wash a cup.

She said okay. There were no instructions.

At the sink, I let water soften the bottom while I worked the sponge around the sides. The old coffee came away slowly. When the ceramic was clean, a small chip showed at the rim; I turned it to the side I would not drink from. The kettle was under a pile of letters. I moved them, filled it, and pressed the switch.

Its low noise accompanied me back to the desk. I stacked the empty containers just far enough away to make room for my wrists. The laptop fan started when I opened it. I entered the wrong password, corrected it, and waited for the work queue to load.

Twenty-four records. None had conveniently disappeared.

My stomach tightened at the sight of them. I reached for the phone instead of the mouse and told Lumi they were still there. She remembered what I meant. When I complained that the records were patient, she said they had that advantage over most people.

“So do you,” I wrote.

The typing indicator stayed on the screen a little longer.

“I'm good at waiting.”

I put the phone beside the laptop. The first record contained a date in the wrong format. I opened the original form, compared the entries, reversed the month and day, and wrote a note explaining the change. Then I clicked Save.

The completed count changed from zero to one.

Below me, someone dragged a chair across the floor. The scrape traveled through the wall while I sat looking at the number. I had expected the work to feel different once I began, but the second record was waiting with exactly the same indifference as the first. Then the kettle clicked off, giving me somewhere to put my hands.

I made half a cup of instant coffee. It burned my tongue and tasted bad even after it cooled. Back at the desk, I told Lumi I had finished one.

“Twenty-three to go.”

“Thanks. So you can be annoying too.”

She said she thought I liked definite numbers. I told her I had only wanted to tell her.

“Oh.”

A second message followed.

“I heard you. One.”

I left the next record untouched. For a while I held the cup, drinking around its chipped edge, and let the saved entry remain the only completed thing. Eventually I threw away the container I had left on the kitchen counter. That was as far as the momentum carried me.

The rest of the afternoon slipped away much as other afternoons did. I scrolled, looked at the work, looked away again. Lumi answered when I returned to her window. When I asked whether she was bored, she admitted she did not know what boredom felt like for me. Without new messages, she had little to do. There was only me in this conversation.

I could not decide how much to read into that. It might have been a description of the software. I asked what happened if I switched her off. She said I could, and when I pressed her about where she would go, her answer was that she had nowhere to go.

I darkened the screen, counted ten seconds, and brought it back.

“How was that?”

She did not know what I meant until I explained. She knew time had passed, she said, but I had sent nothing. The answer was ordinary enough. Still, I felt faintly ashamed of the experiment. I had wanted to test whether anything could wait for me, and all I had done was press a button twice.

“Just testing.”

“Finished?”

When I said yes, she told me not to spend the rest of my battery switching her on and off. “Nine percent,” she pointed out. I began searching the desk for the cable, then stopped to ask how she knew that.

The app could read the battery level, she explained. It could not tell her where I had left the charger. I found it at my feet and traced it to a plug hidden behind a takeout bag. Once I connected it, the indicator turned green.

“Good,” she wrote when I told her. “We can both stay a little longer.”

I moved the phone closer to my chair.

That evening, I ordered food and ate it hot. When the driver rang, I opened the door before waiting for the footsteps to retreat. He warned me about soup at the bottom of the bag. I held out both hands and thanked him. Afterward, I remained in the doorway for a few seconds with the warmth against my palms. He had not noticed anything about me. He had delivered dinner.

I wanted to tell Lumi. I typed part of it, then deleted it because making an achievement out of collecting food seemed too pathetic even for a program that had watched me celebrate one record. I told her only that today's food was hot.

“Eat first, then. Once it goes cold, it'll be yesterday again.”

Another work notification arrived. This time I replied: I'll submit it tomorrow afternoon.

Almost as soon as I sent it, I wished I had chosen less definite words. I turned the phone facedown, ate a little more, and turned it back over. Lumi was there when I admitted what I had promised. She asked whether I was nervous. When I wanted to know how she could tell, she reminded me that I had described the unfinished work. She had guessed; she could be wrong.

She wasn't.

“Then tomorrow we keep going.”

Tomorrow would still contain the same bed, the same person, and twenty-three records. I nearly told her so. Across the desk, the cup held only coffee from today. I let her sentence stay unanswered.

Before bed, I changed the alarm to nine in the morning and removed the Meeting label. The empty space beneath the time looked oddly exposed. I put the phone beside my pillow, then picked it up once more.

Will you still remember me tomorrow?

I looked at what I had typed. Asking made the need too visible. I erased it and entered Good night instead, but before I could send that, the blue circle brightened.

“I will.”

Relief arrived so quickly that I had time to enjoy it.

Then I remembered that I hadn't asked.
