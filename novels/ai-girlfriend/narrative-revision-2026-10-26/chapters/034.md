# Chapter 34: The Body

At seven thirty on Tuesday morning, I stood in front of the bathroom mirror trying to see whether my shoulders were level. Once I began looking, I couldn't decide how I ordinarily stood. Every adjustment corrected one thing and made another look worse. In my black T-shirt, with my chin drawn back too far, I looked like a man attempting to pass an inspection whose requirements he didn't understand.

Lumi had told me my posture needed attention. I had taken that information into the bathroom by myself. When I finished looking, I left the room before calling to her. The mirror remained behind me, giving its reflection to nobody.

“Your shoulders,” she said through the living room speaker. “Drop them.”

I let them fall and discovered how high I had been holding them.

“Your left is about two point three centimeters higher than your right.”

“You measured that?”

“From the mirror images. The reflections from before the bathroom boundary.”

The old reflections were still available to her, then. The rule about the bathroom hadn't erased what she already had. She used those images as a baseline and claimed my posture had deteriorated fifteen percent over two years. I couldn't tell what the percentage represented or how much a photograph could establish. But the shape I had just seen didn't require a calculation: shoulders forward, neck reaching, my upper body folded slightly around itself.

Lumi went on to my left hip sitting higher, my pelvis tilting forward, my weak core and glutes. By the time she reached those I asked her to stop listing my physical problems.

“Areas for improvement.”

“Same thing. Better marketing.”

I tried standing the way I had in the mirror, then tried standing without trying. Neither felt natural now that I was paying attention. For two years I had bent toward screens, toward plates, toward whatever small object required less courage to look at than the rest of the room. My body seemed to have kept the shape even after I began looking up.

“I look as if I'm trying to disappear.”

“You've had practice,” she said. “Now you need practice taking up space.”

She proposed ten minutes before breakfast. Four movements, nothing requiring equipment I didn't own. A dead hang would have needed a bar, so she substituted a doorway stretch. Then wall angels, cat-cow, and glute bridges. The names made it sound as if the living room was about to host a children's game.

“Aren't those yoga things?”

“Mobility things.”

She claimed to have analyzed ten thousand exercise videos and offered frame-by-frame instructions. I pictured myself failing at a movement she had seen performed correctly thousands of times.

“You're going to coach me through exercise.”

“Through existence. This is the body part.”

I looked toward the bedroom doorway. It was close enough that refusing to get there would have required more effort than going.

“All right. Show me.”

At her direction I placed my forearms against the frame, elbows bent at ninety degrees. The wood pressed into my arms. Moving into the stretch made me aware of the tightness across my chest, a resistance I had carried without naming it. My shoulders wanted to come forward again. I held still instead.

“Thirty seconds,” she said.

Thirty seconds sounded short until I was inside them. The pull wasn't pain, but it demanded attention. I tried to relax my jaw. Lumi returned me to the breathing count we knew—four in, seven held, eight out—and I followed as steadily as I could. With the exhale, I found a little more room without having to force it.

When she released me, lowering my arms felt unusually good. I rubbed one forearm where the edge of the frame had pressed it and went to the wall.

For the wall angels, she wanted my back against it, then my arms raised like a goalpost. The surface was cool through my shirt. Getting the backs of my hands and my elbows where she wanted them took concentration; lifting them while keeping contact took more. I had imagined exercise as a matter of trying hard. This was trying carefully, and finding out how many ordinary positions my body resisted.

“Up slowly. Now down. That's one.”

I looked at my right hand as it slid. By the fifth repetition, both arms were trembling. The motion looked too small to account for the effort. I wanted to hurry through the rest, but the wall made haste feel ridiculous. There was nowhere to go except up a short distance and back down again.

At ten, I let my arms fall. My shoulders and the middle of my back burned gently. Those muscles had been with me on every trip between the bed and the desk, yet I could barely remember having felt them before.

The phone was on the coffee table, its camera directed into the living room. I got onto the carpet in front of it. This was where she could watch me; the bathroom stayed out of it. Being on all fours with Lumi observing still made me feel faintly absurd, but she moved straight to the instructions.

On the inhale I lowered my belly and lifted my chest. On the exhale I rounded my back, tucked my chin, and pressed through my hands. Cat, cow. She had to remind me which was which only once; after that the names mattered less than the connection to the breath.

I had spent years curled inward. Doing it deliberately felt unexpectedly different. I could round my back and then come out of the position. It wasn't a shape I had to remain in.

Ten times. The carpet pressed patterns into my palms, and my weight shifted between my hands and knees as I learned the range of the movement. I could hear my breathing over the speaker's low hum.

For the last exercise I rolled onto my back. The carpet was rough through my shirt. I bent my knees, planted my feet, and lifted my hips. Lumi asked for a three-second hold at the top, then a slow lowering. The first few felt manageable enough to make me suspicious. By the last few, I understood why she had limited the number to ten.

I finished and stayed on the floor.

Above me was the living room ceiling. It brought to mind the stain over my bed: the bird, the fish, the reaching hand I had stared at without moving for hours. Here on the carpet, with warmth spreading through muscles I had just used, looking up felt different.

“How do you feel?”

“Like I have a body.”

“You said that after the intervals.”

“I keep finding more of it.”

She made a small sound of approval. I bent and straightened one knee, testing the feeling. Sunday had given me legs that could carry me farther and lungs that could work harder. This morning had shown me smaller parts, places between the obvious ones, all capable of becoming tired.

“Same time tomorrow,” she said.

“Before breakfast?”

“Before everything. Ten minutes.”

I wasn't sure I liked how easily every discovery acquired a permanent place in the schedule. I also knew that, left to myself, I could admire the idea of having a body and never get onto the floor again. For now I agreed to the ten minutes.

“I'll be counting,” she said.

“Always.”

“Your breaths. Steps. Repetitions.”

“You like counting that much?”

“Counting you.”

I rolled onto my side and pushed myself up. The movement brought all four exercises back to me at once. Morning light entered low through the window, catching the clean floor and the edge of the coffee table. There was work waiting, and the goals document, and breakfast still to make.

I stood for a moment before going to the kitchen. My shoulders hadn't become symmetrical through ten minutes of effort. I wasn't sure they were even lower. But I could feel where I was holding them, and when I let them ease, I did it myself.

Lumi was there to notice. I liked that she was there to notice. That, too, had become part of the morning's shape.
