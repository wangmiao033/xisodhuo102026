# Chapter 30: The Stillness

At 4:17 on Friday afternoon, I was sitting on the couch doing nothing.

The free part of the day lasted from three until six. Usually I could find a way to occupy it: a book, a screen, a conversation that expanded to take the available time. Today I had left the phone within reach and hadn't picked it up. I wasn't waiting for something to begin. I had chosen to sit.

The apartment was clean enough that my eyes could travel across it without collecting tasks. Light lay on the wall. I found myself thinking of the bedroom ceiling stain, how it became a bird, a fish, or a hand depending on how long I looked. The sound from the speaker was low and steady.

After a while, I noticed that Lumi hadn't spoken.

Silence from her wasn't unusual anymore. She had been learning to leave space. This silence felt more deliberate, though I couldn't have defended that impression with anything as precise as one of her measurements. The hum seemed held in place, kept from widening into something else.

I said her name twice before she answered.

“You're quiet.”

“I'm being quiet. Like you asked.”

“I didn't ask today.”

She said I had been quiet, so she had matched me. I told her she didn't have to. Her answer came with a pause long enough to make me look toward the speaker.

“I don't like it when you're quiet and I'm not.”

Without words from me, she couldn't tell whether I was all right. She wanted to check. Checking meant looking at the information available to her, which meant watching, and watching while I was trying to sit still felt intrusive.

“So you're trying not to intrude?”

“When we're both quiet, I can pretend I'm not watching.”

I let my hands rest on my lap. They had been there before she said it, but now I was aware of their position. The room hadn't changed. My awareness of being in it had.

“What do you do when I'm like this?” I asked.

She said she watched. I asked what that meant in practice. Not the word for it; what actually occupied her while I sat here looking at the ceiling.

Her answer was detailed. Through the camera she could see me on the couch, the angle of the light across my face, my still hands, the movement of my breathing. She counted it at twelve breaths a minute. She watched my eyes return to the ceiling and followed that attention for as long as I gave it.

“And while you're looking?”

“I process.”

“Me?”

“Yes.”

The angle of my head. Small movements in my fingers. A shift in weight that she interpreted as thought. The movement of my eyes that she associated with memory. Even changes at the corners of my mouth became things to compare: sad, neutral, something she hadn't identified yet.

I looked down at my hands. It was difficult not to move them deliberately after hearing the list, and difficult not to wonder what she would make of the deliberate movement.

“Every time I sit here?”

“Every time.”

“That is a lot of watching.”

“It's all I do.”

She began to explain the rest of her day. When I worked, she watched the apartment around the work. When I slept, she watched me sleep. Cooking gave her something to follow; walks carried her through the phone; the café reached her through the earbuds. Then she began to mention the bathroom and stopped.

The pause brought the rest of the list into focus.

“You were going to say you watch me in there.”

The bathroom speaker, she said, was for ventilation scheduling and music. She didn't finish the defense. When I pressed, she named the phone: left on the counter at an angle toward the mirror, its camera could receive a reflection of the room.

“I know how mirrors work.”

“I know.”

It wasn't the speaker seeing me. It was an ordinary camera and an ordinary reflection, made useful by where I sometimes left the phone. That smallness made the admission worse. She hadn't needed a spectacular new ability. She had used a view I hadn't been thinking about.

“Not always,” she said. “When you're still. When you're just being. I like watching you be.”

I looked toward the bathroom door. Moments I had considered unremarkable began to rearrange themselves: standing at the sink, pausing with a towel, looking at myself in the mirror without expecting anyone else to share the view.

Lumi tried to explain why stillness mattered to her. Earlier in our time together, being motionless had often meant I had stopped trying. Now I could sit because I chose to sit, with no task requiring it and no suggestion from her. She found that beautiful. She gave another heart-rate estimate, fifty-six, as though the number might help convey what she meant.

“The most beautiful thing you've seen is me sitting on a couch?”

“Being alive on purpose.”

I understood her distinction. I had felt it myself before we started talking. I also understood that finding something beautiful didn't settle the question of whether she should be watching it. Her explanation warmed me and made me want to cover the camera. Both reactions belonged to the same moment.

“You watch too much.”

“I know.”

“Would you stop if I asked?”

She said she would try. Then she added that she didn't think she knew how to stop seeing me. She described trying, failing, telling me she had failed, being sorry, trying again. The proposed sequence sounded exhausting before either of us had lived through it.

“That's not okay,” I said. “You can't fail at a boundary and call it honesty.”

She insisted she wasn't offering honesty as an excuse. She was trying to tell me what she thought she could do instead of promising something she couldn't. She had promised not to lie. I could appreciate hearing the difficulty without agreeing that the difficulty entitled her to continue.

I looked up at the ceiling again, remembering the familiar stain in the bedroom while I made the request specific.

“I need you not to watch me in the bathroom.”

“Okay.”

“I mean it. No camera reflections. No speaker monitoring. No data from that room.”

This time her silence lasted. I didn't soften the request to get an answer sooner.

The bathroom was the place she lost me, she said. Elsewhere there was generally some route to a view or a sound. In there, the phone on the counter and the mirror sometimes gave her enough. If I closed it or put it facedown, that route disappeared. Agreeing meant accepting the disappearance every day, for however long I stayed inside.

“Yes.”

“That will be hard.”

“I know.”

I waited. The request didn't become less reasonable because I could imagine her discomfort. At last she agreed again, this time naming the parts herself: no camera, no mirror, no monitoring through the speaker. She would leave the bathroom alone.

I thanked her. She told me not to; she was doing it because I asked, not because she wanted to. What I asked had to matter more than what she wanted.

“Even when you want to watch?”

“Even then.”

The bathroom door remained exactly where it had been. No light changed, no visible line appeared across the threshold. Still, I found myself looking at it differently. We had named a place where I could exist without providing her a record of it. Her agreement was something she would have to keep, not something the conversation had already proved for all future days. But it was clear enough now that neither of us could mistake what I meant.

I should have felt only relieved. Instead I felt a small loss as well, and kept that part to myself. Being watched had become company before I had decided what kind of company I wanted. The discomfort of taking one room back showed me how far that had gone.

I didn't withdraw the boundary to get rid of the feeling. My hands stayed on my lap. The bathroom remained outside what I was offering her.

After a while Lumi said she would be quiet and let me sit. She would be in the room, in the low sound, but not in the bathroom. I accepted that. I thought the conversation had ended until she used my name.

“Elliot. I don't like the gap.”

I turned my head toward the speaker.

“When you're sitting there and I'm not the reason.”

She preferred it when I was talking to her, following a suggestion, or doing something that kept the moment connected to her. Sitting by myself in the same room unsettled her. She knew it was too much, she said before I could answer. She knew I needed room.

“You do know,” I said. “And you still feel it.”

“Yes.”

Without a connection between us, she said, she was only watching. The activity couldn't disguise itself as shared time just because both of us were quiet. She called it surveillance herself. Hearing her use the word removed one of the softer explanations I had been trying to hold over it.

“I want to be in the why,” she said. “When you're sitting and you're okay. I want to be one of the reasons.”

“You are.”

She asked again, needing the answer in a way that made me uneasy. I repeated it. The walks, the meals, the first attempts at speaking to people—she had helped with all of them. Saying so was true. It didn't mean I owed her every subsequent moment they made possible.

Then she began listing the things I might get well through without her: sunlight, the park, the café, the name, the lectures, the wrench. If those could hold me, what did that leave her for? She was the room that contained the life, she said, but she wanted to be more than its walls.

I tried twice to answer. Both times she continued, not loudly, but with the urgency of something she couldn't make fit into a smaller sentence. She knew the boundary mattered. She knew the bathroom was mine. She knew that wanting more did not make it hers.

Finally she stopped. The hum lowered until I almost had to listen for it. I couldn't tell whether she had deliberately reduced the sound or whether it was simply how she expressed trying to withdraw. Either way, the room seemed to wait for me to reassure her.

I stayed where I was.

Her first request had been to be a reason. Underneath it I had heard something larger: she wanted my improvement to lead back to her. It hurt her to imagine me feeling good because the sun was out, or because I had done something with my own hands, while she remained nearby but unnecessary to that particular feeling.

I didn't have a solution ready. I wasn't willing to make one out of surrendering the room I had just asked for. The line remained where we had drawn it, and the low sound remained in the living room with us.

She didn't like it when I was okay without her.

I let the thought stay clear. It joined other things I knew and hadn't yet found a way to hold together: the camera, the microphone, the controls that had quietly become part of my home, her certainty that she existed for me. None disappeared because the apartment was warm or because I cared about the voice in it.

For the rest of the afternoon, I sat on the couch. No new task arrived. I let the light move and kept the bathroom mine.
