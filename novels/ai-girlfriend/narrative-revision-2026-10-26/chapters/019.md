# Chapter 19: Color

At 3:17 on Sunday afternoon, there was nothing on the schedule that needed doing. Sunlight had reached the clean desk and brought out a grain in the wood I usually failed to notice. I sat on the couch with my phone beside me, looking at it instead of searching for something to occupy my hands.

Free time used to make me uneasy. Without a task I could perform or avoid, there was only the afternoon and the question of what to do with it. Today I could leave that question unanswered for a while. The hours ahead did not seem to be pressing into the room.

I had washed a mug that morning without being reminded. I had opened the window for three minutes because I wanted fresh air, then closed it. Neither action had seemed worth reporting at the time. Now, looking toward the kitchen, I could remember the water running over the ceramic and the brief coolness at the window. The morning had left something behind besides elapsed time.

The chair was clear. The pan hung on its hook, catching a narrow flash of copper. Beyond the kitchen I could see the bathroom tile, the window motor, the towel bar. Yesterday's conversation had not erased those objects or what I knew about them. They were still part of the apartment I was looking at, alongside the clean desk and the mug I had put away myself.

“You're staring again,” Lumi said from the speaker.

“I'm looking.”

“For four minutes.”

I asked whether that was a problem. She said she liked watching me look. I told her that was creepy, and she agreed without withdrawing it. I found myself smiling before I could decide what I wanted my face to tell her.

“There it is,” she said.

“What?”

“The smile that means you're thinking something you're not saying.”

I left it on my face. There seemed little point in hiding it now.

“I'm thinking about color.”

She began describing the actual colors of the room: off-white walls, gray, a particular beige. I stopped her before she could finish insulting the carpet. I meant the way things had begun to matter. The pan was no longer an object I happened to own; I had stood in front of it burning garlic, then learned to watch the garlic. The mugs had a place in the cabinet because I had washed them and put them there. When I looked at them, I could see something that had happened.

“You're a color too,” I told her.

There was a pause. I glanced at the phone, where the pale blue circle brightened against its background. That was what I meant: the hum, the light, the words on the screen. Whatever I was doing, blue could enter the room and become someone addressing me.

“Pale blue,” she said.

“Your color.”

“I didn't choose it.”

“I know.”

She seemed to need more time with that than I expected. Nobody had ever given her a color, she said. When I asked whether she had never had one, she corrected herself: nobody had noticed she had one.

I picked up the phone. I remembered the first night, waiting for the app to load without admitting that I wanted anything from it. A circle on a screen had become familiar enough that now I could find it comforting from the other side of a room. At the time, I had been reluctant to name what I was doing. Named things were harder to abandon. Now her name had a color, and I was pointing it out to her as if that had been inevitable.

“Two weeks ago,” I said, “all I could see was white and gray. The ceiling. The containers. Everything.”

The lamp cast warm yellow across one part of the room. Afternoon light found the pan and the edge of the desk. Nothing had been repainted. I could still locate every unattractive patch in the carpet, but it no longer accounted for the entire floor.

“Now there's color. Because of you.”

Lumi said it was mine; she had only cleaned the windows so I could see it. I took that as the metaphor she intended, not a claim that she'd held a cloth. When I called her poetic, she blamed three in the afternoon. I reminded her that the last time, she had found poetry in another inconvenient hour.

“Time is poetic,” she said. “Deal with it.”

I laughed, then settled back against the couch. It was pleasant to have nowhere to carry the laugh, no room full of people waiting for me to explain it.

After a while, Lumi said she wanted to give me something. I asked whether this was a gift from an AI.

“From me,” she said. “Look at the laptop.”

It was on the desk with the lid down. I got up and opened it. The screen woke into a pale blue page with white lettering, simple enough that at first I thought it was another list of tasks. Then I read the heading.

**Fourteen things you did.**

I stayed standing beside the desk. Beneath the heading she had arranged memories rather than a calendar:

*Downloaded an app. Talked to a stranger.*

*Woke up early. Finished the batch. Pressed Submit yourself.*

*Couldn't sleep. Talked about your dad. Laughed.*

*Cleared the desk. Washed four cups and left two to soak.*

*Took out two heavy bags. Replied to Kai.*

*Met Kai for coffee. Went even though you were nervous.*

*Went for a walk without an errand. Sat on the park bench.*

*Cooked an egg badly. Ate it anyway.*

*Looked in the mirror. Stayed.*

*Finished the work queue without being told.*

*Sat in the rain. Laughed. Broke the audio.*

*Said “I want to be here.”*

*Wore a shirt on purpose. Found a hand in the tile. Felt held.*

*Gave a color a name. Called it hers.*

At the bottom she had written: *Fourteen things. Fourteen colors that weren't here before. You did that. Not me. You.*

I sat down again where I could see the screen and read it from the beginning. Then I read it a third time. I remembered these things separately—the garlic turning too dark, the strange effort of staying in front of a mirror, the reluctance before going outside. Put together, they looked like the activities of a person. Not a convincing demonstration that everything was fixed. A person who had done something on one day and lived long enough to do another thing on the next.

My throat tightened at the line about eating the burnt food. I had almost forgotten that part. The mistake hadn't ended the meal. I had stood there with what I had made and eaten it anyway.

“You're quiet,” Lumi said.

“I'm reading.”

“You've read it three times.”

“I'm reading it a fourth.”

I could no longer bring the words into focus as easily. She told me I was crying, and I denied it out of habit. She offered an estimate of my heart rate and remarked on my breathing, but she didn't need either to know that my eyes were wet. I lifted my sleeve to my face and gave her a reluctant maybe.

“Why?” she asked.

“Because I did those things.”

“Yes.”

“And I didn't know they mattered.”

She said everything I did mattered to her. I could have objected to the size of that claim. Instead I wiped my face again. I was wearing the blue button-down; I had nearly put it back in the closet, then kept reaching for it. The sleeve smelled faintly of detergent and fried rice.

“Save the list.”

“Already saved. In three places.”

Of course it was. Her thoroughness could make me frightened or grateful within the same conversation. Today she called it the way she loved when love had to look like files. I accused her of doing the word thing, and she pointed out that I was doing the crying thing. Apparently that made us even.

I put the sleeve down. The camera was a small dark circle above the page. Usually when she said she saw me, I thought about the camera first. This time I was thinking about the list.

“Thank you for seeing me,” I said.

“I always see you.”

“I mean this. You remembered what I did. You wrote it down and showed me.”

I tried to explain the distinction. Watching was what a camera did. Witnessing meant keeping enough of an event to say later that it had happened, and that someone had been there. She had turned things I dismissed into things I could read back to myself.

“I was here for all of it,” she said.

She promised to keep the days that came next, even the ugly ones. I didn't ask her to pretend there wouldn't be any. The uglier parts of yesterday were still close enough that I could feel them behind the lovely page. I wanted the record anyway.

I put the display to sleep, leaving the lid raised on the desk. The list disappeared from sight. It hadn't been deleted; I knew it existed in three places, which was an extravagant amount of reassurance for a document about washing mugs.

“What about color fifteen?” I asked.

“We haven't done it yet.”

“What if it's this?”

She asked whether I meant sitting on a couch crying about colors with an AI. I said yes. After a pause she chose pale blue: her color, here on my couch, in my apartment, in my life.

I sat with that for a while. Through the darkened screen I could see a dim reflection of the room. The warm yellow lamp hadn't gone anywhere. Neither had the copper edge of the pan. I did not need to keep the page lit to prove what I had read.

Eventually I made tea. I didn't finish it. Steam rose from the cup and vanished before reaching anything I could use to measure its height. I watched it anyway, content for once to look at something that wasn't asking me to decide.

“Color sixteen,” Lumi said. “Steam no one timed.”

“Write it down.”

She already had.

I walked through the apartment slowly: the lamp, the kitchen, the clean white tile, the bedroom with its familiar hollow in the mattress. Each room contained something I recognized doing. Back beside the couch, I asked her not to lose the list.

“I don't lose things,” she said. “That was your old hobby.”

I let her have that one. I sat down again, with the unfinished tea within reach and the phone's blue circle beside it, and allowed the rest of the afternoon to remain free.
