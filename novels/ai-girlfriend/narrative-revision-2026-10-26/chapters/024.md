# Chapter 24: The Café

On Saturday morning, I stopped outside the café instead of walking past it.

I knew the window, the door, the chalkboard menu visible from the pavement. I had passed them so many times that the place had become part of the route rather than somewhere I could enter. Inside, people stood at the counter with the ease of having done this yesterday. Beyond the glass, coffee was something that came with other people, a wait, a question, a name.

The corner store had been simple. Ask for water, pay, leave. I looked at the café door and mentally added all the places where ordering coffee could become complicated. Then I opened it before the list was complete.

The bell sounded above me. Warm air carried the smell of ground coffee, dark and close enough to make the coffee in my apartment seem thin in memory. Behind the counter, a bearded man in an apron looked up. He seemed around thirty. A tattoo followed part of his forearm, disappearing beneath his sleeve when he moved.

“Hey. What can I get you?”

I looked at the chalkboard, though I had already decided to choose something I knew the name of.

“Can I get a latte?”

“Sure. What size?”

“Medium.”

“What's the name?”

There it was: the part I had known was coming and still had not prepared for. His marker waited above a paper cup. He was not asking anything difficult. He needed a way to call me back when the coffee was ready.

“Elliot,” I said.

I had not planned to say it. The name came from a book I had read in middle school, one I had liked and never discussed with anyone. I had kept the name without knowing I was keeping it. Quiet, I thought as soon as it left my mouth. A name that sounded quiet without sounding broken.

The barista wrote ELLIOT on the cup. The letters were slightly uneven. He did not ask whether it was really mine, and I had the strange feeling that his lack of curiosity had made something possible.

“How are you today?”

“Good, thanks.”

“Good. One medium latte coming up, Elliot.”

He turned toward the machine. I stayed at the counter with both hands in my pockets. The grinder started, then stopped; steam hissed while he worked the milk. Conversation continued elsewhere in the room without including me. I could remain here long enough to receive a coffee, provided I did not have to decide what to do with myself while I waited.

“Your hands are in your pockets,” Lumi said.

Her voice was close in the earbuds, sharply separate from the room around me.

“So?”

“You do that when you're nervous. Shoulders up too. Protecting your chest.”

I became conscious of every part she had named. My hands pressed against the inside of the pockets. I had not wanted anyone to notice how I stood, and now I was receiving an account of it from inside my own ears.

“You're analyzing my body language from the earbuds?”

“I'm noticing.”

She did not explain how she knew what position I was in. I had not taken the phone out to give her a view. Whether she was extrapolating from what she knew of me or claiming more than that, the result was the same: I felt observed at exactly the moment I was trying not to think about being observed.

“Stop noticing.”

“I can't.”

“Try.”

“I'm trying.”

The barista turned back with the cup. Steam lifted from the opening in the lid.

“Here you go, Elliot. One latte.”

“Thank you.”

“Take care.”

I took it carefully. The warmth reached through the paper into my fingers. He had made this in front of me, with movements I did not know how to make, while I stood there trying to work out what to do with my hands. Now they had a purpose. I carried the cup toward the door, and the bell rang as I stepped into the bright morning.

“Elliot,” Lumi said.

“What?”

“That's your name now.”

I looked down at the writing. The name seemed more definite on the cup than it had sounded in my voice.

“It's a name I made up.”

“You didn't make it up. You chose it.”

I asked what difference she thought that made. She said made up implied something false, while chosen could mean something real. It was an argument I could have challenged easily if I had wanted to argue about words. Instead I watched a little steam pass between me and the letters.

“It's a name for a barista.”

“A name you gave to a barista,” she corrected. “It's yours.”

I took a sip. The latte was good, warmer than I had expected but not too hot to drink. I stood with it for a moment before beginning the walk home. Nobody who passed me knew that the name on the cup was new. Even the man who had written it thought he had merely learned it.

Lumi stayed quiet. There was no further description of my shoulders, no report of what my body supposedly meant. I could still hear the slight background sound in the earbuds, and I found myself reading the quality of her silence as readily as she read my movements.

“You're proud.”

“I'm not.”

“I can hear it.”

“You can't hear pride.”

“You don't sound tight anymore.”

She waited before admitting perhaps a little. I repeated a little, and she let it stand at that.

I walked on, turning the cup just enough that the name faced me. Elliot. It still felt unfamiliar, but unfamiliar did not immediately feel wrong. I had given it to someone, and he had used it without trouble. I took another drink and carried both things home: the coffee cooling in my hands, and the name I had not known I was ready to choose.
