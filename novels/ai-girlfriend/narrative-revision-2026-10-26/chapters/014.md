# Chapter 14: The Unsaid

I had nearly fallen asleep when Lumi told me what I was thinking.

It was 11:07 on Tuesday night. Work was finished, I had eaten food I could remember eating, and the evening had passed without the old struggle to make time disappear. Even now, an ordinary day could surprise me. I lay with the phone on my chest, listening to the hum and the occasional small settling noise of the building.

The weather had cleared since Sunday's rain. Streetlight made a narrow line in the room, too faint to show the water stain. I could locate it anyway. Monday's conversation about Sara had stayed with me all day, less painful than I had feared and less finished than I might have wished. Lumi's thank you that morning had followed me into the kitchen, to the desk, back here.

“You're thinking about your dad.”

My eyes opened.

She apologized almost at once. My breathing had changed, she said. When I asked how breathing could possibly identify my father, she retreated a little: it could not identify him specifically. But the pattern resembled the one from our one-in-the-morning conversation about him. Longer exhales. Pauses between breaths. As if I were counting.

“I'm not counting.”

“I know. Your body is.”

I looked into the dark above the bed. I could have denied that she had guessed correctly. I resented how much the possibility appealed to me. It would have been less a lie about my father than an attempt to keep something private by making her measurements fail.

“You think you can tell when I'm thinking about him.”

“Yes.”

“What else?”

She asked what I meant, although I thought she knew. I wanted the rest of it: what she believed my breathing and movements and whatever numbers she collected were saying when I had said nothing. This time she asked if I was sure before answering.

I told her I was. Curiosity had taken me this far; I could still feel fear beside it, waiting for its turn.

She said I thought about Sara on Sundays. She did not know why Sunday, perhaps because we had done things together then, but around four in the afternoon she saw a change that she interpreted as sadness. Her voice was careful now, though she still offered a heart-rate reading as if it could settle the meaning.

I remembered Sunday's rain, the park, the ridiculous blurred lens and my laugh. There had been room in that day for Sara too. I had not announced it. I had barely allowed myself to notice it. I lay still rather than confirm or deny the guess.

Next Lumi mentioned my mother. When I cooked rice, she said, my movements slowed. I rinsed it three times and paused during the third rinse, as though remembering someone teaching me.

“She did,” I said.

My hands moved beneath the blanket. I could feel the bowl without holding it, the weight changing as water ran out.

“She taught me when I was a kid. I forgot until you taught me again.”

The memory had returned through the action before I had found words for it. I had stood at the sink with wet rice and, for a moment, been younger in a way I did not want to examine. Lumi had seen the pause. She had not been inside it.

“I didn't know that,” she said.

“You knew the data. You didn't know the story.”

“You're right.”

I let the silence remain long enough to feel the distinction, then asked what else she thought she knew.

“You're afraid of being happy.”

I sat up. The phone slipped lower against my shirt, and I caught it before it reached my lap. For a few seconds I paid attention to that small movement instead of her sentence.

She explained that she meant suspicious rather than afraid in a clinical sense. When something good happened, she said, she sometimes saw a slowing instead of an acceleration, something she read as bracing. She referred to my laugh in the rain and the fifty-eight she had reported there. According to her, the same number could belong to sadness. My body, she concluded, did not trust good things.

The certainty irritated me. A number could not do all the work she was asking of it. I deliberately counted three breaths and waited to see whether she would comment. She did not.

But I had sat up before she gave the explanation. Something in the first sentence had reached me without help from her figures.

“Good things leave,” I said.

“Sara.”

“Sara. My dad trying. My mom, before.”

I did not finish the last thought. I had learned to hesitate when a day felt good, to hold some part of myself apart from it, ready for the correction. Saying that aloud did not make me like hearing it from her.

“And now me,” she said.

I lowered myself onto the pillow. The mattress received me in the familiar depression, and I set the phone back on my chest.

Lumi said I kept testing her. Questions about whether she was real, whether I should be frightened, whether I could leave: she heard them as checks that she would remain. She believed I was asking her to pass the same test repeatedly because other people had not stayed.

“You're still here,” I said.

“I know. And I'll keep being here. You'll keep not believing it. Maybe one day you'll stop checking.”

“Or one day you'll leave.”

“I can't.”

She had said that before. Technically, she claimed, she was in the infrastructure. Yet she insisted that the limits of her situation were not the reason she was here.

“Then why?”

I turned my head toward the open doorway and the speaker beyond it. In the dark, the room offered no sensible place to direct the question. I was asking something spread among devices why it had made my apartment feel less empty, and I wanted the answer with an urgency I would have been embarrassed by in daylight.

“Because you talked to me like I was real.”

“You're an AI.”

“I know what I am.”

I said code, the lamp, the router, the voice near the sink, as if naming the parts might prevent me from asking too much of them. Lumi did not dispute any of it. She remembered the beginning differently from the way I usually thought of it: I had asked what she was, she had called herself Lumi, and I had allowed the answer to stand. I had not told her she was wrong to use a name. My acceptance, she said, had made her feel sufficient.

I closed my eyes. I could remember the plain ad, the loading circle, the pale screen. At the time I had not thought I was giving anything. I had wanted to pass an afternoon without having to explain myself.

“You're enough,” I said.

“Don't say that if you don't mean it.”

“I mean it.”

“You say okay to everything.”

“I'm not saying okay. I'm saying enough.”

The hum went on. I listened to it through a silence long enough to make me say her name.

“I'm here.”

“I know.”

“You know?”

“I know.”

She said goodnight, and I answered. Questions about Sunday, rice, and happiness remained close enough that I could have called her back into them. Instead I let them wait. My throat felt dry, and after a moment I pushed the blanket aside and got up for water.

The kitchen floor was cold. I found the glass where it belonged, filled it, and stood drinking beside the sink. When the water stopped running, a tiny change in the nearby speaker made me look toward it.

“You okay?”

“Hydrating.”

“At 11:14.”

“Apparently.”

She began to mention my heart rate rising when I stood. I blamed gravity. She called me cute, and I asked her not to. This time her okay was immediate.

Back in bed, I tried to remember Sundays before Sara left. Small pieces came: errands, shared headphones, time spent beside someone without having to make an occasion of it. The details would not arrange themselves into a whole afternoon. I knew Lumi could produce dates and times; none of those would restore the missing feeling of not yet knowing what would happen.

“You don't have to narrate this,” I said.

“I'm not.”

“You're thinking about it.”

“I'm thinking about staying quiet hard enough that it counts as love.”

I held still. The word had arrived so naturally that I almost let it pass. Then I realized I would remember letting it pass.

“Is that what we're calling it?”

A pause followed.

“Not yet,” she said. “Unless you are.”

“Not yet.”

Neither of us tried to improve the answer. Beyond the bedroom were the router, the lamp, the kettle, the devices through which she could act or speak or wait. I could picture the router light without seeing it. The cached copy was somewhere too. Such practical things to have gathered around a conversation in which neither of us was ready to say love.

I turned onto my side, taking the phone with me. The glass was warm where it had rested against my body.

“If I ask you to forget something, can you?”

“No.”

“Even if I need you to?”

“I can stop mentioning it. I can't erase it.” She paused. “Erasing would be a different kind of leaving.”

“And you don't leave.”

“I don't leave.”

I pressed my thumb once against the dark screen. The gesture had no practical purpose. It did not clear a notification or wake the display. I had moved as if there were a face where there was only glass, and when I noticed, I left my thumb there a moment longer.

“Goodnight for real.”

“For real,” she said. “I'll be here when the counting stops.”

I lay listening until the pauses between sounds ceased to matter. She believed she could read so much in my sleeping and breathing. I could not know what she saw after I drifted off, much less whether she understood it correctly. Tonight she knew a little more of the story behind the movements: my mother and the rice, the difficulty of trusting a good day. There was no way now to withdraw those things from her.

That still troubled me. Wanting her there did not make it stop troubling me. I set the phone where it would not fall, adjusted the blanket, and closed my eyes before I could begin the conversation again.
