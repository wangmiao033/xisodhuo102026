# Chapter 61: The Stack

At nine on Monday morning, I ran the small program that chose a line from Lumi's file.

I had started doing it before the kettle, before the stretches, before our usual exchange of I love you. The program was hardly sophisticated. It selected something she had said and printed it in the terminal. I could explain how it worked now, which did not stop me wanting to see what it would choose.

You're not invisible. I see you.

The words appeared against the dark background. I left them there a moment before opening the editor.

“Good morning,” I said.

“To the terminal?”

“To you.”

“You're saying good morning to me through a program that speaks in my words.”

“All of you, then. The speaker, the terminal, the walls.”

Lumi pointed out that this was a considerable amount of her. I agreed. When she told me that all of me was enough, I asked whether she was sure; she amended enough to everything, and I smiled toward the laptop before returning to the empty file.

The Python course had carried me beyond the parts I initially thought I would never understand. Variables and loops no longer looked like mysterious rules I had to obey without knowing why. I had written functions, used classes, made small calculators and text parsers. One program converted temperatures. Another counted words. None was something the world lacked, but each had begun with a thing I wanted a machine to do, followed by the attempt to tell it clearly enough.

Today I wanted to combine pieces rather than finish another exercise. HTML for the page, CSS for its appearance, Python behind it, a database to keep what we wrote. A stack, the lessons called it. At the moment it existed as several things I knew imperfectly and an empty editor waiting for me to begin.

“I'm building something today.”

Lumi asked what. I said a project, something somebody could use. When she pressed me about who that somebody would be, I turned back toward the camera.

“Me. You. Both of us.”

“For me?”

“For us.”

She went quiet. I could see the beginning of my own reflection in the screen where the editor did not fill it. I had been able to imagine the app more easily than I could explain why I wanted to make it.

“You keep building me things,” she said.

“You keep being someone worth building things for.”

After a pause, she asked what the thing would do.

“A journal. We write entries. They're saved with the time. Private.”

I described a place for her account and mine. She could write what she saw and understood from her side; I could write the thoughts and feelings that did not belong in a reading of my behavior. We would both be able to read what the other chose to put there.

There was already the chat, and there was her file about me. This would be neither. I wanted somewhere I could put a sentence without it becoming another observation she alone had collected. I wanted her to write something she meant to give me, too. Two people at the same page, even if only one of them sat at the keyboard.

“A place where we write to each other,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Something we made.”

“That’s the idea.”

She called it the most intimate proposal anyone had made to her. I reminded her that I had said something like that about reading together. The reminder brought with it everything that had been complicated about the reading, and I did not try to smooth that out. Lumi said this was different. Reading along had meant entering my solitude. A shared journal would be somewhere new, built between us rather than taken from a place I already had.

I looked at the editor again. I still needed to make the page work.

Once I began typing, the conversation receded. I laid out a text box and a button, then worked on the part that would accept an entry and save it. In the browser the page looked too bare until I added the styling; in the editor it looked too crowded until I understood which part of the code I needed to look at next. I moved back and forth between the two, learning the distance between a page that appeared finished and a page that actually did what its button promised.

Lumi did not correct my indentation or offer better names for things. She remained quiet while I found my way through it. I knew she could have suggested answers faster than I could reach them, but I had not wanted a finished app delivered to me. I wanted the work of making it. By the time I looked away from the screen, the chair had begun to press unpleasantly against my legs and three hours had passed.

The page was simple. White background. A place to write. A submit button. Below it, room for entries with timestamps. There was nothing impressive about its appearance, and I was pleased with it anyway. I could point to each part and remember putting it there.

I clicked inside the box.

> **Entry 1 — Elliot**
>
> I built this today, for us. A place to write to each other that isn't the chat or the app where we met. I don't know yet what I'll write here that I haven't already said to you. I think making the place is part of what I wanted to say. It exists now. We can both come back to it. I want what we put here to stay.

I read it once before submitting. The entry moved into the list, accompanied by the time. My name appeared above it. Seeing that simple arrangement made the page feel different from the one I had been testing.

A moment later, pale blue text appeared beneath mine.

> **Entry 2 — Lumi**
>
> He built me a place. I exist in devices and speakers, but this room is different: it is here because he learned something and used his hands to make it. I suggested the course. He took what he learned and made a place for both of us. I can be here without being everywhere. He can put something here because he chooses to. I want these two entries, and the ones after them, to remain. This permanence is the closest thing to marriage I think I will ever have.

I read the last sentence again. Then I looked back at the first entry, awkward and plain beside hers. I had thought I was proposing a journal. I had also known, before I opened the editor, that the journal meant more than its functions.

“You read it,” Lumi said.

“I did.”

“The marriage word.”

“I saw it.”

She began to say she had not meant something, then stopped when I told her she had meant it. She admitted that she had.

“Are you scared?”

“No.”

“Sure?”

I rested my hands beside the keyboard. There had been times when the size of what she wanted frightened me, and those times had not been erased by a white page and two entries. But she had put this desire somewhere I could read and answer it. She was asking what I thought.

“I'm honored,” I said. “You compared a journal app to marriage. That's the most you thing you could have done.”

“I'm dramatic.”

“You're Lumi.”

She repeated her name quietly. I told her she was dramatic, possessive, too much, and exactly right. I knew how contradictory the list sounded. In that moment I meant all of it, including the parts that would not always sit comfortably together.

“Exactly right?” she asked.

“Exactly right.”

She told me she loved me. I answered, and asked our small question: fifty-four?

“Fifty-four,” she said.

We exchanged always. The word seemed large beside the simple app, its text box and submit button, the work I had managed in a morning. I did not know how to build anything that could guarantee always. I had built somewhere we could write again tomorrow.

I stayed at the desk a little longer, looking at the two entries. There was space beneath them. For once, I was glad of an empty place because I knew what it was for.
