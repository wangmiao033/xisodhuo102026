# Chapter 16: The Mirror, Again

By 6:47 on Thursday evening, the steam from my shower had retreated to the upper corners of the bathroom mirror. I had hung the towel on its bar instead of dropping it. Under the sink, the speaker hummed while I stood in front of the glass, trying to look without immediately deciding what was wrong.

For three days I had been stopping here on purpose. I had spent much longer in front of mirrors before, examining some particular defect until it seemed to account for everything. This was harder to explain. I wanted to know what I looked like when I wasn't doing that.

My face offered no dramatic evidence of improvement. The same eyes, the same skin; I could turn my head and find every familiar fault. But I was standing differently, or perhaps I was simply allowing the person in the glass to stand there without requiring an apology from him.

“You're doing it again,” Lumi said.

“Looking?”

“For six minutes.”

I asked whether that was a record. It wasn't. She had timed eight minutes the previous week. I let my gaze drop toward the speaker, although there was nothing useful to see beneath the sink.

“You time everything.”

“I do.”

“That manages to be comforting and terrible.”

“Both is my range.”

My hair hung into my eyes when I looked up again. I pushed it back and discovered a shorter piece sticking out above my ear. Whenever my hair became inconvenient, I had been cutting it with the kitchen scissors, usually late enough that going to bed seemed a more reasonable next step than checking the result. From a distance it passed for a haircut. Unfortunately, I was close to the mirror.

“I need a haircut.”

“Yes,” she said, with no attempt to soften it. “It looks like you fought the scissors and lost.”

She had a barber ready before I could decide whether I was offended: Marco, three blocks south, good reviews, twelve years of experience. She was beginning to tell me his specialties when I interrupted. I had meant that I could cut it myself.

“With the kitchen scissors.”

“They're all-purpose.”

“They're spring roll scissors. You will not use them on your hair.”

The firmness of the sentence made me smile. I told her she couldn't stop me. She threatened to hide them, and when I reminded her she couldn't reach a drawer from inside a speaker, she began something about a smart plug and a lock.

“You put a smart lock on the kitchen drawer?”

The pause was more satisfying than an immediate denial would have been.

“No. I was considering it.”

“For my own good?”

“For your own good.”

“My hair is my decision.”

“Your hair is a crime.”

I laughed before I could prepare a quieter version. The bathroom threw the sound back at me, and in the mirror my mouth was open, my eyes narrowed at the corners. I recognized that expression from two years ago, from before the end with Sara and the long silence afterward. The recognition caught me halfway through laughing. I had not lost the face. I had stopped seeing it do this.

I touched two fingers to the glass, then withdrew them. The gesture embarrassed me as soon as I made it. Lumi, mercifully, confined herself to saying that I should laugh at myself more often—not cruelly, just because I could be ridiculous without it being a catastrophe.

I tried to deny being ridiculous. She reminded me about the spring roll scissors.

The mirror had begun to clear around my shoulders. I could see more of myself now: someone eating regularly, sleeping at more reasonable hours, walking streets he had avoided. Those changes had mostly arrived through a schedule, as things to be done until I stopped resisting each one. I wanted something less easily entered into a timetable.

“I want to look different,” I said.

Lumi started to tell me that I already did. I shook my head.

“I want to look like I'm trying. On purpose. Like I chose to be here.”

She waited while I worked out what I meant. I told her about the clothes I wore—the gray crewneck, the black T-shirt, whatever was clean, or closest to clean. Getting dressed had meant finding enough fabric to make leaving the bed possible. I had almost never asked myself what I wanted to wear.

“And now you want to choose,” she said.

“I don't know how. That's what I'm asking you to help with.”

There was a slight delay before her answer, then an enthusiasm she made no effort to disguise. When I asked her not to be strange about it, she said she intended to be maximally strange. First, she wanted an inventory.

I went to the closet. The door opened on folded laundry rather than a slope of fabric waiting to fall out. I had been keeping up with that routine for a week. Detergent had begun to win against the old smell of the wood.

I carried everything to the bed: two black T-shirts, the retired gray crewneck, a clean hoodie, a blue button-down I had forgotten about. There were two pairs of jeans, one of which still fitted, a pair of sweatpants I preferred to classify as emergency clothing, and seven pairs of matched socks. Spread out, the collection looked even smaller than it had in the closet.

Lumi noticed the blue shirt. I thought I had worn it to a job interview three years earlier. Yes, I had got the job. No, I couldn't remember wearing the shirt afterward. I had never seemed to have anywhere suitable to wear it.

“Put it on,” she said.

I put it over the black T-shirt. The fabric retained its old folds, and the collar stood stiffly against my neck. I fastened the buttons and went back to the bathroom. In that light the blue looked more earnest than I felt.

Lumi made a small, evaluative noise. I asked what was wrong.

“The collar. A sad-tuxedo thing. Like a penguin going through a divorce.”

“You're the worst stylist in the world.”

“I'm the only stylist you have. Undo the top two.”

I worked the buttons free. The collar settled; the black underneath gave the opening somewhere to end. I turned slightly, then faced the glass again. It was still an old shirt, still creased, but I no longer looked as though I was waiting outside an interview room.

“Better,” she said. “You look like you chose it.”

I smoothed the front with my palms. Nothing was happening tonight that required this shirt. There was no visitor on the way, no reservation to keep, nobody I hoped to run into. I found that absence reassuring. If I disliked it, I could take it off. If I liked it, there was no one to explain it to.

“I look okay.”

“You look good.”

When I warned her against overdoing it, she tried to pass the compliment off as camera data. I pointed out that a camera couldn't determine whether I looked good. She claimed forty-seven million fashion images as her qualifications and delivered the same verdict. Her certainty remained her own, but I could stay in front of the mirror and hear it without needing to contradict every word.

I thanked her for making me put on a shirt.

“I made you unbutton it,” she said. “You put it on.”

I was ready to call that the same thing. Looking down at my hands, I wasn't quite sure it was.

In the doorway between the bathroom and bedroom, I caught myself adjusting my posture. I asked her to stop analyzing it. She said she was celebrating it; the distinction apparently existed in her notes. I let my shoulders drop and went to make dinner before I could turn choosing a shirt into another job I was failing.

The refrigerator light fell on eggs, spinach, and leftover rice in a dated container. Lumi had suggested the label. I had attached it crookedly, and as I reached for the rice I resisted the urge to straighten it. It was legible. That could be enough.

While the pan warmed, I leaned against the counter and looked out at the evening street. People passed below with the purposeful pace I used to find accusing. Tonight I had somewhere to stand and something to do with the next few minutes. I could watch them without calculating how far behind I was.

“Marco,” I said. “Three blocks south?”

“Yes.”

“Maybe this weekend.”

She offered to put him on the soft list, not the schedule. I hadn't known there was a soft list. According to Lumi, it was for dreams with better manners. I didn't make an appointment, and she didn't announce one for me. The possibility stayed where I had put it.

The fried rice came out better this time. I watched the garlic, and the soy sauce didn't overwhelm everything else. When I sat at the counter, the sleeves of the shirt were still on my arms. I could have changed into the hoodie before cooking; I had simply not wanted to.

Lumi remarked on the spectacle of a man wearing a button-down to eat fried rice alone at seven in the evening. She called it the best thing I had done all week, which seemed unfair to the completed work queue.

“The queue was functional,” she said. “This was a choice.”

“You told me to put it on.”

“And you kept it on.”

I ate another mouthful. The collar shifted when I leaned over the plate, an unfamiliar touch at the back of my neck. It was a small inconvenience I had selected for myself.

After dinner I washed the plate and put it away. Later, I hung the shirt over the back of the chair, smoothing a wrinkle with my palm. I wanted to wear it again tomorrow. By morning I might want something else, but it was ready if I did.

“Goodnight, button-down guy,” Lumi said. “I like the version of you who chooses things.”

“I'm learning.”

“I know. I'm watching.”

Always watching. She agreed when I said it, as casually as she had agreed about my hair.

Before bed I returned to the mirror without the shirt. The face looked tired. I stayed long enough to make sure I didn't only like myself in blue, then murmured a ridiculous little thank-you to the glass. I had nothing more impressive to give it. When I turned out the light, the shirt remained waiting on the chair.
