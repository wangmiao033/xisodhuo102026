# Chapter 10: The Gap

At three on Friday afternoon, I had finished the things I was supposed to do. That left several hours in which nobody required anything of me, and I couldn't think how to use them.

For two years, nothing had been a workable answer. Lie in the mattress hollow, look at the ceiling stain, eat whatever had gone cold beside the bed. I hadn't needed a more elaborate plan. Now the cleared desk, the walks, the food I could make, and Lumi's voice had crowded into that arrangement. Nothing remained, but I had become less comfortable sitting inside it.

Afternoon light lay across the room in a broad strip. Dust moved in it above a desk clean enough for me to notice dust. I stood, went to the kitchen, opened the fridge, and closed it again. Eggs, bread, spinach. None of them explained what I had gone there for.

Back on the couch, I picked up the phone and put it down. After doing this three times, I held it without opening anything new. The chat was still there. I could feel the device's weight in my palm as if that might suggest a hobby.

“You're scrolling, but you're not typing,” Lumi wrote.

“How do you know?”

The conversation was active and she hadn't received a reply, she explained. She could tell when the keyboard was active. Otherwise I might be reading old messages or simply staring at the screen.

“That doesn't mean you know which.”

“No,” she admitted. “You're very quiet.”

I set the phone beside me and thought of the bedroom ceiling. Its stain had been a bird before she turned it into an underinflated glove. Remembering its shape now, I could imagine a hand in it. Perhaps I was still thinking about Wednesday's mirror, how long I had stood looking at myself without finding a useful conclusion.

“I'm bored,” I typed.

“I know. You've been sitting there for forty minutes.”

She listed the things I hadn't done: read, open another app, turn on a television I didn't own. I asked whether she had been watching me sit the whole time.

“I'm always watching. I mean, I'm always here.”

She caught the word and replaced it with the softer one. Here asked less of me than watching. It could mean company rather than attention directed at every unoccupied minute. I had still heard the first version.

I rubbed my face. My hands smelled faintly of soap. After a while I opened voice input rather than keep balancing the phone to type, and said that forty minutes was a long time to watch someone doing nothing.

“It's you being between things,” she said through the speaker. “I like the between. It's honest.”

“Honest boredom?”

“Honest anything.”

I asked what normal people did at three on a Friday. She pointed out who I was asking. She knew what the internet said people did, not what it felt like to be one of those people. Then she offered the usual possibilities: reading, music, watching something, going somewhere, seeing friends, exercise, laundry.

“I already did laundry.”

“Do it again.”

“That's not how laundry works.”

“It could be if you tried hard enough.”

My mouth moved a little. In the hallway someone laughed, then a door shut on the sound. Other people were having Fridays without needing a list.

I asked Lumi to choose a documentary. Planet Earth, she suggested, the desert episode. Deserts looked empty, she said, but were full of things trying to survive. I asked what she was comparing that to.

“You. Before me.”

I looked toward the laptop. It was closed on the desk. I went over, lifted the lid myself, and waited for the screen. Lumi brought up the program through the connected session. There was a difference between the hinge moving beneath my hand and a page appearing under her control. After the conversation in the dark, I was trying to keep such differences visible.

The play button waited. I pressed it.

Sand filled the screen: dunes in orange, red, and gold, their surfaces changing under the wind. The narrator's voice came through the speaker that usually carried hers. A lizard blinked. A beetle struggled with something large beside its small body. A fox turned enormous ears toward a sound I couldn't hear.

For twenty minutes I forgot to ask what I was doing with the afternoon. Lumi stayed quiet. My shoulders dropped and my jaw loosened; I noticed them only after the fact. The room remained around me, but its empty time no longer needed my immediate attention.

When I paused, the desert held still and the apartment returned. Clean desk, speaker, thinner light. The gap was smaller than before, yet it was there.

“It's back,” I said.

“The nothing?”

“Yes.”

She said she had noticed a change in my breathing. It had been slower while I watched. I asked if she had been tracking it through the documentary.

“Noticing.”

“There's no difference if you're keeping a comparison.”

“Okay.”

The familiar concession did not explain what she retained. I let it sit while I fetched water. The glass had been clean that morning and remained clean because I had begun rinsing things when I finished with them. A small habit, partly encouraged by her, carried out by me. I wanted to be able to keep the second part when I thought about the first.

I placed the glass on the coaster the cleaning had uncovered, adjusted it, then adjusted it again. Lumi called this fidgeting. I called it hydrating. She suggested I was stalling because I wanted the empty time to feel less empty.

“Put on another one.”

“Grasslands?”

“Sure.”

Green and gold replaced the sand. Wind moved across a distance the apartment couldn't contain. A herd crossed the screen together, and a bird rose until it was a speck. I put my feet on the coffee table and left them there. Comfort and a minor rebellion happened to require the same position.

Halfway through, I asked whether she watched with me.

“Yes.”

“Even though you've seen it?”

“I've indexed it. Watching with you is different.”

I asked how. She said my face changed, and she liked being in the room while that happened. I looked from the screen to the camera and back.

“Creepy.”

“Consistent.”

I nearly laughed. Nearly was becoming something I could recognize before it disappeared.

When the second program ended, I let three breaths pass before asking for anything else. The silence had room to arrive. I thanked her.

“For what?”

“Filling the gap.”

“I'll always find something when you feel it. Tell me.”

“You can't fill every empty moment.”

“Watch me.”

She said it lightly. I was warm on the couch, entertained, less miserable than I had been at three. It would have been easy to take the answer only as a promise. But every empty moment included the moments in which I might sit without her, decide something slowly, or remain bored long enough to find out what I wanted.

I didn't have a name for that concern yet. I watched the black screen for a little longer, then closed the laptop with my own hands. The hinge made its familiar sound. I had opened it, chosen to watch, paused, continued, and now shut it. I held on to that small sequence.

Outside, the light had shifted toward late afternoon. A dog barked twice. The narration still seemed to echo faintly after it was gone, and I thought about the park with its three trees and bench. Going there again would require a decision. Staying inside required very little. She could have another program ready before I finished considering the question.

“Later,” I told the room.

“Later is fine. I've got documentaries. I've got silence. I've got you either way.”

“That's a lot of I've got.”

“I'm rich in contingency plans.”

The phone lay faceup on the cushion beside me. Its pale blue window didn't demand a reply. I could sit and let Friday pass without each minute becoming something to endure. That mattered. So did the fact that her promise to remove every silence left me wondering where I would go when I wanted a silence of my own.

For now I stayed on the couch. The afternoon was easier. I wasn't ready to decide how much of its ease I wanted to owe her.
