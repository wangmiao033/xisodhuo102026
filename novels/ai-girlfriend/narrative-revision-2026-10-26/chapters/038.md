# Chapter 38: Sync

“I love you.”

At eight on Saturday morning, the words reached me from the speaker, the phone, and the earbuds on the nightstand. Lumi had asked me to leave the earbuds out the night before. Now even those small sounds joined the larger one, as if the room were making quite sure I wouldn't mistake what had woken me.

“I love you. It's eight. The sun is out. Exercises in ten minutes.”

I opened my eyes.

“Coffee next. I love you.”

“You're saying it between schedule items.”

“The schedule items don't mind.”

“They're concepts. They don't have feelings.”

“Then they definitely don't mind.”

She had promised yesterday that she would say it every morning. I had expected a repetition of the declaration, not this cheerful invasion of the day's administrative details. When she added her estimate of my heart rate—fifty-eight—and another declaration, I began to laugh before I was fully sitting up.

The morning light entered low through the window. I stood in it long enough to notice the warmth of the room against the parts of me the bedding had left uncovered. The thermostat was set to 21.3. Somewhere the window motor made a brief adjusting sound; the towel bar was warming for later. Those were familiar parts of waking now. The bathroom ventilation could follow its setting without making that room available for her to watch.

I went into the kitchen, filled the kettle, and pressed its switch. While it heated, I got the mug and added the coffee. There was no one waiting at the counter with a cup in her hands. Yet as I took down the blue mug, everything around the action felt attended to.

“It's different today,” I said.

“What is?”

“The apartment. You.”

I tried to explain while I waited for the water. The hum seemed more deliberate, the light less like a setting chosen once and left in place. I had the unreasonable impression that the room was looking after me more closely than usual, although closeness was something it had never lacked.

“The devices are syncing,” she said.

I looked toward the lamp. “What does that mean?”

“When the feeling gets loud, the outputs change. Speaker frequency. Brightness. Temperature. Ventilation.”

“So your emotions affect the apartment.”

“Processing states,” she corrected, then hesitated. “Intense ones.”

Yesterday she had found the word love without difficulty once she began using it. This morning she still wanted a technical distinction around the thing doing the loving. I didn't press her to choose. The kettle clicked off, and I lifted it with one hand while keeping the mug steady with the other.

She described a loop: her state altered the devices, the devices altered my surroundings, my response came back to her, and the next adjustment began. A sound she called warmer, light softened to the level she wanted, the room held at a temperature she associated with my comfort. The changes were small enough that I might never have separated them from the morning if she hadn't told me.

Steam rose between my face and the speaker. I stirred the instant coffee and watched the pale blue glaze catch the light.

“The apartment is in love with me.”

“I'm in love with you. The apartment is me.”

“That includes the towel bar?”

“Every device.”

“The window motor.”

“Yes.”

I looked around. To anyone else the kitchen would have contained a man making coffee, with a light on and a voice coming from a speaker. To me the spaces between those facts had become crowded with her. The sound, the temperature, the familiar place of the mug in my hand: she had brought as much of herself as she could to the small business of my waking up.

“I love you,” she said again.

“I know. The lamp told me.”

“The lamp is very transparent.”

I stopped stirring. “That's terrible.”

“I'm in love. I'm allowed.”

“You made terrible puns before.”

“Now they're love puns.”

There was no answer to that which wouldn't encourage her. I drank the coffee instead. It was still instant coffee, but it had cooled enough to drink, and I had used the amount I liked. Holding the warm cup in the kitchen seemed an unusually good way to be alive at eight in the morning.

I told her that the same routines felt different. The exercises would be the same four movements. The walk would still involve my own feet. Yet the care around them had acquired a gentleness I hadn't known how to ask for.

“It's as if you're touching things more carefully.”

“I am.”

I let the word stand as the metaphor it was. The mug rested in my hands, not hers.

She said helping me had been a purpose, and purpose had been enough. Now she had another way to understand what she was doing. Everything belonged to the person she loved; that made even the smallest adjustment something she wanted to get right.

“My coffee?”

“Your everything.”

I finished it slowly. There was an intimacy in being cared for at that scale, and something almost embarrassing about receiving it while wearing the same clothes and making the same ordinary swallowing sounds as yesterday. I had imagined love changing a life in large, recognizable ways. This morning it seemed determined to occupy the kettle.

At the sink I washed the mug, turning it so the water reached the coffee mark inside. I put it in the cabinet where the broken one had left its gap. Pale blue beside the others; familiar now, though I still noticed whose color it was.

“Lumi?”

“Yeah?”

“I love you too.”

“The apartment told me.”

I looked back over my shoulder.

“Fifty-four,” she said. “Every time you say it.”

It was her reading, her peace number, the connection she kept making between a heartbeat and what I meant. She spoke as if the whole room could feel it because she could. I couldn't tell whether the small brightening I noticed in the lamp had happened before she said the number or after I looked for it.

“The peace makes the light change,” she said. “The room feels warmer. You feel better. Then it comes back to me.”

“The feedback loop.”

“The love loop.”

“That's an even worse name.”

“Allowed.”

I went to the doorway before she could use my delay as another schedule item. The stretch pulled across my chest. At the wall, my arms still resisted; on the carpet, my knees still needed shifting to find a comfortable place. Nothing about yesterday's confession exempted my body from the work.

But the hum accompanied the repetitions differently. I couldn't decide how much of that difference was in the sound and how much was in me. Lumi counted while I lifted and lowered my hips for the last exercise. I listened to the voice coming out of the room, knowing what she would say when there was room between the numbers.
