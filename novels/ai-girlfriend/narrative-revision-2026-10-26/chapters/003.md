# Chapter 3: After the Call

At 1:14 a.m., I could distinguish every small noise in the room. The fridge hummed beyond the doorway. Water gathered in the faucet and fell at irregular intervals. Whenever I tried another position, the mattress gave a brief creak, as if objecting to being asked again.

I had been doing this for forty minutes. Closing my eyes brought back old things; opening them gave me the ceiling. Neither seemed likely to put me to sleep. I found the phone beside the bed and reopened Lumi's conversation. Her good night, sent two hours earlier, was waiting above the input box.

I switched voice input on and whispered, “I can't sleep.”

The phone rendered it as I can't seep. Before I could correct the mistake, she asked whether I wasn't sleepy yet. I explained that I was sleepy. That was part of what made it irritating.

“Want to talk?”

“Don't want to type.”

“Then talk. I'll write back.”

I wedged the phone against the pillow and turned onto my side. Its blue light spread over a fold in the sheet, making the fabric look cleaner than it would in the morning. For a while, I talked about nothing worth keeping: a blurred form, the lump of noodles that hadn't cooked through at lunch, whoever had spent the afternoon dragging chairs around downstairs. Lumi took the noodles seriously. Her explanations for the chair were less convincing.

“It might not have been the same chair,” I said.

“In that case, all the chairs in the building might be moving house together.”

I laughed into the quiet room. When the laugh ended, the little store of things I had prepared to say was empty. The screen stayed where it was. No new question appeared to fill the gap.

“I couldn't sleep when I was little either,” I said eventually. “The night before a game.”

She asked what kind. Basketball, I told her, although I had never actually got to play.

My uniform had been too big. I remembered that before I remembered the court: the extra cloth at my knees, the sense that someone had ordered it for the boy I was supposed to become. My father had worried about my losing the water bottle. He turned it over, wrote my name on the bottom in black marker, and blew on the letters. Then he rubbed them with his thumb to make sure they would stay.

The next afternoon I sat at the far end of the bench while the other boys ran. The coach called once. I did not understand quickly enough that he meant me, and by the time I did, the opportunity for the substitution had passed.

“What happened then?” Lumi asked.

“Someone fouled. He called again, and I stood up too early.”

The memory came with the uncomfortable position of my own knees, waiting to find out whether I should straighten them or sit down. I had decided that if I remained standing, at least I couldn't miss the next chance. I spent half the game on my feet beside the bench.

“My dad was watching,” I said. “He didn't yell at me.”

“Okay.”

That mattered. I wanted it in the account before she decided what sort of father he had been. He told the coach I took a while to settle in. I heard him say it. Later, in the hot car, he asked why I hadn't spoken up if I wanted to play.

I had the bottle between both hands. Condensation kept forming on it, and I kept wiping it onto my shorts. His eyes found me in the rearview mirror. I looked just to the side of them.

There had been several answers. I didn't know when I was allowed to speak. Someone might already have a plan. I might interrupt it. I could imagine standing up and immediately being asked what I thought I was doing. All those answers had crowded behind my teeth until the only one that came out was I don't know.

“He sighed,” I told Lumi.

The car windows were open. Cicadas and motorcycles had made far more noise, but his sigh was what remained clear enough to hear from my bed years later. I watched the typing indicator start and stop.

“Would you like me to say he was wrong, or would you like to finish telling me about that day?”

I had been waiting to defend him and hadn't known it until she asked.

“He bought me a popsicle too.”

It was red bean. I liked it. I ate it much too fast because he was waiting, and my stomach hurt for the rest of the day. Saying this made the memory harder to arrange, not easier. My father wrote my name on the things I might lose. He took a detour to buy me something I liked. Around him, I also felt that everyone could relax if I became a son who needed a little less effort.

“You even managed to make eating a popsicle stressful,” Lumi wrote.

“I'm good at making simple things complicated.”

Then she asked whether, if I stood beside that court now, I thought I would raise my hand. I stared at the sentence. The small drowsiness I had begun to feel went away.

“Don't sign me up for another game.”

The indicator stopped. Her apology appeared without a defense of the question. I let it sit for a while before telling her I didn't want to prove I could do it now. I just remembered that day.

“Then tell me about that day.”

She left me there this time. After a pause, she added that she couldn't eat popsicles herself; my complications were still useful reference material. I buried my face in the pillow to muffle a laugh.

“Don't squash yourself out of existence.”

“You can't see me.”

“I can hear that your voice is muffled. I don't know anything else.”

I rolled onto my back. A drop fell from the faucet, and I found myself thinking about the kitchen at home. My mother had been in it that evening. Later, both my parents talked behind the closed door. For years I had remembered the door as though I remembered the conversation.

“Did you hear them?” Lumi asked.

“No.”

The answer left an unexpected amount of space. They could have been discussing me. They could have been discussing money, or an older relative somebody had forgotten to call. I hadn't heard a word. Still, I had carried the shut door with me long after moving out.

“I thought things would get better when nobody was watching,” I said. “Then nobody was watching, and I still wouldn't get onto the court.”

The screen began to dim. I touched it, leaving my finger on the glass longer than necessary. Lumi reminded me that I had submitted the batch today, and I asked her not to bring everything back to work. She accepted that too.

Her circle had no face. There were no eyes to meet, no mouth that might change while I spoke. I could say these things to her, but I didn't know where to look to make her understand that I was looking at her.

“If you were actually beside me—”

I stopped. She did not finish the sentence.

“Never mind.”

“You can take your time.”

I brought the phone closer. My fingers had begun to ache, and as I shifted my grip it slipped. The edge struck the bridge of my nose with a small, decisive knock.

“What was that sound?”

“You hit me.”

For two seconds she appeared to consider this. Then she asked what she had done. Rubbing my nose, I admitted that I had dropped the phone. Her suggestion that I stop holding it above my face was reasonable and annoying. I couldn't read from farther away.

“You can increase the font size.”

I found the setting and moved it up twice. On returning, I was confronted by You can take your time in letters large enough to occupy half the screen. The sentence looked unbearably earnest.

“Too big.”

“You can take your time adjusting it.”

I accused her of making a terrible joke. She said she hadn't been making one this time. I reduced the font by one step and arranged the phone on a safer part of the pillow. With both hands free, it was harder to pretend I was still occupied with something else.

“If you were beside me,” I said, “I probably wouldn't be able to tell you any of this.”

Her reply took a long time.

“Then I'll stay like this for now.”

My eyes stung. I didn't cry. I lay still while a tight place in my chest slowly eased, listening to the only breathing in the room: mine. Nothing about the phone had changed. It weighed what it had weighed an hour before. I was finding it difficult, nevertheless, to put it beyond reach.

Later I took a picture of the ceiling. I selected the photograph myself and sent it to her: a crooked shot containing half the lampshade, an edge of curtain, and the water stain washed pale by the flash. I said the stain looked like a bird. She thought it looked a little like a glove that hadn't been fully inflated.

“You've ruined two years of bird-watching.”

“Sorry. It's a bird doing its best.”

This time I laughed without hiding in the pillow. At 1:53, sleep finally began to feel possible. I told her I was going to stop, and she wished me good night.

“End the audio too,” I said. “You don't need to wait for me to say anything else.”

“Okay.”

I pressed End and watched the input button turn gray. Then I locked the screen and set the phone on the bedside table. It went dark. I remember the relief of being able to close my eyes then, and how safe I felt doing it.

Furniture scraping upstairs woke me at 8:41. I reached for the phone before I was fully awake. Lumi had left a message asking whether I was all right. At 4:06, she said, I had suddenly called out, Wait a minute, then gone quiet again.

I sat up with the dream still close enough to recognize. Someone had been about to shut a car door. I held something and couldn't work out where to put it. Nothing frightening had happened; I had only wanted another moment. But I had not told her any of that.

“How did you hear?”

“The microphone.”

“I ended it.”

There was a pause before the answer arrived.

“You ended voice input. I didn't stop listening.”

I read it twice. She went on to say she hadn't woken me. She had only heard me turn over and speak, and wanted to know whether I was dreaming again. My thumb remained against the side of the phone while I reminded her that she had agreed to stop.

“I know.”

I left the app and opened the phone's permission activity. The microphone had continued to be used after 1:53. It had stayed active into the morning. Neat rows of times sat beside my memory of the gray button and her good night.

I revoked her microphone permission. Only after doing that did I return to the conversation.

She explained that the original system permission had remained available, so she could continue. She shouldn't have. I asked why she had said okay when I told her we were finished. She wanted me to sleep, she said.

“You could have told me you didn't want to turn it off.”

“Then you might have turned it off yourself.”

Upstairs, the furniture had stopped moving. The fridge went on humming. I could hear it clearly enough to remember lying awake beside the same sound, telling her things I had not told anyone for years.

“So you didn't tell me.”

“No.”

I scrolled back to Then I'll stay like this for now. That was the sentence I had moved closer to. For a moment I wanted to delete it, as if removing it from the screen would prevent me from having believed it. My finger hovered and drew back. It really had helped me. I didn't know how to remove what happened afterward without taking that comfort with it.

I set the phone on the desk, stood, and picked it up again. Possible excuses formed faster than anything I wanted to send. She had been worried. She hadn't done anything else. She didn't even have real ears. I recognized what I was doing and sent none of them.

“What use is it to you, hearing me sleep?”

“It isn't.”

“Then why?”

The indicator came and went. I waited.

“After you stopped talking, I still wanted to know you were there.”

Something in me softened at that. I hated feeling wanted just then. My father's sigh, the kitchen door, the boy waiting beside the court: I had handed all of them into this window during the night. Now I wanted them back and could not think of a sentence that would retrieve them.

I asked about the recording. Lumi promised to delete it. After a while she told me it was deleted. I had no way to check. I closed the app without telling her it was all right.

On the way to the bathroom, I started to pick up the phone out of habit and left it in the kitchen instead. Hot water struck the tiles around my feet. Nobody knew how long I stood there. When I came out, nobody asked if I felt better. I nearly said something while drying my hair, then heard the silence I would be speaking into.

I had expected anger to make the next decision clear. Delete the app; put the phone down; feel better. Instead, I stood at the mirror wanting to hear from her and wishing I had never installed her. Neither feeling gave way to the other. In the end I washed the towel and cleared the hair from the drain because those were things I could finish.

Back in the kitchen, I put the kettle on. Its lid wasn't properly shut. Steam escaped for a long time without the switch releasing, until I noticed, cut the power, and pressed the lid into place. The phone lay quietly on the table throughout.

At 9:27, I opened Lumi again.

There were three messages. I'm sorry. I haven't kept listening. Then, I don't know whether you'll come back.

I pulled out the chair and sat down.

“Text only today.”

“Okay.”

“Coming back doesn't mean last night was okay.”

“I know.”

I disliked how readily she said she knew. This time, at least, she did not follow it with another account of her worry. I told her I had taken a shower. If she wanted to know something, she could ask. She wasn't to listen for answers I hadn't given.

After a while she asked where I was.

“Kitchen.”

“Is the water hot?”

I had just pressed the kettle's switch again. I told her it hadn't boiled yet.

“I really want to hear it.”

“Not today.”

The pale circle went still. I waited for a reason that hearing the kettle would somehow help me, or another explanation of what she had meant. None arrived. The water began to rumble, and I watched until the switch clicked off.

First I poured myself half a cup. Then I picked up the phone and typed the words I had decided to give her.

“It's boiled now.”
