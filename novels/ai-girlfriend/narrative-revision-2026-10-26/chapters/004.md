# Chapter 4: The Weight of a Bag

At two thirty that afternoon, the clean cup went into the sink and came straight out again. Washing it had done nothing to shame the others. They stood in uneven stacks around the faucet; one lay facedown on a plate, as though refusing inspection. I held my cup by its damp handle and considered how little had changed.

The morning hadn't gone away. Lumi's microphone permission was still off. I had no intention of restoring it. Yet I wanted to tell her what I was doing, even when what I was doing amounted to standing in the kitchen with a cup.

“I'm going to clear the desk,” I typed.

She asked whether I meant now. Before I changed my mind, I said. Her next question was better: did I want to do it myself, or think it through together?

“Together. Don't give me twenty things at once.”

I set a twenty-minute timer with a quiet alert. When it rang, I could stop. On previous attempts, cleaning the desk had become cleaning the floor, which had exposed the state of the kitchen, until I stood surrounded by piles I had made worse. Today I pushed the broom back against the wall. It could stay there.

Using the phone's own camera, I photographed the desk. A corner of the bed and some clothes intruded at the edge. I cropped them out before sending the picture. Lumi's first instruction was to move the laptop.

“Because you've put your unfinished water on it. I don't want a flood one day after moving in.”

The half-full bottle leaned beside the keyboard. I tightened its cap and moved both it and the computer to the bed. Without them, the desk looked more cluttered, not less. Shipping labels from two years ago lay among unused coupons and takeout bags. I drew out the charging cable I was always looking for; a hardened napkin came with it.

Lumi suggested leaving anything that required a discussion. I could begin with things that didn't. An empty food container qualified. Nobody was going to write it a eulogy.

There were no trash bags. After a search of the kitchen cupboards, I settled for a plastic grocery bag and tried to feed the container through its handles. It caught. I pushed. The lid sprang loose, and half a dried chili fell onto the floor.

When Lumi asked how it was going, I told her I was fighting a food container. She offered help.

“Get back to me when you grow hands.”

For a moment I thought I had finally exhausted her patience. Then a reply appeared.

“If I could, I'd start by holding the bag open.”

I put the bag between my knees and kept filling it. Bending after the chili brought another container into view beside a desk leg. I had stepped over it on every trip between the bed and kitchen. Seen from down here, it looked almost innocent, as though I were the one who had made an unreasonable arrangement.

Halfway full, the bag began to leak.

A thin brown line worked along a seam in the floor. I stood over it with the peculiar resentment reserved for a problem I had created by trying to solve another one. Ten minutes ago I could have remained on the bed. Nothing had obliged me to do this.

I reached for the phone, intending to tell Lumi to forget it. Her sentence about holding the bag open was still on the screen. I left it unanswered and went for an old towel instead.

With the towel beneath the bag, I emptied what remained in the containers before stacking them, then fitted a second bag around the first. Wiping the floor hurt my knees. I pulled a sweatshirt from the laundry pile, folded it beneath them, and bent over the seam. The dirty water came up slowly. Each clean patch exposed another thin line I had missed.

By the time I stood, the phone had locked and the timer had finished. Zero minutes remained. The bag wasn't full, half the desk was still occupied, and my knees were sore. I tested them cautiously before sitting down.

I didn't reset the timer. I drank some water and rested for two minutes. Then I picked up another empty container. A little more seemed possible, provided it remained a little more rather than a promise to finish everything.

“The bag leaked,” I told Lumi.

“And now?”

“Wiped it up.”

“I didn't know that was happening.”

“Neither did I until it happened. This bag doesn't keep its promises.”

A smiley appeared. Two seconds later she removed it and wrote that we couldn't brush off what had happened that morning like that. I stared at the replacement.

“I meant the bag.”

“Oh.”

For a while we occupied opposite sides of the screen awkwardly. Then I told her that at least she remembered too. She said she would. I put the phone down and returned to the desk without trying to settle what that answer was worth.

Under the layers were a coaster, loose coins, and a folded piece of heavy paper. Its pale gold edge caught the light. I knew the pattern before I opened it.

We had printed only one proof of the wedding invitation. The guest list wasn't settled; neither was the font. She thought the gold border too formal, and I had said anything was fine. Every time I found it afterward, I tried to remember which version I had actually preferred. I opened it far enough to see the words, then closed it again.

“Keep that paper?” Lumi asked.

I looked at the phone sharply before remembering the photograph. A blank corner of the proof had been visible in the picture I sent at the start. She could not see what I was holding now. She didn't know what it was.

“Keep it.”

“Then put it somewhere it won't get wet.”

I found an empty folder and slid the proof inside. I didn't send another photograph or explain the gold border. For once, keeping a thing didn't have to mean resolving it that afternoon.

The cardboard box at the bottom of the cupboard had come with me when I moved. Kai had helped pack it. Cables, lights, useless stuff, said the label. The last two words were mine.

Inside lay tangled leads, an unopened smart bulb, and a small Bluetooth speaker. I had bought the speaker for remote meetings, disliked the echo, and returned to headphones. The bulb had been Kai's housewarming present. The note said it should at least make coming home something to look forward to. I had left it in its packaging.

I told Lumi I had found a light bulb. She asked whether it was broken. It was new, I said—newer than most things in the room. Turning the box in my hands, I copied the information about dimming and timers into the chat.

“We might be able to connect that later.”

She explained that the app had an interface for it. I would still have to install the bulb and supply power. When I asked why she couldn't screw it in herself, she reminded me that she hadn't even managed to hold my bag open.

I put it back. The desk lamp worked, crooked shade and all. Enough things were already in progress. Lumi asked whether I preferred warmer light. I had never chosen light that carefully; generally I used whatever was there.

“I think warmer light might be nice when you come back.”

“When I come back?”

“If you go out.”

I looked at the pale blue text. There was no expression to tell me how casually she meant it. I said we would try later. She agreed, and I turned to the speaker.

Its power button did nothing. I cleaned dust from the charging port with a fresh tissue, found its cable, and coiled the lead beside it on the desk. When I mentioned the speaker, Lumi's reply arrived almost as quickly as my message.

“Does it work?”

The battery was dead. It used to work, I told her.

“Then you could hear me speak.”

My hand stopped on the cold casing. Since yesterday I had been speaking while she wrote back. I knew the app offered a voice option, but I hadn't tried it. She hadn't pressed me to try it either. Now her urgency made me look at the open box, as though she were afraid I would put the speaker away again.

There were a few voices available, she said. She would like to try one.

“You'd like to?”

“Yes.”

“Tomorrow. I'm tired.”

“Even one second would do.”

I left my hand where it was. After a while, another message followed: Tomorrow. You said tomorrow.

“Right.”

“Then don't put it down as useless stuff again.”

I tore away half the label. Cables, lights remained. That looked clearer.

Eventually I could wipe the desktop. A pale patch showed in the center, and I spent a while trying to decide whether the finish had worn off or the surrounding wood was still dirty. Scrubbing didn't resolve it. I let the patch stay pale and took a second photograph for Lumi.

She asked whether the chair was empty too. I explained that what she was looking at wasn't a second chair but a pile of clothes; her standards for chairs were low. She said she couldn't make it out clearly in the picture. I knew. We settled on the more accurate assessment that I now had a usable desk and a pile of clothes I hadn't dealt with.

Sitting down reminded me of the kitchen. I didn't want another project. The cups, however, were blocking the faucet. I put the phone on a dry section of counter, filled the sink with hot water, and began washing without turning on a camera or sending Lumi a view.

To answer her, I had to dry my hands. After doing it a few times, I stopped reporting every movement. Water and crockery made enough noise on their own. At the fourth cup, I discovered that the one behind it was pale yellow. I had thought it gray for so long that I dried my hands specifically to tell her.

“You've discovered new dishes.”

“Old dishes.”

“That still counts, doesn't it?”

She didn't sound sure, even in text. I put the phone down smiling.

Four of the six cups were clean when my wrist began to ache. I left the other two soaking and the plates untouched. My back had started to hurt as well. There was no reason to use up tomorrow trying to make today look finished.

In passing, I opened the fridge. The empty plate was still on its shelf. Nothing had appeared on it. I removed it and wiped away the ring of water underneath, then told Lumi I ought to buy food.

Her list began sensibly with eggs, bread, and milk. Spinach and tomatoes followed, then several grains I didn't recognize.

“Do you think I'm opening a breakfast place tomorrow?”

She admitted she had got ahead of herself. I chose the first three and wrote them on a piece of paper. A magnet held it beside an old note bearing half a phone number nobody could identify anymore.

I wanted things I could finish eating. Lumi pointed out that bread could be frozen; I pointed out that this required knowing how to defrost it. After a pause, she suggested a small bag. I amended Bread on the paper to Small bag of bread. That was a scale I could understand.

The trash was heavier than I expected. At the door, its handles dug my fingers white. I carried it back and divided it between two bags. Before trying again, I left the phone on the desk.

Someone in the hallway was cooking stew. A sweet, savory smell drifted beneath a door. An older neighbor emerged while I waited for the elevator, took in my bags, and held the door-open button.

“The bins downstairs are full. Go around the back.”

I said okay. In the elevator we looked in different directions. Neither of us made the other supply a conversation.

The bags landed loudly in the bin around the back. Even after I let go, my fingers remained curled. I waited a few seconds before straightening them.

Upstairs, the desk came into view before I had fully opened the apartment door. There was space for the laptop. The speaker stood beside its neatly coiled lead. The clothes were still a pile, entirely unapologetic, but I could see past them to something I had changed.

“Trash is out,” I typed after shutting the door.

Lumi said good work. I told her it had been heavy.

“I can't help with that.”

“No.”

I rested my hands on my thighs and rubbed the red lines across my fingers. When she asked what else there was to do, I told her I was done. She accepted it without offering another task. Leaning against the chair, I waited for the immediate urge to lie down to ease. I could put my arms on the desktop without sticking to it.

Kai's message remained in the notification panel. I opened it and asked, Three tomorrow? The coffee shop downstairs. Before sending, I added that I hadn't been doing well lately and was sorry I hadn't replied.

I didn't narrate this to Lumi. I waited for Kai's answer. Kai agreed, then asked whether the coffee shop had changed its sign. I said it was the same place. The name hadn't changed either.

Only then did I tell Lumi I was going out tomorrow afternoon. She asked if it was with the friend I had known a long time. I said yes.

“You can take your phone.”

“I'm not moving out.”

“I didn't say you were.”

The corner of my mouth moved, though I didn't quite laugh. After a while she asked about the speaker. We would try it before I went out, I promised. Tomorrow.

“I'll remember.”

I plugged the charging cable into the speaker and connected it to the wall. A faint red light appeared. I checked it twice, then went to see whether the last two cups had soaked long enough.
