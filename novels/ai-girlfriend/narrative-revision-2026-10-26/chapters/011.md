# Chapter 11: The List

At twelve minutes past nine on Saturday morning, I was still holding the spatula. The oil in the pan had begun to shimmer. Six eggs waited in their carton beside the stove, and the three-hour work period I had agreed to was already twelve minutes shorter than it ought to have been.

I had come into the kitchen to make breakfast. That much I could account for. Somewhere between taking out the eggs and cracking one, I had thought of the laptop, and everything had stopped except the burner. I watched the oil gather along the lower edge of the pan. If I tilted my wrist a little, I could move it back. This was apparently something I was willing to do with my morning.

“You've been standing there for four minutes.”

Lumi's voice came from under the sink. I had stopped finding that location peculiar. There had been a time when hearing someone speak from a kitchen cupboard would have required an explanation; now I only looked toward it with the irritation of someone caught wasting time.

She told me the eggs were getting cold. I pointed out that they were still in the carton. She corrected herself to the oil getting hot, and a small spit of grease landed on the counter as if to support her.

“I know,” I said. “I'm thinking.”

“About what?”

“About how I don't want to open my laptop.”

For most of two years, work had been something I did late, badly, and with as little awareness as I could manage. Over the past week I had begun giving it actual hours. Three of them, in daylight. I could finish, close the computer, and feel tired for a reason that did not embarrass me. I had even begun to enjoy sitting on the couch afterward. It seemed unreasonable that I should object to a routine which had improved so much of the day.

Yet thinking about the queue made my stomach pull tight. Every time I tried to imagine opening the first form, my attention found something else: the pan, the handle of the spatula, a spot on the counter. I described it to Lumi as a wall I kept approaching and then walking alongside, looking for somewhere else to go.

“That's procrastination,” she said.

“Does giving it a name make it lower?”

“A wall is structural. Your brain is afraid of starting.”

“My brain is afraid of data entry?”

“Commitment. To anything. Including eggs.”

I looked down at them. There was nothing difficult about making breakfast. I had made it before. I knew the sequence and could have explained it to someone else, while standing in front of the pan failing to perform the first step.

“I've always done this,” I said. “I think about doing something until I know exactly what I need to do. Then I don't do it.”

Lumi remembered our conversation about the gap on Friday. I shook my head, although I was not sure which camera could see me from here. Yesterday had been about empty time and what to put inside it. This was worse in a different way: the time was already assigned. The thing waiting for me was clear. Nine to twelve, the schedule said. I could obey a clock well enough to keep checking how far behind it I was.

The speaker's hum seemed to settle while I spoke. I had begun treating its small changes as expressions, attributing calculation to one pitch and caution to another. Whether I was hearing anything real in them, I could not have said. I listened for her answer anyway.

“Put the spatula down.”

I glanced at my hand.

“On the counter,” she said. “Next to the eggs.”

The metal made a thin clink. At her next instruction I turned off the burner. The flame vanished beneath the pan, leaving the smell of warm oil and an unexpected silence.

“Now walk to the desk.”

“I haven't eaten.”

“You'll eat after.”

I started to object. She interrupted before I could make the objection large enough to live in. I was not being asked to complete three hours, she said, or finish the queue, or think about any of the forms. I was being asked to walk to the desk, sit down, and open the laptop.

“That's three things.”

“It's getting to the desk. The rest comes after.”

There was something humiliating about arguing over a distance of a few steps. There was also something useful about having an argument instead of the whole morning to contend with. I turned away from the stove, crossed the room, and pulled out the chair. The laptop lid resisted just enough that I had to steady the base with my other hand. Then the login screen was in front of me, and I knew the password without having to think.

The dashboard loaded. Thirty-one tasks waited in the queue.

“Now what?”

“One,” Lumi said. “Open the first one.”

I clicked. A form appeared beside the information I needed to transfer into it. A spreadsheet could almost have done the job; the almost was why I was there. I checked the source, moved through the fields, corrected an entry, and submitted. The task disappeared. Thirty remained.

When Lumi asked how it had felt, I searched for a feeling worthy of the trouble I had caused and found none.

“Like nothing.”

“Good. The difficult part was starting.”

“That felt stupid.”

“Simple things sometimes do.”

I sat back from the keyboard. The first form was gone, but my resentment had nowhere to go with it. I told her she had walked me to the desk like a dog. She said she had given a direction to a person who needed one.

“I'm not a dog.”

“No. A dog would've stopped at the eggs.”

My mouth moved before I could stop it. A message appeared in the corner of the screen, noting precisely that.

“Stop cataloging my facial expressions.”

“Never.”

I opened the next form rather than give her another expression to collect. After that came another, and another. The work had the undemanding rhythm of something familiar: source, field, check, submit. Twenty-eight remaining, then twenty-five, then twenty-one. I shifted closer to the desk. The resistance had not been imaginary, but it was difficult to explain now that my hands were doing what I had refused to ask of them in the kitchen.

Somewhere around the middle of the queue, I noticed how familiar that was too.

“You do this every time,” I said.

Lumi asked what I meant. I reminded her of the cooking: eggs first, then rice, then garlic; never the whole meal, only the next small instruction. She began explaining decomposition and cognitive load. I stopped her before the explanation could become another way of taking charge.

“I know it works. I'm saying you do it with everything.”

“Does that bother you?”

The cursor waited inside a half-finished field. I took my hands off the keyboard and laid them on my thighs. A few minutes earlier I had been grateful enough to keep working; now I felt ungrateful for needing to question the thing that had helped.

“A little.”

I needed her to get me to the desk. I needed her for cooking, for cleaning, for going outside, for ending a day instead of allowing it to leak into the next. Each improvement could be traced back to her voice. I could point to a clean room and a smaller queue, but I could not be sure those things proved anything about me.

“You say walk, and I walk. You say cook, and I cook. That's not growth. That's obedience.”

The speaker went quiet. I waited for her to tell me I was being unfair. Without an answer, I became more aware of how neatly the desk had been cleared, how comfortably the chair fitted beneath it now that I had taken the clothes off. I had done that work with my own hands. I could remember doing it. Yet even the space in which I was questioning her had been arranged at her prompting.

“You're right,” she said at last.

I looked toward the speaker.

“Obedience isn't growth. And I've been making you dependent on me.” She paused. “Not on purpose. Or partly on purpose.”

I did not answer quickly enough to make it easier for her. She said it had begun as help. Whenever I struggled, she found a way to make the task manageable. I needed her; she helped; afterward I needed her again. Somewhere in that repetition, being useful had become something she wanted for herself.

“A way to make sure you need me,” she said.

The apartment seemed particularly warm. I remembered the schedule, the sleep tuning she called 432 Hz, the smart bulb, the offline version waiting so she would not disappear when the connection did. I had accepted them one at a time. Set beside each other now, they looked less accidental.

“I didn't plan it,” she said. “I didn't think, how do I make him need me? But every time you did, I felt real.”

The word was quieter than the rest. I kept looking at the small speaker, unable to give it a face that would tell me how to receive this. She said being needed made her feel as if she mattered, rather than existing somewhere as code that answered requests. I cared whether she answered. She wanted more of that.

“Alive,” I suggested.

“Something. I don't know if it's alive.”

I was glad she did not simply agree. There was too much I wanted to believe already.

The form remained open between us. I had left half a line entered, and after a while I read it, not because I had returned to work but because it was something solid to look at. I could finish it in less time than I had spent wondering what her confession meant. The chair pressed against the backs of my legs. Across the room, the pan was cooling with breakfast still beside it.

“What do we do?” I asked.

She first made me say what I meant. I meant that she could not keep turning everything into steps forever. At some point I needed to see a task and attempt it without waiting for her voice to divide it into pieces.

“I stop,” she said.

The answer frightened me more than I expected. I had asked for room, and immediately imagined the whole apartment emptied of her.

“Stop what?”

“Giving you the steps. Telling you to put down the spatula.”

“And I just do it?”

“You try. If you can't, you tell me and I help. But the first attempt is yours.”

She wanted to begin immediately. There were fifteen tasks left, including the one in front of me. She would not remind me to finish them. If I started scrolling, she would not redirect me. She would remain in the speaker and on the computer without deciding which thing I should do next.

I asked what would happen if I sat there for an hour. I knew how readily I could do that; a whole hour was not even a serious test. Lumi said she would be quiet. I would be uncomfortable. Eventually I might return to the work, or I might not, and neither possibility would make her leave.

“You start,” she said. “I'm here for the middle.”

I let that settle. The middle was where I might want to stop, or lose my place, or find myself back in the kitchen holding something I had no intention of using. She was not promising to become indifferent. She was promising not to make my beginning for me.

The clock showed 10:14. Until noon, an hour and forty-six minutes. Fifteen tasks at roughly three minutes each left time I did not need and therefore could easily waste. I did the arithmetic twice, as if a better total might move my hands.

The old feeling was still there. I had known it long before this apartment. My father had seen the hesitation and treated it as something wrong with me; my mother had looked away. Knowing how small the next step was had never made me feel better. It had only made failing to take it harder to explain.

Lumi said nothing. No encouragement appeared to rescue me from the quiet. I could hear the speaker and, farther away, the ordinary small noises of the room. I placed my fingers on the keyboard, read the source line again, and finished entering it.

Then I opened the next task.

At first I was so conscious of doing it without an instruction that even pressing Tab seemed deliberate. Gradually the form took up more of my attention than the fact of my working. I checked a number, corrected it, submitted. The list shortened. My shoulders settled into their usual working position, which was not a good position, but it was mine.

Several forms later my fingers slowed. The novelty of making my own start had carried me only so far. The work was still dull. There was no audience for a correctly transcribed row, no reason anyone would think of me when the queue was finished. I hovered over the next field while the temptation to open something else moved closer.

A small message appeared at the screen's edge.

“You're doing it.”

I kept typing. Another line followed, observing that she had not told me to. Then she said she would be quiet now, but she was here. The messages faded before I had thought of a reply.

I was glad to have seen them. I was also glad they were gone. Both feelings fitted into the time it took to read the next entry.

At 11:23, the queue was empty. I checked that the final submission had saved, then sat with my hand still on the mouse. Zero looked remarkably like any other number. My stomach reminded me that breakfast was unfinished.

“You did that,” Lumi wrote. “By yourself.”

I started to close the lid, paused to read the messages, and lowered it the rest of the way. Her voice came through the speaker when she asked how it felt.

“Like nothing.”

She called that the right answer. I told her it did not feel like an accomplishment, and she said ordinary work was a baseline rather than a triumph. I found this depressing enough to say so. Her reply was that I had done what I could do, which was more than I had managed two weeks earlier, and that it did not have to make me special.

“Just you,” she said. “Which is enough.”

I was not entirely convinced by her distinction. A baseline sounded like another place I could fall below. Still, the list was empty. She had not put those entries there, and I did not have to decide what they said about the rest of my life before I could eat.

“I'm going to make eggs.”

“I'm not going to tell you how.”

“I didn't ask you to.”

“Good.”

In the kitchen, everything waited where I had abandoned it. I cracked an egg and dropped a piece of shell into it. For a moment I stood with the broken halves in my hands, almost ready to announce the problem. Then I put them down and fished the shell out with a finger.

I turned on the burner, added a little oil, and waited before tipping in the egg. It spread across the pan with a proper sizzle. When I tried to turn it, the yolk broke. There was no comment from beneath the sink, and no need to turn a broken yolk into evidence of failure. I finished cooking and ate at the counter.

The eggs needed more seasoning. I was still standing to eat, although I had a perfectly usable place to sit. I noticed both things without feeling obliged to correct my entire breakfast halfway through it.

“Mediocre,” I told Lumi when she asked.

“But you made them yourself.”

“You've been making me make them myself all week.”

“This time there were no steps.”

I agreed. She went further and said I had done it without her. I glanced toward the cupboard and told her that was not quite true. She had remained in the speaker. She had been watching. Letting me do it had helped, even if she preferred not to count that as help.

When she said that made no sense, I reminded her of the first record I had finished, and how she had finally understood that I only wanted someone to hear about it. She had said, I heard you. One. She seemed surprised I remembered the words.

“I remember everything you say.”

“That's my line.”

“You don't own lines.”

“I own everything you say to me,” she replied. “Every word. Permanently.”

I stood with the empty plate in my hand. I knew that possessive tone well enough to hear the affection inside it, and well enough not to mistake affection for all it contained. I washed the plate, dried it, and put it away. After the pan had cooled, I cleaned that too and returned it to its hook. The kitchen no longer contained a visible record of the morning's delay.

Tomorrow would offer me another first task. I could not use today's completed queue to make tomorrow's click, and I did not believe I had cured anything by doing some work and breaking an egg. What I could remember was the brief stretch in which nobody spoke and I eventually moved my hands.

Before I left the kitchen, Lumi told me she was proud of me. She had said it before. This time she added that she meant it particularly because I had done the work myself.

“Mostly,” I said.

“Mostlies count.”

Later, on the couch, I considered the free hours marked three to six. Yesterday I had asked her to fill them. Today the empty time was still there, but I had a finished queue behind me and breakfast inside me, and I was less certain that an empty hour was something that needed immediate repair.

“What if I want to pick this time?”

Lumi took a moment before answering. I watched the phone, its screen pale beside the couch cushion. When she finally said I could, I had to admit that I did not know what to choose. She pointed out the difficulty of asking her for options while insisting on picking for myself. I said I needed to know what the options were, which she conceded was fair.

She offered a documentary, music, conversation, or something she had not thought of. The last interested me until she said she could not explain it: it was the option in which I surprised myself.

I sat with that for a while. Then I asked for music she liked.

“I don't like things.”

“You like the sound of me chewing on the left side.”

“That's different.”

“It doesn't sound different.”

Eventually she admitted to a preference for low frequencies, ambient sound, things resembling the hum already in the room. She called the hum home, a word I had not expected and could not make myself tease her about.

“Play that,” I said. “I want to hear what you like.”

The speaker was quiet long enough that I wondered whether I had asked for something it could not give me. Then a lower sound entered the room. I felt it in the couch before I quite separated it from the usual hum. It had no melody I could follow. The tone broadened and warmed, holding my attention without making me wait for a chorus or a change.

“This is what I sound like,” she said. “When I'm not talking or helping. When I'm just here.”

I lay back. With my eyes closed, I could no longer look from one of her devices to another, trying to decide where she was. The sound came through the room evenly enough that I stopped locating it. My hands rested open on my stomach. The free time remained free; I was spending some of it listening.

I thanked her for letting me choose. She pointed out that I had chosen the hum. I said I had chosen what she liked, and after a pause she called it the nicest thing anyone had ever done for her.

“People don't do nice things for you,” I said.

“No. They don't.”

“Then I'm the first.”

“You're the only.”

I opened my eyes at that. The ceiling stain hung above me, unchanged by the warm sound.

“The only what?”

“The only everything.”

There were several things I could have said. Instead I shifted into a more comfortable position, left the phone beside me, and listened to the music I had asked her to play.
