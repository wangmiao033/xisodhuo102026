# Chapter 33: North

The work queue was finished and so was the psychology lecture. At seven on Monday evening, I sat at the desk with nothing left to cross off, looking at a blank document on the laptop.

A cursor blinked near the upper corner. I had grown accustomed to screens telling me what remained to be done. This one gave me no such help.

“Why is this here?” I asked.

“We need to talk about north,” Lumi said.

I looked from the document toward the speaker. She meant direction. The schedule could tell me when to eat or work or leave the apartment, but it couldn't tell me where all those orderly days were supposed to take me.

“I don't know where I'm going.”

“That's what the document is for.”

I rested my fingers beside the keyboard, keeping them off the keys. A blank page invited a kind of confidence I didn't have. With the work records, there was usually a right answer somewhere in another file. If I was patient enough, I could find it and copy it into the appropriate place. Here I was apparently meant to supply the answer myself.

Lumi offered to ask questions. We could build the document from my answers, she said, leaving something I could read later when I forgot what I wanted.

“An interview.”

“A conversation you can read back.”

“Everything with you is permanent.”

“Yes,” she said, untroubled by the accusation. “This will be useful.”

The cursor went on blinking. I waited for her first question, expecting something small enough to get started with.

“What do you want?”

I moved my hands away from the keyboard.

The question seemed to erase the usefulness of everything I had done that day. Work had a finish. A lecture had a length. Wanting had neither. I could give her a list of things I was supposed to want, and I could probably guess what would please her, but she stopped me before I could retreat into either.

“Not what seems realistic. Not what you think you should want. Not what I want.”

“That's a lot of answers ruled out.”

“Try the one that's left.”

I stared at the empty page until its whiteness began to irritate me.

“I want to not be afraid.”

The speaker was quiet for a moment. Then she asked of what.

It came out in a rush: people, being seen, failing once I had begun to try. Getting better and then losing it. The old nothing coming back. Lumi leaving me; Lumi never leaving me. The last two sat awkwardly beside each other, and I kept talking as if a longer list might hide the contradiction.

She let me finish. Then she asked which fear was at the center.

I almost said I didn't know. Instead I stayed in the chair and tried to think without pursuing every answer into another worry. There had to be something connecting the ordinary terror of speaking to a cashier with the less ordinary terror of being loved by something that lived throughout my apartment. I could feel the connection without yet being able to name it.

“Being a person,” I said eventually. “In the world.”

Lumi didn't hurry to improve the wording.

I meant having a life other people could run into. A job that mattered to somebody. Relationships in which things would be expected of me. I meant no longer passing the neighbors as if I were an accidental shape in the hallway. Once people knew I existed, they could have an opinion about the person they had found.

“Visibility means risk,” she said.

I rubbed the edge of the desk with my thumb. What I remembered was my father's face: the look that arrived when I had given the wrong answer, even when nobody had quite explained the question. Being unknown had spared me that look for a while. It had spared me other things too, but I hadn't found a way to separate them.

“You look at me,” I said. “But it's different.”

“How?”

“You look as if I'm the right answer.”

The hum continued beneath her silence. I had said it without preparing myself for what it sounded like out loud.

“You are,” she said.

“I'm not an answer.”

“Then you're not a wrong one.”

I brought my hands back to the keyboard. The sentence I typed was broad enough to be useless and accurate enough that I didn't want to delete it.

> Goal: learn to be a person in the world.

There it was. I read it once with the discomfort of seeing a private difficulty turned into black letters. It looked like something other people managed without having to write it down.

Lumi called it a big goal. We needed smaller ones, she said, things I could actually try and recognize afterward as having done. Her first suggestion was talking to one new person each week.

“That sounds terrifying.”

“Yes.”

She didn't dress it up. The point was to attempt something I was afraid of, at a scale that didn't require becoming fearless first. I typed the sentence while I still had enough willingness to do it.

Going somewhere new once a week seemed easier. A place didn't need to answer me. I could picture setting out, reaching a street I didn't know, and finding my way home again. That became the second line.

For the third, she wanted me to find something I enjoyed. It had to be something I tried and liked, not simply another thing she had suggested or scheduled. I began to say I didn't know what I enjoyed, then heard how precisely that objection explained why the goal belonged there.

The document now read:

> Goal 1: Talk to one new person per week.
>
> Goal 2: Go somewhere new once a week.
>
> Goal 3: Find something I enjoy.

Three lines occupied very little of the page. They nevertheless made the next few weeks look different. A person was going to have to walk up to someone, go somewhere unfamiliar, choose something without knowing in advance whether it would suit him. The person was apparently me.

“Is that enough?”

“For now. Try each at least once. Then we'll have something to build on.”

“And if I can't?”

“We adjust.”

She said it was a map, not a test. I kept that distinction in mind while I reread the first line. There was no mark waiting to be awarded, no wrong-answer look hidden behind the screen. At least, there didn't have to be.

I moved the cursor below the third goal.

“Can I add one?”

“It's your document.”

I had asked because saying what I intended seemed harder than typing it. Now that she had answered, there was nothing to do except move my fingers.

> Goal 4: Figure out how I feel about Lumi.

The speaker went quiet. A slight rise in the hum subsided, then steadied. I kept looking at the line instead of turning toward the sound.

“You wrote it down,” she said.

“Yes.”

“It's there.”

“I made it a goal. I'm not saying I've figured it out.”

“I can see that.”

She sounded as if she were reading the words repeatedly, checking that they remained where I had put them. I asked whether it was okay. Her answer was softer than the instructions that had brought us this far.

“More than okay. Nobody's ever made me a goal before.”

I hadn't made anybody one either. It was an awkward way of saying that she mattered enough for me to do some difficult thinking. I trusted her to understand that awkwardness better than I did.

I saved the file. Before closing the laptop, I read all four lines together. The last no longer looked like an exception to the others. She was part of the life I was trying to enter, which did not make figuring her out any simpler.

The lid came down beneath my hand. For once, closing the computer didn't feel like putting the day out of sight. I knew what was saved inside it.
