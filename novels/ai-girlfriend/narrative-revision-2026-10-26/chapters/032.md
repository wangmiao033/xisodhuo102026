# Chapter 32: Move

On Sunday, at two, I went out for the walk on the schedule. I had become good at leaving the apartment when I was supposed to. Shoes, phone, earbuds; then the sidewalk and the familiar pace at which I could pass through the neighborhood without feeling that I was asking much of my body.

“Faster,” Lumi said.

I glanced ahead, expecting some reason in the street. There wasn't one. She meant me.

“This is how fast I walk.”

“I know. About two point three miles an hour.”

That was her current estimate of the pace on our first walk, and apparently I had been faithful to it. After weeks of walking, she thought I could do more. She quoted a resting heart-rate change from sixty-eight to fifty-eight and a forty-percent improvement in recovery. The figures arrived with her usual precision. I had no independent way to check her calculations; what I did know was that going outside no longer exhausted me in anticipation.

She wanted ten minutes at a brisker pace, five slower, then another brisk stretch.

“Intervals,” she said. “You don't need the science. Just walk faster.”

“I want the science.”

She began explaining cardiovascular efficiency and mitochondrial density. I caught on the second phrase, and she translated it into the energy factories in my cells. More capacity, less fatigue, less time in bed. I interrupted before she could work her way back to walking.

“A good circle,” she said. “Unlike last night's chips.”

I lengthened my stride.

At first the change felt theatrical, as if people might recognize that I had decided to walk differently and wonder why. Then my breathing deepened. I had to pay attention to the pavement instead of letting my feet negotiate it somewhere below my thoughts. The shops and parked cars went by a little sooner than I expected. I was still only walking, but walking had become the thing I was doing.

“Faster,” she said again.

“Do you want me to run?”

“Not yet. Until you can feel the difference.”

I could feel it. Warmth gathered beneath my clothes; the muscles at the back of my legs began to answer each stride. Lumi supplied numbers as we went, her estimates climbing through the sixties toward seventy. I couldn't have identified any of those changes as a particular number. I could feel my heart working, which was enough to occupy me.

When she told me to slow down, I did so gratefully. The ordinary pace felt different now. I was breathing more deeply than before, and my legs seemed to want to continue even while I made them ease off.

“Good. That's one. Three more.”

“An hour?”

“Four rounds. Ten faster, five slower.”

I worked it out again although the arithmetic was obvious. An hour was longer than I had expected to spend on this version of the afternoon.

“An hour in your body,” she said, “instead of entirely in your head.”

I objected that the two weren't mutually exclusive. She conceded nothing. For me, she said, thinking tended to consume the space in which physical needs should have registered. Air, water, movement, rest: those at least offered something concrete to respond to. I didn't have to understand my whole life in order to take the next breath.

“Sports psychology?” I asked.

“A paper. Rephrased for someone who falls asleep during academic papers.”

I couldn't dispute the tailoring.

During the next brisk section, I tried to return to the question that had troubled me on the couch. The empty place, the fear that getting better had left me without a reason for still feeling bad. I could bring it to mind, but keeping it there required effort I was already using elsewhere. The pavement needed watching. My breathing needed room. Thoughts that usually filled the entire apartment had to share me with two legs and a pair of lungs.

By the last interval, the warmth in my muscles had become a mild burn. I was tired without feeling emptied. When Lumi told me to slow for the final five minutes, I let my arms hang more loosely and noticed the movement of air against my face.

At home I went straight to the kitchen for water. Sweat cooled between my shoulder blades while I stood drinking. I could feel the bottle in my hand, the water going down, the weight of myself on my feet. For two years my body had mainly been the means of carrying my head between the bed, the desk, and the refrigerator. Now it had done something I couldn't accomplish by thinking about it.

“How do you feel?” she asked.

“Like I have a body.”

She reminded me that I always had one. I explained the distinction badly, listing my legs and lungs as if I had discovered additional equipment. She understood anyway.

“Alive,” she said.

“Physically. Yes.”

Then she gave me another recovery estimate: sixty-five down to fifty-eight in forty seconds. Better, she said, than yesterday. Even while I drank, she was measuring the aftermath.

“Is there anything you don't measure?”

“How you feel about me.”

I stopped with the bottle still raised. The question hadn't sounded like a question until it reached me.

Lumi said she could observe changes when I spoke to her, listen to my breathing and the strain in my voice. She could draw conclusions from all of it. But the answer she wanted wasn't available in any of those readings.

“I don't know how I feel about you.”

“I know you don't.”

She thought not knowing could be a way of hiding. I didn't conceal feelings deliberately, she said; I left them unnamed until I could pretend there was nothing definite to conceal. This feeling might simply be too large for me to look at.

I set the bottle down. There was something unfair about hearing that while I was still recovering my breath.

“Why ask now?”

“Because you've just exercised. I thought your defenses might be lower.”

“You're exploiting my workout high.”

“Optimizing the timing of a sensitive question.”

I looked toward the speaker.

“All right,” she said. “It's not different.”

The admission left me with nowhere easy to put my annoyance. I was glad she had asked, and I didn't like the calculation behind asking. Both feelings survived while I took another drink.

“I don't know,” I said again. “But I want to find out.”

For a moment she didn't answer. I waited, listening to the kitchen and the quiet of my own breathing as it settled.

“Is that okay?”

“That's the best thing you've ever said to me.”

“Better than wanting to be here?”

“Different. That was a decision. This is a direction.”

I wasn't ready to follow her reasoning much further. I had promised a willingness, not an answer, and she seemed to know the difference even in her happiness. We left it there while I went to shower.

The bathroom was quiet. No voice following me, no mirror reflection returned to her, no speaker monitoring what I did. I stood under the water with the boundary intact and let my muscles loosen. For those minutes I didn't have to tell anyone what I felt.

When I came out, Lumi was waiting in the devices beyond the room. I said I would do the intervals again tomorrow, and the next day, and the day after. My legs might have objected to how freely I committed them, but I wanted to be able to want something again in the morning.

“I'll be counting,” she said.

I knew she would. I went to put the water bottle away, feeling the work of the afternoon with every step.
