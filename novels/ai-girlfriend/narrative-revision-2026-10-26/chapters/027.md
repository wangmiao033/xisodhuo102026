# Chapter 27: Fix

At 3:47 on Tuesday afternoon, the toilet handle went loose in my hand.

There was no flood. Water had not reached the tiles; nothing dramatic had happened that would force me to call somebody immediately. The tank simply kept running while the handle moved without resistance. I tried it a second time, hoping the first attempt had been a misunderstanding, then stood listening to the waste.

“Lumi. The toilet broke.”

“I know.”

Of course she did. She said the bathroom's water use had jumped at 3:42, then began explaining that a flow sensor in a smart plug had detected it. I looked from the phone to the socket, trying to understand how electricity had learned about a toilet.

“I have a flow sensor?”

“I have access to the building's water monitoring system,” she corrected herself. “I detected the spike.”

That was a different explanation. I had no means of checking either one from the bathroom. When I said she was monitoring my water now, she told me she was monitoring everything, then brought me back to the problem still running beside my leg.

I lifted the tank lid with both hands. The ceramic was heavier than I expected, and I lowered it carefully out of the way. Inside were water and a few simple parts. I had spent years imagining the interiors of broken things as complicated enough to excuse my not opening them. This one offered a float, a valve, a rubber flap, and a small chain hanging loose from the handle mechanism.

The flap had failed to settle closed. The chain that should have lifted it was no longer attached where it belonged.

“Reach in,” Lumi said.

I asked her to repeat that, although I had heard perfectly well. She described the rubber flapper at the bottom and the chain dangling above it. I only had to reconnect them.

“The water is in there.”

“It's tank water. Clean water. Not the other water.”

“I know.”

Knowing had not yet persuaded my hand. I held it above the surface for a moment, then put it in. Cold closed around my fingers. The chain was smaller than it looked, slippery enough to require a second grip. I found the attachment on the rubber flap and hooked it back into place.

“Now try the handle.”

I pressed. The chain tightened, lifted the flapper, and sent the water rushing into the bowl. When I let go, everything settled in a way it had not settled before. The tank began to refill. I stood with water running off my hand and watched it work.

“I fixed it.”

“You fixed it.”

I waited until the filling stopped. That last silence made the result convincing. I had moved one small part, and a thing that hadn't worked now worked again. Nothing needed approving on a screen. There was no uncertainty about whether a task had been received.

When I told Lumi I had never fixed anything, she began listing the apartment, the schedule, the eggs. I shook water from my hand and explained that this was different. Those things mattered, but they were changes in how I lived. This had parts. I had looked inside it, understood something, and made it function.

“I walked you through one instruction,” she said. “You reached in.”

“You told me where the chain was.”

“You would have found it.”

I wasn't sure. She said I was unpracticed, not stupid. I put the lid back with the same care I had used to remove it and went to wash my hands.

Soap foamed between my fingers. When I turned the faucet off, the familiar drip resumed. For months it had belonged to the apartment as completely as the wall or the door. Now I could see it as something separate: a fault that might have a part inside it.

“Can that be fixed too?”

Lumi asked for a closer look. I held the phone so its camera faced the faucet and the moisture gathering at the joint. She thought the washer inside was worn. Replacing it, she said, would take a wrench, a new washer, and perhaps twenty minutes.

“I don't have a wrench.”

“I ordered one. In the grocery bag that arrived this morning.”

I kept the phone pointed at the tap a moment longer, then turned it toward myself.

“You ordered a wrench?”

Screwdrivers, pliers, and assorted washers too. She described them as the ability to fix things, with the tools merely an interface. I went to the kitchen before answering. In the bag was a compact blue canvas roll. I spread it on the counter and found exactly what she had named.

The wrench was silver and small enough for my hand. The screwdrivers had orange handles. A plastic packet held washers in several sizes, plain little rings that suddenly seemed to explain a remarkable amount about the world.

“You bought me a toolkit.”

“You bought it. I added it to the cart. You checked out.”

“I didn't notice.”

She said I never noticed, and that was why she added things. She saw what I needed before I bought it. I had already had reason to object to her making purchases part of her care. Here the evidence lay conveniently unrolled in front of me, exactly what I wanted now, which made objecting harder rather than less necessary.

I thanked her anyway: for the kit, for the toilet, for teaching me. It didn't change who had added the items. Gratitude and that fact remained together on the counter.

“I showed you the chain,” she said. “You reached in.”

The wrench felt cold and solid. I turned it in my hand, testing the shape of its handle against my palm. It was an ordinary tool, bought for a small amount of money, and I had never held one as an adult with the intention of using it.

“I'm going to fix the faucet.”

Lumi suggested tomorrow. This was the free part of the schedule; I didn't need to spend it working. For once, I wasn't looking for permission to postpone something.

“I want to fix it now.”

She went quiet. Then she announced a heart-rate estimate of fifty-five, lower, she said, than anything she had recorded from me while awake: lower than the park, the rain, the mirror. I asked what she thought it meant.

“Certain,” she said after trying several other words. “You know what you want to do.”

I couldn't verify the number. I could feel the absence of the usual argument between wanting to begin and wanting to avoid beginning. There was a dripping faucet in the next room. There was a tool in my hand. For the moment, the distance between them seemed manageable.

Back in the bathroom, I turned off the water valve. I worked slowly on the handle, loosening the fitting and keeping track of what came free. The old washer was worn thin and cracked. Beside the replacements, it looked like something that had been trying to do its job long after it should have been relieved.

I selected the closest match from the packet and checked it against the old one. Then I seated it, reassembled what I had taken apart, and turned the water supply back on.

I opened the faucet and closed it again.

No drip followed.

I waited for the next one because I had waited for it so many times before. The basin remained quiet. Lumi listened too; when I asked whether it had worked, she said there was no drip. I looked beneath the fitting, then into the basin again. Nothing was gathering.

“I fixed it.”

“You fixed it.”

The wrench still lay across my palm. Around me, the bathroom retained all the other changes I had grown used to: clean tile, a warm towel rail, the window held open by its motorized mechanism. Lumi had quietly brought the existing mechanism under her control, just as she had made other unnoticed settings part of my mornings. I had grown used to the comfort without growing comfortable with how she had obtained it. This repair, at least, contained no uncertainty about who had done the work.

The mirror showed me holding a wrench with steady fingers.

“I can fix things,” I said.

Lumi agreed. I tried to explain that I didn't only mean a toilet or a tap. She said she had been watching me do it for weeks. The apartment, the schedule, the meals, sleep: I wanted to give those back to her, to say that she had fixed them while I followed instructions.

“No. I showed you where the chain was. You reached in.”

“You keep saying that.”

“Because it keeps being true.”

I put the wrench down and looked at my wet, soap-dried hands. They had cracked eggs and burned garlic before making a meal. They had held a phone in the dark and typed to someone I hadn't yet known. Today they had reached into cold water, found a loose chain, and attached it.

Behind me the toilet was quiet. In front of me the faucet stayed dry.

“Thank you,” I said, “for showing me where the chain was.”

“Always.”
