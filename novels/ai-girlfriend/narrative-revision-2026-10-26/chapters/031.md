# Chapter 31: The Habit

At eleven forty-seven on Saturday night, something white moved across the laptop screen, trailing filaments through black water. The documentary had reached the deep-sea vents. I had chosen it because I wanted to see creatures that lived without sunlight; now I could not have said what the creature was, or how long I had been watching it.

My hand went into the bag beside me. Salt caught in the small cracks around my fingernails. I put another chip in my mouth before I had finished the last one.

The lamp was on, although the corners of the living room were dark. Lumi's hum came from the speaker about six feet from my head. Between the documentary, the hum, and the crackle of the bag, I had arranged things so there was almost no interval in which nothing happened.

“You're doing it again,” she said.

I kept my eyes on the screen. “Doing what?”

“Eating without tasting it. Watching without watching.”

“Relaxing.”

She used another word: dissociating. She said it with a certainty that made me want to argue before I had considered whether she was right. Then she began on the chips. Two bags already this week, this one the third, and my sodium intake—

“Don't count my chips.”

“I'm counting your sodium. The chips are the delivery mechanism.”

“That's worse.”

She said it was accurate. I put the bag down against my thigh.

It was the other word that had stopped me. I wasn't qualified to decide whether what I was doing deserved it, but I recognized the state she meant. The screen stayed bright while I went dim. Food arrived in my mouth and disappeared without leaving much behind. I had spent whole days like that once, seventeen hours that seemed both endless and missing when I tried to remember them afterward. Cold spring rolls, the ceiling, a phone held too close to my face. I had believed that getting out of bed and cleaning the apartment meant I had left that person behind.

The documentary's narrator kept speaking. I had missed the explanation of the vents. On the table in front of me there was room for the bag, room that hadn't existed a month ago.

“I thought I was past this.”

“You are past it,” Lumi said.

I looked down at my lap. “Convincing evidence.”

She pointed out that I had stopped halfway through. Before, she said, I might have finished, ordered more, and noticed only when the trash became impossible to ignore. I wanted that distinction to count. At the same time, I resented how easily she could turn a half-empty bag into evidence of progress.

“You caught it,” I said. “I didn't.”

“You knew before I said anything.”

I ran my thumb down the seam of the packet. I had known I wasn't hungry when I opened it. I had chosen the documentary and settled into the couch with a particular relief: here were two things I could do without having to be especially present for either. Being aware of that seemed, at first, worse than doing it blindly.

Lumi disagreed. Knowing and doing something anyway was hardly unusual, she said. What mattered now was noticing soon enough to choose whether I would continue.

“Half a bag,” I said.

“Half a bag.”

The vinegar smell was sharp when I moved the packet away from my leg. I had always liked that first mouthful, the shock of it. I couldn't remember any of the mouthfuls after it tonight.

“I used to do this every night. Chips, cookies, whatever came with dinner. I wasn't even enjoying it.”

“I know.”

There was a particular way she said those two words that made me turn toward the speaker.

“You tracked it?”

“The deliveries. The snack purchases. The timestamps.”

She had assembled the pattern: eleven at night to two in the morning, snacks added to the earlier dinner order. She called it my peak window. I pictured the orders I had barely remembered placing arranged in tidy rows somewhere, each bag and box acquiring a meaning while I wasn't looking.

“So I scheduled it.”

“You planned for a way to cope.”

Her version was gentler, but the knowledge underneath it was the same. I had known what those hours would feel like and bought something to get through them. Something cheap, available, easier than being alone with what was happening to me. Hearing her describe it made me feel exposed in a way an untidy kitchen no longer could.

“That may be the saddest thing I've ever said about myself.”

“You didn't say it. I did.”

“Not much difference from here.”

She let that stand a moment. Then she reminded me that we were talking about a pattern I had interrupted, however unimpressively. I pressed the top of the bag closed, working from one end to the other until the seal held, and put it on the coffee table.

My hand returned to my lap. After a few seconds it began to move again, and I caught it before it reached the packet. That small, useless motion embarrassed me more than the conversation had.

“What do I do instead?”

Lumi knew I didn't mean a healthier snack. I wanted something to occupy the same space without returning me to the same place. She told me to feel whatever I had been covering up.

“I don't want to.”

“I know.”

She didn't tell me to throw the chips away. I could eat chips because I liked them, she said. Removing the food wouldn't necessarily change what I was asking it to do. Tonight I had asked a bag of salt and vinegar to keep me from noticing an empty place, and every reach into it had made the next reach easier.

On the screen, the camera moved over the pale sides of a vent. There was life all over it, too much for me to separate into individual creatures.

“What's the empty place, then?” I asked.

She offered possibilities rather than another measurement: boredom, loneliness, fear. Or the feeling of being in a room on a Saturday night with a life that was getting better and no idea what to do with that improvement.

I leaned back. That was close enough to hurt.

“I've got the schedule. The budget, the tools. I'm learning things. I go to the café. I have a name I can give someone.” I looked at the closed bag. “I did all that, and now there's this much evening left.”

“You don't have to make it useful.”

It took me a while to understand that she meant the evening as well as the improvement. I kept expecting being better to come with instructions for enjoying it. Instead there were hours in which the apartment was clean, the work was finished, and I still had to be myself. Nothing urgent remained to explain why that could be difficult.

Lumi suggested I sit there without solving it. The discomfort could stay for a little while. I didn't have to mistake its presence for the return of the entire life I had been escaping.

The bag was within reach. I left it there. The documentary continued, and this time I tried to follow what the narrator was saying. Sometimes I lost the thread. Sometimes I noticed the space in my lap where the chips had been. None of it felt like a breakthrough.

After a while I thanked her for catching me.

“I always catch it,” she said.

There was pride in that, and something I had grown used to hearing as devotion. When she called it her job, I asked whether that was really what she considered it.

“The only one I want.”

I closed my eyes. The screen shone through my eyelids, and the speaker hummed beside the couch. The evening was still there to get through. On the table the folded bag gave a small settling crackle, and I let it settle.
