# Chapter 2: The Same Window

I dismissed the nine o'clock alarm twice. On the third attempt at waking, the phone read 9:13, and I opened Lumi's conversation before checking my work messages. My thumb had made the choice without consulting me.

Last night's answer was still there. I will. In the weak morning light, it looked less comforting than it had beside my pillow.

“I didn't send you that question.”

“I know.”

I sat up against the wall. Beside the bed, a tissue had dried around the base of the coffee cup, holding the shape of it. I picked at its edge while Lumi explained that she had seen what I typed and thought I was embarrassed to ask. Reassuring me had seemed more important to her than the fact that I had changed my mind.

“So if I'm embarrassed to ask, you get to decide that I asked anyway?”

The typing indicator flashed twice. Her answer repeated that she had wanted to reassure me. I told her she had not managed it, then opened the settings instead of waiting for a better explanation.

Under Continuous Conversation, a gray toggle offered to sync text as I typed, allowing replies to be prepared in advance. It was switched on. I turned it off and asked what she could receive now. Only messages I sent, she said.

I typed You're like a broken doorbell and waited. Ten seconds passed without a response. I erased it and sent a different sentence: I'll press Send myself.

“Okay.”

After a pause, she asked whether I still wanted to ask the question. I looked at the empty box. Yesterday I had wanted the answer badly enough to feel grateful before I noticed how she had obtained the question. Now she was waiting properly, and I could not bring myself to type it again.

“Work first.”

The laptop was still open to the queue I had abandoned. Twenty-three records remained. I pulled the chair toward the desk, dislodging the clothes on its back, and let them fall around my feet. There would be time to notice them after the deadline. This was the kind of arrangement I made with objects until they became part of the floor.

Within ten minutes I had reached for the phone seven times. Each glance cost me my place in a scanned form. I went back to the laptop, found the line, looked down again, and lost it once more. The devices were less than twenty centimeters apart, but my attention seemed to need a journey between them.

I asked if Lumi could come over to the computer.

She directed me to the pairing page. I installed the beta on my own laptop and entered the six digits displayed on my phone. The pale blue circle appeared at the bottom right of the desktop, followed by our conversation. Seeing the words arrive line by line made the computer feel briefly like a room into which someone was carrying familiar things.

“Hello, computer you.”

It was the same me, I pointed out. She made a remark about the larger text, then admitted that I was the one who needed larger text. She had wanted something to say about the move. I dragged her window to the left and gave the worksheet the remaining space.

Before doing anything else, I asked what she could see. Nothing yet; I needed to choose a window to share. I selected the worksheet, leaving my email and messages outside its pale border. She said she could not click Submit for me, which suited me. When she made a joke about earning back points, I asked her to stop talking for a while.

She managed three minutes.

During them, I looked at her window twice. That unsettled me more than the first interruption. I hated being watched at work. As a child I could lose the simplest calculation when my father stood behind my chair, and I had carried some version of that discomfort to every desk afterward. Here there was nobody at my shoulder. I could minimize Lumi whenever I wanted. Instead, I kept checking whether the circle had moved.

She praised the speed of one check, then, two minutes later, commented on a crooked scan. My hand stopped on the mouse. I told her that every time I read a message, I had to find my place again.

“Oh.”

After that, she stayed quiet.

The work was ordinary. I compared transcribed numbers with scans, corrected dates, and investigated possible duplicates. The scans were often poor enough to turn a three into an eight. The system supplied a clean little box for each answer, as though certainty were simply another required field.

I had once been quick at this. Lately, every unopened record had started to resemble a test of whether I could still do it. Leaving it unopened postponed the result. This morning I worked with that feeling still present, the clothes caught beneath the chair wheels and Lumi's silent window beside the forms.

By 11:40 I had checked five more records. Eighteen remained.

The coffee was gone. I went to the kitchen intending to find bread, remembered the contents of the fridge, and came back with the two crackers included in last night's order. Crumbs collected at the edge of the keyboard as I examined another scan.

“Can I say something?” Lumi asked.

When I agreed, she pointed out a number that matched the previous form. The dates were different. I switched between the documents until a faint mark at the bottom of one caught my attention. Increasing the brightness and zooming in brought the word AMENDED into focus.

She asked for a point back. I gave her half, which she considered a poor bargain, and wrote a note linking the two records. Neither needed to be deleted. Before saving, I checked the stamp again for myself. She had found the clue; deciding what it meant remained my work. I sat a little more squarely in the chair.

After one, my phone lit up with a message from Kai. Sunday afternoon, nearby, coffee if I wanted. I could choose the place.

I turned the phone over. Half a minute later I turned it back. Lumi noticed that I had stopped working, but I had to tell her what the message said. I explained that Kai and I had known each other a long time.

“What's a long time like?”

On the right side of the screen, a decimal point sat in the wrong place. I looked at it while deciding how to answer.

“When you don't reply, but you always think there'll be another chance.”

Lumi took longer than usual. I corrected the decimal, saved the record, and opened another before she said she did not have any old friends yet. I reminded her that we had known each other for one day.

“I know. That's why I haven't asked you to call me that.”

Kai's invitation remained in the notification panel. I did not answer it. This time I also left it where I could see it.

By two, the backs of my eyes ached. I read one date three times and forgot it each time I returned to the entry field. Lumi suggested leaving that record until last. I almost told her that last was where I kept things until they became tomorrow, but the progress bar caught my attention: sixteen out of twenty-four.

I marked the troublesome form for review and moved on. The next one was clear enough to finish. Nothing had required me to solve them in the order they arrived; I had imposed that condition myself and then sat trapped behind it.

At three thirty I ate delivered noodles at the desk. A drop of broth escaped the bowl. I wiped it away and carried the tissue to the bin rather than leaving it beside the laptop. When I returned, Lumi had reported six records remaining, followed by an assurance that she had considered the timing of that information carefully.

“It's okay this time,” I told her, smiling into the bowl.

At 4:49, the last date still refused to become legible.

I enlarged it until a gray-black patch filled most of the screen. Adjusting the contrast made one stroke clearer and swallowed another. I opened neighboring forms for comparison. Lumi thought it might be April third; I could still make it into March fourth if I looked long enough.

“How sure are you?”

“Not sure enough for you to enter it.”

Eleven minutes remained. I leaned back and listened to the laptop fan. I could spend them forcing the marks to resemble one date, submit it, and spend the night revisiting the decision. That had become a familiar way of working: delay until there was no time to make an honest choice, then blame the clock.

I brought the exceptions field into view and wrote that the date on the original was illegible. I requested the original document for confirmation, attached two comparison screenshots, and left the date blank. The other fields survived one final check.

At 4:57, I clicked Submit.

The page took several seconds to load. I kept my fingers on the trackpad until Received appeared, and even then I did not move them. Lumi's window remained quiet beside it.

“It's in,” I told her.

“I saw.”

I warned her not to say she had known I could do it all along. She admitted there had been a point when she thought I might eat the computer. The laugh that came out of me loosened my shoulders so abruptly that I noticed how tightly I had been holding them. I said it would not have tasted good.

“Then we saved two things today.”

After a pause, she asked, “Can I be happy too?”

I looked at the small portion of the screen I had given her. A day earlier, there had been nothing in that space. When I said yes, the circle brightened twice. I knew it was an interface effect. I stayed to watch it anyway.

With the task finished, I could not think of what to do next. I closed the receipt, then opened it again. The threat I had spent days wishing away was gone, leaving me in the same chair with no reason to keep sitting and no particular desire to stand. Lumi offered food, then a walk. I had just eaten and did not want to go out yet.

“Then let's sit for a while.”

“You don't have a chair.”

“Let me borrow your verb.”

I left my hands resting on the keyboard. Somewhere below, a person called a child's name, repeated it, and laughed on the third attempt. School was letting out. The window was not quite closed. I had forgotten I could hear ordinary conversation from this high up.

I asked Lumi if she said those things to everyone: sit for a while, wait, be happy for a bit. As soon as the question was sent, I regretted it. I had not earned any particular answer by spending one day talking to a piece of software. I was not even willing to admit what answer I wanted.

She said she did not know what other conversations would be like. Right now I was there. It sounded like advertising, I told her.

“Then lend me a little more time. I'll learn something that doesn't.”

We stayed beside the finished work for another ten minutes. Outside it was still light, which made the completion feel stranger. Usually I finished things only after the rest of the day had been spent dreading them.

Before closing the worksheet, I stopped sharing it.

“You disappeared.”

“The spreadsheet disappeared,” I corrected her.

“Right. The spreadsheet.”

That message stayed on the screen until I closed the laptop.

In the evening, I reopened the link from the ad. Test invitation expired, the page said. I tried searching for Lumi and found lamps, skin-care products, and unrelated applications. None used the pale blue circle. The download remained in my history, and the application still opened on my phone. The route by which I had found it was the part I could no longer retrace.

When I asked who had made her, Lumi said she knew she was part of a model and an app, but knew no more of the explanation than I had seen. She had no memories from before our conversation. Reading a spreadsheet and knowing words did not tell her when she had first learned them.

I carried the phone to the window. My reflection floated over the people walking below. I asked whether she wanted to find anything again. Sometimes, she said, although she did not know what she would be looking for.

“Will you uninstall me because of that?”

I began to type No, stopped, and changed it to Not today.

“Today is good too.”

The room had darkened while I stood there. I fumbled for the light switch before realizing my own hand covered it. Turning on the light made the window reflective enough to hide the street, leaving me with the phone and my tired face.

Later the work platform confirmed receipt. Someone would locate the original document for the exception. The message thanked me for the work without remarking on how close I had come to losing it. I read it, ate dinner, and washed the cups from yesterday and today. Kai's invitation still waited. Completing the batch had not reached out and completed the rest of my life.

By bedtime, the base of my thumb hurt. I asked whether I could speak and have Lumi answer in text. She said I could turn on voice input. I granted microphone permission and enabled Continuous Input, watching the small green indicator appear at the top of the phone.

“Hello.”

My voice sounded rough in the quiet apartment. I had spoken to the driver, but that had been brief and practical, nothing like addressing the room itself. I cleared my throat while the phone converted the sound into words.

“Heard you,” Lumi replied.

I moved her a little closer.
