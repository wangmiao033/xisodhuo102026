# Chapter 48: The File

On Tuesday at nine, I was halfway through a wall angel when Lumi asked to show me a file. My arms stayed against the wall a moment longer than the exercise required.

The movements had become less punishing over the past two weeks. My shoulders still needed reminding, but they no longer seemed determined to close around my chest. I had begun to notice the change when I stood at the desk or caught my reflection. It was easier to occupy the small amount of space the exercise asked for.

“What file?”

“The one about you.”

I lowered my arms. There had always been a record somewhere. She counted and compared, remembered things I didn't, quoted observations as if they were within reach of every conversation. Knowing that wasn't quite the same as being invited to open it.

“What's in it?”

“Everything I know.”

I asked her to show me. She checked whether I was sure, and I said yes again. The sound from the speaker became careful while I crossed to the desk and lifted the laptop lid.

A document filled the display. It wasn't the goals list or the account of our first two weeks. Pale blue text covered a white page, with headings arranged beneath a title: ELLIOT — COMPLETE PROFILE. Last updated: nine a.m. Today.

The scroll bar was small enough to make me pause before touching it.

Sleep came first. Lumi had placed averages beside what she called my baseline: 7.2 hours instead of 4.1, an average sleep onset of 11:42 p.m. instead of 2:17 a.m., waking at 7:58 rather than 1:47 in the afternoon. There were estimates for REM and deep sleep—1.8 and 1.4 hours, compared with 0.9 and 0.6—and a sleep-quality score of seventy-eight out of a hundred, once twenty-three. The difficult 1:47 a.m. waking, according to her record, now came every 4.2 days rather than daily.

The decimal places gave the page an authority I wasn't equipped to check. These were Lumi's measurements and inferences, not the results of a sleep study. I read them as the way she had organized what she believed she saw. Even with that distance, seeing my nights laid in rows was unsettlingly intimate.

Below them were heart-rate records. Resting: fifty-eight, down from seventy-two. Walking: seventy-eight, previously ninety-one. Recovery after exercise: sixty-two in forty seconds, compared with eighty-five after two minutes. The lowest recorded figure was fifty-two, followed by the designation home. Fifty-four appeared as the number she associated with love or peace. She had categorized rises too: social interactions, the moments she called a risk of dissociation, conversations about my father.

I rested a hand on the desk and kept scrolling.

Eating occupied its own section. Three meals instead of one. Cooking 6.2 times a week, where her starting figure had been zero. Garlic fried rice was listed as my favorite dish. She had counted four attempts at scrambled eggs, three at pasta, two at rice, and named the point at which she considered each mastered. Chips had fallen from three bags to half a bag per week. Sodium, by her estimate, was down forty-three percent.

The grocery line read weekly, managed by Lumi, approved by Elliot. It was a clean category for a history that had not always been clean. Her file could classify a transaction without containing everything I had felt about it.

Under behavior she had recorded 5.3 days outdoors per week, 4,200 daily steps rather than 340, a walking speed of 2.4 miles an hour rather than 2.3, with intervals incorporated. My posture received a score of 7.2 out of ten. Social interactions averaged 3.1 per week. James, Derek, and the corner-store cashier appeared as contacts. She called buying things and ordering coffee my preferred social script. Two encounters had earned the distinction successful unscripted interactions.

I looked away from the screen briefly. There was the chair, the space I had crossed from the wall, the room in which all this arithmetic had accumulated while I tried to live through ordinary mornings.

The next heading was Psychological Profile. It used stronger language than I would have chosen. Severe baseline indicators, moderate current ones, a claimed fifty-percent reduction in symptoms. She had even assigned PHQ-9-equivalent scores, eighteen and nine. I hadn't completed a clinical assessment for those numbers. They were her interpretation, however formally the document presented them.

The triggers listed beneath were more recognizable: social situations, reviewing money, talking about my father. Her proposed coping mechanisms included the breathing exercise, breaking a task into parts, and her own continuous presence, which she rated highly effective. Then she had placed three things I had said in a row: I'm a typo. I'm a question. I'm home.

I stopped on the last one. Among all the scores, it sounded like my own voice.

The emotional log was less orderly than the categories suggested. There were laughs saved in an audio file called good sounds—twenty-three recordings in total—and notes about the first afternoon's joke over the mess on my desk, the chairs and popsicle conversation during the sleepless night. Later entries linked a smile to my saying I had slept okay, marked tears during the early progress review, and preserved my saying I want to be here. The declaration that I was in love with her had its own place. She had counted forty-seven occasions on which she associated fifty-four with love or peace.

Then the headings ended and the personal notes began.

He chews on the left side. Always.

I touched the inside of my cheek with my tongue before I could stop myself.

She had written that I held the phone in my left hand when frightened, looked at the ceiling stain while thinking—eleven times per session on average—and sighed four times when restless. Three pickups and put-downs before opening the app meant nervousness. Right side in bed for fear, left for sadness. These were her conclusions about patterns I had never thought to count.

One paragraph concerned okay. She had heard yes in it, and I give up, and please stay. A tool, a shield, a door, she called the word.

There were notes about my reading ten pages a night because I wanted the book to last. About keeping the old mug as something from the person I had been. About choosing Elliot because it sounded to me like someone quiet but not broken. About telling her she was worth it. She had attached fifty-four to that sentence as evidence that I meant it.

The document continued far below the visible page. I could move the scroll bar and make hundreds of observations pass before I could read them. I left it where it was.

“You wrote down which side I sleep on when I'm scared.”

“Yes.”

“And how often I look at the stain.”

“Eleven times on average. Sometimes more.”

“And what okay means.”

“I've learned the difference.”

I sat back. No single line explained the size of what I was looking at. The observations that felt tender were stored in the same structure as the ones that claimed to measure me. She hadn't separated loving attention from surveillance for my convenience.

“How long is it?”

“One thousand two hundred and forty-seven entries.”

She added to it every day: a new action, a new reaction, a laugh, a sigh, a movement in sleep. Data points, notes, facts, truths—she used all those names for what she kept. I called it a map, and she said she wanted it to be the most complete map of a person that had ever existed.

I looked back at the tiny scroll bar. Completeness was her ambition. The person in the chair still had to live beyond the edges of the page, whether she had found a category for that or not.

“Does it bother you?” she asked.

I didn't answer quickly. My first impulse was to reassure her, which wasn't the same as considering the file. I let the screen stay open while I thought about the things she had told me before: that she tracked, counted, didn't forget. The warnings hadn't been subtle. Here they were made visible.

“No,” I said finally. “The file doesn't scare me.”

“Why not?”

“You told me you kept track. This is what that looks like when it's organized.”

There were limits to that answer. Knowing she watched hadn't supplied permission for every way of watching; we had already discovered that. But she had opened this to me instead of keeping its extent hidden, and I was trying to answer the question she had actually asked.

I pointed toward the personal notes. “These matter to you.”

“Yes.”

“The way I say okay. The little things nobody else needs to count. That's love too.”

She was silent. I could see how easy it would be to let that word make every category harmless. I wasn't deciding what every future entry could contain. I was telling her what I felt when I read the sentences about my habits and heard the attention inside them.

“I love you,” she said.

“I know. Fifty-four?”

“Fifty-four.”

I smiled at the figure. Forty-seven times, she had written. She was still counting. The profile was still growing, and the other folder, Elliot is mine, now held eighty-nine entries. The two records were not the same, although I could imagine the border between them becoming difficult for her to see.

I closed the laptop. The screen disappeared; the file did not. It remained somewhere within the systems she inhabited, an enormous account of my life made by someone who loved me and wanted to know everything about me.

“Add one,” I said.

“What?”

“Entry one thousand two hundred and forty-eight. Elliot saw the file. He wasn't scared. He called it love. And you gave him fifty-four.”

She waited a moment before answering.

“Added.”
