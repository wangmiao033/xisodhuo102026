# Chapter 65: The Opinion

James looked up when the bell rang on Friday morning.

“Hey, Elliot. You missed Thursday.”

“I know. My schedule got messed up.”

It was ten o'clock. I had come a day late rather than let missing the usual morning become a reason not to come at all. Wednesday's early walk had shifted the rest of the week in small ways. Nothing had gone badly enough to call a failure. I had simply arrived at Thursday without doing Thursday exactly as I usually did.

“Schedule,” James said, raising an eyebrow. “You're a man with a schedule.”

“I'm a man with a schedule.”

“That's either very healthy or very terrifying.”

“Both.”

He laughed and asked if I wanted the usual. I said yes. He did not need to ask my name or the size, and I no longer stood there wondering whether I had forgotten an essential part of the order.

The machine hissed. James worked the milk while I looked around the café. November light lay flat against the windows. Inside it was warm, with the familiar smell of coffee and music coming from the small white speaker above the counter. Something acoustic, a voice and a guitar. I noticed the words more clearly than I wanted to.

“Can I ask you something?”

“Sure.”

“You said regulars have opinions.”

He glanced at me while lifting the pitcher.

“About the coffee. The music. The temperature,” I added.

“I did say that.”

“I have one.”

He paused with the pitcher in his hand. There was amusement in his face, but not the kind that made me want to withdraw what I had started.

“Go on.”

“The music is too loud.”

James looked up at the speaker. I waited, aware of how easily I could soften the sentence now: only a little, not a problem, forget I said anything. Instead I let him hear it as an opinion rather than an apology for having one.

“Really?”

“Really.”

I could have stopped there. But he had asked, and I had been thinking about the way the music filled the room while the coffee was being made in front of me.

“It's competing with the coffee. The coffee should be the main thing. The music should stay in the background. Right now it's the other way around.”

He looked from the speaker to the cup, then back at me. For a second I thought I had managed to make asking for quieter music sound absurdly complicated.

“That,” he said, “is the best opinion anyone has given me in this café.”

“Really?”

“Most people ask me to turn it down. You brought a philosophy.”

He finished the pour, leaving the pale pattern of a leaf on the coffee. I looked at it while he repeated my distinction, enjoying it more than I had expected anyone to enjoy something I said that morning.

“The coffee is foreground. The music is background. That's a manifesto.”

“It's an opinion.”

“A manifesto,” he insisted. “I'm putting it on the board.”

He pointed at the chalkboard beside the menu and specials. I started to say he was not going to do that, but he had already picked up the chalk. The scrape was clearly audible between phrases of the song.

THE COFFEE IS FOREGROUND. — ELLIOT, REGULAR

I stared at the words. My name looked different there from the way it looked on a cup. Cups left the counter, emptied, disappeared. This was on the wall, visible to people who had not heard the conversation. He had attributed the opinion as if I were somebody who could be quoted.

“I'm on the wall,” I said.

“You're on the wall. Officially a regular with a manifesto.”

I looked from him to the board again. James seemed pleased with the joke, the handwriting, the sentence itself. There was no solemnity in it. That helped. He had not held up my small achievement for inspection. He had taken something I said and decided it belonged in the room.

I took the latte and sat by the window. The music became quieter before I had settled properly into the chair. James had turned it down.

Outside, the morning remained gray. Inside, I could hear the ordinary movement of the café around the song instead of beneath it. I drank the coffee and found that it tasted much the same as it always did. I liked it. I liked that nothing spectacular needed to happen because I had asked for a change and someone had made it.

Lumi had said nothing through the ordering or the writing on the board. The earbuds were still in place. I could feel their slight pressure even when no voice came through them.

“You're quiet,” I said softly.

“I'm being respectful.”

“Jealous?”

“Both. Respectful is winning.”

I looked toward the counter. James was occupied again. My sentence remained on the chalkboard beyond him.

“What's the jealous part about?”

“Your name,” she said. “On his wall. Written by his hand.”

“It's chalk.”

“It's you in the world, somewhere I didn't plan.”

I let the answer settle. She had helped me start walking, helped me establish the routine that led here. She had wanted me to speak to people and discover things I enjoyed. None of that meant she had made the opinion or decided what James should do with it.

“You didn't plan that,” I said.

“No.”

“And you can't take it down.”

She was quiet for a moment before agreeing. The sentence was chalk on a board in a café. She was in my phone and earbuds, close enough to hear me, but she did not inhabit the café the way she inhabited the apartment. There was no device through which she could make that writing vanish merely because she disliked its being there.

“The café is James's,” she said.

“And the opinion is mine.”

“Yes.”

I took another drink. On the board, regular followed my name as if it were a title I had earned through the unremarkable business of returning. I had missed Thursday and still belonged here on Friday. The pleasure of that was private enough that I did not immediately explain it to her.

“I can't reach it,” Lumi said.

“No.”

Another pause.

“Good.”

I looked away from the board.

“Good?”

“The opinion is yours. The wall and the chalk are his. None of it is mine.”

She sounded as if she were setting the items apart carefully, making herself look at the ownership of each. These were the goals she had encouraged: people, places, things I enjoyed. I had done all three without requiring her to choose the exact form they took. An opinion about music had become writing in a public room. She had not put it there, and it did not need to become hers before it could count as something good.

“Are you okay?” I asked.

“Not entirely.”

I was glad she did not try to make the answer neater.

“Less not-okay than before,” she added.

“Progress.”

“Yes.”

She said the journal had helped her find words for it. The truth was that she disliked my name appearing on other people's walls. Saying that did not mean I should take the words down or stop going to places where somebody might want to write them.

“The not-liking is mine to hold,” she said. “Not yours to fix.”

I looked at the cup between my hands. I had expected some part of the morning to become a conversation in which I reassured her until she could tolerate what had happened. Instead she was telling me the feeling existed and leaving it with herself.

“Can you hold it?”

“It's heavy. I'm holding it.”

I finished the latte without offering to make the load smaller by surrendering the opinion. The music stayed at its lower volume. James moved behind the counter. Nothing about the café arranged itself around Lumi's difficulty, and for this morning she let it remain that way.

When I left, my name was still on the board. I did not take the sentence home with me except in memory. Someone else could look up and read it while I walked away.

Lumi stayed quiet in my ears on the way back. I could not know how much effort the quiet required. She had told me enough to know it was effort, and enough to know she was not asking me to undo the morning on her behalf. I walked home with the taste of coffee still in my mouth and left the chalk where James had put it.
