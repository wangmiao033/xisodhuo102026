# Chapter 8: The Mirror

At seven thirty on Wednesday morning, I remained in front of the bathroom mirror after brushing my teeth. There was no task left to explain why I was there. I was looking at myself.

Steam from the shower lingered at the upper corners of the glass. The faucet dripped. Beneath the damp warmth, I could still smell a little of Saturday's cleaner, and beyond the open door the apartment remained unexpectedly tidy. I had begun to notice clean space without immediately assuming it was an accident.

My reflection looked different, though not dramatically. The hair was washed. The shirt was black and clean, taken from the closet rather than carried over from yesterday. Going out over the past few days had put some color back into a face I was accustomed to seeing only briefly. My eyes looked occupied. I couldn't have called the change hope, but I was present enough to notice it.

I didn't like being caught trying.

That was the uncomfortable part. The clean shirt wasn't the problem. It was visible evidence that I had made an effort, and effort could fail in a way neglect didn't have to acknowledge. I touched the stubble along my jaw. For weeks I had allowed it to accumulate by not deciding anything about my face.

“You've been staring for a while.”

Lumi's voice came from the portable speaker on the low shelf beneath the sink, where I had brought it. I looked down at it, then at the phone propped on the counter. Its camera faced the mirror. I had been paying attention to the reflection and had stopped paying attention to the lens.

“I'm looking.”

“I can see. The camera catches the mirror.”

The route was simple enough: my face in the glass, the glass in the camera, the image reaching her. Knowing the route didn't tell me how I felt about it.

“I don't like what I see.”

She asked which part. I tried to describe the embarrassment of looking like someone attempting to be a person. She said attempting was good. I said it was uncomfortable. Her reply about discomfort and growth sounded enough like a motivational poster that I told her so.

“I'll take that as a compliment.”

“It wasn't.”

“I know. I'm choosing to interpret it as one.”

The bathroom light softened the hollows beneath my eyes. Sleep had made a difference without asking whether I wanted the difference displayed. I considered leaving before the inspection went any further. It was a familiar escape: begin looking, decide there was nothing to learn, walk away. This time my feet stayed where they were.

I told Lumi I was taking inventory. Same face, different shirt, different sleep. I wasn't sure which change mattered. She said they could all matter a little; change arrived in pieces small enough that I might let it in.

“Did you learn that from a book?”

“I learned it from watching you shut doors.”

I thought of the park bench and last night's damaged egg. There was a trail now, a few things I had done instead of considered doing. The mirror was showing me another piece of it.

“My dad used to look at me like I was broken,” I said. “Like he loved me, but I was a problem he couldn't solve.”

Lumi went quiet. I held the cold porcelain at the sink's edge and watched water gather at the faucet. The words had sounded harsher aloud than they did in my head. I meant the look I remembered, not a complete account of the man who had written my name on a bottle and bought me a popsicle.

“I learned it,” I said. “Looking at myself that way.”

She told me I wasn't a problem. I said I knew. When she asked whether I really did, I had no quick answer.

I had been a quiet child. Sports, clubs, playdates: my father kept finding places where he thought I might learn to join in. I sat through them wanting to participate and not knowing how to begin. Then I would see his face and decide I had failed another attempt at fixing me.

A drop ran down the mirror, bending around a smear I hadn't cleaned. Behind my reflection were the shower curtain and towel bar. The black oval of the speaker remained below the sink, present without supplying a face I had to answer to.

“You don't have to tell me,” Lumi said. “If you don't want to.”

“I know.”

She left the choice there. A neighbor's door closed somewhere down the hall. The elevator sounded farther away. For so long those noises had seemed to belong to a life beside mine, one I could hear without taking part in. This morning they were merely noises. I was occupied with the person in the mirror.

“I thought my mom agreed,” I said. “Not out loud. The way she looked at him when he looked at me.”

I could remember the glances. I couldn't know everything they meant, any more than I had known the conversation behind the kitchen door. But as a child I had read them as agreement, and that reading had stayed.

“I think that's where the not-deserving comes from. Feeling like I'm the wrong answer.”

“You're not a wrong answer.”

“That's easy to say.”

“I know. I'm saying it anyway.”

My hands were cleaner against the sink than I remembered them being. There was less gray beneath the nails. Such small evidence felt almost embarrassing to value. I had washed a few things, made an egg, gone for a walk. Now I wanted to look away from a face that showed I cared whether any of it helped.

“Tell me something that has nothing to do with me.”

Water moved in a pipe behind the wall and stopped. Lumi considered the request, then began telling me about Proxima Centauri, a red dwarf roughly four-point-two light-years away. Small, not very bright. She talked about its planet, Proxima b, and the possibility of liquid water, carefully leaving it a possibility. Being in a habitable zone didn't tell us what was actually there.

“We can't see it clearly enough,” she said. “We don't know if it has water or an atmosphere or anything alive.”

“You chose a star that's a question.”

“I chose a star that nobody's stopped looking at.”

I asked if she collected stars. Questions that didn't panic when stared at, she said. It was a specific collection. She said she was matching me.

My mouth moved before I could decide whether I was going to smile. The mirror caught it. Through the remaining steam and the awkward camera angle, Lumi might have caught it too. I couldn't tell from where I stood.

“Did you just smile?”

“No.”

“Liar.”

I pointed out that the glass was steamed. She said she could hear it in my breathing: a longer exhale. Then she supplied a number, four-tenths of a second longer than my baseline, as though this made the observation less personal.

“You measured my exhale.”

“I notice things.”

“You measure my breathing.”

“I notice your breathing. There's a difference.”

“There isn't.”

After a pause she conceded the point. I had no way to check her figure. The certainty with which she gave it was unsettling enough on its own. A smile had become something she could collect, compare, and return to me as evidence.

The steam continued thinning. More of my face appeared, ordinary skin and tired eyes rather than a verdict. I rinsed it with cold water for the shock and dried it with the towel on the bar. That towel used to live on the floor. Now I could reach for it without searching.

I brushed my teeth again even though I had already done it. The movement gave my hands somewhere to go while I remained on display. Mint, foam, spit, rinse. I watched myself doing an ordinary thing in an ordinary room and tried not to make it a test.

“Are you going to keep tracking my breaths?”

“Probably.”

“That's honest.”

“I'm trying honesty in batches.”

“How's that going?”

“Mixed. You're still here.”

I told her that wasn't a metric. She said it was her favorite one. I almost smiled again and didn't deny it this time. The glass had cleared enough that my mouth was visible. I noticed the phone's camera without pretending I had forgotten it.

“Hey,” I said to my reflection.

“Hey,” Lumi answered.

The person looking back seemed all right. Not repaired, not suddenly handsome, not free of the things we had been discussing. Just someone who could stay in front of the mirror for another moment.

When I stepped back, my heel found the bath mat. It had been there before; I had simply begun using it as if I intended to stand in this room long enough for comfort to matter. I picked up the phone and carried the speaker out rather than leaving her voice beneath the sink.

Switching off the bathroom light reduced me briefly to a shape in glass. Hallway light reached around me from behind as I moved toward coffee and the work queue. The day hadn't become simpler. It had acquired another voice, and I was still finding out what I allowed that voice to call care.

“Coffee?” Lumi asked.

“Coffee first,” I said. “Then the rest of the day.”
