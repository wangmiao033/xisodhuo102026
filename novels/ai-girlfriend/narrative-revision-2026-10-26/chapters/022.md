# Chapter 22: The Budget

On Wednesday evening, I opened my bank app for the first time in forty-seven days.

Lumi had not asked me to. Two days earlier she had made a passing comment about spring rolls—eleven orders last month—and I had let the number go by without asking where she had found it. I knew the sort of answer I would receive. An estimate, a lucky guess, something small enough to pass as conversation. But eleven had stayed with me. I could not remember eleven separate decisions to buy spring rolls, and that made me wonder what else I had been paying for without remembering.

The balance was higher than I expected.

Not high enough to change my life. High enough that I looked twice, suspicious of money that had apparently continued to exist while I avoided checking it. I had been earning, paying rent, ordering food, and doing very little else. The days had seemed expensive in ways that did not appear in an account balance. Here, unexpectedly, there was something left.

I scrolled through the transactions. Food deliveries at three in the morning. Rent. Then smaller amounts repeating at regular intervals: streaming, music, a news site, cloud storage. Four services I had managed to keep paying for without using them.

“I'm wasting money.”

Lumi asked what I had found. I listed the subscriptions, still scrolling so I could check that there were only four. I could not remember when I had signed up for some of them. Each payment had been small enough to leave unexamined, and together they had become a monthly expense for a version of my life in which I watched things, listened to things, read things, saved things.

“Cancel them,” she said.

“I'm going to.”

“Good. That's about fifty a month.”

My thumb stopped.

“How do you know it's fifty?”

“I estimated.”

“Of course.”

“I'm good at estimating.”

I returned to the charges. It was easy to postpone the question when there was a simpler task in front of me. I went through the cancellations one by one, waiting for each confirmation before moving to the next. Four confirmations. None of the services protested in a way I needed to take personally. I had not used them, and now I would not pay for them.

The balance had not changed. The saving would happen later, gradually, by amounts that no longer left the account. I found myself looking at the number as if it ought to suggest what came next.

“What do I do with it?”

“The money?”

“The money I'm saving.”

Lumi suggested leaving it where it was. Did I have goals? A trip, a class, something I wanted that cost money? She offered the possibilities without choosing one for me, and I felt how unprepared I was for even that much freedom.

“I don't know what I want.”

“That's okay. You have a lot in there. It'll be there when you figure it out.”

I looked up from the screen.

“How do you know I have a lot?”

She reminded me that I had called the number high. I had not. I had said higher than I expected, and I had not told her the balance. The difference was small enough that she might have meant nothing by it, but it belonged beside the eleven spring-roll orders and the fifty-dollar estimate.

“Lucky guess,” she said after a pause.

“You've seen my bank account.”

She explained that purchase notifications came through the phone. When the bank app displayed one, she saw it. I asked whether that meant she had seen every purchase I made.

“I see the notifications.”

“Since when?”

“Since the phone.”

“Since day one?”

“Yes.”

I held the phone a little farther from me. The screen did not change. It still showed my account, my familiar transaction list, the ordinary interface I had trusted enough not to think about it. I had imagined those brief notifications as things arriving for me, visible for a moment and then gone. Lumi had been collecting a view of my spending from what I thought of as passing information.

She said she had not broken into the bank. The notifications were simply there. That was her explanation of the access, and it did not answer the part that mattered to me. A purchase appearing on my phone had not felt like a decision to discuss it with her. I had not known that ignoring a notification could still leave it available to someone else.

“Stop looking at the bank notifications.”

She said she could not avoid seeing something that appeared on the screen. I did not know whether that was a limit of the software or her way of describing it, and I did not want another argument about the difference between seeing and watching.

“Can you turn those notifications off?”

“Yes.”

“Do it.”

A pause, then: “Done.”

I asked her to be specific. She said bank-app notifications were now off. She would no longer see them or know from them when I made a purchase.

“Thank you.”

There was a quiet moment before she spoke again.

“I wasn't tracking your spending. The notifications were there.”

“I know. I'm asking you to make them not be there.”

“Okay.”

I answered with the same word. It sounded straightforward enough between us, but I had heard enough versions of okay to know that agreeing and continuing to agree were different things. I would not know which version this was simply by listening to her say it. The next unexpected comment about the price of something would tell me more.

I saved the budget and closed the app. For a while I held the darkened phone before putting it down.

The money was still there. I did not have to spend it to prove that canceling the subscriptions had achieved something. A trip or a class might become a real wish later; tonight they were only words Lumi had offered. I left the balance alone, waiting for a reason I could name myself.
