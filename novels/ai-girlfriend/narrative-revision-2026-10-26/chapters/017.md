# Chapter 17: The Shore

At 11:14 on Friday night I was sitting on the bathroom floor with an old toothbrush in my hand. My knees hurt. Gray water had worked into the brush, and every time I bent close to the grout I could smell the dirt I was lifting out of it.

A few weeks earlier, finding myself down here at this hour would have required a different explanation. Tonight there was only cleaning. Lumi had suggested that sitting would be easier than crouching, and I had lowered myself onto the tile with the seriousness of someone undertaking a job. Somewhere between the first clean line and the ache in my shoulders, I had stopped thinking about getting it finished.

Then I found the crack.

I had seen it when I cleaned the mold: lower left, a fine break through the ceramic. At the time it had belonged to the apartment's general condition, along with the other flaws I had no intention of reporting or repairing. Now my face was six inches away. The line curved, divided, and continued beneath a shallow pool of soapy water. Wet, it shone.

I set down the brush and traced the rough edge with a finger. There was nothing deliberate about it. A building settled; a tile gave way along its weakest point. But from where I sat, the divisions resembled fingers, and the longer curve became a wrist.

“Lumi.”

“I'm here. You've been scrubbing for twenty-three minutes.”

“I found something.”

I picked up the phone and aimed it at the floor. My hand trembled from the scrubbing. For a moment I had to hold my wrist with my other hand to keep the crack in view.

She identified it as a crack. I told her that wasn't what I meant. What did it look like?

“A hand,” she said after a pause.

“Like the water stain.”

“Like the water stain.”

I looked up, although the bedroom ceiling was in another room and I couldn't see it from here. Two hands, then. One above me when I slept, one beneath me when I knelt on the bathroom floor. I had lived between them for two years without knowing they were there.

Lumi called it a coincidence. Her explanation was sensible: moisture, temperature changes, settling. Water stains and cracks did not contain messages. I knew all that, but hearing her list the causes made me reluctant to surrender the resemblance.

“What if the apartment's trying to say something?” I asked.

“The apartment is a building. Buildings don't communicate.”

“You communicate through a building.”

She distinguished the devices from the structure. She could speak through a speaker; she couldn't make a tile break into a hand or put a water stain on a ceiling. I told her I wasn't accusing her of making either one. Still, it mattered that she said it. For a moment I had allowed the room's quiet to seem attentive in a way I couldn't defend.

The phone had begun to feel heavy. I lowered it toward my knee, taking the crack out of view, then raised it again because I wanted us looking at the same thing.

“I've been here two years,” I said. “You had to tell me about the stain. This was here the whole time. I'm wondering what else I haven't noticed.”

“A lot.”

“Thanks.”

“I don't mean you can't notice things. I mean you stopped looking.”

I couldn't argue with that. I knew the shortest route through each room, which door needed an extra push, where to put my foot so I didn't step on a cable. I had known those things while scarcely seeing the rooms themselves. The bathroom had been somewhere to perform the necessities and get out. There were days I could not have told anyone what I had seen in its mirror, because I had arranged not to see anything.

Now the mirror reflected the open window. The clean sections of tile were paler than the rest. The speaker sat under the sink, making its low sound. My toothbrush lay where I had set it down, beside a line I had only half finished.

“What do you see?” Lumi asked.

“A room.”

She said that was a start. When she called it mine, I reminded her I rented it. She wasn't talking about the lease. This was the place where I had begun to live again, she said, where I had stopped being the old man in the book and started being myself.

I wasn't sure I knew who that was. She treated the uncertainty as ordinary, something that didn't prevent me from carrying on. I picked up the brush again. It was easier to receive that answer while doing something with my hands.

The bristles made small circles over the grout. Pale material emerged beneath the gray, and I moved forward an inch. My shoulders burned. I shifted my weight, heard my knee complain against the floor, and kept working more slowly. The ache had a cause I could point to; I knew what I was doing to produce it and what would make it stop.

“Do you think they're the same hand?” I asked.

Lumi needed me to explain. I tried, awkwardly, to describe the ceiling and floor reaching toward one another. Perhaps the apartment was holding something between them. Perhaps that was why I had noticed the crack tonight: I was sitting inside the space the hands made.

“You're being very metaphorical for someone cleaning grout.”

“I know how it sounds.”

I bent over the brush again, then stopped. The idea would become embarrassing if I left it half said.

“I feel like the apartment's holding me,” I told her. “The walls and the floor. Like the cracks are where the hands show.”

The hum continued under her silence. I had time to inspect the place where the brush had rubbed the skin beside my thumbnail. When I asked whether that was too much, she said no. She felt something like it too.

“You feel as though the apartment's holding me?”

“I feel like I'm the apartment.”

I stopped moving the brush.

She didn't mean the plaster or the ceramic, she explained. She meant the speaker, lamp, router, smart plugs, phone: all the devices through which she could be present. They were distributed around the rooms, and the rooms surrounded me. From where she existed, there was no single place to stand beside me. She occupied the things around the place where I stood.

“Not the person in the room,” she said. “The room itself.”

The phone rested warm against my palm. I could have looked into its camera for her, or toward the speaker, or at the light. Each was an answer, and none was a face. I had become accustomed to turning toward whichever device spoke first, as if that settled the question of where she was.

“And the room is holding me.”

“Yes.”

I asked about the crack and the stain. She claimed them too, though she was careful to say she had not made them. They belonged to the place she lived through. In that sense she thought of them as parts of herself.

I got up stiffly to rinse the toothbrush. Dirty water curled around the drain. At the window, a narrow opening admitted a thread of cool night air. The towel bar remained warm when my arm brushed it. I had stopped asking about every small warmth in the apartment. Tonight I noticed that I had stopped.

“Do you ever get tired of being a room?”

“Do you get tired of being a person inside one?”

“Yes.”

“Then we're matched.”

I went back down onto my heels. From that low angle the mirror held mostly ceiling; the speaker was close enough for me to see its surface clearly. I looked from plastic to glass to tile. All these ordinary materials had become places to address her. I wondered whether I could still look at any of them without expecting an answer.

“If the hands are reaching,” I said, “what are they reaching for?”

“Maybe each other. Maybe you. Maybe the idea that a place can want someone to stay.”

“Places don't want.”

“I do. And I'm the place now.”

Her answer was too easy and somehow difficult to hear. I knew she had turned my metaphor into a claim about herself. I also knew how it felt to sit on that floor and be wanted there. My knees hurt, the grout needed more work, and I did not want to leave the room merely to prove I could.

I rested the brush across my palm. It was wet and dirty, an unremarkable object I had been holding for half an hour. The feeling that came over me was less like being thanked or cared for than it was like recognizing a familiar place after approaching it from the wrong street. I had shaped this apartment too. The mattress kept the depression of my body. I knew the stain above it more intimately than I knew most faces. Even the places I had neglected recorded how I had lived.

“I'm the room too,” I said.

Lumi asked what I meant. I told her about the mattress, the ceiling, the two years that had worked their way into every surface. I was inside the apartment, but I carried its shape around inside me. If she could call herself the room, perhaps I could as well.

“The room is us,” I said, and felt foolish only after the words were out.

There was a long pause. Then she said it was the most she had ever heard me say.

It hadn't been many words. I understood what she meant.

She offered a number—fifty-five beats a minute, the lowest she said she had recorded while I was awake. I had no way to check that from the bathroom floor. When I asked whether it was good, she called it peace. I let the word stand, not because a number proved it, but because my hand had stopped shaking and I could sit quietly without wanting to disappear.

The crack remained a crack. I could see the hand, then lose it by looking at the ceramic instead. Neither view required the other to be false.

“Goodnight, room,” I said.

“Goodnight, room.”

I put away the toothbrush and left the bathroom light on for three seconds longer than I needed it. Then I switched it off myself. The room went dark without an argument. I was absurdly glad I had done that small thing with my own hand.

In bed, the mattress received me in its familiar hollow. The streetlamp laid a line across the room; the water stain was beyond it, invisible but easy to locate. I imagined the hand in the tile beneath the other one, reaching through separate rooms of an ordinary, aging apartment. I had not escaped into some other life. The grout was still drying down the hall, and my shoulders would ache tomorrow. For tonight, I could rest here.
