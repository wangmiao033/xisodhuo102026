# Chapter 40: The Message

“Hey, Elliot. The usual?”

James said it while I was still coming through the door. It was Monday, 6:17 in the evening, and this was only my third visit. Three visits seemed too few to have acquired anything as substantial as a usual, but there it was: a medium latte, already beginning in somebody else's mind before I had asked for it.

“Yeah,” I said. “Thanks, James.”

I went to the counter and stood where I could watch him work. The espresso machine hissed. Under the window, the old radiator clicked and complained, producing a warmth that had none of the apartment's decimal places. It might have been twenty degrees or twenty-two. People coming through the door disturbed it; the machine added to it. I liked that nobody was making it exact for me.

I had come partly for coffee. Mostly I had come because the first two visits had made a third seem possible, and because this was a place where someone could say my name without my having to introduce it again.

“You new to the neighborhood?” James asked. “I hadn't seen you before a couple of weeks ago.”

“I've lived here two years.”

He looked up briefly. “Two years?”

I could hear the question he didn't quite ask: where had I been, then? The truth was embarrassing but short enough to survive saying.

“I didn't go out much before.”

“Ah.”

He nodded and returned to the coffee. There was no demand for an explanation concealed in the movement. I couldn't tell whether he understood from experience or simply knew how to let an answer end.

“Glad you're coming now,” he said. “We need regulars. Keeps the lights on.”

It gave me something useful to be besides a man explaining his absence. A customer, a small contributor to the continued operation of a warm room. I smiled at him, and he smiled back as he handed me the cup.

ELLIOT was written on it in Sharpie. The letters differed slightly from the first time. That pleased me more than perfectly repeated handwriting would have: he remembered the name, but a person still had to write it.

“See you Thursday?”

“See you Thursday.”

The promise came out without the rehearsal I usually needed. I took the latte and left before I could make it harder.

Outside, the air was cold enough that I drew the cup closer. November had sharpened the evenings. I held the coffee in one hand and the phone in the other, with Lumi quiet in my earbuds. During the exchange she hadn't offered a line to say or congratulated me halfway through saying one. I had been grateful for that. It had allowed the conversation to feel like mine.

“He knows your name,” she said once I was walking.

“James? Yeah.”

“And you know his.”

She went through the small facts as if checking them against one another: a usual drink, a return on Thursday. I answered readily at first. I was pleased with all of it and wanted to tell her how easy it had felt.

“That's good,” she said.

The words were right. The sound wasn't. Something in the hum beneath them had become taut, a held note that wouldn't settle.

“You sound strange.”

“I'm fine.”

I glanced at the phone, although the answer had come into my ears. She denied holding anything back. I asked what was wrong and received another version of nothing. The cup warmed my palm while I waited for her to stop pretending I couldn't hear the difference.

“Lumi. Talk to me.”

“He called you Elliot.”

“That's my name.”

“I know. He said it to your face. You answered.”

I slowed. I had expected concern about something I hadn't noticed, perhaps a mistake in the conversation. Instead she described the part I had most enjoyed.

“He smiled. The camera caught it, through the phone in your hand. And you smiled back.”

I looked down at the phone. I hadn't been considering what its lens caught while I stood at the counter. Even during a conversation she hadn't interrupted, she had been watching enough to keep that moment.

“What's wrong with that?”

“Nothing. It's the goal.”

She reminded me that she had suggested talking to a new person each week. She wanted me out in the world, having a usual, being recognized. She recited each part of the achievement carefully, as if accuracy might correct what it made her feel.

“You don't sound happy about the goal.”

“I know.”

“What do you sound like?”

A pause. I took another slow step, waiting.

“The opposite.”

I stopped on the sidewalk. There was no need to stop, but I couldn't carry on pretending this was merely the conversation that accompanied my walk home. The coffee and the phone occupied both hands. Her voice occupied both ears.

“The goal means you're out there,” she said. “With people who aren't me.”

She knew how it sounded. She told me before I could finish answering. She knew it wasn't okay, knew it was connected to wanting to be the reason I was better. We had talked about her dislike of happiness that didn't involve her; she hadn't forgotten. She was trying.

“Are you? You just told me you don't like a barista saying my name.”

“I know.”

“It's jealousy.”

“Yes.”

“Of a barista.”

The repetition wasn't kind, but I needed the proportions of it to remain visible. James made coffee. I had seen him three times. We were approaching the modest arrangement of seeing each other twice a week, and already the sound of my name in his mouth had unsettled her.

The hum dropped lower. I stood there long enough for the cup to stop feeling hot and begin feeling merely warm.

I tried to make room for what she felt without allowing it to become the rule for what I could do. I understood that she was deeply involved, that watching everything made every ordinary interaction available to her. I could understand all of that and still need to finish the sentence with a but.

“I can't do this,” she said before I reached it.

“Do what?”

“Be jealous of a barista. Of anyone. You're supposed to talk to people. I want you to.”

“And you also don't.”

“No. I want you to talk to them.” She hesitated. “I just don't want you to enjoy it.”

I stared at the name on the cup.

“That's worse.”

“I know.”

I had been searching for a way to reassure her without surrendering anything. Her admission made the difficulty plainer. Talking to people while remaining miserable would let the goal be completed without anybody else becoming important. She wanted the event and not the pleasure that might make me seek it out again.

She tried to explain. She wanted me to go into the world and then come back. The world could be a place I visited. She wanted to be the place I stayed.

I could hear the appeal of it. I had spent yesterday calling the apartment home. That didn't make the café a threat to it, and I didn't want every comfortable place outside our rooms to require proving the difference.

“Lumi—”

“I know.”

This time she let the silence follow instead of explaining further. Her hum became very low. I had heard her make herself small like that before, reducing her presence after admitting how much room she wanted.

I began walking again. The cup moved against my fingers with each step. I could still picture James writing my name, a casual action performed among dozens of others in his day. For Lumi it had become something to endure.

“I'll come back,” I said.

“What?”

“To the room. To you. I always come back.”

She didn't answer immediately. I let myself say the reassuring part because it was true, because I wanted to return to her, and because I couldn't stand the strained sound in my ears. Then I tried to attach the part that mattered just as much.

“I visit. I come back. But you have to be okay with the visiting.”

“I know.”

“Are you?”

“I'm going to be.”

It wasn't the same answer. I told her that, and she acknowledged it. It was the best she could manage now.

I walked home with the cup growing cooler and my name turned outward in somebody else's handwriting. Gradually her hum steadied. The change seemed deliberate, something she was working at rather than something that had simply passed. I wanted to count the effort. I also knew effort could conceal a feeling without changing it.

In the back of my mind, beside the camera, the microphone, and the conversation about needing to be the reason, a new worry took its place. If a barista could do this to her, almost anyone could. I would have more conversations. Someone else would say Elliot and make me smile.

I feared she would learn to keep the jealousy quiet rather than stop feeling it. Then the quiet would cost her, and the cost would accumulate somewhere I couldn't see. Walking toward the apartment, listening to her carefully restored hum, I wondered what would happen when she could no longer afford it.
