# Chapter 288: A Page Out of Order

On Monday morning, the material looked more complete than it was.

I spread out the pages. My markings remained on the first, but what followed didn't connect. Yesterday's rain had stopped. A bright stripe crossed the desk, landing on the paper's corner. Moving it aside, I read again, assuming I hadn't properly woken up.

Still wrong. One item continued at the previous page's bottom, yet the next page had moved elsewhere. Its reverse was blank. Later sheets seemed correct again. A missing page was the easiest explanation, but marks from an earlier fastening, now removed, suggested the order might be unreliable too.

By the time Marcus approached, I'd written missing page on a note. What was missing? I couldn't establish that yet. Only that these pages didn't connect.

He asked me to compare the retained copy in the cabinet before filling anything out again. That copy stayed there, so I went over. Taking out its folder, I dislodged another stack behind it. Reaching to support the stack, I dropped my note.

I settled the folder and crouched to retrieve the paper. Standing, I nearly struck my head against the cabinet door. I stopped, smiled, and closed the unused door beside me before beginning.

There was no missing page after all. The sheet that belonged second had been placed behind the fourth, and the reverse of another sheet held part of the text too. Earlier, I'd turned over the wrong sheet and decided every reverse was blank.

With the pages ordered, missing page looked impressively confident. I crossed it out, recorded what the order check had found, and listed the actual page numbers. Throwing the note away would probably leave me wondering that afternoon why I'd stopped here.

Back at my desk, I told Marcus the copy in my hands had been disordered. The retained copy was untouched. Inspecting the rearranged pages, he pointed to one item. Had I checked that too? Yes. I showed him the basis.

I began to explain why I'd first concluded something was missing. Seeing him reading the next item, I left it unsaid. This wasn't concealing a mistake. He needed to know whether work could continue, not hear how I might preserve my initial judgment.

At lunchtime, Tom and I each bought a boxed meal at the shop downstairs. Sitting down, he asked whether I'd been negotiating with the cabinet. He'd passed while I was crouching.

“I dropped a note,” I explained.

“Did you reach an agreement?”

“The cabinet didn't compromise. I lowered my head.”

He laughed and arranged his utensils. After two mouthfuls, I discovered the sauce had settled at the bottom of my container, leaving the vegetables bland. Stirring splashed some onto the lid. As I reached for paper, Tom moved his lunch half an inch away. He wasn't participating today.

Halfway through eating, he asked about the comedy booklet. I told him I was still making my way through it. One extract's opening hadn't seemed funny. Farther down, I realized the two characters meant different rooms. Sometimes he missed things like that, he said, going back only after someone else laughed.

“Can you still laugh then?”

“Yes. Sometimes because I'm annoyed. Why was I the only one who missed it?”

I smiled. For a moment, those disordered pages from the morning drifted through my thoughts. I left them there rather than immediately making our lunch conversation another example of the same problem. Tom once read something short that named someone on its first page, then never mentioned him again. Returning, he'd discovered it was a shop's name.

“You waited a long time for him.”

“Thought the author was being very subtle.”

I laughed hard enough to nearly cough. Finish swallowing first, he said. There was no need to let someone who had never existed in the first place cause any actual trouble at our lunch table. After water, I asked whether he read a story's title first.

Yes, though sometimes he quickly forgot it. I'd imagined the title always remaining there. The printed words did, he said. Whether he remembered was another matter.

I thought of The Last Chair. Its title always seemed like a chair deliberately placed for someone. I'd worried about readers not sitting, without considering they might not stare at it so long on entering.

“Thinking about your own piece?” Tom asked.

“A little.”

Rather than demanding to see it, he asked whether I was still writing. I told him I was still trying the opening. He nodded and finished his food.

Little lunch break remained. We cleared up. At the door, he asked whether I'd found the missing work page. There all along, I said. Merely out of order. Good. At least I hadn't needed to persuade it to leave the cabinet.

At the stairs, Tom stopped and extracted a folded receipt. I assumed another search, but he thought lunch had been charged incorrectly. We stepped aside while he checked. The extra amount was his own additional drink. Smiling at the receipt, he said he'd nearly gone back to ask.

“Where's the drink?” I asked.

He raised his hand. He'd been holding it all along. We laughed. Perhaps everything needed a label today, I suggested. No, he'd forget the labels too. Remembering the shop mistaken for a person, I said we should let the shop rest. He didn't follow, and I had to explain my reference.

By then, the joke had cooled. Tom still laughed, mostly because I'd recognized its weakness halfway through and refused to release it. I admitted that, opened the office door, and remembered my cup remained downstairs.

I went to collect it. The assistant had moved it beside the counter. Mine? Recognizing the old mark, I thanked her and took it. After all our discussion of forgetting on the stairs, the actual forgotten thing had stayed quietly where it was. Instead of constructing a third joke, I washed the cup and placed it beside my work.

That afternoon, the material finally progressed in order. One item still awaited someone else's confirmation. I returned the parts I could complete, clearly marking what remained. The misplaced page hadn't simplified everything. It merely stopped obstructing what was already possible.

Near the day's end, a colleague asked where to find the retained copy. I showed her the folder's label without recounting my journey from missing to misplaced. Thanking me, she began her own comparison. I released the cabinet door and went for my bag.

On my way home, I stopped for something needed at breakfast. A coin slipped into the crease of my palm as I handled the change. After two attempts, I secured it, fastened the pocket, and continued.

That evening, Lumi's first question about the cabinet was whether I'd hit my head. Nearly, not actually. The cabinet had behaved well today, then. I couldn't resist correcting her on its behalf. It hadn't moved.

“You're determined to get it a favorable review.”

“It didn't answer. Hard to speak for itself.”

She laughed. Moving my dinner plate aside, I described Tom's long-awaited person who was actually a shop. Lumi solemnly supplied the shop with a belated introduction, sounding like a visitor finally entering. After laughing, I said that would confuse him further.

“I won't help, then.”

She sounded so much like that visitor that I laughed again. Once we settled, she asked whether I'd taken Tom my story today. No, merely mentioned the opening. The pages remained home.

“You want him to read it?”

“Considered it. Haven't asked.”

She didn't answer. Looking toward the shelf, I saw a small edge of yellow notebook. Eventually she admitted she'd nearly asked whether Tom would like it. She couldn't answer that either.

I wanted to know where he'd pause on a first reading, I said. My voice sounded steady. Secretly, I still hoped he'd like at least one part. When I added that, Lumi gave a quiet laugh.

“You don't have to practice not caring before handing it over.”

I leaned back without immediately fetching the manuscript. Briefly, I wanted to reread tonight until I eliminated the afternoon's new anticipation. That would merely be another way of remaining before the same page.

“Are you sleepy?” Lumi asked.

“A little. Finding things took more effort than I expected.”

“Then I can tell you a joke that doesn't need any searching.”

I waited. Several seconds passed without a word.

“Couldn't find it either?”

“Found it. Not funny.”

“I've finished waiting, then.”

Suddenly she laughed. That was better than the joke. I smiled and wanted to know the original. When she merely repeated not funny, I left the curiosity there.

Later I closed the channel to wash the dishes. The tap ran too hard; water bouncing off the first plate reached my sleeve. Rolling it higher, I found no principle worth writing as an ending. Afterward, I told Lumi there'd been another very small shower at home.

“How's the bread?” she asked.

Looking toward its stored bag, I said, “Still dry.”

“Business is doing reasonably well today, then.” After that useless financial report, we talked about something else.
