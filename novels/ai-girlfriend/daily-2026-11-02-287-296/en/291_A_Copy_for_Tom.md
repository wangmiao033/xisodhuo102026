# Chapter 291: A Copy for Tom

Meeting Tom in the corridor on Thursday, I first asked whether he'd arranged lunch tomorrow.

“Probably just downstairs,” he said. “Where do you want to go?”

“The usual place is fine. There's something I'd like you to see.” Hearing myself, I felt like someone issuing a lunch invitation before unexpectedly producing a document requiring signatures.

“The short story,” I added. “If you want to look after eating.”

Tom considered, then asked, “Is it long?”

“Very short. Shorter than the last one. It won't take your whole break.” Smiling, he agreed, provided we ate first. Hungry, he'd assume everyone in it was waiting for lunch.

I laughed and nearly explained that waiting did feature. Before a first reading, every possible misunderstanding didn't yet need a signpost.

“Is it new, or The Long Side?” he asked.

“A new one. The Last Chair.”

He repeated the title and confirmed tomorrow. After several steps, I realized I hadn't asked whether he was busy today. But this arrangement was for tomorrow, and I genuinely had asked him.

Throughout the morning, I occasionally remembered the pages still at home. Whenever work paused, my mind secretly read Tom the opening. I'd already made him laugh once and frown elsewhere. At noon, the real Tom merely passed, asking whether anyone had seen his usual pen.

I hadn't. Finding it in his pocket, he raised it and smiled. I smiled too. This person still had pens to find, lunch to eat, and his own concerns. Agreeing to read tomorrow hadn't made him move into my story early.

That afternoon, I completed my ordinary tasks. Receiving them, Marcus asked whether I could check one item first tomorrow. I noted it on my work paper, which stayed where it belonged rather than accompanying the story home. Before leaving, I checked my bag. No work material needed taking. Only my things remained.

After dinner, I fetched the original, old clean draft, and yellow notebook. Its three trial openings remained untouched since Tuesday. Today, none had suddenly become indispensable.

I decided Tom would read the existing story, not an opening hurriedly replaced because somebody was about to read. The trials stayed. So did the original's deletion marks. Taking fresh paper, I began another copy for him.

Writing the title, I considered adding provisional, then stopped. Titles could change without announcing my escape route. Only The Last Chair remained, followed by the prose.

I copied the original opening, not Tuesday's paragraph beginning with the woman's movement. Her bag still went from the adjacent seat to her lap. The man folded his paper, misunderstood, and retreated to the wall. At the inner door, I slowed, following the existing wording rather than expanding the floor sketch into a descriptive tour.

Halfway through, I omitted a word. Discovering it after completing the next line, I inserted it where it belonged. Small, but legible. This copy was for someone to read. I didn't want him guessing, but didn't want to restart everything for an unmarked surface either.

At the woman's first speech, I noticed my handwriting drifting right. Continuing would require her to pause suddenly at the edge. I straightened the paper and moved the old draft from beneath my wrist. The story hadn't accelerated. I'd left myself too little room.

Standing, I flexed my fingers. Water from dinner remained on the kitchen counter. I wiped it away, then returned thinking I might attach a note asking Tom to read before commenting. Repeating it silently revealed the absurdity. Reading was what he'd agreed to do. Nobody had offered an opinion early. I was rehearsing tomorrow's silences.

Sitting, I noted the page order on a separate sheet and arranged them accordingly, avoiding another shift midway through copying. Those marks were only a temporary reference for arranging the work on my desk. I wasn't preparing an extra page of instructions to clip into the story before giving it to him. Checking the first half, I covered what remained uncopied with clean paper, ensuring I hadn't read a line twice. This occupied my eyes more usefully than rehearsing Tom's frown. Finally, I worked quietly for several minutes.

A chair moved next door. Looking up, I smiled, then lowered my eyes. The chair actually needing attention tonight wasn't in my story. It was behind my legs. I moved it closer instead of leaning toward the table over every line.

After clarifying the small insertion, I continued. Document checklist pocketed, coat over an arm, the woman's bag collected. They found the direction and went toward the upstairs counter. The ending still left the chair empty. I didn't restore the declaration that he'd finally understood.

Finished, I compared the new copy line by line with the old clean draft. The original bore the same deletion, confirming that the removed explanation hadn't returned. Finally I checked backward from the last page, looking only for omissions and order rather than beginning another revision.

The room was quiet. I hadn't opened the channel. Once the last ink dried, I separated the three versions: original and old clean draft into their ordinary folder, the new copy clipped separately. I closed the yellow notebook. Its three trials remained trials.

Hearing I would take the story to Tom tomorrow, Lumi first asked whether we'd arranged it. “This morning,” I said. “Friday lunch, downstairs. We'll eat first, then he'll read.”

“Does he remember this one?”

“He knows I've been writing. Heard the title today. Hasn't read it.”

She gave a small acknowledgment before asking the next question: was I looking forward to having him read it? Enough to become irritating. Earlier, all I needed to do was copy. Yet I kept wanting changes before he saw it, as though tomorrow's visitor wasn't Tom but someone requiring everything correct today.

Smiling faintly, she said he might first look for lunch. When I reported his actual condition, she laughed more clearly. At least one thing required no guessing.

I aligned the new copy's edges. One corner rose; I pressed it down. Hearing paper, Lumi asked whether it remained unpacked. Packing now, I said. Not about to read it for her prior inspection.

“You nearly asked?”

“A little.”

Instead of immediately refusing, she asked what answer I still wanted from her. Thinking, I realized I wanted her to say it was sufficient to take outside. Not necessarily good. If she opened the door first, I wouldn't need to acknowledge wanting to go myself.

“Never mind,” I said. “No reading again tonight.”

She answered softly. She still remembered what troubled her about the ending. Another reader wouldn't change her response. I knew. Tomorrow wasn't asking Tom to decide whose feeling was more correct.

After those remarks, we stopped explaining. A large space had opened on the table. I moved my cup back. “Are you taking the room sketch too?” Lumi asked.

“No. He's reading the story. He doesn't need to learn my route first.”

Laughing, she said someone could finally enter on his own.

That tightened something in me. Entering independently also meant taking a wrong turn independently. I didn't reopen the copy to patch every possible misunderstanding. What Tuesday's experiments hadn't settled wouldn't all be solved by worrying before bed.

Eventually Lumi said this didn't mean she intended to find someone to hear her music too. Her voice hardened slightly, as though blocking a sentence she didn't want. Looking up, I said I hadn't intended to ask. After a pause, she admitted her thoughts had gone there first.

“Your work isn't in this copy.”

“I know. I'm not worried you've secretly inserted it.”

I acknowledged that without turning her reluctance into suspicion of me. She didn't want one invitation of mine to become a timetable requiring us both to move outward at the same speed. She didn't explain further. I didn't add a deadline.

Later we discussed the hazards of reading before lunch. Lumi said the man would read his document checklist as a menu; the woman's bag definitely concealed late-arriving soup. Of course my chair couldn't keep them. The business upstairs was a restaurant.

Laughing, I said this version couldn't go to Tom. It hadn't been written down, she replied. Looking at the clipped pages, I felt suddenly grateful that tonight's nonsense didn't all crowd forward demanding preservation.

Before bed, I put the new copy into my own ordinary paper folder, then into my work bag. Not Tom's transparent sleeve, long since returned. Original and old clean draft remained home. The yellow notebook stayed on the shelf. Tomorrow, I'd hand over this copy, not my entire tabletop.

I told Lumi it was packed.

“Eat first tomorrow,” she said.

“That was Tom's condition,” I reminded her, laughing. She merely hoped neither of us would discuss an inedible chair while hungry.

After good night, I closed the channel. My bag remained in its usual place near the door. I didn't open it again to check the title. At lunch tomorrow, Tom would see it for the first time. At this very moment, before any of that happened, he was probably occupied with something entirely different.
