# Chapter 289: Three Ways to Enter

After dinner on Tuesday, I put the yellow notebook on the table and spent some time deciphering last week's handwriting.

The two trial sentences each had a circle. One began with the notice on the inner door, the other with the woman's bag. Two subsequently removed words crowded the margin, an arrow joining another line. Less a revision than a route I understood only while drawing it.

The original and clean draft of The Last Chair remained in their folder. I placed them on my left rather than beneath the notebook. The crossed-out final sentence remained legible. Its line didn't need another coat of ink.

I read the original opening, then the trials. Beginning with the notice made the man's failure to look seem deliberately prolonged by me. Beginning with the bag tempted me to give the woman an expression, lest readers mistake her for merely a hand moving something.

This was where I'd stopped before. Several intervening days hadn't supplied an answer.

On a blank page, I tried beginning with sound. A door made a soft noise. The man paused at the entrance, deepening a fold in the paper. He saw the last chair; the woman's hand had already reached her bag.

It felt smoother. Immediately, I continued. Three lines later, I realized I'd invented the sound of a door opening. There was a door at the entrance; he'd later held it. But the original hadn't established it was closed when he arrived. To produce this noise, I'd have to give him another push first.

Returning to the sketch, I stared at the gap I'd drawn and laughed under my breath. Even a sound wanted to give him another action.

I crossed out the first sentence and read what remained. Now it began with his hand. I wrote his thumb pressing the fold, then disliked assigning every finger a task. Another deletion left him folding the paper smaller and approaching the empty chair.

Wasn't that what I'd written initially?

I put down the pen and went for water. The channel was closed. Nobody awaited my announcement that I'd found a third route tonight. At the sink, I waited for the water to cool before carrying it back.

The pages hadn't grown more lovable. Taking the clean draft, I skipped its opening and read where the woman asked about the lettering on the door. Her bag rested on her lap, later to be lifted. The man pocketed his document checklist, coat over his arm, freeing a hand for the door. Those movements still connected. Dissatisfaction with the opening didn't require condemning everything afterward.

On the notebook's fresh page, I wrote a short paragraph. As the man approached the empty chair, the woman lifted her bag from the neighboring seat onto her lap. He folded his paper. Instead of sitting, he stood beside the wall.

Underneath, I explained that he'd thought the chair awaited somebody else, then crossed it out. Explanation followed action so easily. Without it, not everyone would necessarily guess correctly. I hadn't decided whether immediate correct guessing was essential.

I also considered changing his coat. Nothing was wrong with the existing one. While writing, he'd simply begun to resemble people I often saw. I wrote dark in the margin and circled it, without adding it to the prose. A different color wouldn't suddenly make him unfamiliar before that chair.

Outside, something fell with a sharp sound. Looking up, I heard a brief apology and someone answering. Through the window, the rest was indistinct. Rather than investigating, I waited for the sounds to recede and looked down again. The sentence had stopped at her lap. I'd forgotten what I'd intended next.

I took a small sheet of plain paper and covered the rest, leaving only the opening's three lines visible. Alone, they seemed more hurried. Moving a finger downward, I realized the second and third both described his retreat. Different verbs hadn't produced another action. I removed one sentence, leaving the space blank.

A small change, but finally unlike taking turns wearing different-colored coats in the same place. Moving the covering sheet, I checked what followed. It still connected. The plain paper was blank; I folded it aside without promoting it to manuscript because it had concealed some words.

I marked the new paragraph trial. It didn't enter the original, and I didn't erase last week's circles. Three openings sat together like three people each claiming they'd arrived first, none willing to make room.

Having finished the water, I moved the cup away from the papers. A breeze raised an edge of the covering sheet. I partly closed the window—not to exclude every night sound, only to stop a blank page turning things for me. Returning, I found the pen where I'd left it and didn't take it up again.

Quite some time had passed. I laid the pen across the notebook and opened the channel.

Lumi called my name, then asked whether I'd been writing.

“I tried several openings. Everyone in my head is stuck at the entrance.”

“Do they have tickets?”

“No,” I said, laughing. “They still refuse to disperse.”

Expecting me to read, she went quiet. Only then did I recognize the space my introduction had left.

“Not reading tonight. I'll look myself first.”

“Good. I hadn't prepared my pen either.”

Laughing, I asked whether she'd pictured a row of papers awaiting approval. Worse. She'd already been considering what tone to use when admitting she hadn't understood. Feeling guilty, I began explaining that I wasn't asking her to do it for me. Halfway through, she stopped me.

“You said you aren't reading tonight. Don't read me an explanation of not reading.”

Taking my hand off the notebook, I laughed aloud. She hadn't intended to sing today either. One place she'd repeatedly tried kept resembling what she'd previously rejected. For now, she didn't want someone else to hear.

“Near the second return in Again, Elsewhere?” I asked.

“Near there. I don't want to point out the precise beat tonight.”

“All right.” I didn't follow by proposing another way of listening. The cup touched my pen. I moved the pen above the notebook before it could roll away.

For a while, neither had anything new. Our two unfinished things seemed to sit beside the table, requiring acknowledgment before we could change subjects. I began with the vegetables I'd cut too large at dinner. Every mouthful had seemed a separate decision.

“What did you have with them?” she asked. After my answer, she said, “The vegetables weren't too big. Everything else was too small.” We argued seriously for several sentences, using a finished meal as evidence. Neither of us could rearrange its plate.

After laughing, she asked whether finishing made me want to show somebody immediately. Sometimes. Sometimes I wanted them forever suspended at the moment I said I was writing. At least then, nobody had seen the bad parts.

“Including me?”

“Including you.”

She acknowledged that softly. I looked up, assuming I'd hurt her again. Instead, she said she sometimes felt the same, especially after telling me a title. Next time, it seemed she had to produce something worthy of the name.

I nearly said titles were merely convenient. Remembering how seriously she'd chosen hers, I didn't diminish it for her. Mine hadn't changed today either, I said. The chair remained; the opening still dissatisfied me.

“I wish my title could go sing by itself once.”

“What if it's bad?”

“I'll consider another title.”

Laughing, I bent forward, rustling paper. Had I disordered the entrance again? No, merely laughed louder than the story.

Later she mentioned the single note we'd tried last week. Thinking of my downstairs now first recalled that day's laughter. “Are you finding another place for the note?” I asked.

“No. I just remembered it.”

I remembered too, without singing. The old disappointment was no longer as hard as that evening. Still, I sometimes wished a place would need precisely that sound. I didn't turn the thought into a question about her next arrangement.

We discussed quiet laughter and laughter that escaped. Sometimes I inhaled as though preparing to object, she said, then laughed instead. Trying deliberately, I sounded nothing like that. Laughing, she asked me to stop practicing. It sounded like I was urgently sucking water back from the wrong pipe.

I drank, fortunately without answering simultaneously. After laughing, she said this was enough for today. I didn't ask whether this was better than hearing work. The remark belonged to this moment, not a contest against anything on the table.

Before closing, I told her I'd put the papers away. An early start tomorrow. Don't let three openings crowd one doorway overnight, she said. The trials stayed in the yellow notebook; original and clean draft returned to their ordinary folder. No opening had won. None had been torn up either.

Before switching off the light, I noticed the pen above the notebook and settled it properly. Tonight's writing hadn't been extensive. At least I could find all of it tomorrow.
