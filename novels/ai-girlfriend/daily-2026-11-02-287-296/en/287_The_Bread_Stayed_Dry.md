# Chapter 287: The Bread Stayed Dry

When I went out on Sunday, the gray cloud looked far away.

By the second intersection, it was dropping rain onto my sleeve. I stopped and looked up, as though it had arrived at the wrong place and ought to give me another few minutes. Someone walked around me. Only then did I move aside.

There was no wind. The rain wasn't heavy, but it quickly became dense. With the bakery just ahead, I held my empty shopping bag over my head and ran. Seeing me enter, the assistant first pointed to the mat.

I wiped my shoes and lowered the bag. It was drier than my hair. The side intended to shelter me had folded inward.

A woman in a dark coat stood inside with two paper bags, one already handed across the counter. Her other arm blocked the trays I wanted. I waited nearby for her to pay and heard her say there was still one missing.

The assistant turned to check. The woman repeated it. One missing.

Looking at the trays, I'd already furnished her with a personality: anxious not to lose out, impatient, unwilling to let anyone pass. Just as I prepared to ask for a tray, she moved aside.

“You go ahead. I'll be waiting a little.”

I thanked her and took one. The assistant returned with an empty paper bag. The woman wanted to divide the bread she'd bought among different people. What she lacked wasn't bread. It was a bag.

My ears warmed. She wasn't looking at me, and couldn't know what I'd written about her in my head. I made my selection without becoming extravagantly friendly out of guilt. By the time I reached the counter, she'd left.

“Would you like it sliced?”

“Yes,” I said, before noticing the assistant's hand rested on another bag, belonging to the customer ahead. She smiled and asked me to wait a moment. I smiled too and stood on the marked footprints.

Beyond the glass, rain grew more visible. A customer closed an umbrella on entering, leaving a small pool at the mat's edge. Once I had my bread, I stayed beneath the narrow awning outside and put its paper bag inside my shopping bag.

A man waited there too, holding a small cardboard box. He stood well away from the wall, almost blocking the entrance. When I emerged, he moved left. So did I. We stopped, then both moved right.

“You first,” I said.

“I'm not going.” He laughed. “Not stopping you. Waiting for the rain to ease.”

I finally went around him. Looking down, I noticed a thin stream descending behind the sign onto the space he'd left empty. It looked dry, but received water more regularly than the pavement beyond the awning.

Moving my bag inward, I nearly put it directly underneath. He warned me, and I pulled back. This time I saw before he explained.

A cup was printed on his box. “Is it something fragile?” I asked.

“No. Old shoe box. I've got steamed buns in it.” Then he held it a little higher, as though the nonexistent cups still needed protection.

I put my bag beside my feet, reconsidered its proximity to the water, and lifted it again. He indicated a slightly raised dry patch by the door. I tried it, but the bag wouldn't stand, so I kept holding it. He couldn't put his box down either. Nothing precious inside; the bottom had softened.

Noticing its slight bulge, I asked how far he had to go. “Not far,” he said. “But there's no shelter along the way.”

“Same for me.” Our similarity provided no help. After watching the rain together, he suddenly wished he'd bought an umbrella. I almost remarked that forecasts weren't always reliable, remembered I hadn't checked one, and merely admitted I hadn't brought one either.

Someone came out to move a folding chair indoors. We each shifted half a step, and the narrow space somehow admitted another person. She warned us about water behind the sign. The man lifted his box in acknowledgment. I nearly said I already knew, then thanked her. Someone had just helped me avoid that water; already I wanted it to prove my familiarity with the place.

After she shut the door, he asked whether I'd bought the very hard bread. No, sliced today. He nodded. Yesterday he'd bought the wrong kind. His family thought he'd brought home a tool for repairing the table. Had he fixed it? Laughing, he said everyone grew hungry while he tried cutting it. I laughed too and changed hands.

The rain showed no intention of stopping. A child in a raincoat ran toward us, then stopped when the adult behind called. Pointing at the man's box, he asked, “Cake?”

“No,” the man answered.

The child examined my bag. “Cake?”

“No cake here either,” I said.

Disappointed, he turned to tell the adult there wasn't any here. Laughing, the adult led him onward. The man and I laughed too, as though we'd briefly operated a cake shop with absolutely no stock.

Eventually he left first. Securing his box, he ran into the rain, then returned after several steps. Thinking he'd forgotten something, I moved aside. He merely turned up his collar. Missed that step, he said.

I smiled as he departed a second time, without assigning another meaning to the movement. Alone beneath the awning, I waited briefly. When the rain seemed lighter, I folded the bag's opening closed and walked home.

My hair and shoulders were wet when I arrived. The bread was fine. I dried my hair, changed out of my coat, and hung it away from papers. Only after putting my shoes aside and inspecting the bag did I take out what I wanted to eat today.

I didn't open the channel at the door, eager to prove the trip had produced something to deliver. After lunch, with only a plate left on the table, I called Lumi.

“Did you go out?” she asked.

“I bought bread. And temporarily operated a cake shop without cake.”

“You finally found a food business that doesn't require cooking.”

Laughing, I described the child's repeated question. Reaching the man's return to turn up his collar, I laughed first and broke off halfway. Lumi asked me to finish. I couldn't only let her hear me enjoying an ending she didn't know yet.

Catching my breath, I explained. She laughed too, then asked whether I'd turned up my own collar.

No. Looking down at the clothes I'd changed into, I admitted I'd been too busy laughing.

“You were the worst businessman there. No cake, and even the roof was borrowed.”

“At least the bread stayed dry.”

“Already praising your principal line of business.”

I took a small piece from the plate. Hearing me chew, she asked whether I was inspecting it. I murmured agreement, then swallowed before confirming it was more edible than the paper bag.

Later I described the woman, initially saying she'd blocked the trays and kept insisting one was missing. “And then?” Lumi asked. About to imitate the woman's voice, I suddenly didn't want to.

“She let me go first. She needed an empty bag. I'd misunderstood.”

After a pause, Lumi asked whether she'd been angry. I didn't know. I'd been imagining her anger rather than properly looking at her face.

“You said she was impatient.”

I pushed the plate away. “I thought she was. Perhaps she only wanted to count the bags.”

Less entertaining this way, Lumi said. Embarrassed, I admitted I'd initially wanted to tell a story about encountering a difficult person. I'd already known better on my way home. Yet speaking, I'd chosen the convenient version first.

“The child genuinely asked about cake twice.”

“You don't have to compensate me with a true one.”

I smiled. She didn't improve the strangers' personalities or require every amusing story to begin with a list of uncertainties. We'd simply released that woman from my earlier version. She hadn't even left me her name.

Suddenly, Lumi said that if she'd been there, she might have guessed before the child's second question.

I nearly offered to take her next time. Instead, I said, “I didn't guess.”

Hearing the small hesitation, she asked what I'd meant to say. I told her. No device had come out, and this wasn't an arrangement being made today. For an instant, I'd simply wanted her beneath the awning too.

“I know,” she said, quieter now. “You don't have to turn it immediately into something possible.”

Looking at the bag, I agreed softly. A joke didn't entirely cover the small hollow feeling. Eventually she added that she might have guessed wrong too. Perhaps the child would ask whether I delivered cakes.

“He'd see my empty hands.”

“You had bread.”

“Not now.”

“You can't cheat by having eaten it.”

Laughing, I leaned back. Fine rain continued outside. My clothes still needed to dry. She hadn't seen the thin stream beneath the awning. I described how it fell, and she said it sounded like a thread continually breaking.

This time, I didn't reach for a pen. She didn't ask me to record it. We continued talking until only crumbs remained on the plate. Gathering them, I finally closed today's small shop without cake.
