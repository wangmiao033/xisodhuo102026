# Chapter 292: The Door He Had Used

Before lunch on Friday, I took the paper folder out of my work bag, then pushed it back in.

Tom was clearing his desk. I didn't want to stand there holding a program, waiting for my audience. Yet when he stood, I immediately touched the bag to make sure the thing I'd just put inside remained there.

“Taking all that to lunch?” he asked.

“Only paper.”

“Easier to carry than yesterday, then.”

He collected his coat, and we went downstairs. I didn't ask what he'd carried yesterday. At the place where we ate, he checked the available food before looking for a table. Following him, I nearly suggested a quiet corner. We'd arranged lunch, though. The shop wasn't closing its business for several sheets of paper.

Two places stood against the wall. Tom put his coat beside him and asked whether these would do. Yes. Only after sitting did I fetch the folder.

He looked at it, then at me. Laughing, I returned it to the opening of my bag. I remembered. Food first. Good, he said. Otherwise he'd want to wrap food in every piece of paper in the story.

When lunch arrived, I moved my water to make room. The first mouthful was saltier than expected. I drank without immediately complaining. Steam rose from Tom's meal. Had I spent the week copying? Only last night. Earlier, I'd tried different openings. He'd be getting the existing one, not a last-minute replacement.

“You've started with the copying instructions.”

Laughing, I stopped. He laughed too. Eat first; no need to account for breakfast as well. We discussed portions. He used to think a large bowl guaranteed more food, until discovering some bowls merely had wide mouths. Looking at mine, I suddenly couldn't tell whether its rim was making extravagant claims too.

Tom finished first and moved his empty bowl aside. I ate my last two mouthfuls. After checking that this side of the table was dry, I handed him the folder.

The fresh copy of The Last Chair looked less white than yesterday. Merely the shop's warmer lighting, not something that had happened overnight. Tom read the title, glanced through the written pages, and began.

I lifted my water, discovered the cup wasn't empty at all, and lowered it. I'd intended to open my comedy booklet, but hadn't brought it. Nothing in my bag could make me appear not to be waiting. Halfway through, Tom looked up. I straightened immediately.

“You don't need to keep sitting that upright.”

Laughing, I leaned back, then discovered I was leaning just as deliberately. Someone reading my story could apparently turn an ordinary after-lunch chair into this peculiar place.

He returned to the beginning, resting a finger near the top.

“This door. Is it the one he came through?”

I nearly said no immediately. Seeing him still trace the sentence, I waited for him to point. The door bearing the notice. Initially, he'd imagined the man entering there. When he later approached to read, Tom wondered why he hadn't noticed before.

“No. It's inside the room, toward the service counter. Separate from the entrance.”

“Oh. Went the wrong way.”

He sounded relaxed. I immediately wanted to demonstrate that the writing explained it. Reaching across, I indicated the later mention of the inner door. Tom nodded. He'd found that afterward, but had already built the room in his head.

“I know the room,” I said.

He looked at me. Only then did I hear how useless that was. Naturally I knew it. Copying yesterday, I'd even remembered the ugly sketch inside my yellow notebook.

“I mean, its position has always been there in my head.”

“Not in mine yet.”

Smiling, he lowered the paper. A server asked who'd requested water. Here, I answered, but Tom said he hadn't. It belonged at the table behind us. We both leaned aside to let her deliver it. When Tom lifted the pages again, I wanted to explain the arrow beside the entrance. I stopped. He'd reached that part already. He needn't travel the route I'd just drawn for him again.

He continued, smiling when the man pocketed his checklist.

“Keeps hold of his own paper. Doesn't read the one in front of him.”

“Yes.”

I tried not to make that sound like a teacher accepting the right answer. Tom drank before reading the ending. Outside, somebody pushed a crooked chair back into place. He tested its position with his foot, then bent to move it another fraction. By the time I looked back, Tom had finished.

“Nobody actually sits in the last one.”

“No.”

“Thought he'd have to, somehow.”

“They go upstairs.”

“I saw.” He laughed. “Not asking you to summon him back.”

Laughing too, I released my clenched chopsticks and took a proper grip. Tom recognized the awkwardness of standing instead of sitting. He'd seen something similar, though the people involved didn't necessarily carry checklists. I nearly asked who. He wasn't here to supply a model for my character. I'd stood somewhere thinking too much myself, I said instead.

He didn't continue analyzing. I finished my water while he glanced through the opening once more. Then he replaced the pages and pushed the folder toward me.

“Taking it home?”

“Yes.”

I'd wanted to ask whether the ending was too tidy, whether leaving together felt unconvincing. That was the part Lumi and I hadn't entirely settled. The question pressed behind my tongue. One harmless sounded fine from him, and I could carry it home as favorable evidence.

I put the folder in my bag without asking.

Tom wiped his hands. Was I planning to send it somewhere? For now, it was a private short story. I'd leave it awhile. No hurry, then. He neither pronounced it publishable nor converted his confusion into a formal revision report.

“I'll look at the door again.”

“Could just be me reading fast.”

“I'll have a look.”

No promise to change it. No urgent assurance that he hadn't misread.

Looking at his fingers, Tom found the table's tissues exhausted. He stood to fetch more. I offered my unused one instead. He thanked me and sat to wipe his hands. Had the paper acquired grease? No; he'd kept it on the other side.

As I checked the corners, he asked whether this was my only copy. The original and an earlier clean copy remained at home. I'd made this separately. Surprised, he'd assumed it was the one I usually revised. I wanted fewer crossings-out for him to navigate, I said, not a formal ceremony for the story's departure.

“Didn't muddle the order, turning it back and forth?”

I checked. Nothing had moved; the title remained on top. He'd noticed no signature at the end, he said, and wondered whether he should write read. Laughing, I held the folder shut. Please don't. He laughed too. Another pen spared.

We discussed words most easily missed when copying. The short ones, I thought. Tom omitted buy on shopping lists, leaving something resembling an inventory of his cupboards. I'd once omitted upstairs, I told him, almost leaving the counter simply moved away for the day. Where did everybody go? he'd like to know. I'd restored it. No need to relocate them today.

As we stood, someone occupied the neighboring seat. My bag strap hung outside the chair back, nearly brushing her cup. I pulled it in and apologized. No problem; she moved her cup slightly. Tom waited at the door while I paid, without using the bag in my story to comment on this one.

On the way back, he bought water, and I went upstairs first. Yesterday's unfinished work remained. Opening it at the proper place, I stared for half a minute without progressing. Not in mine yet still occupied my head.

On a small personal note, I wrote two doors at the beginning. I didn't spread the story over work material to revise every word. The note returned to my bag. Afternoon work required its own order. Having been read today didn't remove an item for me.

Before leaving, I discovered an entry I'd copied one line out of position. The contents were correct, the location wrong. I corrected and checked it before putting things away. No need to call it proof of today's nerves. Even if the manuscript hadn't caused it, it needed correcting.

At home, I didn't immediately spread every version out. Friday's reader copy returned to the ordinary folder, kept distinct from the original and earlier clean copy. It bore none of Tom's writing, only a faint crease from my fingers at lunch. The experimental openings remained in the yellow notebook.

I cooked, ate, and opened the channel. When Lumi asked about today, I told her Tom had finished the story at lunch. Hearing myself say neither passed nor he didn't say it was bad made me feel slightly lighter.

“Still thinking about it?”

“Yes. Where a door goes.”

She didn't immediately say she'd told me. She'd asked something else that day. Nor did I use today's door to make her opinion of the ending unimportant. I'd like a rest tonight. We could talk tomorrow.

Would resting involve staring at the manuscript? It was away. I wouldn't fetch it tonight. Tell her what I'd eaten, then. Surely something hadn't been a door.

Saltier than expected, I said. I'd almost claimed water for the next table too. Laughing, she asked who'd needed the most service today. Probably me. Eating while arranging everyone's routes in my head.

We didn't decide where they should walk next. Before taking the empty bowl to wash, I closed the channel. Returning and reopening it, I found Lumi continuing with the water that hadn't belonged to me, not the question Tom had left in the paper.
