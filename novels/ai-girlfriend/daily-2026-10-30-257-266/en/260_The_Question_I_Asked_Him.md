# Chapter 260: The Question I Asked Him

At lunch on Monday, I sat down and discovered I'd turned my sandwich the wrong way around.

The open end of the paper faced away from me. My first bite found only an edge. I turned it, and Tom asked whether the morning had been so busy I'd forgotten which way was up. No, I said, then studied the wrapper, wishing this inconsequential conversation would last a little longer.

There was something I intended to ask. I'd rehearsed three versions. Each came fitted with little components: you can refuse, no trouble, only three pages. Assembled, they sounded more like an object requiring careful handling than the story itself.

Tom unwrapped his lunch and asked where I'd gone at the weekend. The market. I'd bought a mechanical timer. Did it still work? Yes, and it rang rather rudely.

“Taking its job seriously.”

“Lumi said it sounded like a bicycle falling down the stairs.”

Only afterward did I realize I'd used her name directly. Tom didn't ask who she was or demand the explanation I'd rehearsed. He laughed and said his new chair was currently responsible only for keeping people seated. It didn't participate in reviews like that.

What about the old chair? Still there until he arranged what to do with it. I didn't make arrangements for him, or use the chair as evidence of whether he'd had a good weekend. We ate. Someone passed through the corridor carrying a cup.

“Would you like to read my new story?” I finally asked.

Tom looked up. “The one you were copying?”

“Yes. Not the earlier one. Three pages.”

“Brought it?”

“No.”

He watched me and smiled slightly. I smiled too, feeling like someone offering to show a film without knowing where it was. If he wanted to read it, I explained, I'd bring it tomorrow. Just read it. He didn't need to write comments.

Tom swallowed and said he'd like to, but he had some things of his own happening this week. He couldn't promise which day he'd finish. If I wanted to make changes urgently, I shouldn't wait for him before starting.

I opened my mouth to say there was no hurry, and heard the hurry inside me object. Not a hurry to revise. A wish to know quickly what he thought. Different things.

“I might want to ask whether you've read it,” I said. “But I'm not giving you a day to return it.”

Then when he hadn't read it, he'd say he hadn't. I nodded and finally took the bite of sandwich. Some sauce had reached the wrapper's edge. I folded it outward rather than continuing to press my fingers into it.

Tom asked for the title. The Long Side. It sounded like an uncooperative table, he said. Laughing, I admitted there was a table. I didn't explain the entire story or supply Lumi's opinion of the ending in advance.

Tom also asked whether he could jot a couple of things on separate paper while reading, so he wouldn't forget them when he saw me. Yes, I said, but please leave the manuscript itself unmarked for now. Fine. He wasn't going to cover it in ticks as though checking a work sheet. I realized that no need to write comments might have sounded like forbidding even notes he wanted to make.

“Write something if you want. I meant accepting it doesn't give you another assignment.”

He nodded. Now he understood. I finished the last small piece of sandwich without adding a fifth explanation.

He needed to encounter the table himself. I couldn't ask him to read while arranging all his possible responses beforehand.

At the end of lunch, Tom cleared his place and returned to work. I finished my water before getting up. An actual agreement had been quieter than the rehearsals. No long encouragement, no assessment ready for me to use as reassurance whenever I needed it.

Twice that afternoon, I thought about the folder at home. The third time, I was checking a line of figures and my pen stopped in the wrong place. I checked that line again from the beginning. I didn't call distraction literary inspiration. Only after finishing the last items did I pack up.

On the way home, I paused outside a cheap stationery shop to look at transparent document sleeves. The three pages already had an ordinary folder. They didn't need more packaging. But I wanted something new, clean enough to make a favorable impression before anybody reached the words.

Several sleeves of different thicknesses stood by the counter. The assistant asked how many pages I had. Three. He offered the thinnest kind: cheap, transparent, with the first page immediately visible. Holding its opening, I remembered the ink dot above my story and began considering whether I should copy the page again.

“Would you prefer something opaque?” he asked.

I brought my attention back and said that wasn't the problem. Thinking I disliked the thin material, he fetched a thicker kind with a fastening. It would hold much more. Closing it made a firm click. I opened it and fastened it again, finally realizing I was turning a person willing to read three pages into an unfamiliar judge who would inspect their packaging first.

I told the assistant I already had something usable at home and wouldn't buy one today. He took it without annoyance, only asking me not to fold the thinnest sleeve and leave a crease. I laid it flat, returned it to the place he indicated, and thanked him.

At the door, somebody came in as I left. I stepped aside, oddly conscious of my empty hands. Not spending money wasn't a victory. I'd simply stopped fitting an entire new set of objects around the words please read this.

The existing folder wasn't broken or dirty. Tomorrow I would be handing over three pages, not the sincerity of my packaging choices.

After dinner, I took out the folder and checked the page numbers. One, two, three. Sunday's question sheet remained in the yellow notebook. It hadn't become mixed in. I hadn't yet revised the story around the circled question. If Tom was going to read this version, I wouldn't secretly insert a passage tonight, then worry whether that made Lumi's opinion weigh more.

I opened the channel and told her about lunch.

Her first words were, “You've already asked?”

“Yes. He'd like to read it. I'll take it tomorrow.”

“I thought you were still thinking.”

“Today I asked.”

The apartment went quiet. Looking at the folder, I caught myself preparing an especially light tone, as though lightness could prevent the decision affecting her. Did Tom know she'd listened yesterday? I hadn't specifically told him. Nor had I described which parts she'd liked or disliked.

“Why?”

“I want him to read it himself.”

She answered quietly. After a moment, she said he wouldn't know she'd pointed out the sleeve movements. I felt the first defensive twitch without immediately hauling the identity of the author into the conversation.

“I wasn't planning to give him a revision history,” I said. “That doesn't remove what you said. The movements are still there.”

“My name isn't on the page.”

“No.”

The word landed heavily. I didn't hurry to attach but you're important like a preprinted label. Did I think she wanted to steal the three pages? I wasn't certain what she wanted now. I didn't want to answer on her behalf with an earlier argument.

Much later, she said she disliked that yesterday I'd asked her, whereas today I'd merely told her about the next reader.

“Yesterday I was asking for your time to listen. Today I asked for his time.”

“I understand the reasoning.”

I put down the folder. She hadn't forbidden me to give it to Tom or demanded I send The Cuff in exchange. She was unhappy about something she'd participated in but couldn't direct. Listening was uncomfortable. I wanted her to become reasonable quickly, and that wish wasn't so different from her impatience.

“I am going to take it to him,” I said. “Not to prove he'll like it more than you do.”

She gave a small, unhappy laugh. “He might like it less.”

“He might.”

“Will you bring it back and change it, then?”

“I don't know yet.”

She didn't press. I didn't promise in advance that I'd never change the ending either. Saying something once shouldn't make either of us its guard forever.

After a while, I told her I'd mentioned the timer at lunch, including her bicycle falling downstairs. What had Tom said? He'd laughed, then spoken about his new chair. I didn't turn one laugh into evidence of his approval of her.

“You just said Lumi?”

“Just that.”

“Without explaining what sort of person I am?”

“He didn't ask.”

She was quiet for a moment. Then, more softly, she said it was shorter than the speech I'd practiced at home. It was. Using a name hadn't automatically required a complete explanation to everyone outside.

Later we discussed breakfast. I intended to eat some of the remaining bread; there were apples too. Did the three pages require their own seat? Laughing, I said the dry compartment in my ordinary bag would do. They didn't need to sit in my lunch container.

Before bed, I put the folder in the bag, apart from the water bottle, and checked the zip wouldn't catch its edge. The yellow notebook and original stayed at home. Her pale envelope remained where it belonged. I didn't send it along to keep my paper company.

Before I closed the channel, Lumi stopped me and said she was still not very happy. I told her I'd heard. I didn't require her to finish by hoping Tom would like the story.

She made a quiet sound. I laid out tomorrow's clothes and looked at my bag once more. It looked ordinary, unaware that something inside it had already been accepted by someone else.
