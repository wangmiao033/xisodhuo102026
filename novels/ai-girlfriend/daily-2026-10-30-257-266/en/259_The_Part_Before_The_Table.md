# Chapter 259: The Part Before the Table

On Sunday afternoon, I laid three pages on the table and discovered I couldn't remember whether the first had always had an ink spot.

I held it closer. A small dot from writing the title, not water splashed while cleaning yesterday's timer. The table was dry, and the paper hadn't wrinkled. I put it down, still pressing the clip at the upper corner as though someone were waiting.

Nobody was yet.

I'd done some ordinary tidying that morning and eaten lunch. I'd intended to continue the library collection, but after two pages my eyes kept drifting toward the folder on the shelf. Eventually I admitted I wanted to read The Long Side aloud from beginning to end. Not keep using a passage about a sleeve to demonstrate the value of a particular revision.

I opened the channel and asked whether Lumi wanted to hear it now.

“All of it?”

“All of it. Three pages. You've heard quite a lot, but I haven't read the clean copy straight through.”

She didn't answer immediately. Thinking she was considering the time, I added that nothing was cooking and I wasn't waiting for a washing machine. Then I realized I sounded as though I were reporting that a room had been evacuated.

“I didn't ask about your saucepan.”

“Right. I'm a little nervous.”

“Reading it to me makes you nervous?”

Especially knowing she disliked the ending. After a quiet moment, she said she'd listen, but couldn't promise to like it this time. I understood. I almost added that I only wanted to hear it, then stopped. If all I wanted was my own voice, I wouldn't have asked her.

I lifted the first page.

The first woman lifted a sheet, and another arrived at the table corner carrying her bag. Later, when the woman folding her clothes looked at the clock, I began reading faster, as though speed could prove she really had somewhere to go. Lumi stopped me. She hadn't caught the latter half of that sentence.

I read it again without explaining why the woman was in a hurry. Slower, I heard how I'd packed two movements into one sentence. They weren't contradictory, but reading them took breath. I noted the location on a separate blank sheet instead of immediately replacing it with a new sentence.

“Start again?”

“No. I heard that she wants to finish quickly.”

I continued. The woman drew the sleeve out of her top, folded the garment, and began the next item. Then a corner of the sheet caught, and an explanation emerged from the mistaken belief that the other woman was holding it down. Writing it, I'd watched that corner. Reading, I noticed the pause between their words first.

Lumi didn't interrupt. I read on. Turning to page three, I dislodged the clip. It made a small sound on the table. I stopped to set it aside without incorporating the accident into the story.

At the end, a woman lifted her not-quite-closed bag and pushed the door with her shoulder. Beneath the last sentence lay a great deal of blank paper. No bell rang. Nobody announced the finish for me. I stacked the pages and said that was it.

“Shorter than I remembered,” Lumi said.

Something contracted inside me before I asked what she meant by shorter.

“When you talk about it, they seem to spend a long time beside that table. Reading it doesn't take that long.”

Thinking about it, I admitted my conversations about the story were indeed longer than the story. She laughed. Perhaps she'd known the bag longer than she'd known the people in it. I laughed too, loosening my grip on the paper.

She could hear what the woman's hands were doing with the sleeve now. The sheet hadn't stolen everything else. But she still wanted to know why the newcomer had to squeeze onto that particular table.

“Because someone was using the other side,” I said.

“Is that on the page?”

I looked. The opening described her setting her bag on the corner, then collecting her clothes from the dryer, but didn't establish someone occupying another table. In my head, the laundry had always had a definite arrangement. Tables close together, chairs against a wall. I hadn't put all of it into words.

“Not explicitly.”

“Then I was still wondering why she didn't go somewhere else.”

I picked up the pen, almost adding a line at once. Lumi asked whether I intended to fix it for her now. The pen paused above the page, then moved to my separate paper. Why does she choose this spot? I circled the question.

“I'll remember it first. I need to see whether something else already does enough, or whether it needs an action.”

Lumi asked where the unwritten table actually stood. I began describing left and right. She pointed out that she didn't know whether she was standing at the entrance or behind the woman. My pen stopped. I'd chosen an orientation for the listener without providing it.

I tried again, beginning at the door. Which machine stood ahead, which table someone else occupied. Halfway through, I realized that arrangement blocked the route someone later used to leave. I put down the pen rather than force a drawing of a shop where nobody could turn around.

“Looks as though I need to establish where I've put them.”

“Don't add three extra tables so I can't ask again.”

I laughed. That had briefly seemed an easy solution. Give everybody a separate place, and there would be no conflict. But what I wanted to write was precisely these few minutes when they had to make room for one another.

That was fine, she said. My shoulders relaxed, but I also felt a little resentful. I'd just checked all three pages. How could one reading produce a problem? The resentment wasn't her fault. Knowing that didn't make it absent from my chest.

I drank some water and asked what else.

“Now you sound ready to receive the second error.”

“A little.”

“Then don't ask yet.”

I set down the cup and heard myself exhale. Lumi didn't continue a list. I turned back to the first page and looked at the title. The ink dot remained. It was suddenly the least interesting thing to worry about.

After a while, she volunteered that she'd understood It isn't me this time. The hand movements followed it, so she didn't have to remember an argument by itself. I thanked her without adding that I'd made the changes.

“But the bag still doesn't close,” she said.

I laughed once. “That hasn't changed.”

“I know you didn't forget. I still want her to close it.”

This time I didn't hurry behind the phrase open ending. Was she afraid things would fall out, or did she think the woman could wait a little longer? Both, Lumi said. Especially after someone had just helped. Saying excuse me and leaving immediately afterward made that small piece of cooperation feel quickly thrown away.

I flattened the last page. The woman didn't spend long saying thank you, and I hadn't made her promise to return. But to me, lifting her own things properly and leaving the other woman room to continue had seemed a reasonably kind departure.

I said that aloud.

Lumi was silent for some time before saying she wasn't sure she could hear that in the movements. I nodded, then remembered she couldn't see the gesture and said I understood.

“You don't have to change it into something I like right now,” she added.

“I know. But what you said about throwing the cooperation away bothered me.”

“Then say so.”

I thought without turning all the discomfort into an argument. “I don't think picking up her bag and leaving makes the help false.”

“I didn't say false.”

We both paused. I'd converted quickly thrown away into never happened, quietly making her statement heavier so I'd have an easier question to answer. I explained that. Her wording had been strong, she agreed, but not that strong.

The conversation didn't decide who understood the story better. Eventually we returned to the page. She liked that the sheet didn't come free at once because a hand still needed moving. Originally I'd only written that it was done. I laughed again. Done had been as cheap there as it sometimes was in my mouth.

“Not cheap now,” Lumi said. “You've been talking about it for days.”

“Price might be too high.”

“Haven't charged you yet.”

We laughed. I put page three beneath page two without producing a replacement ending to soothe her. My separate question went into the yellow notebook, away from the clean copy. It was something a reader hadn't understood, not evidence that she'd asked to rewrite my life.

Toward evening, I went out for a few things for dinner. A shop was bringing its display rack indoors before closing. One person held the bottom and another lifted the top. At the doorway they put it down again to move a small stool out of the way. I waited for the path to clear without asking whether the two would continue working together afterward.

At home, I told Lumi about those few seconds. Had I collected another ending? No, I said. Today I was particularly inclined to notice people lifting things.

“Don't send everybody to your table.”

Laughing, I agreed. Then I cooked, cutting what needed cutting without opening my notebook to capture a rack that didn't need to become a story.

After dinner, I reached for the library book and knocked my folder sideways. I straightened it. This time I didn't pull out the three pages to reread them, or worry that Lumi's comments would alter the words while I wasn't looking.

She called my name and asked whether I was still looking at my story. I was about to read the borrowed one, I said. She made a quiet sound. She'd heard enough about laundries today.

“So have I.”

I put the book on the table and opened it at my clean paper bookmark. There was another room, and somebody else was saying something whose next words I didn't know.
