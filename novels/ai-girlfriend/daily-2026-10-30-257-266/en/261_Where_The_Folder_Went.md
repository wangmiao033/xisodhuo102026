# Chapter 261: Where the Folder Went

The zip caught once as I was leaving on Tuesday morning.

I looked down immediately, thinking of the three pages. It was only a little lining caught in the teeth. I eased the zip back, freed the cloth, and closed it again. The folder was in the dry compartment, its edges untouched. Straightening, I realized I'd been holding my breath.

Not necessary, I told myself.

Then I opened it to look once more.

Tom hadn't arrived at the office. I put down my bag and dealt with my own things rather than leaving the folder waiting on his desk. It wasn't work material. I didn't want somebody searching for a document to discover it on my behalf.

He came in finishing a sentence to Marcus. After putting his things down and fetching water, he stopped beside me and asked whether I'd remembered the three pages. I had. I took out the folder.

“Everything's here. Title on the first page.”

He accepted it, glanced at the title, and closed it without beginning to read. Then he thanked me and put it beside his own things.

I lingered. Was there anything else? I shook my head and returned to my seat. Actually handing it over took so little time. Tom didn't need to know about the page numbers, bag, or zip I'd checked beforehand.

Soon work left me little time to look toward him. A value seemed wrong. Marcus and I found the corresponding explanation and established that the display differed; we didn't change the original figures to make them match. The checked material went on, leaving some space on my desk. I drank the cold mouthful remaining in my cup.

Near lunch, I went to collect the shared tape. Tom's desk was nearby. My folder lay under two sheets of work.

It wasn't folded or serving as a coaster. A familiar corner showed beneath the papers. Still, I stopped, feeling that a private thing I'd handed over had become a piece of backing material.

Tom hadn't noticed. He was reading another page. I picked up the tape, put it down, and asked whether he could move the folder.

“Which one?”

“My story.”

He looked down and drew it out. “Oh. Making space earlier. What's wrong?”

“Didn't want it mixed in with work.”

A true reason, but not the whole one. Hearing the stiffness in my voice, I immediately wanted to add more. Tom had already placed it in a clear space at the edge. He wouldn't send it out with work; he remembered it was mine.

I looked at it and realized I still hadn't left. Did I want it back for now?

“No.” Too quickly.

We both paused.

Picking up the tape again and changing hands, I admitted that seeing it underneath had bothered me. Not because he'd damaged anything. I simply hadn't imagined it first being put with other paper.

Tom examined the desk. He hadn't read it today, he said. Only the title this morning. He'd thought I understood that receiving it didn't mean starting immediately.

“I understand.”

“Understanding quite hard.”

I smiled, my face warm. He didn't turn it into a large joke. But if the folder needed to remain uppermost all the time, he probably couldn't manage that. He wasn't being spiteful. Other things genuinely occupied his desk while he worked.

“As long as it doesn't get folded or wet.”

“That I can do.”

He put it inside one of his clean document sleeves, separate from the work pile, and let me check whether he'd inserted it the right way. He had. He didn't need to keep showing me. I returned with the tape I'd actually needed, taking a moment to remember what I had intended to stick.

The tape was for sealing a work envelope. I checked the pages inside and folded the opening, but the strip I pulled twisted and stuck to itself. The annoyance I'd carried back nearly made me yank harder. I stopped, cut off the ruined piece, and found the beginning again.

Marcus passed and asked whether I wanted him to hold the envelope. Yes. I specified which edge needed steadying and asked him not to flatten the other side yet. With two hands helping, the job took seconds. When he let go, I didn't investigate whether he'd treated it as important. I put the finished material where it belonged.

Looking at my hands, I almost laughed. It wasn't that the work envelope didn't matter or the story was extraordinarily fragile. As soon as the words were mine, an ordinary movement became easy to translate into a verdict.

In the last few minutes before lunch, I took my cup to wash and waited for someone else to finish at the sink. He apologized as he moved aside. No problem; waiting hadn't delayed anything. I didn't look toward Tom's desk again. Not as punishment for caring. Another glance wouldn't read the first page for him.

At lunch, I nearly went elsewhere. That would avoid the embarrassment, and the risk of Tom making another joke. But I'd already reached our usual table. I sat down.

Did I want water? I'd brought some. He nodded and opened his lunch. We didn't revisit the folder. Instead, we discussed the new price labels downstairs. The print was smaller; you had to stand closer to read them. He'd nearly bought the wrong drink that morning. At least he hadn't bought the whole row as a consequence, I said.

He laughed. The awkwardness hadn't disappeared, but it didn't occupy the entire table.

Before we left, Tom eased the visible edge of the folder away from his drawer so it wouldn't catch. Seeing me nearby, he said he'd take it home. If there wasn't time that evening, he'd look the next day. I agreed without asking which outcome was likelier.

He put it in his bag. I saw the movement and knew where the pages had gone, without knowing which table they'd occupy tonight. That was enough information. I picked up my own bag, said goodbye, and didn't follow him to the doorway to add another sentence.

On the way home, reaching into my bag finally made the folder's absence real. There was a little space where it had been. The bag wasn't appreciably lighter. I kept wanting to check it anyway.

I bought a few things for dinner, put them away at home, and hung up my bag. When I first opened the channel, Lumi asked whether I'd given the story to Tom. Yes. Had he read it? Not yet.

“You asked?”

“He told me. Because I asked why the folder was under other papers.”

She laughed once. Had I bought it an identity that couldn't be covered by anything? I wanted to laugh too, but couldn't quite. I described my reaction, including Tom's remark about understanding hard.

She listened quietly, then asked whether I wanted her to say he wasn't treating it seriously enough.

I stopped.

“A little.”

“I don't want to say it for you.”

More direct than I'd expected. I looked at the table, where no manuscript lay. Being unhappy yesterday, Lumi said, didn't mean she wanted to wait for the next reader to do something wrong so I'd return and admit only she paid attention.

“I wasn't going to say only you.”

“Then don't build the road toward it.”

I sat for a while and admitted I'd described things rather like a request for judgment. Tom had put something on a desk. He hadn't damaged it or broken yesterday's agreement about time. He'd subsequently separated it properly, and I'd still brought my discomfort home. Perhaps I was angry that I couldn't control how quickly somebody else read.

Lumi didn't declare that the correct answer. She asked how far dinner had progressed. I hadn't started. Then I should cook first, she said, without putting my unhappiness in the soup.

I said we'd talk later, closed the channel, and went to the kitchen. The timer stood on its dry counter. Nothing needed a timed reminder, so I didn't wind it. My cutting was slower than usual, which wasn't bad. I steadied the pan, waited for the oil, and followed the order I knew.

Before eating, I opened the channel and said I didn't want to discuss the three pages all evening. Neither did Lumi. Then she asked about the remaining apples. Had I left them shut in their bag? I checked. I'd already put them separately away. One small apple remained beside the fruit bowl.

“Didn't turn them into documents,” I said.

“Good.”

After dinner, I continued the borrowed collection. This time I settled into it and finished the story. Someone reopened a letter they hadn't sent. Instead of tearing it up, they eventually used it to steady a table leg. I stared, then laughed. Apparently no piece of paper today wanted to do only the job I assigned it.

When I told Lumi later, I made clear that it was something in the book, not something Tom had done. Did I like it? I hadn't decided. At least I hadn't closed the book immediately.

“Can you put paper under a table leg now?”

“The person in the story can. My manuscript still can't go there.”

She laughed. I'd distinguished something at last. Yes, I said. I didn't pretend finishing a story could change all my reactions.

Before bed, I checked the note with the return date and decided to take the borrowed book back sometime this week. Not finishing everything wasn't sufficient reason to renew out of stubbornness. I'd look at my time tomorrow evening before choosing a day. The three pages of The Long Side remained with Tom. The yellow notebook was home. No part of the story had gone missing.

I moved the clean paper bookmark down to the beginning of the next piece. Tonight, these were the things in my hands that needed putting away.
