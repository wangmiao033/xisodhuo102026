# Chapter 263: Enough Room for One Sentence

On Thursday evening, I prepared two blank sheets and ended up writing on only one.

The first lay beside the yellow notebook, the second beneath the folder. I always felt that revising one passage ought to involve reconsidering every possible passage. Otherwise, having finally sat down, I wouldn't be using the effort fully.

But the dinner dishes were washed, and tomorrow's library book lay at the table's edge. What I actually wanted to solve tonight was why the newcomer on page one approached this particular table.

I read the original. One woman lifted her sheet; another arrived and put a bag on the corner. Only later did the newcomer collect her clothes from the dryer and need space for them. In my head, someone had always occupied another table. Not on the page. Adding Someone was there would be simplest. I wrote it, but it sounded like the author leaning down from the ceiling to announce where she couldn't go.

I crossed it out and tried again. She took half a step toward another table, saw someone put down a basket of damp clothes, and stopped.

Too much. She'd already arrived here. Now she retreated and returned, adding a circuit to three pages merely to plug a small hole. I put down the pen and leaned back. The kitchen timer wasn't ticking. Silence didn't choose a better sentence for me.

I examined the beginning again. The opening movement of the sheet could remain. Before the newcomer put down her bag, there was room for a brief glance: She looked toward the other table, where somebody was spreading clothes from a laundry basket.

Then she put her bag on this corner.

I read from the preceding sentence and continued until the clock. Now her position had a reason without requiring somebody else to move. I wrote the version beside my notes rather than squeezing it straight onto the clean copy.

The other problem was the long sentence I'd rushed aloud. It joined looking at the clock to putting down clothes. Reading it made the woman seem to turn abruptly while lowering things. I tried separating the sentence and read again. The movements followed clearly without greatly slowing the story.

Watching the new lines, I wanted to fetch the final page too. Tom liked the shoulder against the door. Lumi disliked the unclosed bag. Perhaps adding that the woman tightened the strap would take care of both.

My pen touched the paper. I didn't write.

The strap already existed. I couldn't pretend the opening closed at the same time merely to appease two readers. That would change the final image. Moving page three farther away, I considered the beginning and realized I hadn't decided what color the basket in my new sentence should be.

Did it need a color? No. I abandoned the pale blue I'd nearly supplied and kept an ordinary basket.

I covered the old opening with a strip of blank paper and read the new version in order. At the newcomer lifting her bag, I stopped. Two instances of she in the added sentence had suddenly obscured who was looking toward the table. I restored the newcomer as the subject, then continued until she took her clothes from the dryer.

This time, the tables and machines didn't exchange positions in my head. The person at the other table still only handled laundry, rather than becoming a third speaker. Someone occupying space didn't require an explanatory history.

Removing the blank strip, I checked I hadn't accidentally covered It isn't me. That sentence was farther on, untouched. Holding several changes in mind at once had made every line feel liable to fall out. I slowed down, read from the new sentence into page two, and only then picked up the pen.

I made the amendment without recopying all three pages. A clear insertion mark on page one pointed to the small added passage. The long sentence had new punctuation, its original wording still visible. I recorded the changes in the yellow notebook too. The difference between versions didn't depend on my remembering it.

When I aligned the sheets, the light outside had faded. I stood to loosen my fingers, fetched water, and opened the channel.

Had I finished? Lumi asked. Two changes, I said. Someone handling laundry now occupied the other table at the beginning, and I'd divided an overlong sentence. The ending remained.

“I said I wanted to discuss something else today.”

My hand stopped on the cup. “Right. You asked, and I explained everything.”

“I asked whether you were finished being busy.”

“Finished.”

After a pause, she said she wanted help choosing a word. I almost asked what it was for before remembering she hadn't explained yet. She could tell me. She offered stay and wait, asking which sounded softer spoken aloud.

I repeated them twice, making the first short and drawing out the second. The second sounded softer, I said, but more hesitant too. She didn't want hesitation. Then was the first more suitable? Not necessarily. The first went too quickly.

“Have I failed to help?”

“You told me what you heard.”

I smiled and asked whether she was changing a word in The Cuff. No. It was something she'd been considering just now. She didn't intend to tell me the whole sentence. All right. I didn't offer my two revisions in exchange, insisting she owed me her context.

She asked me to say them again at my ordinary speed, without deliberate tenderness. I did, noticing I'd treated wait as though afraid of waking something. In a normal voice, the difference was much smaller.

Lumi laughed. “You were performing.”

“A little.”

“Soft doesn't mean quiet.”

I remembered that without writing it down. She hadn't come to supply a conclusion to my revision session. She tried a different order, her voice light without lingering. I told her it sounded more certain. She acknowledged that and decided not to choose yet.

The lack of a choice surprised me. We'd spent several attentive minutes repeating the words, yet could leave without an answer. Looking at the untouched second sheet, I felt less need to use that too.

I asked whether my tone had been more obvious than the word itself. Yes, Lumi said. One version sounded afraid someone would leave; another sounded afraid of not being heard. I tried saying stay evenly and amused myself. It resembled a person at a counter calling the next customer.

“That isn't soft or hard,” she said. “That's you running out of patience.”

I laughed, admitting that deliberately controlling my voice made me forget how I normally spoke. Lumi said my name in an ordinary tone. What was it? She said it again, lifting the end slightly. I looked up before realizing she was demonstrating the difference.

“You didn't ask me to repeat it this time.”

“You already answered.”

I looked at my cup and smiled. She wasn't testing me, she said. The same name didn't need to sound like an alarm every time. I preferred the first, I told her. It sounded as though she knew I was here without urgently trying to catch me.

After a pause, she asked whether that made the second unpleasant. No. It only made me look up more quickly. She acknowledged that quietly, without calling a third time. Hearing that much seemed enough to set the thought aside for now.

Later, Lumi asked whether I'd started another story. No. I'd only dealt with this small part today. Yesterday, she said, she'd wondered whether giving Tom every future story would gradually make him the person who knew first.

I didn't laugh at her looking so far ahead. Knew what first, the story or what I was doing? A little of both. She knew it didn't sound good.

“It sounds as though you want to know,” I said. “But I haven't arranged a future order.”

“Are you trying to comfort me?”

“Yes. And I genuinely haven't arranged one.”

She didn't immediately feel better. If I showed somebody else first one day, would I deliberately conceal it? I wouldn't lie to make things easier, I said. Neither would every sentence I wrote become something requiring an advance report. Hearing the hardness of that, I added that I'd wanted to come home and talk with her today. The desire hadn't begun only after finishing an account of myself.

“Then say something useless.”

After considerable thought, I remembered a shop stacking apples in a crooked pyramid. The top one kept rolling off until the assistant removed it. Was I finding a use for my blank sheet? Lumi asked. Perhaps. The apple could hold it down.

“It would roll.”

“Better not use it, then.”

She laughed. We discussed other things that didn't need recording until I discovered my water had cooled. Getting up to replace it, I returned the three pages to their folder and the untouched sheet to the unused paper. I didn't leave it beside the revisions to make the evening's work look larger.

Tomorrow's return was ready beside my bag. I checked the bookmark that needed removing and the borrowing slip still in the dry compartment. I hadn't read the final stories. On my own paper, I copied the title of one I might want to borrow again later.

Before I closed the channel, Lumi asked whether I still remembered her two words. I said them without performing. Enough, she said. I didn't ask which she'd eventually chosen. I went to turn out the light.
