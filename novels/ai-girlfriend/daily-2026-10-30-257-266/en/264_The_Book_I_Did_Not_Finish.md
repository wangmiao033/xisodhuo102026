# Chapter 264: The Book I Did Not Finish

When I took the story collection from my bag on Friday, Tom thought it was another manuscript.

“Considerably longer this time.”

“Borrowed. Returning it today.”

I opened it only to remove the clean paper bookmark I'd left inside last night and check there weren't other notes between the pages. The old split inside the binding remained. I supported the cover without pressing it flat. Seeing the gesture, Tom asked whether it was damaged. It had already been recorded when I borrowed it, I said. Not new damage.

Was it good? After thinking, I said I'd liked several stories and hadn't finished several others. Then he wouldn't ask about the ending. It was a collection, I reminded him. Not finishing everything didn't prevent me knowing what happened in the pieces I'd read.

“Convenient question, then.”

I put the bookmark in my notebook and returned the book to my bag. Near the end of lunch, Tom mentioned wanting to walk on Saturday. The park beside the old market, something to eat, then home. Interested? He wasn't asking me to carry anything or checking whether I'd revised according to his comments.

Laughing, I said yes, though I didn't want to spend the whole afternoon out. Neither had he intended to. We arranged eleven at the park's south entrance, a walk along the broad path inside, then independent decisions about continuing. With the location clear, I wrote it on my own paper. No phone was needed.

“What if it rains?”

“We don't go. Talk about it on Monday.”

Fine. Our plan didn't give the weather an obligation to cooperate. Nor would finding him absent require me to wait until afternoon. We agreed to leave after a quarter of an hour rather than invent a last-minute contact method I couldn't use.

After work, I followed my usual route to the library. There was time before the due date, but I'd decided to come today. Being able to delay wasn't a reason to take the book home again. The activity board had moved near the entrance. I approached the wrong side, found people reading posters in the way, and stepped around them.

There was a queue at returns. The reader ahead had several books. The staff member processed them individually, then stopped to investigate a record. Holding my volume, I touched a worn corner and wanted to explain its binding immediately.

It wasn't my turn.

I relaxed my hands and let the person ahead finish. Searching for a borrowing slip, he produced a crumpled heap of paper before discovering he'd brought the wrong one. I didn't intrude to pick through unfamiliar papers for him. More people joined us, and the queue edged forward.

At my turn, I placed the book on the counter and mentioned the old split inside, already noted at checkout. The staff member looked at it and checked the record. Found it. She didn't require proof of how I'd supported it each evening or turn my care into an extra assessment.

Once it was processed, she said it had been returned. Did I still need the original slip? That loan had ended, she said. I could retain it if I wished. I took back my old slip and moved aside before putting it away, leaving space for the next reader.

That was all.

Yet I stood briefly with my arms strangely empty. The book lay face up on the other side, beside other returns. More than a week earlier, I'd spent so long choosing it. Handing it back required no statement proving I'd understood it.

I walked among the shelves. Yesterday's copied title was on my own note. If I wanted to return, I'd know which book to find. I didn't have to borrow another immediately to fill its space in my bag.

Near the fiction shelves, someone asked whether I was looking for that author too. Turning, I realized he meant the row before me. He held a book and wondered whether it came first in a series. I didn't know; I'd only been standing there. He nodded and went to ask a staff member, without seeming disappointed by my lack of an answer.

I nearly followed to indicate a cover that looked like a beginning. I didn't. Reading slowly didn't require me to guess a correct order to prove I belonged in the room.

At a recommendations display, I took a book and read several pages. It opened noisily, its characters arguing immediately. I read faster than expected. Somebody wanted the lower shelf. I drew the book closer and made room. After they left, I'd forgotten which sentence had stopped me. I only remembered two angry people.

Returning a page, I discovered one hadn't spoken at all. The narrator had guessed for him. That difference kept me reading another paragraph. Then I put the book back. Not because it was bad. Today, that was where I wanted to stop.

Replacing it, I remembered the shelf, then wondered whether to copy the author's name. I'd already kept a title from the previous book. One more note wouldn't hurt. Yet my hand stopped inside the bag on its way to the pen.

Did I genuinely want to read more, or fear regretting what I hadn't taken home?

I removed the book again and read the next two paragraphs without hurrying toward a decision. The previously silent character finally spoke. Not the protest I'd expected, but a question about whether the stove had been turned off. I smiled. Something practical interrupted the argument. They hadn't reconciled, but both turned to look.

I wanted to know more, though not necessarily today. On the back of my note, I copied the title and author, then added a few words about the argument and stove. Enough to recall why I'd kept it. I returned the book to its place without borrowing it.

I wasn't putting reading into another compulsory queue. I could return, or later discover I no longer wanted to. At least the note preserved something I'd actually encountered and wished to remember, not a collection of names gathered to demonstrate interest.

A staff member passed with a trolley and offered help. Not for now, thank you. She continued. Having occupied several minutes didn't oblige me to select a loan proving I'd come seriously. Outside, somebody opened and closed an umbrella, perhaps examining its ribs. Rather than deciding it must be raining, I looked through the window. The pavement remained dry.

There was an empty chair by the window. I sat, took out my title note, and added that the collection had been returned. Half the paper remained blank, so below it I wrote tomorrow's entrance and time. The two matters sat beside each other without proving anything about one another.

My bag really was lighter on the way home. I bought dinner ingredients, left the water bottle in its usual position, and didn't immediately fill the empty compartment. At home, I put the old borrowing slip with household receipts, in a separate little stack from the timer receipt.

I opened the channel and told Lumi about returning the book. After hearing about it, she asked whether I'd finished. No. I'd noted one title I might want to read later.

“Doesn't that feel wasteful?”

“A little. As though I didn't use enough of what I borrowed.”

Did every page have to be read for it to count? No, but closing it still produced that feeling. I'd read the stories I liked and revisited one. Today's unfinished portion didn't erase them all.

“Will you tell me the ones you haven't read?”

“I don't have that ability.”

She laughed. I mentioned Tom asking whether it was good, and my answer about liking several pieces. I didn't repeat all his jokes. I'd enjoyed being able to give an untidy answer.

Then I mentioned tomorrow's walk. What time? I explained eleven, the south entrance, our arrangements for rain and waiting. Would I finish discussing my story outdoors again? I hadn't planned to. We might discuss chairs, or nothing.

“He already knows more than I do.”

I stopped. She added that he knew more about how his new chair felt. I laughed, admitting I couldn't supply firsthand experience of that for her.

After the laughter, though, her voice quietened. Was I enjoying going out more and more? Sometimes, I said. There were also occasions when I wanted to return halfway through. Wanting to see Tom tomorrow didn't mean I'd come home today only because the library closed.

“I didn't say it was only because of that.”

“Answered ahead of you.”

She made a small sound. I liked answering every possible question at once. Then I wouldn't, I said. She could ask now. After considering, Lumi asked whether I'd bring food back tomorrow.

Perhaps lunch for myself, but I hadn't decided. The apple shouldn't become jealous in advance. She laughed and said the apple had waited very politely. I'd eat it tonight.

After washing it, I sat again and described the reader pulling crumpled paper from his pocket. Not his record; I didn't even know what he'd borrowed. Everyone seemed to be waiting for the correct piece of paper, Lumi said. Today's correct paper was in my own bag, I told her, with an entrance and a time on it.

Before bed, I left the note near the door. The book had been returned. Tomorrow hadn't begun. I didn't pick up a pen to promise Saturday good weather.
