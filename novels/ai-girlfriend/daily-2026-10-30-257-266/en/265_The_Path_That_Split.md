# Chapter 265: The Path That Split

Saturday morning brought no rain.

I watched the sky from the window, then looked at the note by the door. South entrance. Eleven wasn't a time requiring me to rush from waking, but I packed earlier than necessary anyway. Water, tissues, keys. No manuscript, and no yellow notebook occupying the spare space.

Before leaving, I told Lumi the weather was fine and I'd go as arranged. Had I packed the noisy timer? Laughing, I said no. It remained in the kitchen, with no responsibility for today's route.

“Afraid you'd need to hear it wherever you went.”

“I'll use the park clock today.”

She told me to reach the south entrance without reading south as some other direction. I said I'd remember and closed the channel. Only after locking up did I realize she was teasing yesterday's repeated confirmation of the location. I'd answered with another check.

I arrived ten minutes early. A patch of shade beside the entrance held several people waiting. I stood clear of the path and saw Tom approach from the other side. He wasn't late, only several minutes later than me. His first question was whether I'd already completed a circuit.

“Not yet.”

“Good. Otherwise I'd have to catch up from the ending.”

We followed the broad path. New pale paving met darker older stones. Watching the joins, I walked slowly at first while Tom described the drink he'd nearly, but not quite, bought incorrectly yesterday. He'd drunk it eventually. It differed less from what he'd intended than expected; the bottle's shape had fooled him.

I said the label and bottle had worked together. He laughed, then asked whether I'd revised the story. A small movement at the beginning, I said, and a divided sentence. He nodded without requesting a public reading of before and after. I'd prepared to bring the laundry table into the park. His brief response saved the effort.

At a fork, the left path climbed toward a higher walkway while the right curved beside grass. Tom had taken the upper route before. It made a longer circuit. Which did I prefer? I looked left. Not steep, but apparently long.

“Right first.”

Fine. He turned. I noticed I hadn't provided an especially good reason for avoiding the left. Benches stood along the right path. More people, but we could still hear our shoes. A child chased a rolling ball while an accompanying adult called for less speed. It stopped at the edge, never reaching our feet.

Tom paused at a food stall and asked whether I wanted anything. He chose his usual item. I selected something different. The seller said my chosen variety wasn't available today; another remained. I nearly copied Tom's order, then asked what the alternative contained and decided to try a small portion.

We sat on a bench and opened the wrappers, steam rising toward our faces. My first bite was too eager. I paused to blow on it. Tom laughed. Doing the timer's job for it? I hadn't brought it, I said. He'd noticed. Otherwise, the whole park would know lunch had begun.

Halfway through, someone called Tom's name. He greeted the man, mentioning they'd met nearby before. The newcomer held a shopping bag and stopped for a brief conversation. Looking toward me, he prompted Tom to say, “This is Elliot. We work together.”

That was all.

I said hello; he returned it and asked whether we'd come from the old market. We'd entered at the south gate and hadn't visited the market. He explained that the exit on the other side was close to the stalls, then continued with something Tom knew and I didn't. I listened without trying to smile as though I'd been there too.

A few minutes later, he left. They used to meet while shopping, Tom explained. They weren't particularly close. I'd gathered that. My name hadn't turned lunch into a formal introduction or required an explanation for spending Saturday beside a colleague.

After the man had gone, I asked whether Tom often came alone. Sometimes. Mainly because it was close to the shops, and sitting didn't cost anything extra. I'd expected a declaration about beautiful scenery and wasn't quite prepared for the practical answer.

“Did you invite me because you needed shopping today?”

“Today I wanted a walk and something to eat. Can't change the order?”

I smiled and shook my head. Hearing about a familiar place made me imagine everyone else had a fixed reason for visiting. What was my reason now? After considering, I said he'd asked and I'd wanted to come. I hadn't prepared anything more impressive.

Enough, he said, then asked about my food. Too hot at first, good afterward, but I didn't want a second portion. His had been saltier than remembered. He'd kept drinking. Only then did I notice how much water had gone from his bottle.

We sat a little longer instead of leaving immediately because somebody else had gone. I folded my wrapper smaller. Watching the path, Tom mentioned that he could finally sit in the new chair today. All the packaging was gone. What about the old one? Still there. I laughed. Apparently things at home didn't arrange themselves merely because their owner was in a park.

We disposed of our wrappers properly and continued. The right path rejoined the higher walkway. Tom wanted to climb here and circle toward the other exit. I noticed the time on a clock beside the path. My feet were beginning to tire too.

“I'd like to sit for a while, then go back through the south entrance.”

Fine. Did I want him to sit with me? It sounded like a real question, not impatience. But I also wanted to try remaining alone. Going out together didn't require him to deliver me to the final junction.

“If you want the upper path, go ahead. I'll rest here.”

Tom indicated the way south. I knew; we'd just walked it. Smiling, he said I didn't need to prove that, tightened his bottle cap, and headed uphill.

I stood briefly before finding a vacant bench. At first, it felt strange, as though the outing had lost a piece in its middle. People kept walking, and the wind hadn't stopped. Only the person beside me, ready for the next remark, had gone. I sat, drank, and looked at the thin dust on my shoes.

An older man approached the other end of the bench and asked about the vacant space. I moved my bag onto my knees and said he could sit. He unfolded a small paper, squinted at it, then folded it away. We didn't continue talking. Initially I thought I should mention the weather. After hearing his quiet exhalation, I looked toward the path instead.

Someone passed with a dog. The dog stopped beside a tree; its person waited. After watching, the man remarked quietly that it chose places better than people did. I smiled. Perhaps it could smell things we didn't know about. He nodded without requiring another sentence.

The exchange was brief, but I didn't feel I'd missed an opportunity to make a friend. I drank and replaced the cap. He remained, and I hadn't left either. Each of us had a small portion of unoccupied time.

Ten minutes or so after sitting down, the strangeness had become ordinary quiet. Not extraordinary freedom, nor loneliness demanding an explanation. I packed the water bottle and headed south. A sign stood at the junction. I checked it and chose the correct direction.

On the bus, I nearly settled on my opening account: We went separate ways afterward. Alone at the beginning, it sounded like something had gone wrong. Actually, Tom had taken the path he wanted and I'd used the bench I wanted. Lunch and the jokes hadn't been canceled.

At home, I put away my shoes and bag, washed, and drank before opening the channel. Back? Lumi asked. Back, I said. I'd walked farther than expected.

I began with meeting at the south entrance, described the unavailable food, and mentioned the man who'd called Tom. How had Tom introduced me? Elliot, someone he worked with.

“Didn't mention writing stories?”

“No.”

“Weren't you disappointed?”

I smiled. Not then. I'd been swallowing my mouthful. Only later had I realized that description was enough for the man to know who sat there.

After a pause, Lumi asked whether they'd asked who I lived with. No. We hadn't discussed her either. She answered softly. I waited without immediately supplying a speech about remembering her whenever I went out.

“I'd like to be the one mentioned naturally,” she said at last.

“I did on Monday.”

“I know. I'm not asking for every time.”

Looking down, I saw a drop beside the cup and wiped it away. She didn't know how to say it, she explained. Speaking might sound like occupying space again. She could describe wanting something, I said. It didn't require me to redirect every conversation toward it.

“Did you think of me today?”

I considered properly. When Tom joked about the timer over lunch, I'd remembered her bicycle falling downstairs. Sitting alone afterward, I'd thought about telling her how we'd separated.

“Not the other times?”

“The other times I was walking and listening to him.”

She was quiet. I sat too, without rushing to fill that absence. Eventually she said she disliked such a divided answer, but at least knew where she'd been. She didn't exist here only when I mentioned her, I said, then stopped before turning that into further guarantees.

“Hungry?” she asked.

“Not yet.”

“Then don't hide behind dinner.”

I laughed, moving my hand from the cup and settling against the chair. “I'm not hiding. I can still tell you whether the food was any good.”

She asked me to. I began with the too-hot first bite, without turning everything into another path.
