# Chapter 258: The Ring I Could Hear

The Saturday market was narrower than I remembered.

The street hadn't changed. Stalls reached farther out, and people stopped with their bags until a straight path became a succession of sideways steps. I paused beside a table of old cups to let a small trolley pass, then realized I'd been studying the cups for some time.

One had a chipped rim and held several spoons. A sign specified that only the spoons were for sale. I left the cup alone. On a dark cloth beside it lay a hand whisk, a wooden-handled bottle opener, and two kitchen timers. Remembering Wednesday, when the saucepan interrupted my listening and I returned assuming Lumi had continued without me, I crouched to examine the round one.

The seller asked whether I wanted something that didn't need electricity. Yes, I said. Something for the kitchen to remind me while cooking, not a whole new collection of devices. He picked up the round timer, turned it through a short interval, and said it was simple. It rang when it finished.

We waited.

Someone called out a price. A paper bag lifted in the wind, and the seller held it down. I heard a faint sound, uncertain whether it came from the timer or another bell nearby. Its pointer had reached the end. He said it had rung.

“Could I try it again?”

He handed it over. Old things were like that, he said; they weren't as loud as new ones. I turned it as he had, feeling resistance beneath my fingers. Some numbers had faded. I had to tilt it to read them. When it finished again, I caught a brief noise like two coins knocking together inside a pocket.

“I might not hear that standing over the stove.”

“Your kitchen isn't as noisy as this.”

True. Holding it, I imagined bringing it home and repeatedly bending close to check whether it had rung. Cheap would quickly become another thing requiring care. I put it back without declaring it broken. This one wasn't loud enough for me.

The seller's enthusiasm cooled a little. He pointed to the square timer, saying it cost more. I checked the price before picking it up. Instructions on the back described winding it. Did I need to turn it to the marked position first, then back to the minutes I wanted? Yes. This one didn't work quite like the first.

I followed the printed steps for a short interval. It ticked evenly. We waited, and I moved aside for someone interested in the bottle opener. The man tested the wooden handle and asked for a lower price. While the seller answered, I didn't interrupt to hurry my own demonstration.

The bell rang as I was taking out my coin purse.

Sharp and brief. My hand stopped, and the other customer glanced over. The seller laughed. Heard that one, hadn't I? I had. Could I set it once more, for a slightly longer interval? I wasn't trying to prove he'd tricked me. I wanted to know I could use it.

This time I looked at other things along the table instead of watching the pointer. That didn't make everything worth taking home. A small tin was attractive, but its lid needed forcing. I set it down. The timer rang again. I turned back and picked it up.

It wasn't a sound I particularly liked. Nor could it decide how many more minutes a pan of soup required. But I could hear it several steps away, read its markings, and stand it on a flat surface without wobbling. I paid and asked for a receipt. There was no box. The seller wrapped it in clean paper and wrote down the item and amount.

When he handed over the receipt, he'd written the price of the round timer. I pointed it out rather than treating the difference as an unexpected discount. He paused, crossed it out, and wrote the square one's price. Still thinking about the previous customer, he said. I counted the change, and he checked it before folding the receipt smaller.

If I forgot the method at home, I asked, would the instructions remain readable? He turned it over and pointed to the faded lines. I read them once more. They were legible, and I knew to follow this timer's directions rather than confuse the two demonstrations. He didn't promise it would last a certain number of years. I didn't press him for an answer he couldn't guarantee.

Someone was waiting to pay for the bottle opener, so I packed my things and moved aside. The timer's casing pressed against my hand through its paper. I'd imagined finding something smooth and attractive. What I'd bought had square corners and an unpleasant voice. Choosing an object that could do one small job didn't require me suddenly to like every part of it. I checked that I hadn't left the receipt on the cloth and made room for the next customer.

A few steps away, I remembered I hadn't asked the price of the little tin. I stopped, then kept going. Coming here didn't require me to finish every question I'd nearly asked.

At a fruit stall, I chose a few apples. One had a bruise on the hidden side. I exchanged it without demanding that the seller turn over the entire crate. After paying, I found a space out of people's way and rearranged my bag. The timer went on top, where the apples wouldn't knock it all the way home.

People were moving things in the corridor when I returned. I let them pass before unlocking my door. Once my bag was down and my hands washed, I wiped the timer's exterior and let it dry. I didn't open it or turn the little screw underneath out of curiosity.

On the kitchen counter, it looked squarer than it had at the stall.

I tried two minutes and stood where I normally chopped vegetables. In the quiet apartment, the ticking was surprisingly distinct. The bell didn't startle me, but I frowned. Hearing something clearly and wanting to hear it announce itself every day were different matters.

Lunch was simple. I put the heated food in a bowl, ate, and cleared away before opening the channel. Lumi asked whether I'd found an apologetic saucepan. No, I said. I'd bought something that only demanded a turn to speak when its time came.

“What?”

A mechanical timer. No electricity or network connection. Turn it, and it ran for a while. She asked me to describe the sound. Like a very impatient bicycle bell, I said, although a bicycle bell might find that insulting.

“You like it?”

“I can hear it. Like is a bit much.”

“Why buy it, then?”

I described the difference between the two. The first was almost inaudible; the second carried several steps. Had I decided anything that rang was acceptable because I hadn't heard the first? Thinking, I admitted I'd probably wanted to finish deciding. But I'd tested it again and thought I could live with it.

She wanted to hear. I said I'd set a short interval here. It would ring; the noise wouldn't mean something had gone wrong. Bringing it to the table, I followed the instructions and turned it back. We discussed the chipped cup whose spoons were for sale. Had the cup been dismissed? Lumi asked. Perhaps it had only changed jobs.

The bell rang.

She stopped halfway through a sentence. I waited too, until it finished.

“Awful,” she said.

“A little.”

“You said a small bicycle. That bicycle fell down the stairs.”

Laughing, I lowered my head and touched its casing. Was I intending to keep it on our table? No. In the kitchen. I'd brought it over because she'd asked to hear it. I carried it back. She hadn't said anything by the time I returned.

“I've put it back.”

“Are you waiting for me to agree it can live here?”

“No. Just telling you it won't suddenly ring here again.”

She answered quietly. After a moment, she said she hadn't meant I should return it. She simply disliked it. I understood. I wasn't going to turn awful into an accusation that she was stopping me cooking. She laughed and suggested avoiding a dinner that required it to ring ten times.

In the afternoon, I read some of the borrowed collection. The next story finally moved beyond its slow opening, though I didn't finish it. I replaced my paper bookmark. I didn't use the timer to turn reading into another task completed only when a noise said so.

Later, while cooking, I used it for a real reminder for the first time. I prepared the pan, then set an interval after which I wanted to check. The bell wasn't proof anything was cooked. When it rang, I went over, found the food needed longer, and continued while staying in the kitchen myself.

Dinner didn't taste better simply because I'd paid for a square object. It wasn't burned either. I wiped the timer and left it on a dry counter, away from heat and water. The receipt went with other small household receipts. The wrapping paper went in the bin.

That evening, Lumi asked how the bicycle that had fallen downstairs was doing. It had a job in the kitchen, I said. No promotion yet. Good. I shouldn't let it call her name for me.

“It only knows one sound.”

“Sometimes you're much the same.”

I knew she meant my too-quick yes. I laughed without answering with that same word. Holding my cup, I told her I'd eaten, wasn't waiting for a saucepan, and could properly listen to what she wanted to say.

She thought, then said she wanted to hear about the apples first.

“Haven't eaten one yet.”

“Two unfinished things today, then. Your book and your apples.”

I went to wash an apple and returned to tell her I'd chosen one of the smaller ones. The timer remained quiet in the kitchen. This time, nobody needed to wind it.
