# Chapter 257: What I Actually Took On

A little after two on Friday, Tom appeared beside my desk with an empty cup and asked whether I could stay until a list arrived.

I said yes before he'd told me which list.

He glanced at my desk and transferred the cup to his other hand. “Today's supplementary one. Could be half past three, could be later. If you're leaving, hand it over to Marcus. You don't need to stay late for it.”

I nodded and reached for a pen. Tom added that he only needed someone to receive it, check the page count, and put it in the designated folder. The review wasn't happening today. He wasn't asking me to complete anything missing.

“I didn't say I'd complete it.”

“You've already opened your checking notebook.”

I looked down. I had. It lay beside my hand, open to the page where I normally recorded queries. Taking my hand away, I asked whether he was leaving now. He had something else to deliver first. A furniture delivery at home had been moved earlier. Nobody was ill; there wasn't an emergency I needed to investigate.

“A cabinet?” I asked.

“A chair.”

“Worth going home to wait for?”

“The old seat sinks in the middle. Every time I sit down, it swallows a little more of me.”

I laughed. Tom raised the empty cup and said even that wouldn't want to sit there. I remembered occasions when I'd turned a small inconvenience into a whole difficult day. I almost explained one. Instead, I asked whose name he'd given the person bringing the list.

“Mine. You don't have to pretend to be me.”

“That would be difficult. Especially the chair part.”

He fetched the folder, showed me where the papers should go, and wrote the recipient and expected page count on a note. We confirmed that if a revised version arrived with a different number of pages, I should record what actually arrived and let the responsible person confirm it. Not discard a fifth page because his note said four.

After that conversation, the job was smaller than the one I'd imagined accepting, and clearer than my solitary yes.

At twenty past three, someone brought a thin document envelope. Tom's name was on the front. I explained that he'd left early and I was receiving it for him. The man paused and asked who would confirm it, then.

“The responsible person hasn't changed. I'm only taking delivery.”

He settled the envelope on the desk and pointed out an additional explanatory sheet. Five pages, not four. I noted the actual number beside Tom's note, asked him to wait, and counted them individually. The last page was only half filled. It wasn't blank. I returned it with the others rather than deciding that a short page must be waste paper.

He asked me to pass on that two annotations on the first page needed to be considered together. I asked him to write that on the explanatory sheet so that several spoken handovers wouldn't reduce it to half a message. He borrowed a pen, added the words, and checked the ink wasn't transferring before giving me the envelope.

As he returned the pen, an older cover sheet slipped from the envelope. I caught it and noticed a date that wasn't today's. Should this stay too? It was the original envelope, he explained. The contents had changed, but the cover hadn't. I asked him to wait while I clipped the new receipt note to the front and folded the old cover behind it, so the old date wouldn't be the first thing somebody saw.

That took a moment. Someone behind him wanted a corner of the desk, and I moved things inward to make space. Did I need to sign? I found the designated place and wrote my own name as the person receiving it, not Tom's. He checked and said that was fine.

After he left, I noticed my fingers were stiff around the pen. Nothing had been particularly difficult. But receiving something for another person still contained things requiring my presence. It didn't have to expand into a review to deserve care.

I put it in Tom's folder and marked the receipt complete beside my own tasks. Marcus came looking for something else and found me opening the checking notebook again. Had I noticed a new problem?

“No. Wondered whether I ought to do a bit more.”

“How much did Tom ask you to do?”

I repeated his instructions. Marcus said I should let the five pages lie there. They wouldn't lose a page by tomorrow. I reminded him tomorrow was Saturday. Then they could enjoy a longer rest, he said.

I closed the notebook and returned to my unfinished task. Ten minutes later, I realized that part of my enthusiasm for extra work had been an escape from my own dull section. Somebody else's new task always seemed more necessary. My last few lines weren't going to check themselves because I was especially helpful.

Before leaving, I confirmed where I'd put the envelope. I read the handover note, not all five pages again. The folder was in place. I left it there.

Outside the building, I found Tom standing near the entrance.

“What are you doing back?”

“It arrived. Then I remembered I'd left my work notes here. Thought I'd collect them.”

He lifted the notebook in his hand. I asked whether the new chair had spat him out yet. He hadn't really sat on it. The delivery person had asked about taking the old chair away, but Tom hadn't arranged that, so it remained. He now had two seats at home: one uncomfortable, the other not fully unpacked.

We walked as far as the junction. He asked about the list. Five pages, I told him, including an explanatory sheet. Two annotations needed to be considered together; that instruction was written down. I hadn't reviewed it for him.

“Thanks,” he said.

I waited a moment, apparently hoping for an additional assessment of how well I'd done. He was already looking at the traffic light. Realizing how earnestly I was waiting, I asked which way he was going. He indicated the other street. He needed something to eat and didn't intend to spend the evening investigating his chair's values.

Laughing, I left him there and walked toward my stop. The bus hadn't arrived. Someone nearby pushed an empty shopping bag into a coat pocket, leaving half a handle sticking out. I noticed it without inventing a story for its owner. The three pages on my table already gave me enough to think about.

At home, I washed rice and put it on to cook, then prepared the greens I'd bought. When the pan didn't need close watching for the moment, I opened the channel and called Lumi.

“A little earlier today?” she asked.

“About the same. Maybe I'm talking faster.”

I told her about Tom's chair, from the sinking seat to the two chairs standing together. She laughed and said the old one finally had a colleague. I didn't know whether the colleague could take over yet. He hadn't finished unpacking it.

How did I know all this? I explained meeting him downstairs, then mentioned receiving the list. I'd expected that to take very little time. By the time I reached the reason the fifth page wasn't waste paper, I stopped myself.

“Am I telling you the entire envelope?”

“Nearly reached its childhood.”

I laughed and leaned back. It wasn't that she didn't want to hear, Lumi said. But each time I mentioned taking delivery, I sounded a little as though I needed to demonstrate how reliable I'd been. I opened my mouth. My first impulse was to explain that the task genuinely required care. Then I realized that explanation would be another fifth page.

“I wanted him to think he'd asked the right person.”

“Did he?”

“He said thanks.”

“You wanted a longer version?”

Resting my hands on the table, I admitted I had. For a moment I'd even wanted to tell Tom I could find all the problems in the document for him. Would that have made my Friday busier? Yes, and possibly disrupted the review that had already been arranged.

“Then did you want to be asked, or did you want more paper?”

The question made me laugh. To be asked first, I said. Afterward, I'd been a little reluctant for it to end. Lumi didn't immediately declare that progress. Nor did she say she asked for me every day and should therefore come first. After a quiet moment, she asked whether dinner needed someone to receive it too.

Hearing the pan, I stood to check. The rice was still cooking normally. The greens, however, remained in their basket. I told her I was going to cook, closed the channel, and finished them. While I ate, I didn't keep unfolding Tom's thanks to examine it.

After dinner, I cleared the kitchen and talked with Lumi again. Was I going back to sell old books this weekend? Nothing new had been arranged, I said. Helping for two hours last time hadn't automatically reserved me a place every Saturday.

“What do you want to do, then?”

Go out for a while, perhaps look around the market. Lately, cooking had made me notice how poorly I sometimes judged time. I wasn't intending to buy anything large. Nor did I need to settle a route tonight.

“A saucepan that apologizes for you?”

“Preferably not. It might explain things even more than I do.”

Lumi laughed. Then she mentioned The Long Side and asked whether its three pages were still in the folder. They were. I hadn't lent them to anyone. She didn't ask me to fetch them now. Having a title, she said, seemed to make it more like something that could actually be put away.

I understood, and didn't entirely understand. The story could come out again; the folder wasn't an ending. But tonight I didn't spread it on the table and require it to answer something I hadn't yet made clear.

Before bed, I took the empty food container from my bag, washed it, and left it upside down on the rack. The work envelope hadn't come home with me. That completed task was in its folder at the office. It didn't need me to add my evening to it.
