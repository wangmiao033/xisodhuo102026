# Chapter 460: The Box Isn't for Sale

A small grey car was parked outside the reading room on Saturday morning, its boot open. What Marnie needed me to lift wasn't books. It was two folded tables that wouldn't fit all the way in. She told me to keep my end up so a screw sticking out of the edge wouldn't catch the paint. I backed up the step and understood at last why she hadn't opened the front door. A collection of umbrella stands and a row of folded chairs, brought in the day before, still covered the ground in front of it. We had to take the tables through the side door, put them up inside and then clear the way for people. Yesterday I'd thought putting up a couple of tables would take ten minutes. Nobody had promised me that.

A leg dropped out from under the first table and trapped my cuff. I flattened my hand before pulling it free. The place I'd mended held. Marnie was back at the car for the other table. She called through the doorway to see if I wanted a cracker. She'd bought two bags and discovered when she opened one that they weren't sweet. I said I'd have one when the table was up. She approved of the order of things. When she came back she gave a leg a kick to make sure it didn't wobble, then pushed a cracker into my hand. She took a bite of hers and said it tasted as if she'd dug it out of a pile of salt.

Yesterday's box was under the window, a couple of feet from the tables. I picked it up and caught a finger on a flap of tape when I slid my hands out from beneath it. Seven books weighed more than they looked. The cover of The North Bank on Foot was a dull brass yellow, and its bottom corner had a crease that lay right across my thumb when I held it there. I didn't take the book out to give it a place of its own. I remembered it was the one Tess wanted. I remembered, too, that we'd agreed to leave it to Marnie, not wait until she was out by the car to make that decision easier for myself.

Someone tapped on the front glass twice. I'd been expecting Tess and looked up to see a man with both hands on the door handle and an old canvas strap hanging from his elbow. Marnie asked me to open the door halfway and tell him we didn't start yet. He was looking for the post office, he said. That whole end of the street was fenced off. Was he supposed to come in and wait here for that, too? I let go of the door and showed him how to go back the way he'd come. He ran a couple of steps in the right direction, then came back for the strap he'd dropped on the threshold. Marnie had been standing behind me the second time. She said the light on the left was on now, and since we'd already opened the door once we might as well not keep people out.

Tess wasn't among the first people in. By the time she arrived, a woman in a blue jacket was bending over the box, counting the seven books. Her shopping bag stood by her shoe. There was a square-ended cardboard tube in it. She wanted to know whether SOLD AS A LOT meant the books had to stay together or that whoever bought them had to take the box. She had a bag and would rather leave the box for the next person. Marnie said the price was what it was because Val, who'd donated the books, had wanted the seven of them to stay together if they could. That was exactly what the woman wanted to hear. She looked after a shelf at the community centre on the west side where anybody could take out a book. Some people went walking across the river with one book tucked away, and if they found the others still on the shelf when they brought it back, the next person would have the same chance. She ran a finger over the folded corner. “And this one's here. Good.”

I'd seen Tess when she stood by the box the day before. Today she didn't even push forward to claim a place at the table. Her pencil was still in the breast pocket of her grey coat, a bit of bare wood showing at the top. She told Marnie it was all right if the box had to be sold together. She wouldn't take another six books home just to shut them up in a cupboard. The woman drew her hand back and asked whether Tess had arranged to come for one. Tess said she'd only asked about it. Then she reached inside her coat for a piece of thin paper, folded to the length of a ruler. Between two pale lines of handwriting there was a route drawn so hard I could see the track the pencil had left before I knew what it was meant to show. “My grandfather walked this bit. The book might show a bridge. He remembered a bridge, but he didn't put down which end you could get down from.”

Marnie didn't move either woman's bag nearer the box. She picked up her price card and looked for a moment at a little patch of ink that had bled into it. She was willing to hear another suggestion, she said, but not one that left her to deal with a collection of loose books after everybody took the one they fancied. Under the table there were two boxes that had been there three years. Getting some room under the table was one reason she'd wanted to run the sale. The woman in blue was called Joan. She said she had the money for the lot and would be able to carry it on the bus. But she couldn't put one of the books on the community shelf if somebody needed to take it home and keep it. Other people would be waiting for it.

I was about to say I could take a picture of the page for Tess. I already had my fingers pressed against the edge of the book, but I didn't open it. The bit she wanted might not be on a single page; it might be spread across two books. It was another question altogether whether somebody would want to leave a book they'd just paid for lying spread out on a table while I worked out how to photograph it. I asked Tess if she needed to take the book away or wanted to see first how the bridge was drawn, if it was there. She considered this and said she'd look for the bridge first. Joan took the tube out of the bag and stood it on the floor. “I don't mind her looking. I'd rather not stand here with the bag empty while she copies every page in either direction.”

I borrowed two little wood blocks from the next table to make a lopsided book rest. Tess unwound a scarf from her wrist and put it under the spine herself. Marnie used the price card to mark the space beside the rest and asked us to leave the other books together for now. It was only after we'd done all that that I noticed how much of the table we'd made available for one book that somebody might buy. It was the corner where people were supposed to put down their selections while they went back for another. A man with a pile of detective stories had been standing beside me for a while. He asked if I could leave him somewhere to get his hands free. I moved the book rest in by half a foot and Tess shifted a step. Joan didn't use that moment to ask about the price again. She used her toe to nudge an empty basket back under the table for Marnie.

We found a bridge, only it wasn't Tess's. The book showed a railway bridge. Black dots marked the piers and a note said there was no way through underneath. Tess's grandfather had written three words beside his line: SHALLOW, STONES LAID. Next to them he'd drawn what were plainly little circles you could step onto. Tess held the rubber end of her pencil by one of the black dots, then tried another, and asked whether any of the other books showed the north bank. Joan remembered a grey ferry booklet at the bottom of the box. She'd seen it above the rim before the sale but hadn't picked it up to check. I didn't turn the box over to look. Marnie moved the four books on top herself and took out a narrow booklet held together with crooked staples. It wasn't a tourist guide. It was a collection of hand-drawn maps of the old landing. There, in a stretch marked as shallow water, were the uneven little stones from her grandfather's note. Tess didn't exclaim. She just moved half a step so I could stand where she was and see. On the map the stones went only as far as the sandbar. Water beyond it took up a section of the paper that was almost white. Tess turned her note over to show me a little line he'd written upside down. If it rained on the way back, go round by the main road. She'd always thought he'd put that in because there was nowhere to get out of the rain on the bridge.

Finding the crossing hadn't settled the sale. Marnie set the book Tess had come for beside the ferry booklet she'd used. Tess said she wanted the booklet now. She didn't need The North Bank on Foot. Joan said the booklet hadn't come out of one of the other volumes. If it were gone, the remaining six still wouldn't look like an incomplete set on the shelf. She wanted to know whether anybody else was waiting for one, though, and what Marnie would charge. Marnie thought about it. There was still half a day's sale left. She'd have to make a separate entry and two new prices if she split the books; she didn't try to charge the morning's price for what was left. She asked me to watch the door for three minutes while she got the particulars right. By the time those three minutes had come and gone, I'd sent two more people the proper way to the post office. If she'd only had one purchase to record, she could have got back to her cash box a few minutes sooner.

Joan took six books and Marnie made another entry in her ledger. Tess paid for the ferry booklet, wrapped the scarf she'd put under the book around her neck again, and slipped the thin paper between the pages. She didn't ask me to explain the map to her again. She said, “All this time I thought he walked over a bridge.” Joan heard her and put the handles of her bag together. “If the stones are still there, from the road they'll only look like stones.” They left one after the other, in different directions. I carried the empty box over to the stack. A flap of tape had curled into the bottom and caught the back of my hand for a moment. It wasn't something I particularly needed to keep.

I could have gone as soon as my jobs before the opening were finished. Instead I stayed long enough to hear several people being given change. Marnie took the remaining salt crackers from behind her ledger and told me to take a bag with me. I said I'd have another bite, that was all. She broke a cracker in half and kept the other half herself. Someone at the door asked if we had a big cardboard box. I said we did and went back for it. It wasn't till I brought it out that I saw he'd already chosen a pile of books he could carry snugly between his hands. He didn't want to buy the box. He was afraid the corners would cut a hole in the waterproof jacket he wore to cycle home. Marnie said the empty box wasn't for sale. He could just take it. I held the bottom steady for him while he put the books in, one stack at a time. He thanked her, not me. This time when I slid my hand out, the tape didn't catch.
