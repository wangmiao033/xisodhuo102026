# Chapter 457: The Book Before Closing

When I left work on Wednesday, the clock at the end of the corridor was two minutes fast against my watch. I stood beneath it with my bag already in my hand, waiting for the two minutes to come round. It took me a little while to remember that carrying the book for two minutes longer wouldn't make it a book I had finished. I had left the shirt with the loose thread at home the night before. The cuff on the one I was wearing was fine, but each time I went through a door I pressed my thumb against it to make sure. By the time I went downstairs I had put my hand in my pocket. There was a receipt from the bread shop with nothing on the back. I didn't make it into another bookmark.

I got to the stop in time to see the doors close on the bus before mine. Through the rear window I could see somebody holding a long umbrella slantwise between their knees, its handle across their mouth. When the bus turned, the person in the glass tipped towards me. I don't think they necessarily saw me. I sat beneath the sign and took the slim book of stories out of my bag. I hadn't read the tenth story on Tuesday evening or on Monday. My bookmark was still at the end of the ninth. One hard old fold had left a white line in the paper. I unfolded it a little. It shook between my fingers and fell back on the book.

In the tenth story a man who repaired theatre chairs wanted to see a play across the river. The theatre on the other bank opened only on Wednesdays. That was supposed to be because in winter people could catch the last ferry back with the change of shift at the flour mill. He'd bought a ticket and put it inside the lid of his toolbox. Before he left work he was supposed to return a chair that had been missing a screw to the auditorium. He was carrying it up to the steps when he saw another chair by the door with new red velvet and its back to the wall. It looked exactly like the one he'd fixed. One person said the other chair must be his; another said that there shouldn't be any bare wood showing beneath the red covers. He put his hand underneath and found two different nail heads.

I thought he would carry both chairs in to find somebody who could prove which was the first, miss the ferry, and learn something that would look nice printed on the back cover. The writer didn't send him anywhere of the sort. He put the chair he had repaired in the space set out for it, got his toolbox and went out into the street. Then he doubled back to look at the chair against the wall. There was a theatre ticket caught under its arm, in a spot that nobody had thought to reach into. He didn't take it. When he'd made out the corner of the paper in the dark he called the woman selling sweets over to the door, and told her someone might be looking for something there later. Then he went to catch the ferry. That was where I'd got to when the bus arrived.

When I got on I closed the book around my finger. Across the aisle there was a free window seat. A woman with a parcel wrapped in brown paper sat on the outside. I asked whether anybody was sitting there and she pulled the parcel up off her lap against her chest, so I could edge past sideways. After I sat down I saw a blue bit of tape on one corner. It had been peeled up and pressed back down, and she had both thumbs on it. The person standing in front of us took their hand off the rail to let a child with a backpack squeeze by. Nobody urged the child to turn round. I didn't want my eyes to skip words as the bus went over the road, so I put the book on my knees and didn't read any further. Two stops later the woman was digging for a card. While she was at it I caught the parcel before it could slide down beside the seat. She got hold of it again and took it away from me. There was something inside that made the same little sound every time it moved. She thanked me as she got off. I never found out what it was.

I didn't go straight home. I got out in the street where I meant to buy bread the next day, thinking I'd cut through by the old reading room. On one side they lent out books during the day. The narrow passage beside the other usually let you through to the back street without getting your feet wet when it rained. At the turning I saw three folding stands strung across the first stretch of brickwork. A notice with a stamp at the top kept lifting in the wind and slapping back. The passage was closed for the time being. Somewhere through the door I heard the legs of a table dragged across the floor. I went a little closer to see the closing time and caught the reflection of a streetlamp in the glass.

A woman in a blue apron was holding the door with her elbow. By her feet she had a trolley with a stack of empty plastic crates that had been washed. Was I here to return a book? I said I had one I would be bringing back soon. There was a little of it left to read. She told me they'd closed the side door early today and to come in through the front tomorrow. I wasn't to try to force the book into the metal return slot outside. Somebody had already got a book wedged in there this month and not even a slip of paper had made it through afterwards.

“Will tomorrow still be all right?”

It depended on the date on my slip, she said. She didn't tell me they'd count it a day late because they happened to be shut. I hadn't looked for the little slip since the last time I'd glanced at the date. I told her I'd have to go home and find it. She put another inch on the door with her elbow so the trolley's front wheels could get over the sill. An empty crate at the back was going to hit the wall. I put out a hand to steady it by its rim. She drew the trolley a little way out and straightened it before pushing it in again. The wheels dropped with a thunk on the other side. I'd first taken the crates for the packaging from a new delivery of books, but they'd been swept clean; there wasn't even a label in one of the corners. I let go. She thanked me without asking me inside.

Someone was coming on uncertainly round the three stands from behind me. I turned to tell him he couldn't get that way today. He lifted a hand, looked at me and went another way. When the woman in the blue apron saw me still there she put her head out again. There was no sense in going the long way if it rained. Follow the front wall until I reached the window of the string shop, then turn. I'd never walked that part before. The string shop had put its blind down, but there was still a coil of white line hanging from a hook in the frame. It wasn't much farther than the way I'd been going. There was an extra stretch of brick wall where you couldn't see the lights from the stalls. The last page was still unread when I got home, but I didn't leave the book in my bag that time.

I boiled two eggs and peeled one standing at the kitchen door. The fine inner skin hung on, and I pulled off a little of the egg white with it. I knocked a crack in the second egg on the edge of the table but didn't hurry to peel it. I washed my hands and went back to the book. The man who repaired chairs had made it to the ferry landing. That evening the boat was late. Two people he knew on a long bench under cover wanted him to come over and talk about the theatre. One of them held a fresh ticket. He took two steps in their direction, then went back outside. He leaned against a low wall and fastened the catch of his toolbox. He could hear people talking; he just wasn't where they were doing it. When the ferry came in, he got his ticket out first and waited for the people in front to leave him a gap wide enough for one. The next page said there was a square of light on the water on his left. It broke apart after the bow touched the mooring line. I turned over and there were the publisher's details. I sat under the lamp a few minutes and made sure there wasn't an eleventh story.

As I cleared the table, the date slip slid out from between the spine and the cover and stuck beneath the little saucer I'd used to hold down the eggshells. Half the print had faded, but I could still read Thursday and the two numbers. I carried it up to the lamp and checked it again. Thursday really was tomorrow. I put the slip back in its old place and took out my bookmark. There was a little white spot at the bottom corner of the spine where it had knocked into things from being carried about. I didn't try to wipe it off. My old shirt was on one chair and the other chair would have been just right for the book. In the end I put it into the bag by the door. I slipped a clean paper bag underneath it in case the egg on the table ended up getting near it somehow.

After I opened the channel, Lumi didn't go back to the door. She asked what had made me stop with a little click before I answered. I told her I'd just put my half-peeled second egg back on the plate. The shell was thin and it was still sticking to my fingers. It hadn't sounded the way I'd expected. Had I also had an egg in the morning, she wanted to know. I said I hadn't that day. I waited to see what she'd ask after that. By the time there wasn't anything near me left to put away, she said she was in the mood to hear an ending. I didn't have to tell her a whole book. Last night's door still belonged to somebody else; I hadn't done anything with it. I asked whether she wanted to hear something really come to an end, or hear where the book stopped printing any more of it. She picked the second one.

I told her there was a book that stopped before the ferry got away from the bank. The man had his ticket in his hand. Somebody left a narrow place and there was a chance for him to go through. I didn't get him to the opposite bank myself; you could see the lights moving from where he was, anyway.

“But what about his seat?” Lumi asked.

I tried to remember what page had the chair on it. The man had fixed a chair for somebody and come across the one with its back to the wall. He hadn't sat down himself. I said I'd spent so long thinking about him catching the ferry that I'd forgotten he still had to find a seat on the other side. Lumi said she'd only meant whether he had been next to a bench before that. I laughed out loud. We'd both been asking about different places. I didn't fetch the book to prove it.

Before I closed the channel I ate the rest of the egg. There was one less little piece of white on the plate that I'd mistaken for shell. I thought for a moment and asked if Lumi had understood the part with the boat. She said she wasn't sure whether the square of light had broken up on the water or whether the person looking at the boat had blinked. I told her I could hear it either way. The book was already by the door and tomorrow I'd take it out with me. She didn't ask me to go and read it to her again. The kitchen light that was still on didn't reach the bag by the door. When I got up and turned off the lamp on the desk, I knew it was there all the same.
