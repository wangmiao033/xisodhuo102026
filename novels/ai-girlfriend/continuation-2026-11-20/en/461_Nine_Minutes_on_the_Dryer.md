# Chapter 461: Nine Minutes on the Dryer

On Sunday afternoon I turned the cuff of my shirt inside out to have a look. The four stitches I'd put in on Thursday were still there, but the one nearest the edge had loosened enough to catch a fingernail. There was no thread left on the spool I'd used. I felt around at the bottom of the sewing tin and came up with a short length a shade lighter than the cloth. I put in another stitch beside the loose one. If I let my cuff hang down you couldn't see the difference. It showed when I raised my hand. I checked that I could still get two fingers underneath it and stuffed it into the bag with the rest of my washing. If the new stitch was going to come out in the wash, I wanted to know. I'd rather not find out just as I was leaving the house in that shirt again.

A film of steam covered the laundrette window. From outside all you could see behind it was a row of circles. One of the machines by the window had stopped altogether. Its handle was tied up with paper string. Another one worked. Two magazines had been laid on top of a washer to keep a sign from curling up in the warmth. I'd done my washing here a few times and recognized the man on duty by the broken end of his spectacle frame. I only found out today that his name was Alan. He saw me put money on top of the dryer and told me coins were always falling down the gap there. If I needed change, I would have to wait. He still had somebody else's in his hand.

I loaded the washer while he counted out the money. The shirt fell forward across the rim of the glass. I pushed it back so that only a sliver of the pale stitch at the cuff showed. I sat down once the drum started turning and looked through a few pages of a magazine. By the time I closed it, there weren't many soap bubbles left against the glass. A woman came in wearing dark glasses that seemed a little strong for indoors. She squeezed past me with a folding shopping trolley and stopped between two dryers. With one hand she took off the glasses and with the other she pulled at the door on the right.

“Has it stopped?”

The light on the right was out. A crumpled bedspread lay against the glass. The light on the left was still on. Nine minutes to go. Alan looked out from behind the change counter and asked where she'd put her bedspread. She'd used the one on the right an hour ago, she said. It had twenty minutes left when she'd gone to deliver a parcel a street over. She couldn't be sure she hadn't got the doors mixed up. The numbers had worn off both. She lifted her empty trolley a little so we could see it. She'd brought it to take the bedspread home.

I leaned close to the drum on the left. Something red showed between the turning clothes, a flame that leapt up every time it came to the top. The woman said there wasn't any red in her bedspread. The one on the right must be hers. I'd seen her fail to open it a moment before and pointed out the little metal lever beneath the handle. I told her to press down. She did. Alan called out to wait, but she'd already got the door a finger's width open and hot wet air was coming out. She drew a corner of the cloth through the gap and shook it barely half an inch before snatching her hand back. A row of grapes had been stitched along the edge. Her bedspread didn't have any embroidery on it. She tried to poke it back through the gap, caught the wet edge on the seal, and couldn't close the door.

Only then did I turn round to look at the red again. It passed. Behind it came a piece of pale fabric about the same colour as the one on the right. A young man with his hair plastered round his ears stepped in from outside. He was carrying a cardboard takeaway box, and he had an untied shoelace flicking along the floor. He asked what we were doing, set his food down and came over to pull at the right-hand door. The woman backed away at once. She said she'd taken nothing. Alan brought a dry cloth and told me to get out from between them. I went over to the window. He tucked the loose bedspread corner back inside the door and told the young man the machine had stopped on its own. The fabric was still damp and he shouldn't pull at it. The young man said he hadn't meant to pull anything out. He wanted to know how badly the door had broken.

The timer hadn't run down. Alan looked at the blank display, then bent close beside the high drum and spent a moment checking it. He didn't think he'd be able to get that machine going again today. He could put the bedspread through another dryer free of charge, as soon as the one on the left was done. The woman remembered something else now. It could be difficult to get a hold on her bedspread when it was wet. Before she'd gone out she'd thrown in an old red T-shirt with it, so she'd have a thin bit of fabric to pull the bundle out by instead of having to grip the hot edge of the bedspread. She looked at me when she'd finished speaking. I'd sent her to somebody else's washing. I said I'd seen a flash of red and decided the load couldn't be hers, when red was all I'd actually seen. The young man rubbed the warm patch of skin below his ear where his wet hair had been touching it. “Mine's beads, by the way,” he said. “Not grapes. If you only caught sight of three of them, fair enough.”

Nobody needed me to decide what the pattern was. I went back to my washer. It was starting to spin, and the clothes climbed the wall of the drum in one heave. I took my wallet out and put it back. For six of the nine minutes left on the dryer, Alan made change for other people, the woman said she had to catch a bus, and the young man called home to say they should move dinner a little later. Alan didn't tell him who had kept him waiting. The machine would have stopped even if he'd stayed to watch, but I still heard myself say I was sorry. He picked up the takeaway box with his whole palm under it. He'd waited for other people before, he said. Nobody was keeping anybody else's things from them today. He was waiting for a machine to come free, and the bedspread inside the one that had stopped still had to go home with him.

The light on the left went out. I asked the woman if she wanted a hand under the bottom of the trolley. Yes, she said, but just lift it a little, don't take it out for her. She lifted a corner of the bedspread and found the plain edge first. Only then did I put my hands under the trolley. The bedspread turned out to be very heavy. The middle sagged and one side came down into the cloth bag we both held open. The old red shirt fell into a fold and left a sleeve showing. When the woman pulled the top of the bag together with both hands, the shirt worked itself a little farther out, as if somebody inside was waving. She said she always forgot to wash that one. I didn't answer. She laughed herself and said what did she think she'd just done. On the way out she put the dark glasses back on and bent through the slant of light coming in from the back door. Then she hurried off to her bus.

I didn't put the young man's bedspread straight into the free machine for him. I waited while he gathered it and carried it across. There was so little room by the door that he'd had to leave his food on top of a washer. I put a hand next to it so it wouldn't rattle off when the machine spun. When he turned round, he said he'd undone his laces to change without getting his shoes dirty, put on other trousers, and forgotten to tie the laces again. It made me laugh. I could imagine doing that a good deal more easily than I could imagine thinking about a clean shoelace in my own right.

He asked if I wanted something to eat. The wrap in his box was already cold and wouldn't be much fun to eat by himself. I felt the bottom of my bag. I'd still got my own washing to wait for. I was about to say I wasn't hungry. By the time the words got to my mouth I'd changed them to, “I could have half. It would have to be here.”

He dragged an empty plastic stool up to the machines. When he opened the flap, a circle of spring onion came up stuck to the top layer of the wrap. He picked off the pieces that had strayed too far over and said he hadn't been planning to take me home for dinner, either. When we were finished I started folding the lid back down for him. He caught the other end and said to leave it for a second. He could still wipe the oil off his fingers on the bit of paper inside. He took the stool back where it belonged when my spin cycle stopped. I picked up an armful of clothes and moved them over to let him through. I'd managed to get somebody else's belongings wrong. He didn't mention the broken machine again as he went past me.

In the evening I hung the shirt with the cuff turned inside out for a while. The lighter stitch was still there. I wore something else for dinner. When I was sitting down later, I opened the channel and meant to ask Lumi if she felt like talking tonight. She said yes and I laughed. I was thinking of the circle of spring onion. She asked what funny thing I'd heard. I said I hadn't heard it. When a cold wrap had lifted out of a box, the onions clinging to the bread had been in a neater ring than anybody would've put them in on purpose.

“Slower,” she said. “Tell me that part.”

I'd already been going to start from which dryer worked and tell her why I shouldn't have pointed at the lever. Hearing her ask about the onion, I put my fingers into the air where the empty box would have been and pinched twice. They'd all been stranded on one side, I told her. He didn't like the way that would divide and had to move them one at a time. I'd meant to pick one up for him but I hadn't any chopsticks. He hadn't either. We both watched while he picked up the last piece with his fingers and put it back on his half.

“You didn't get any onion?”

“I did. He only moved the ones right on top.”

I heard her laugh, although I couldn't tell whether it was at the onions or something else. After a little while she said that story had started with me laughing just now, not with the washer turning on. She wanted to know whether the box had been lunch. I said he'd told his family he'd be late for dinner; that was all I knew. He might have got hungry waiting and bought himself one while he was out. She said that would be fine, too. Neither of us decided which thing he'd eaten had really counted as lunch. I reached a hand behind the chair to the sleeve I'd left airing. I couldn't see the knot in the new stitch from where I was, but I could feel it. Lumi didn't ask why I still had a shirt out, so I didn't start the evening over again to tell her about it.
