# Chapter 462: Feet Towards the Front Door

On Monday someone laid twelve handouts on the low table in the front hall. I put my cup beside the third one, even though there was plenty of bare table next to it. My nose was a little blocked. I only caught the smell of the photocopier paper when I bent down. The handouts were meant for people coming in that afternoon. The staples sat so close to the edges you could prick yourself if you turned the pages in a hurry. I moved the cup and caught sight of the first page. Under START FROM THE FRONT HALL there was a drawing of somebody in boots. The boots were big enough to pass for a pair of snow shovels. I didn't open the rest.

A colleague with glasses came through the corridor and hooked a half-open cupboard door shut with his foot. He asked me to do him a favour and not give any of the handouts out as people arrived. They were due at three. He'd told the front desk somebody would take them in. He'd changed the place they were going to meet that morning, he said. It was quieter there. They'd be able to hear one another without raising their voices. Wasn't the place already printed on the paper, I asked. Where they started hadn't changed, he said. Where he was taking them had. They might pick up the handouts and be off after the arrows if we gave them out before it was time to go.

Just before three a man was standing by the entrance with his hands pinning down the ends of his scarf. He wanted to know if he could take a copy yet. He'd arrived early and didn't know what side his friend would come in from. If he had the paper in his hands he'd have something to do while he waited. I told him to hold on. He went two steps towards the window, then came back. “It's not that I want to go in yet. I left my glasses in the car. I want to see how big the letters are. If they're too small there's still time for me to go back for them.”

I pinched the copy nearest my cup and opened it somewhere the print looked dense enough to give him an idea. He bent towards it and pointed at the top right corner. Why wasn't there a page number here? I thought his hand must be covering it. He looked again himself. I'd lifted the back of the last sheet after all. On the next sheet there was heavy black print at one end and something that looked like an upside-down clock at the other. He asked whether he turned left here or turned the whole page round. I laughed once. I'd taken that for a joke. He didn't laugh. He asked again. This time I set the handout on the table and found there really was a page in upside down.

It was worst where there was a map. When I turned the whole thing, an arrow that had pointed left swung round to point right. The line above it came into focus and the line below had its feet in the air. We tried folding the page out of the way without blocking the map on the next sheet. That didn't work. He put a finger where his glasses ought to have been and considered it. He could go and get them, he said, but there wasn't much they'd do for this. His friend came in as he spoke. She saw him looking down and assumed we'd already started. I had to move the eleven other copies back from the far end of the table before they took them. I could simply have scooped the pile up without a word. The man was still looking at the copy I'd pulled open. I said somebody had put in a page upside down, and please don't go where it tells you yet.

My colleague with glasses returned, holding a borrowed glass water jug from somewhere. He looked at the page and didn't ask who had printed it. He said to check whether all the copies had it. If there was time they could replace that sheet. I opened the bottom one and found the very same arrow upside down again. They hadn't put in a wrong page by hand, one copy at a time. The machine seemed to have done the whole job that way. He tried to dig out a staple and didn't get anywhere. The edge of the paper left a fine scratch across the back of his hand. I told him to put it down for a minute. He couldn't bring himself to give people a map with even the page number upside down. He had six minutes until he was meant to start. The copier was down the other end. If he spoiled one sheet, he wasn't certain he'd have time to go back again. And if he had to give a handout to everyone personally, he couldn't wait at the door for the other two at the same time.

The man who'd arrived first had put his scarf in his pocket. He didn't think anybody needed to understand the whole map right away as long as they could find somebody here, he said. If they needed it during the visit, they could take one copy, go as far as the wall together and then open it. I asked him what he hadn't been able to find in the drawing. It turned out he'd been asking on his friend's account. She still had her coat on. She said he'd asked her to come and they'd talked about the part where she wouldn't be able to stand for long. She'd wanted an idea how far they'd have to go. And if he went to get his glasses she might have stayed on her feet by the door waiting for him without knowing where he was. He said he didn't need the glasses any more. He just wanted to know if there was somewhere to sit. He didn't say the map wasn't wrong.

I found two clean chairs in the corridor and brought them back, leaving an opening between them wide enough to walk through. My colleague asked me to choose a copy whose lines I could see well. He drew the short bit we were actually going to walk on a separate piece of paper. Six steps from the hall, two tiles with a join between them, a door on the left as soon as you'd got past. He scrawled over his first attempt and drew it again from straight in front of the hall. At the bottom right I saw he'd put in a pair of feet so small they scarcely had a shape. The man laughed. The feet knew which way to go this time. His friend heard him and loosened her grip on her bag at last. If she could sit down for a while, she said, she could go in without knowing what the whole building looked like yet.

Two more people came in before they started, one with a minute to spare, the other right behind him just as the last minute went. I moved the chair nearest the corridor a little way out to make room beside it for the newcomers. The woman decided she wanted that one. She turned round and picked up the bag she'd left on the first chair. It hadn't occurred to me until then that fetching two chairs wasn't the same as knowing where she'd want to sit. In the end nobody took one of the handouts. The visitors went in along the route he'd drawn. I left the twelve copies in the hall with a separate note on top saying what was wrong so the person on the next shift wouldn't deliver them after all. My colleague went in with the others and left the glass jug behind on the low table. He came running back for it a moment later. I asked him if he'd seen another empty cup. He only knew about the cups he'd been giving people water in. He didn't know which one I meant. I looked at the empty table and realized I'd already taken mine back to my desk. Someone at the front desk reached over to where the jug had been and wiped away a fainter ring next to the one the jug had left. I let him finish without telling him he'd wiped up my cup's mark instead.

When I got home my hand still remembered the touch of a staple on one of the handouts. It hadn't broken the skin or left anything I'd need to cover. I ran my hand under the tap for a bit. Drying it with a cloth, I thought of the man standing by the table asking which way the feet pointed. That wasn't what he'd said. He'd asked whether he should turn left or turn the whole page around. The feet only came into it because my colleague had drawn two very small ones. I hadn't even reached the table for supper, and I'd already joined the two jokes in my head and started working out where to leave a gap so Lumi would laugh at the right moment.

I did open the channel that evening. What I told her was that I'd stopped a pile of upside-down maps before anybody picked one up and walked off. My colleague had drawn a replacement. I'd brought out the chairs, and in the end nothing was delayed very long. It sounded as if I was rather good at solving things. Lumi let me get to the end and asked why I'd laughed before I'd started. She only had that intake of breath in my voice to go on. I couldn't answer straight away. I went through the story again from the beginning: where people stood when they arrived, who had been going to get his glasses and then decided not to. By the time I got to the end, I couldn't remember the line that was supposed to make anybody laugh. Lumi said she hadn't asked me for a second set of minutes.

I didn't quite see what was wrong with telling it like that. Then I held my hands where the man in the afternoon had stood with the ends of his scarf. I didn't have a scarf. I'd only got my clean cloth. The man had raised a finger to the place where he expected the page number. I'd seen him hesitate for a moment because what he took for the first page might be the last. He hadn't wanted me to show him his way round the whole building, even at the start. He'd only wanted to know whether he'd have to make another trip. I told Lumi he'd come in and I'd turned over the wrong paper next to my cup. When I got to my colleague shrinking the boots down to two crooked footprints, I didn't stop and wait. I laughed. There was a little laugh from her, too, a beat late. When I asked her what it was about, she said, “I'm thinking about where you put that cup.”

She said she might like to hear a little more of that kind of thing some other time. I asked what kind. This time I didn't tell her she meant the things I hadn't got all the way through or the things I'd got wrong. She said it could be anything. It could be funny or it could not be funny. The main thing was not to swallow it again just because I couldn't call it an event. She didn't tell me it would make us get on better, or ask me to save up a whole afternoon's worth. What she'd asked for first was the one bit I'd cut without even thinking about it. If I couldn't think of anything next time, she said she probably had something she could tell me first. We didn't settle what time that might be.

I thought of it when I hung up the cloth. I hadn't actually seen the two feet well enough to say they both were feet. One could have been half an arrow left over from when he'd redrawn the second door. I didn't go back to correct myself. If Lumi ever told somebody else about those feet in a story of her own, she'd have to decide which direction they went in. I hadn't given her a map today.
