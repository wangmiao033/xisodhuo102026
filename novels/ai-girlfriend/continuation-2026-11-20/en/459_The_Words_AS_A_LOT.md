# Chapter 459: The Words AS A LOT

On Friday afternoon I'd meant to wait till after work to go to the reading room. I met a colleague who'd come out early on the stairs, and only then saw how much brighter it was outside than usual. She had two slips in a short envelope and asked me to check whether both had been signed. I didn't need to take it from her. She pulled it open sideways and I saw both lines. I nodded. She put the slips away and went ahead of me, without asking whether I was going to post anything myself. The brightness outside wasn't from the sun. A cover had been taken down across the street and the daylight that had been building up on the glass all day had suddenly found a way out. I passed the string shop. Today a slender cord hung beside the door with two little spoons of different colours tied at the bottom. The wind wasn't strong enough to bring them together.

Inside the reading room two low stools had already been set out opposite the counter. Each had a handwritten notice not to sit there. The things above them would be weighed in a moment. Yesterday's glass jar wasn't by the door, but I saw two of the wooden clips wedged between sheets of cardboard to prop up a sloping corner. The tables were still folded against the wall. I didn't open them now. I took off my coat and went to find Marnie. She had a ledger with carbon paper in it, and was using an envelope to cover the boxes already signed, leaving the last one for the man who had brought books. He wore a knitted hat. He pressed his hand to the empty space, stopped just as he was about to sign, and said he'd have to get his surname clear. He couldn't write slowly enough with this pen. Marnie found one with a rounded tip under the counter. He signed at last and carried his books to the space she'd marked out in red beside him. She said it didn't matter if the labels were inside or clipped to the outside so long as she could find them. He looked round at us and put the books down. There was a wet edge on his hat that didn't touch any of them.

After he'd gone Marnie pointed to the big cardboard box near my feet. The edge was soft. I didn't pick it up, but squatted to see whether the whole side had got wet or just the fold had softened. The greyer inner layer was still stiff; only the top of the taped seam had gone fuzzy. I told her she could lift it from underneath instead of letting the weight hang from the sides. She said Val had brought it. Seven books about the history of the local walks, with old atlases and little guides among them. Val hoped they'd sell them together instead of having two books left after everyone had picked through them. Marnie didn't tell me this couldn't be changed. She showed me the list of titles that could be read with the names still covered and said Val hadn't made it a condition on signing; Marnie herself would have to decide. She stood the first book up for me. There was no price on it. I said I couldn't tell one book about the place from another either. Would I just be setting up the two tables tomorrow, then? Yes, she said. Come half an hour early. I could go when the tables were up; she didn't need me at the till. I reckoned it would take less than ten minutes. She put the book back in the box and cut off a length of packing string to see whether it was long enough. The end went round her elbow and came loose again almost at once.

A woman had stopped on the other side of the low stools. She had dark hair and a short length of pencil showing in the breast pocket of a grey coat, with the point up. I hadn't heard her come in. She looked into the box and moved back a step with her coat pocket still open. I thought she'd brought books too. She asked whether there was a walking guide with a dull brass-coloured cover. Not the atlas with the picture of a bridge, but the one with The North Bank on Foot on the spine. There was a fold slanting across the corner of the cover, right at the bottom, she said. You could feel it without the cover breaking apart. The end was on the right. I looked down. The third book leaned sideways. Between two blue ones I could just see a strip of brownish yellow. Marnie lifted it out, held it up from the bottom and turned it towards the woman. She recognised the name without putting her hand straight out for it. That was when I knew she hadn't come to leave a book.

Her name was Tess. She'd asked in two second-hand shops before coming here. She got a piece of photocopied paper from her left pocket. It unfolded to less than half a sheet. She moved her finger slowly along a handwritten road name. Her late grandfather's trail notes told her which way to go round from a certain tree, but never said when the approach to the riveted bridge had been taken away. She thought an old guide might help her find out where the route used to begin. She didn't say she meant to go that moment, or ask anybody else to come with her. “The new route is walkable,” she said. “I want to know where he went in.” She noticed me looking at the copy and turned the writing back towards herself, showing me only the little bit where the bridge was named. Marnie held the book till Tess had seen enough of the cover. She didn't set it down on the corner propped up by the clips.

“How much is this one?” Tess asked.

Marnie looked at the price card that hadn't been filled in. “The sale starts tomorrow. We'd meant to sell this box as one lot.”

Tess drew her forefinger back and laid it across her thumb. “I can't get enough together for a whole box.” The words told me she couldn't, not that she simply wouldn't pay for more than one. After two seconds she said there wasn't room where she lived for a box of books anyway. This was the one she wanted. If it wasn't for sale separately she could go, and there'd be no need to come all this way again tomorrow. I had my hand on a pen we were using for labels just then. I'd only meant to move it nearer to Marnie. She hung on to the edge of the top book and took the pen with her other hand. She didn't say yes or no straight away. She asked Tess whether, if the book didn't sell, she'd be willing to wait and call after the sale. Tess said she hadn't decided whether to leave a number. She folded the paper the wrong way the first time. She pressed it down, found an old crease shining on the outside, and turned it over to fold it again.

Marnie asked me for an unused tag. On its printed side there were the words AS A LOT on the left and INDIVIDUALLY on the right. Whoever had printed the words had supplied a little square to tick beside each. Marnie didn't tick either. On the back she wrote “Seven books. One donor” with the pen she'd just taken. She said she had to think about what she'd meant to do, and wasn't going to settle tomorrow morning's sale this afternoon. Then she motioned towards the inner room for me to move the box. If I had to sort things first in the morning, I could lift the yellow one to check it, but not put a single-book label on it as a matter of course or take it that they'd put it aside for anybody. I didn't press the box against the cardboard the clips were holding up. I got my hands under it. Three steps on, when I had to turn, all the books came together against one side. The box went over a little with them. Tess half raised a hand and lowered it. For an instant I felt like blaming her for not lending me a hand. As the box went past the old tape on the side of the door I realised I hadn't asked, and the two of us had only stood in the same room a little while. She didn't owe me anything.

I came back for my coat on the sill. Beneath the window was an old painted measuring stick that had once been nailed to the door frame to measure people's height. It was lying crosswise now, holding down a stack of waste paper beside the kettle. When I lifted my coat, one sheet moved and I laid the stick flat again. Nobody was waiting for the old paper to be taken out. A slanting bit of a flourish showed beneath one page, no more than could be seen before. I didn't use the opportunity to turn it over. Marnie closed the pen slowly behind the counter. She left the sign she'd written on top of the box and didn't cut a further length of string.

The man in the knitted hat was gone. The envelope still covered the other names in the ledger. Tess stopped outside and asked if I'd tell her the price, should I happen to see it first in the morning. I said I was coming to put the tables out. I wasn't there to sell books. I could ask inside who'd decide, but I couldn't give her an answer before the doors opened. She said she hadn't been asking me to buy it for her. I said I knew. The words hung a while by the door. Then I said “I know” again, the second time no louder than the first. She turned the pencil round and pushed it back into her breast pocket till only a little of the blunt end showed and went off towards the end of the street. Marnie didn't call her back. Outside, the two spoons finally touched. I heard the small sound before I looked up and saw there was no light from the sun left even on the glass.

At home I took the shirt off the back of the chair. The fourth stitch was still on the underside of the cuff and hadn't stretched into a loop. I thought of the box with its weight on my hands. I could lift it with both of them. I didn't know why I still expected the person beside me to reach out. After I opened the channel I told Lumi there was somebody who wanted to buy a book and I hadn't been able to help, or at least I couldn't promise her anything. I only described one turned-up corner of the yellow cover and didn't repeat the names written on the paper. Lumi asked if the person had asked me to buy it. Once I'd told her no, her second question was whether they'd put the book back in one piece.

“We did,” I said. “I got both hands under the box and carried it three steps.”

She said it would tilt if I'd carried it that way going round a turn. I asked how she knew today's box had tipped. She said she didn't know. She'd been thinking of an empty box. I said there'd been seven books in it. They'd slipped only a little. She waited for me to finish. She didn't want to hear how well I'd remembered seven names, she said; she wanted to hear what had made the weight come out differently in my hands. I spread my left hand and looked at the palm. I told her the weight had gone over to the other side. It wasn't as if anybody had suddenly pulled out one of the books. Lumi didn't tell me whether that one belonged outside the box. I folded the shirt and laid it along the top of the chair back. I didn't have to decide this evening which table I'd lift first.
