# Chapter 464: A Dot of Yellow at the Cuff

On Wednesday I put the shirt whose cuff I'd mended in a bag. Last Thursday I'd put in four stitches from the inside without taking the button off. One had come loose on Sunday, so I'd put another beside it in lighter thread. They'd still looked all right after I'd washed the shirt and hung it up to dry. But that morning, when I picked up the shirt, the back of my hand brushed the right cuff and I noticed that the outer corner had opened a little again. I couldn't end the matter by pushing a thread back inside. I rolled the sleeve up again and decided to stop at the alterations shop after work. Then I got halfway out and had to come back for the bag. I'd hung it high to be sure I'd see it. My eyes passed straight underneath.

The alterations shop was next to a button shop. Both doors were propped open. A man at the counter was holding a pair of black trousers. A car was waiting for him outside. Could they just turn up the trousers once and tack them in two places on the inside, then take it out in a couple of days? A young woman sat behind the counter with a piece of chalk in her hand. A faint angle was already chalked on the black cloth. Two tacks would hold the fold for now, she said, but she couldn't let him walk out wearing them like that. They would scratch the inside of his ankle and the rest of the fold could come down anyway.

He set the trousers down. He needed them for an interview in the morning, he said. Did both legs really have to be measured again today? From the way he held it I could see the left leg was shorter. It hadn't been cut straight, either. Somebody behind the counter put a foot down hard on a sewing machine, but nobody came out. The woman gave him a pen and asked him to write down the latest time he had to leave in the morning. He looked at the pen as if she'd handed him something for cleaning his ears. Eventually he said there was a car, yes, but he could tell it to take the other people and make his own way.

I'd put my bag on the counter. I moved back a bit, but I was the only other customer. There was a mirror by the door. In it I saw myself with a crumpled shopping bag and my free hand still braced on the counter. The woman looked up before I lifted it. The trousers couldn't go through until he'd written down the time, she said. It was fine for me to get my shirt out.

I spread out the cuff on the end of the counter by the window. She gathered the loose thread in one finger and pressed along the fold.

“Right cuff. It came loose just past the button. Did you pull it?”

“Partway out, at first. I thought it had caught on the shirt from somewhere else. Then I put in a few stitches from the inside. It's been washed once. The outside is what came loose again.”

She showed me the row of little dots beneath the cloth. I could still recognize some of my stitches. Two next to the lighter thread had slackened until they lay against each other. She could take them in from underneath with thread close to the color of the shirt, she said. Most likely I wouldn't see that part. At the outer corner two fine layers overlapped and the stitch that used to hold them had come undone too. If the corner kept sticking up I'd keep finding things for it to catch on. She quoted me a price and opened a drawer. She took out two spools. One was close to the color of the shirt. The other was a yellow I hadn't expected her to take out, almost ochre.

“Am I supposed to choose?”

“Not yet.” She turned over a scrap with a row of sample stitches. “Almost all of it goes underneath. The corner takes another stitch. I can do it with matching thread and tuck the whole overlap in, or leave one little stitch on the outside to hold the corner where it is. I don't have to press the old fold out that way, and the line of stitching's a little shorter. Bend your hand here and see if it rubs.”

She didn't put a whole handful of cloth on me. She held the edge down with a fingernail, and I touched the narrowest strip with my wrist. There was only a tip of yellow showing at the end. The knot didn't make a lump underneath. I asked why she'd done the sample in yellow. She said the box had every dark color imaginable, but when she practiced she deliberately didn't use a color that matched the cloth. Mistakes were easier to see. I thought she'd only just started learning and was trying to think what to say so she wouldn't notice the assumption. She looked up at me sideways.

“I've been here six years. I'm practicing with my left. My right gets sore pinching heavy cloth for too long.”

The man with the black trousers finished writing the time. He didn't let me see what he'd put. He passed the paper over the counter. The woman asked him to get his shoes off for two minutes. He did look out of the door first. Then he took his shoes off. One of his socks was black on the sole and the other was white. The trouser leg got a little longer when he changed how he stood. If he bent his knee, all the extra half inch she'd left him went to the front. I stood farther back and watched the man in the mirror step back with me. The person at the machine came out and took the trousers and the slip with the time. She picked up a clip from the floor and asked which was the left leg. The man answered straightaway. Then he looked at the leg hanging loose over the side.

“Wait. Do you mean left as I'm wearing them?”

The younger woman laughed through her nose and sent him back onto the step. He didn't have his shoes on yet anyway. He got up again and she moved a clip. By the time everybody knew which leg was which, the car outside had gone.

My shirt was still spread by the window. She held her hand palm up until I turned the cuff back the right way. Light came through the shop front. The cloth looked different with each movement. She drew two scraps together and folded the one with the yellow dot, then moved both into the light. Outside somebody pushed an empty vegetable cart along the pavement. Its wheels struck a chipped brick twice. I looked for the yellow and didn't find it on the first try. She stood and waited instead of pointing it out. I turned over the sample without the yellow. Its edge was tidier, but when I folded it halfway there was a stiff turn in it. It was easier to tell which one I wanted with the cloth in my fingers than it had been when I only listened to her describe it. Somebody in back asked how many pieces she'd taken in. She gave them a number, swept aside the rag she was going to wrap the cutting ruler in, and cleared a patch for my shirt. Nobody pressed her further.

I asked if she could do the inside stitches with the thread like the shirt and the outer corner with the yellow she'd used on the sample. She could, as long as I didn't expect it to become invisible yellow after one wash. It wouldn't last much longer, if at all, than one matching stitch on the outside. The fold on my cuff simply wasn't stiff to begin with and this was one way of keeping it. She told me the same price she'd quoted after looking at the cloth the first time. No extra for yellow, no discount because it was only a dot.

“Then leave a line of it,” I said.

“A dot.” She wrote it down herself. “You say a line now and when I give it back to you I don't want you thinking I missed half the work.”

I hadn't intended to stand there while she did it. She said there was still daylight and it wouldn't take long. If I had somewhere else to go, I could come back another day. There was a short bench in front of the button shop with a wedge of wood under one end. The person in the doorway took in a box of four-hole buttons and put a hand around the noisy corner as it went down. I sat there for ten minutes or so. I saw the man with the black trousers walk to the corner on his own with his phone to his ear, probably arranging where the car was meant to go. He was still laughing when he reached the traffic lights. You'd never have guessed that not long ago one foot was still in a shoe and he couldn't quite wait to get the other one off. It was only after I'd lost sight of him that I remembered I hadn't been in such a hurry after all to cut off that piece of thread I'd pulled out.

When I went back, the young woman didn't hold up the entire shirt for me. She pinched the cuff and asked me to slide my hand in first. I bent my wrist twice. I could feel the inside fold but not its corner. I turned my wrist looking for the yellow. It was easy to miss unless you flattened the sleeve. It sat right at the end of the fold, a little darker than the one she'd put in the scrap. A machine was running. I said it was good and she couldn't hear me the first time. Then the other person took her foot off the pedal, and the woman let the cuff down and asked whether I wanted it wrapped in paper. I told her I still had the old bag. She folded the shirt with the sleeves inside. When I lifted the bag I realized how light it was. Before I went in I'd held it hard enough to make my wrist ache.

After dinner I took the shirt out and laid it on the table. One sleeve covered the other; only the left was visible. I opened the usual channel and said I'd chosen a different color for the right cuff. Only after I'd done it had I realized the woman hadn't necessarily meant to sell me on that yellow by getting it out.

“You thought you were helping her choose?” Lumi asked.

“At first I thought she was asking me to choose so she wouldn't do it wrong. I think she just wanted me to get a feel for it before I decided.”

“So what color is it?”

“Ochre. Like a very thin bit of mustard. Not that bright.” I pulled the corner of the cloth straight. “Just a dot. When my palm's down like this, I can't see it.”

“Do you want to be able to see it?”

I looked at my hand and made the motion twice, without putting my arm through the sleeve this time. “Yes. I just hadn't intended for it to show all the time. I might not see it myself every time.”

“It doesn't sound like you're talking about the shirt,” she said.

I stopped for a moment. Then she added, “I mean you sounded as if you were asking where to put your hand.”

I took my hand off the table. “I thought you were going to say something else.”

She didn't pretend she hadn't understood. She asked what I wanted her to say. I thought how I'd almost forgotten the bag, even though it was hanging at a place I had to pass on the way out.

“I don't know. I don't always want to hear something nice. Now and then I wish you'd start with one thing you didn't like.”

“I'll start, then. Mustard doesn't have to be thin. You say a bit of mustard and people already know what it ought to taste like on their tongue.”

I curled the cuff a little and let it go. “Mustard isn't all hot.”

“Right. Then you ought to say which kind.”

She didn't say the next thing for me. I thought about that while I carried two washed apples over to the sink and took out one for the evening. The place I bit first wasn't sweet. It was a little sour. When I went back I didn't say anything about the cuff. I started by telling her which side of the apple I'd bitten into.
