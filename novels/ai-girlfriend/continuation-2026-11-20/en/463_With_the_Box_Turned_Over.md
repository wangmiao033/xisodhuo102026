# Chapter 463: With the Box Turned Over

At eleven twenty on Tuesday there was one cardboard box left on the table in the front hall, a little wider than a shoebox. The blue delivery slip was caught beneath it. The white paper on top said SECOND FLOOR, TRAINING, with a whitish streak in the tape above the last few letters. I took it upstairs. Somebody came out of the training room just then, holding the door with his elbow and a half-finished coffee with the hand he had left.

“Under the window?”

“Just don't cover the floor outlet. Thanks.”

They were moving chairs inside. I set the box down beside the others. There was a row of dusty clips along the windowsill. Someone was clipping white sheets up and asking whether printing THIS PAGE INTENTIONALLY LEFT BLANK on a page that was demonstrably no longer blank wasn't being a little pushy. I laughed. The remark stayed with me on the way downstairs. It was later that I remembered there was now somebody who had actually asked whether I'd like to tell her things like that.

Before lunch, the woman in the front hall asked me if I'd seen a box of blue clips.

I went to look by the window first. I'd picked up the idea in one place and went straight back there to look for it, passing both the cabinets downstairs where we put odds and ends. It wasn't until my hand was on the training room door that it occurred to me to ask what the box looked like. There was nobody inside. The tables had been pushed together, exposing a darker patch of carpet where the boxes had been. There wasn't so much as an empty plastic sleeve left. A lid was caught upside down on the edge of the bin out in the hall. It looked like the one from the cup the man had held while he got the door for me.

I went back down and asked. She turned her phone toward me. She'd taken a photograph of the table herself. There had been two other boxes on it and a cleaning cloth spread out beside them. The one at the far right was the one I'd carried upstairs. Two dents ran parallel to each other on its upper corner. I could see a finger's width of blue paper sticking out from underneath.

“That's for the training room, isn't it?”

She moved my mug a little before she spoke. “No. The afternoon group needs it. The training room collected theirs this morning. That's an old receiving label on top. Nobody got it all off. I saw the box had come and took a photo so I wouldn't have to make another call about it. Did you take it?”

“Yeah. I took it up to the training room.”

The elevator dinged in the hall. I thought there must be a softer way of describing what I'd done. I went by the label, for instance, or somebody up there told me where to put it. But she'd asked whether I took it, and half my attention had already gone to considering whether to start the first sentence with well. I didn't say anything more. She looked at the time. The blue clips weren't expensive, she said. There were blank attendance cards inside that went into the clips, counted out by group. Finding another box of clips in the supply cabinet wouldn't fix it. The bags had to go upstairs at twelve thirty. We didn't have to find it this minute, but if we couldn't find it, we should say so early.

I went to find the people who'd been moving the chairs. They hadn't been making space for an afternoon session. They were giving the room back at twelve. They'd divided the things on the carts into two lots, one to go into the small meeting room, the other for building staff to take back to storage. The man with the coffee was counting extension cords by another door. He looked at both my hands when he saw me coming and smiled.

“Don't tell me we just moved the wrong room.”

“One box,” I said. “I may be able to bring it back with both hands.”

He pointed at the pile for storage. There was a box upside down on top, about the right size, with the tape folded down around the corners. I reached for it. A black triangle showed at the bottom. That was printed on the cardboard. It wasn't a pair of dents and it wasn't blue paper. I had my hands on either side of the box by then and let go quickly. I knew he didn't want to unload the whole stack on the strength of one box being roughly the right size. The three below it were marked in handwriting of various sizes. Only the one on top still had a shipping shop's logo printed on it.

“Are you sure it was in this pile?” I asked.

“I'm sure I carried all these from under the window. The three on the right went on the floor first. I can't tell you who took those after that. Want me to ask?”

He'd only counted four cords. I told him to finish and went to the small meeting room. A woman there was trying to knock free a jammed stapler, using a spoiled printout to protect the desk. I didn't touch any of the lids. I asked whether she'd brought any boxes over from training that morning. She said hers was the cart with the black handle. Someone had brought a few more boxes afterward from the right-hand pile. One of them hadn't sat level, so she'd turned it over to put the flat side down.

The old label had gone underneath when the box was turned over, and the blue slip had come into view. A plastic tray covered most of it now. I crouched. The bit of paper sticking out said AFTERNOON GROUP. At the bottom I could see the two dents. I asked if we could lift the tray. Without looking up, she gave the stapler a knock against the spoiled printout. A last piece of staple slid out before she came over.

“Sure. Don't lift it too high. There are little wheels in there and they'll roll out.”

We raised one end just enough. The box was a little heavy when I slid it out from underneath. I could see that the seal was intact without setting the box up on the desk. She helped me read the slip carefully.

“That's it,” she said when we'd finished. “Can you leave a note that you took it? The next one back in here'll think we lost it.”

She pushed a sheet of paper toward me. I wrote down what I was taking and when, and put my name near the bottom. She pointed at the pale grey board and told me to put the note there, not on the desk. Her spoiled printout had four staple holes in it now. On my way out I nearly ran into the person who'd borrowed the cart with the black handle. He moved over against the wall. I checked the floor number on the blue slip and didn't have to look at the old side again.

The woman in the front hall had packed the group's things into paper bags. The spot for the blue clips was empty. I set the box on the table with the writing toward her. She felt the seal.

“Good, it hasn't been opened. Will you hold a bag for me?”

I held the one at the far left. She cut through the tape and took out the blue clips and a stack of cards in batches. The bottom corner of the last paper bag curled out and wouldn't let it close. I held it while she got another bag. It was past noon now, but we hadn't reached her twelve thirty. Only then did she tell me she ought to have pulled off the black label in the morning. She'd walked down the corridor thinking she should do it. On the way back, somebody asked her where the bathroom was, and she ended up holding a slip with directions on it and forgetting which piece of paper she'd meant to pull off.

“I just didn't turn the box over,” I said.

She didn't tell me it was fine. She picked at the tape with her thumb and got the whole old label off. It made no sound when she dropped it in the wastepaper bin.

“Turn it over next time. You can still get a hot lunch.”

All I had for lunch was a sandwich in my bag. The bread had been crushed on the ride in. I went upstairs to check. The collection note was still in place in the small meeting room. On the way past the cleared training room I heard the man who'd held the door for me ask again, from the doorway, “Wasn't this page supposed to be blank?”

“That was the page we left for all the things we say without meaning them,” somebody said. “Why'd you print two?”

I stopped for a second to laugh and hadn't realized how close I was to the door. Somebody inside looked out through the glass and raised a hand. I put mine up too. I didn't call him out.

That night I emptied the water left in my cup and opened the usual voice channel myself. I waited a while, then said, “I actually remembered something pretty boring today.”

“Boring to you, or something you think would bore me?”

“The beginning's a little embarrassing. About halfway through lunch it stops being embarrassing.”

I told her about the two labels and how pleased I'd been to set the box down quickly after looking at the one on top. She asked whether anybody stopped me when I took the box the second time. I said nobody stopped me, but I did leave a note. She laughed.

“You saw the blue slip. You don't have to tell me whether the person who stuck it on looked pleased. You can start with what you ate.”

I told her about the crushed sandwich. She asked what kind it had been originally. I said it had been ham originally, too, but the olives had been squeezed out. One rolled under a desk leg. They'd printed two copies of THIS PAGE INTENTIONALLY LEFT BLANK upstairs, and neither one of those pages could be used to wrap an olive.

“Actually, you can use one,” she said. “You'd have to change the title, though.”

“To what?”

She thought about it. “THIS PAGE DIDN'T GET A CHANCE.”

That did make me laugh hard enough to catch my breath wrong. I'd emptied my cup too early, so I went to fill it again. When I put it back on the table she asked which line had made me laugh. I dried the rim and said, “Next time I hear somebody say it's intentionally blank, I'll probably think of what's under the desk.”

“Then I don't have to remember the man's name.”

I sat down with the cup. “I don't know his name either.”

“Well,” she said, “that's one easy joke to carry around.”

We didn't talk about the box after that. I drank half the water. It occurred to me that I'd told her the most embarrassing part and hadn't waited for her to say I wasn't stupid. She hadn't said it, either. I put the cup I'd need again the next day in its place. There was a strip of bare tabletop left, and I rested my hand flat on it for a little while.
