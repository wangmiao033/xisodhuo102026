# Chapter 465: The Price Tag Was Dry

On Thursday morning I wore the shirt I'd had mended. When I went to do up the right cuff, the pad of my finger caught on a little hard spot. I took it for another loose thread and turned the cuff over. It was the speck of ochre near the button. It sat firmly where it had been put. On the way to work I held my bag with one hand and the rail on the bus with the other without turning the cuff under again.

On the way home the driver put me down before the stop. Two buses ahead of us were blocking it, and none of them could get near the sign. I followed the line of shops. The overhang there was about as wide as my arm, deep enough to walk along under it if I kept close to the wall, not deep enough for two people to pass shoulder to shoulder. A man with graying hair was selling prints from a folding table. He had six or seven in clear sleeves laid out in a row; on the left an open, shallow cardboard box held the cheaper ones unsleeved, each separated from the next by tissue. A price tag was clipped to the rim of the box.

I'd often seen him set up there. That day was the first time I stopped. It wasn't for the prints. A few drops of rain, absurdly large, had just struck the pavement. I wanted somewhere to get my umbrella open while the crowd from the bus went past. The man was already bent over, pulling out a bag big enough for his stock. The bottom was resting on the ground. He felt it with the back of his hand and, finding it damp, pulled it a little farther in.

“Want me to hold the umbrella over you?”

“Over the box, if you could. On the left.”

He meant to slide the whole flat box into the bag. I moved half a step left, just as somebody rode a bicycle along the edge of the street. He didn't ring his bell. The sound of the tires in the water made me look over my shoulder. I tilted the umbrella inward to keep its tips from sticking out. Water running down the panels gathered into a stream and went straight into the box. Before the tissue had time to turn dark, it puckered upwards like a white fan pushed open from beneath.

I reached for the box. The man told me not to pick it up, but by the time I heard him I'd already put my thumb down on the top corner. The edge of the paper sprang up a little when I lifted it.

“Umbrella out again,” he said.

I brought it back over my head. He got the mouth of the bag open at last and slid the box out of the rain first, then put the stack of sleeved prints away. By then I'd already thought of saying I hadn't found my feet properly after getting off the bus. Both of my feet had been planted just fine. When he said the left side, I'd heard him well enough, too.

“I tipped the water into it,” I said.

“I saw.”

He didn't look up. He asked me to lift the lid a little, then began separating the damp sheets. The top tissue had stuck to the print beneath it. Twice he pinched the edge, but he never tried to pull it free by force. He found a clean cloth in the side of the bag and slipped it under the wet corner of the print. The rain was coming down in smaller drops now. It made hardly any noise against the bricks. I could hear a trolley passing behind us, its little wheels making two close clicks at every seam in the pavement.

“How many are spoiled?” I asked.

“Have to look first.” He touched an edge of the second print with the back of his hand. “You can go wait in the bus shelter if you like. You don't have to stand out here while I count.”

I stayed. He lifted the second one and showed me that only the tissue had got wet. The print came free with a little care. The tissue on the third hadn't even been touched. One by one he slid them into spare sleeves, all except the top print. The tissue had only stuck along a narrow strip. With the cloth underneath, he asked me to hold its other end. He lowered his wrist and eased the tissue away along the edge. There was nothing on the wet sheet. He held it up to let me see, then started folding it and thought better of it; he didn't want to squeeze the water out. On the ground the bag was still open, its mouth opening and closing in the wind. He told me I could let go now. I took a look at the print. There was a raised white line in the lower right corner, whiter than any other part of the paper. I hadn't put my thumb down on the ink, but that didn't mean the corner had come through whole.

A woman who'd looked over the table earlier came back then. She said she wanted the riverside print and already had a few notes in her hand. He asked which riverside print. She pointed to the one next to my hand. Only when I looked down did I see that the black below the arch was a reflection, not stone. The water had been cut in a few flattened lines. There was a little uneven break in the one closest to the edge. She meant to put the print inside her father's birthday card. The man showed her the wet corner; he wouldn't just slide it into a bag and hand it to her. She crouched to see it from the side. After a while she said the card was narrower than the print anyway, and cutting off the bent corner would look wrong. Did he have another from the same block? Not in the box, he said; he'd have to print it another day. She put the money away. She did it quietly, without sighing or looking hard at either one of us, and asked if he would be there next week. If the weather was good, he said. On her way off she added that he shouldn't hold one for her yet. She didn't know when she'd get there.

I put the umbrella in my other hand. “I'll pay for that one. Do you want the full price?”

He took the little tag off the box and felt the back. “Well, this hasn't got wet at all,” he said, and didn't answer the question. My hand was in my pocket already, on the money I'd folded over twice. I nearly took all of it out. I knew that even if I paid right now, he couldn't give the woman a fresh print on the spot. But for a second all I wanted was for him to name a price. I wanted him to take the money and say we were square.

“What if I'd like the print?” I asked.

He turned and looked at me. “Would you like it, or would you like something to show for helping?”

My right hand was still in my pocket, the cuff caught at the opening. When I lifted my wrist the ochre stitch showed. I wiped my palm against the cuff, although the palm wasn't especially wet. I said I'd only really looked at the print just now, that I hadn't recognized it as a river until the woman pointed it out to him. I liked the black under the bridge. I didn't know where I'd put it. I'd still take it if I flattened it and the crease stayed. As for the price, I wasn't going to set that for him.

He put the tag on the edge of the table and went to see whether any water had come through the bag. I had time then to take in the rest of the print. There was a rail and a flight of steps down to the river. One looked as if it were missing, its tread hidden by a drainpipe that ran diagonally across it. Inside the rail was a little white shape. I said it looked like an upside-down glove. It was a loose bundle of newspaper coming half open, he said. The person who cut the block had told him. I looked again. The shape didn't grow any writing on it.

The man came back with a piece of brown paper in his hands. He would take half the original price. The other half was what it cost him to learn not to put his bag on the ground next time. I thought of saying that the bottom of the bag had nothing to do with water pouring into the box. He was running a thumbnail down the brown paper to make a vertical fold, leaving a little space where it might otherwise rub the corner of the print. I held my tongue. I counted out the money in two passes, and he found me a small coin in change. I took it. I didn't leave it deliberately on the table.

“Don't roll it up,” he said. “Set it out when you get home. Wait until it's dry before you put a weight on it. If you do it now you'll just get the dry cloth wet and the paper won't improve any. Keep it level in your hand if you're using your umbrella, not under your arm.”

With the parcel on my forearm and the umbrella in my other hand, I went round the first puddle. He called to me. I turned back, thinking I must still have hold of it wrong. He lifted the price tag and asked if I'd taken my change. I said I had, and gave my pocket a shake for him to hear. It made no sound. The coin was already wedged tight in the little fold of notes. He nodded anyway and began to take the table down. By the time I reached the corner, two people carrying food containers came past me on their way to meet the bus. I moved the empty side of the umbrella over a little for them. This time I kept it level.

I unwrapped the brown paper at home. No more rain had reached the print inside, but the corner had begun to curl. The one place I could make some room for it was by the window. I moved two closed books out of the way and set it down there. I put a cloth under the wet corner, not over it, and didn't try to take the white line out by force. At this angle there was more black in the belly of the bridge than I remembered. The ink couldn't have got any bigger over a few steps. I went to hang up my bag and came back to look again. The patch of black still seemed wider than it had on the street.

After supper I opened the voice channel. Lumi asked what I'd like to talk about. I'd meant to tell her that the man had even given me my change. The first thing that came out instead was that I'd got a print wet.

“Do you want to tell me how it got wet,” she asked, “or about the print?”

“How it got wet, today.”

I told her about the stop, the umbrella, and the man who'd insisted on riding past behind my back. Halfway through insisted I stopped. I hadn't actually seen how he rode. He'd come up behind me without touching anybody; that might simply have been his line through the water. Lumi didn't suggest a better-sounding way of putting it. She asked how the print was now. I said I'd bought it and the corner was going up. She asked what was in the picture to begin with. From against the back of the chair, I could see the white crease and not the underside of the bridge. Somebody meeting it for the first time didn't have to hear about the spoiled corner before anything else. I thought a little. I'd look at it myself tonight, I told her. Another day I might want to describe it.

“Okay,” she said. “Have a look first.”

The light had been on for a while by the time I closed the channel. I pulled the window almost shut, and the corner of the print stopped moving. I didn't use the two books to weigh it down. I put the brown paper away and went back to my chair. When I changed my clothes I emptied my pockets. The little coin was still caught between the notes. I shook them open and it rolled half a turn by the chair. I picked it up and put it in the change dish. It struck the other coins on the way in. This time I heard it.
