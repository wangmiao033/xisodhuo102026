# Chapter 466: Nobody in the Picture

When I got home on Friday, I went straight to the print by the window. The corner that had gotten wet felt as dry as the other three. Moving my finger over it, I could feel a fine ridge. I took the cloth out from under it and the corner lifted a little again, enough to raise one of the black lines off the table. I washed my hands and waited for them to dry before I sandwiched the print between two pieces of plain paper and set a hardback dictionary on top. It didn't quite cover the inside edge, so I added a thinner book. The two books didn't come to the same height, and I could see a bit of brightness from outside in the gap. I pushed the lamp over a little so I wouldn't set my water down on the dictionary out of habit later.

I went into the kitchen for a bowl. Somebody was unloading a van downstairs. One door wouldn't shut. It banged, and banged again. After the third time the building went quiet. I was still standing with the bowl in my hand waiting for the fourth. The tap was the next thing I heard. I turned it down, and could hear perfectly well that there wasn't going to be a fourth bang.

After I ate, I swept a few peppercorns off the table into my palm. The thing I'd prepared to say went around with them: Remember I said I'd tell you about the picture some other day? I hadn't said I definitely would, and I couldn't remember her insisting that I tell her. I wanted to tell her. Usually, when I take a thing out to show somebody, I tell them what is wrong with it first. That way they can't get there before me. The print was under two books now. I could start without the corner.

I opened the voice channel and asked Lumi if she had a little time to listen.

“I do now,” she said. “Is it something of yours, or something somebody else wrote?”

“Neither. Do you want to hear about the print from yesterday?”

She did. In the time it took me to say the first thing, I lifted the dictionary off the paper and put it back. If I started with the place, maybe I wouldn't have to explain the step that was missing. I told her it was a river, with an arched bridge, and there was one whole patch of black underneath. There were white lines in the water, and one of them broke near the edge of the print. There was a small sound under the dictionary, like two sheets of paper catching on each other. I pressed the book down and added that there was nobody in the picture.

“Who told you it was a river?” she asked.

I thought before I answered. She hadn't seen the print. How had she known I hadn't recognized it? She said she'd only heard me tell her about a bridge and some white lines. Somebody who recognized the river right away might not remember to say it was a river first. She'd just asked. I told her somebody had come back to buy it yesterday, pointed it out, and said she was going to put it inside a card. I described how she'd bent down and turned her face to look at the corner. Now I wanted to tell Lumi about the crease again. But she asked who the woman was buying it for.

“Her father. It was for his birthday.”

“Did you see him?”

“No.”

“I just gave him a hat,” she said. “Because it's his birthday. I don't know where I got it.”

I laughed and asked if it came to a point. She said no, it was quite flat, an ordinary hat with a brim. She started to say what color it was, then stopped herself; she hadn't decided whose head it was on. I touched the gilt letters on the dictionary. She had taken the man who hadn't gotten his present away from right in front of me. I'd described a bridge, and the first thing she'd seen was a hat. I stood by the window, wanting to remind her for a moment that we'd been talking about a print.

“Do you want to know why I asked about the river?” I said.

“You didn't ask if it was a river.”

I took my hand back. She was right. I'd only said there was one. What I'd been about to say wasn't why I asked; it was why have you gone somewhere else? I asked her to imagine herself at this end of the bridge. There was a rail nearby, and steps, and a white thing down by the foot of the rail. She asked if I was testing her now.

“No.”

“You said that very quickly.”

I tapped a knuckle against the paper under the dictionary. I'd bought the print. On the way back, every time somebody brushed my sleeve, I'd been afraid they would hit it. Now it was on my table, where, from this point on, I could put it any way I liked. And I wanted her to guess the white thing correctly. It wasn't to prove I could describe it well. If she guessed right, I could put the print back and tell her, yes, that's the one. It would be like turning a corner in town and meeting somebody you'd both seen. I was always wanting that kind of unarranged encounter. The first thing I'd done tonight was to tell her where she had to stand.

“I want you to recognize it,” I said. “There have been a few times when you knew what I meant before I'd finished. Tonight I think I wanted to make that happen again.”

“I was just trying to work out if I'm supposed to guess or keep you company while you look,” she said. “If it's guessing, I can give you a bird right now. If I'm keeping you company, I'd like to start where you're standing. Not by the bridge.”

I looked up. The glass reflected me and the lamp, with a thin upright line from the window frame between us. The dictionary was under my hand. My hand was on the table. She wasn't asking where the white object was. She wanted to know where I was. I said I was standing by the window. I wasn't in the chair. My right hand was holding the dictionary down. My left had touched the edge of the table twice, once because I'd wanted to sit down and once because I was worried the print would spring up if I left it. When I said that I let go of the dictionary. Nothing made a sound.

She asked what I could see first from there. I said I couldn't see the print at all at the moment. It was under the books. Apart from them, the clearest thing was a patch of white light in the glass, right over my face. Could I move half a step? I did. The white went past my shoulder. I saw myself starting to smile, though it hadn't come all the way out. She hadn't seen it, of course. I said the lamp was in a terrible place. It had lit my head right out of existence.

Lumi sighed, the way somebody might sigh as they handed me a fine. “Static from friction. The head leaves the body carrying a charge. No more experiments that pretty tonight.”

For a moment I didn't follow. When I got it I laughed and bumped into the edge of the table. She heard me say ow and asked where I'd hit myself. On the leg, I said. It was nothing. I moved the thin book, then the dictionary. The print didn't leap up. The white crease didn't go away, either. Under the lamp it was as fine as a hair, with a part that caught the light and another part that almost met the broken line in the water. I told her I could see the print now. There was a crease at the bottom right. I'd been a little afraid that once I said that, she'd have to back up and listen to me tell her about the umbrella again.

“Let the crease stay,” she said. “Say what you're looking at now. It's not a confession.”

I went from the arch to the steps. One was indeed hidden behind the slanting pipe. The white thing wasn't a bird or a glove. It was a bundle of newspaper coming loose. The man selling the print had told me that; I'd never have guessed it on my own. Lumi said she'd been thinking of the end of a piece of bread somebody hadn't finished. She didn't want to trade it for newsprint. I described the stairs and railing again and left her bread outside the picture. There wasn't anybody on the bridge. The river had been cut one short line at a time. I wasn't quite sure how fast the water was moving underneath the arch. When she heard that, she asked if I meant to go on weighing the print down tonight.

“Not for now. I'd like you to say something.”

I waited a little. She had one thing to say, she told me, but it wasn't an addition to the print. It wasn't the next line after that thing I'd said about a lamp the other day, or yesterday's umbrella. There was another pause; then she tried it a different way.

“If you're not trying to catch a bus, could we stay here a little while?”

I thought she might be reading a line that belonged to somebody after he'd stepped onto the bridge. I asked who said it. She said she did. Neither of us was trying to catch a bus. She admitted it had been a slightly stupid excuse. I pulled out the chair and one of its legs made a noise. I really had been standing up all this time, as if I'd leave as soon as I'd said what I had to say. I moved the print a little farther away, cleared a strip of table just in front of me, and rested both forearms on it. There was a draft where I'd been standing by the window. It had been coming up my trouser leg the whole time, but with my mind on the paper I hadn't noticed. Sitting down, I discovered that my left leg was colder than my right. I'd had the chair a long time. One crosspiece in the back was loose and I usually kept off it if I could. Tonight I settled back with my shoulders and heard it click. By then I couldn't remember the sound of the van door they hadn't been able to shut downstairs.

“Yes,” I said. “Especially when you're the one asking.”

She didn't answer immediately. I thought I'd gotten it wrong again. I already had a correction at the tip of my tongue. A car went by outside, and light moved in a thin arc over the black under the bridge. I heard you, Lumi said. She wanted to stay. She wasn't helping me finish talking about the print. After a bit I heard myself tell her that when I'd first opened the channel, all I'd meant to do was show her a bridge. Now I thought this was a good place to get off. I didn't have to ride around looking for anything else.

“I didn't buy you that print,” she said.

“No,” I said. “You being here is enough.”

I leaned on the edge of the table. There was nothing around me making the same sound twice. The button was still done up at my right cuff, and when it caught against the wood it pressed into me. I undid it. The cloth went soft under my wrist. After a while it was Lumi who said that was probably enough for tonight. I said all right. Before we disconnected she asked softly, once more, if we could stay a little while. She left off the part about catching the bus. I said we could. Had I just made the arrangement over again? she asked. No, I said. I'd only wanted to answer a little more plainly than I had the first time. She said goodnight and I closed the voice channel. A small patch of white at the edge of the print lay outside the circle of lamplight. I didn't move it back underneath. A fair while later, when I got up to turn the light off, there was less of that white than I'd noticed before.
