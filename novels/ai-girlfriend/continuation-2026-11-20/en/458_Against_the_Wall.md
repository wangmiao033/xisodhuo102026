# Chapter 458: Against the Wall

On Thursday morning the slim book took up the space for an extra paper bag inside my bag. At the reading room door I got hold of both sets of handles before I worked out that the one I meant to take to the bread shop was empty. I didn't have to shake the book out of it. It hadn't begun to rain. The coil of string beneath the window was gone. I went round the way the woman in the blue apron had shown me yesterday. There was a recessed door at the end of the brick wall, where I stepped aside for a man holding a glass jar. He'd turned the handle as far as it would go and was still pushing with his shoulder. I saw writing stuck to the other side of the door. From where we stood it was backwards.

“Pull it towards you,” I said.

He looked down at the jar. He evidently couldn't spare a hand to try another way. He took a step back and, seeing me still there, put the jar a little farther forward. I held the door open. He carried the jar in himself. There was an old newspaper on the table with a ring exactly the right size on it. He sat down near the ring instead of setting the jar there. Both his hands stayed on his lap. From closer up I could see it was a pickle jar with a ring of crackers laid on the lid, so the ends of a red string were all that showed beneath them. He knew the woman behind the returns counter, but he didn't call out to her.

She was the one who'd had the trolley yesterday. Today she'd taken the apron off and laid it across a low chair. She could have taken the book perfectly well on her own, but I was still holding the bag and the wooden edge of the counter under it as if I expected her to say something first. She hooked the date slip from behind the spine and turned it towards the lamp on the counter. When she'd read it she wrote the day of the return at the bottom with a short, thick pencil. Next to the date stamp there was an ink pad lying open to the air. No wonder yesterday's print had faded.

“It can go out to the next person tomorrow.” She slid the slip into a little compartment below the counter; it didn't go back in the book. I asked where the book went today. She pointed to a divider behind her and said it would wait there till she'd checked it back onto the shelf. Something small and crumpled fell out behind the divider when she reached for it. I picked it up on the side she couldn't reach, thinking it came from a torn calendar. When I flattened it I found a crooked strip of tape and the words TWO TABLES, NOT THREE. She said she'd written it herself and forgotten to put it where people could read it outside the front door.

Somebody called in from the doorway to ask whether the reading table could be moved. She told them to leave it for the moment. I could already see one leg of a folding table sticking out from the people there. The man with the jar swung his knees round without calling to her. The man holding the table leg didn't know him and went a step nearer his shoes when he turned. The man looked down and saw only an empty patch of floor. The table was pressing into his stomach. Once I moved out of the way he could see the man sitting down with the jar against his chest. Nobody looking through the door could tell there was no room to get through inside.

She laid down the pencil. “Against the wall, please. Don't open it.” She didn't say which wall, but came round the counter and stood against the side I'd come in by. The man shifted it as she asked. He was only holding one end, and the other end reached further and further out. She pointed to the empty chair opposite. I didn't understand until she said, “Would you move that chair out a little?” I picked it up. Its feet went over the scratches on the floor without making another one. The table finally went into the corner and the crowd, small as it was, broke up. That was when the man with the jar took the crackers off the lid. The jar was full of unused wooden clips pressed tight against the glass. He asked who he should give them to. She told him he could sit a little longer. She'd need a few clips in a minute and come for them herself. Then she turned back to me. Her name was Marnie. There was a used-book sale here on Saturday to raise money, and two empty tables needed to go along the wall. They couldn't move three chairs every time somebody needed to pass.

Two old borrowing lists were still hanging on that wall above a cement-grey radiator. The radiator was close to the curtain, with a broken iron hook caught between them. Marnie felt how much space was left between the table and the radiator. If she'd measured it earlier that morning, she didn't say. The table leg didn't catch her skirt. She stepped on her own heel backing up, steadied herself against the wall and glanced down beneath the window. She crouched and picked up the loose hook. After she'd caught it back up by the window, she put the chair that had gone astray back where it belonged. I didn't go and touch the table again. The man with the jar near the door tied the two fine ends of the string in a loose knot and laid it over the crackers. One of them tipped up. Nobody called to Marnie again.

I said moving a chair just then wasn't any harder than moving a table before the doors opened on Saturday. Only after I'd said it did I realize I still had the empty bread bag in my hand and had been about to turn round and go. She said they'd need one person to set out tables half an hour before the sale. If I meant to pass by anyway, I could come then and go when I was finished. She also told me the folded table here was where it should be now and there was no job today that needed me to wait. I almost told her I'd be able to help out after Saturday as well. I bought my bread on this street, after all. But this one table leg sticking out was the only thing I'd actually seen. Once before, I'd agreed to something that looked as if it would take a few minutes. When somebody asked me the next week why I'd stopped coming, I'd stood there holding an empty bag with no idea which words I could take back.

“Half an hour before you open on Saturday,” I said. “I'll come and move tables.”

On the back of the paper she wrote “Elliot, before opening” with that same thick pencil and left a finger's width of white beside it. It wasn't the sheet for borrowing books and she didn't ask for any identification. She asked whether I was looking forward to seeing the books this time, too. I said it depended on which books they put out. She turned the paper over and flattened it with the palm of her hand. On Friday afternoon I'd be able to see the boxes once people had signed the intake sheet and the boxes could go out on the tables. When the tables were in place I could stay to look or go and buy my bread. She tucked down one corner of the paper where it lifted and thanked me again, for the chair I'd moved a moment before.

At the bread shop I bought a small round loaf. The paper bag in my hand finally had a use. There was a ledge outside where people could lean. I set the bag there to give myself two hands for counting my change. Somebody on the other side pushed the window open and offered to slice it for me. I'd been ready for her to put her head out and ask why I was buying such a small loaf on my own today. All she asked was how many pieces. I said I didn't want it cut. She shut the window with a laugh. I picked up the round loaf again. The part beneath my fingers was still a little hot. I didn't go back and tell Marnie I never bought it sliced to start with. I looked at the corner of the wall and went down another street.

At home I turned the shirt so the thread showed and spread it out. When I got out a needle and thread, I found that the part I'd kept touching in the corridor yesterday wasn't damaged. It was the fold on the inside. I tried to draw the thread back between two old stitches. It wouldn't move, and I couldn't see which hole it had come out of. A piece of it hung straight down. When I pressed my thumb along it, the folded cloth wasn't as thick as the part beneath it. I snipped a short length of thread that didn't quite match. I made four stitches along the old fold where the cloth was fraying, holding down only the bit that was likely to work further loose, and tucked the original thread inside. On the outside of the shirt you could find the fourth stitch on the underside of the cuff. I put the shirt on to try it. The sleeve was still wide enough when I reached to hook my bag up from beside the door. I took the shirt off and put it on the chair back again. I hadn't decided I had to wear it tomorrow.

While the channel was open I broke the little loaf I'd just warmed in two. The crust on one half was hard. A little steam came sliding out of the middle and was gone before it reached my mouth. I said I'd taken the book back today. She asked whether I'd borrowed another one right away, and I said no. I asked if yesterday she'd meant for the man with the ticket to sit with other people when she'd asked about his seat. Lumi said he hadn't even got on the boat. All she'd wanted to know was whether there was a spot for him on the bench. He hadn't passed through the crowd. How could she have meant to send him across the river? I swallowed a mouthful before I told her today I'd agreed to put out two tables on Saturday and had just been wondering whether they'd expect me to look after three corridors at the same time. She asked me what the third corridor looked like in my head. I couldn't tell her. It didn't even have walls on either side.

“If you've got the energy,” she said, “would you tell me about one of the tables when you get back? Not the two side by side. Pick one.”

I thought at first that she wanted to hear how I set them out. I'd pay attention to which was lighter when I went. She let me finish, then said she wasn't assigning me a table to bring back now. If I wanted to talk about a new bread van, or about three people all turning round together, I could. I brushed a crumb off my knee and said I did want to know which table was the main character in the play. She said I'd have to ask the people underneath it. It took her a moment to hear how strange the picture was. I couldn't help laughing and nearly crushed the crust in my hand.

I didn't ask again whether she meant to say anything about the door. I didn't put the empty bag I'd used for the book in the everyday drawer, either. I folded it twice and left it beside the door. If I wanted to buy something else I'd be able to take it with me. The half of the loaf I'd torn off still wasn't finished when I closed the channel. I folded the paper round it and left one edge sticking out. I could tell which side was up even through the paper.
