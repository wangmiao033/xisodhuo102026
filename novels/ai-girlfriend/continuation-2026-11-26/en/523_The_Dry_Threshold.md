# Chapter 523: The Dry Threshold

On Saturday the laundrette on the corner put an empty basin out by the door. There was nothing in it. A length of blue cord hung over the rim. A woman steering a pushchair around it ran over the end of the cord, and the basin tipped and made a tremendous noise under her wheel. I was waiting for a dryer by the door. When she looked down, I put out a hand to steady the pushchair. She pulled the handle straight back to herself. I said sorry and put my hand back in my pocket. She bent and picked the basin up on her own. There was a line of writing across the bottom, half worn away. It might have been the name of the shop or a child's name written by somebody at home. She held up the end of the cord and asked where it was supposed to go.

The owner was downstairs cleaning lint out of a dryer. His niece was behind the counter looking for change. She leaned round the corner and called to us not to put the basin back yet. The truck that watered the street trees was coming by. For some reason the man hadn't brought the long hose fitting today. He had a shorter one and had asked whether he could cut through the laundrette's side door and pull it across the yard to water the two young trees in the lane. She'd said yes, but asked him not to bring the water inside. She'd meant to hang the basin on a hook by the side door with the blue cord to catch the drips from the nozzle. She hadn't realised how light the basin was. As soon as she let go of the cord, the wind had caught it.

I paused the dryer, turned my wet sheet over and started it again. Nineteen minutes left. The man had walked around into the lane with the nozzle; the line behind him lay across the floor. The niece asked me to see whether the side door would hold. Somebody had propped it open with a solid wedge, but it was a narrow way through. When two people met there, one of them had to put a foot on the hose. I said the door didn't need to be open that far. We could move the line to the inside edge and give people room against the wall. A man with an armful of sheets said we could just lift it so he could walk through. It would take half a second. He caught at it with the toe of his shoe. From out in the lane the other man called out not to move it. He still had the nozzle in his hand.

“You go first,” I said to the man with the sheets. “I'll ask if he can turn the water off.”

“I'm not in a rush,” he said. “I just don't want to put either of these down.”

He hitched the white bundle a little higher in his arms. I went out the front instead and followed the wall around to the lane. There was a ring of mud around each tree. The man was about to water the second one. Water was making its way round the bed of the first, and I didn't step across it. I said somebody needed to get through the side door. Would he shut the water off until I called? He went round to shut off the supply at the truck, then came back and held down the trigger with the nozzle pointed at the ground to let the remaining water out. He didn't say yes right away. He looked behind me and asked if the weekend queue had gone all the way out the door. I said no, there was just one man with some sheets. When the nozzle stopped dripping, he let go of the trigger.

“Are we all right now?”

“We're all right now,” I said. I went back in and helped move the line.

The woman with the pushchair came back. She'd turned it around. She hadn't been leaving after all, only trying to get her things through the door. The niece asked if she'd park it by the counter. She said yes, but let me get the bag off first. It took me a second to be sure whether she meant she would do it or was asking me to. I held still. She undid the clip herself. The bag slid down all at once, and she blocked the wheel with a knee while she caught the weight of it. I steadied the door beside her. She glanced at me and lifted the bottom of the bag as she went inside. The niece took the basin in. She didn't hang it from the hook this time. She put it to one side of the door frame with an old cleaning rag under it, where it could catch the drips. She wound the blue cord twice round the handle so it couldn't hang out.

There was a folding WET FLOOR sign under the counter. The niece got it out and saw that the lettering was on one side only. From outside it would just look like a slab of yellow. I turned it round and meant to put it in the middle of the threshold. Not there, she said; look where that pushchair has to turn its wheels. I moved the sign beside the laundry basket. When the woman came through again with her bag off the pushchair, the wheels cleared it.

The man with the sheets got through, and I'd thought that was that. Then somebody in the yard swore, so quietly it sounded as if he'd meant it for himself. The water wasn't even on. We'd moved a joint up onto the raised part of the threshold. Nobody had stepped on it, but the door had swung inward a little as the man went by and caught the joint against the edge. The fellow with the nozzle had started to pull it away. He saw me and stopped. I asked if he could feed another foot in from his side. He said someone inside would have to hold the wedge down so the door didn't keep moving and pinching the pipe. The niece had the change tray in both hands. I called to the man with the sheets to ask if he could hold the door with his foot now. He'd put his washing on a spare chair. He came right back and planted his shoe on the wedge. I held the joint and the man fed a little more hose through. This time we put the entire joint outside on the flat paving, leaving only a straight piece of hose across the threshold. He went back to open the supply and returned to squeeze the nozzle gently. Once the stream was steady, he opened it all the way.

My sheet was done by then. I rolled it up. There was a single warm place left inside. The niece asked if I wanted to give it five more minutes. She had a coin she hadn't had to give out in change. I said no, I could spread the sheet out at home and let it dry. She put the coin back in its slot and wound the blue cord a little shorter so the last loop couldn't spring loose. After a while the water in the lane stopped. The man put away his nozzle and walked the end of the hose through the side door and into the basin to catch what was left in it. He emptied it under the tree. When he put the basin away, he didn't ask me to help. Only the man coming back behind a white sheet asked, through his bundle, whether he could shut the door.

“Let me look first,” I said.

I went over. The wedge was still there. The paving underneath it was dry. I took it out and pulled the door to. I heard the latch slide in, quite distinctly.

At home I put the sheet over the backs of two chairs. The space between the chairs and the wall narrowed enough that I had to turn sideways to get through. After two tries I realised I'd pulled the sheet tighter than it needed to be. I gave it a little slack. There was room for my knees. If I saw a piece of blue cord at the laundrette again, I probably wouldn't know whether it was the same one. I thought I'd remember the sound at the door, though. I'd heard it myself. I didn't have to put anybody else there to listen. That evening I went sideways between the chairs to get a glass of water and brushed the cloth with the back of my fingers. The sheet I'd taken off the bed that morning had gone cold. I found only one patch that was still damp. I moved that part closer to the window and drank my water before I went to put the glass down.
