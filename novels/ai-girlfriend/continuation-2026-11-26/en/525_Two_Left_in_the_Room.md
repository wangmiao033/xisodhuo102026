# Chapter 525: Two Left in the Room

“Did you cross off number seven as well?” Mia asked me a little after two on Monday. She was standing in the storeroom doorway with a sheet of paper rolled up in her fist. Only the top right corner showed. I had my cupboard key in the lock and couldn't tell what she meant. “Stool number seven. Your initials are next to it.”

I got the door halfway open and pulled my apron out through the gap before I answered. I hadn't crossed off any stool. The week before, when somebody came to count the chairs, I'd been sitting on a blue one with a dip where a bit of the seat had chipped away. I couldn't see it beside the storeroom door. For a second I thought somebody had put it back in the wrong place.

Mia unrolled the sheet on the lid of a grey bin. It wasn't the list of old furniture they were getting rid of. It was a route for the people collecting it that day. When they got to a room they were to count what was still left to move. A separate list of things to keep had been marked up before that one went out. Once they got to the third room, somebody had started putting the numbers of stools already moved under the heading for things still left. Someone had seen the initials I used on the second sheet and copied them onto the third one. Number seven came straight after. Mia asked if I'd happened to say to take it as well. I said I'd only just got to the storeroom. I hadn't even been in the first room, and I couldn't tell from those lines where seven was now. She turned the sheet to show me the little stroke marks someone had made before they left. There were two next to seven. She thought it was probably on the truck.

I went out with her. There were two people in orange vests. One was up in the back of the truck; the other was setting their empty trolley down. On the next pass they'd have eleven stools to take between two rooms. There were already seven on the truck. The one standing up there asked which was number seven. I said I thought it was blue, and corrected myself almost as soon as I said it. There'd be a label on the underside. I'd probably get it wrong if I went by colour. They eased the tied stools apart, one group at a time. The top one was blue and had nothing underneath it. The one furthest in was light grey. There was a small square of black paper stuck beneath the seat. The man hardly had to bend before he called out from the truck.

“Seven.”

Mia started forward and I asked her to wait. Other things were resting against the stool. They'd fall if she moved it as it was. I asked the man in the back if there was time to leave it ten minutes. They could take what was ready first, he said; it didn't matter if this load was one stool short. Then he asked how many we were keeping altogether. Mia said seven and another one by the wall. I looked at her, suddenly less sure of what I'd remembered. Last week she'd been at the door telling somebody we needed two, pointing the whole time to the wall where the numbered stools stood. I'd thought the blue one on the far side was a given. She held the two sheets one on top of the other. We could go and look in the room, she said. We didn't have to stand there doing sums by the truck.

Some of the floor had been cleared, and there were a few plastic fasteners underneath the table. We went round the room and found one stool still in use, with a bit of white paper on the back. The girl from the next room had put it there herself to say not to take it outside. Mia passed me the list. Two stools needed to stay in here, she said. When we put new labels on the boxes, one person sat at either end to read them; one end was low enough that a person who crouched to get at it would have trouble getting back up. Seven was the right height. Did I still need the blue one? I pulled the stool with the white notice out from under the table a little way. The seat was blue. There was a shallow notch at the edge. A white thread had caught around the bottom of one leg, and I picked it off. I'd been looking for this stool since I came out of the storeroom. It had never been meant to go anywhere.

There was another number underneath the corner of the table, on an older label with dust set into it. I read it out. Mia crouched to look. Six, not seven. That number belonged to the table. We turned the stool and read the white notice properly. It said, “Leave for the person using it,” not “Leave in this room.” If the last person had gone home, somebody following the note might take it away. I went next door and asked. The girl looked up from a folded pile of paper. She hadn't sat on it today. She'd written the notice before yesterday to keep the stool in the room, not to save it for herself. Could I add a couple of words? She said I'd better say plainly who needed another one. I didn't know who would sit on it, I said, but the room needed two. She found another copy of the tally under a bag of paper clips and gave it to me. This one didn't say who was counting either.

I told Mia we could each write a line now instead of trying to do any more with the old sheets. She tore a blank corner off one. I wrote, “Blue stool, no number, still in room.” She wrote, “Grey stool 7, on truck, to come off.” I took the paper back and nearly ruled a line across the blank space below. Instead I asked her to file the sheet with my initials behind this one. Did I mind putting my name on the new sheet, she asked. I said I'd sign the line I'd just written. I wouldn't sign underneath. Whoever saw seven come down could put their name there. She said all right. I wrote my whole name, longer than the marks I normally made on equipment, and we took the paper back out to the truck. The man inside read it, moved two stools that had been leaning on seven, and handed seven down. I reached for the seat while the other man had hold of the legs. There was a spot where the paint had come off one leg. By the time I bent to look at it, the stool was on the ground.

They tied the remaining six together again and drove off. There were still a few to come out of the other room, and Mia and I measured the cleared space near the door. It was wide enough that we wouldn't have to turn anything on end just to get it through. Mia noted on the paper when seven had come back and clipped the sheets together. She was going to take the grey stool to the low end of the table and asked whether I was going that way. I said I'd stay by the door to get the plastic fasteners and I'd be grateful if she'd take the stool in first. She carried it upside down, the black number on the outside. It got through the doorway without touching either wall. I took off my apron before I opened the cupboard again. Only then did I notice that the whole afternoon had gone by without my ever tying it properly.

On my way out, I passed the room again. Somebody was sitting on each of the stools. I couldn't see which person had the blue one or which had the grey. Under the table I could see a hand keeping a loose label from dropping off a box while the person at the other end read the last line. I went on down the passage. The light picked out the toes of two pairs of shoes. One pair shifted half a step as the box turned. The other stayed where it was.
