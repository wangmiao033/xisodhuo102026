# Chapter 524: Where the Track Divides

On Sunday morning I found a badly printed map in the secondhand book I'd bought the previous week. It wasn't a loose sheet somebody had put in. The map was printed on the page. The half that folded inward had gone pale, and I had to open it out to get the river. Two lines followed the water past an iron bridge and split not far beyond it. One went up onto the embankment. One stayed down by the river. I tried to fit them to the place I knew. The bridge was still there. I'd taken the path up to the embankment. I'd never noticed the other one. The book said the wind dropped a little if you went down that way. I opened the window a crack and heard the cars go by in waves. I thought I might try the line by the river today. The book didn't fit my coat pocket comfortably, so I carried it in my hand. Going down the stairs at home, I decided that if I couldn't get there I didn't have to make up for it by going somewhere else.

When I came off the bridge there was no turn that I could see. Someone underneath was brushing water stains off the wall. Each time he moved the brush the handle knocked against the iron railing. I asked if there was a way down below the embankment. He said there was. I'd have to go back a bit. Part of the ground had washed away further along, though, and I wouldn't be able to get beyond the temporary barrier. Was the way up blocked as well? He lifted the brush to about knee height and pointed with it between the rails. There was a man looking at his phone just out of the bridge's shadow.

“Below where he's standing,” he said. “You can still get down by the water a little way. I went down myself yesterday.”

I went back. It wasn't until I got beside the man that I saw there were low stone steps down to the river. The top two huddled under the bridge, with no sign and a broken yellow line along one edge. I opened the book to see whether the turn on the foldout could be this one. The wind lifted the page twice. The man looked over and said it was one of the old ferry maps. There was a dotted line on his phone that was meant to take him to a cycle path. He wanted to follow the river to the next bridge, where he was meeting a friend. He couldn't work out whether he should follow the dotted line or go up and over the bank. Could he have a look at my book? I turned it towards him and kept a hand under the foldout. He didn't reach for it. He tilted his phone the other way to show me. The two paths went closer together on his screen than they did in my book. Maybe somebody had left out a few of the bends when they drew them.

I told him I didn't think he could get to the next bridge down by the water. Up would probably be easier. Had I been down there, he asked. I said I'd just asked the man with the brush.

“Then I'll ask him myself,” he said, and took his phone back.

I hadn't expected that. I'd meant to take the stairs, and had one foot on the first one. I closed the book and asked whether his friend was meeting him down by the river. No, on the bridge. He'd seen the dotted line and thought he might save a bit of the walk. We stood beside each other a minute. I told him if he went up to the embankment, the first way across to that bridge was on the left. I noticed after I said it that I'd put another piece of advice where the first one might have done. He thanked me and went around me. He really did go back to speak to the man with the brush.

Halfway down, a little spur branched away. The old map hadn't drawn that one. I started along it because the handrail went that way. Then I looked down. The rail didn't come out at ground level. The water had worn away the last step. If the man with the phone had followed me, he might have gone that way because I'd put a hand on the rail. I backed up, tucked the closed book under my arm and took the steps nearer the wall. I kept my hand braced until I felt sand underfoot. Someone else came down behind me. Her toe found the edge of the step that didn't go anywhere. I told her the side by the wall went all the way to the bottom. Could she get out the other way, once she was down there? I said I hadn't been that far and the man with the brush said she couldn't. Down to the water was far enough for her, she said. She moved her bag to the other hand and came down two steps. I gave her half a step of room. She went by me and looked down to find her next footing before she went on.

There was a narrow path at the foot of the steps. Empty shells had got pressed into the grey sand. I hadn't gone far when I saw the barrier resting at an angle against a piece of pale concrete. Next to it there was a sign that didn't look solid. The words told people not to go past it. Below them was an arrow they hadn't filled in. The wind really did fall away down there, and I could hear the surface of the water nudging in and out. Behind me there was a stone about the height of my knee. I sat on it and thought I'd see what the book actually said about that part of the walk. The foldout bent back and covered two lines. I held it down, and noticed a tiny bit of ink and a mark next to it that could just have been a fingernail. It wasn't somebody's little addition to the directions. Another person had turned to that page once and put a finger down more or less where mine was. I steadied the book on my legs and read two pages. One of the pictures showed a wooden post that used to stand at an angle on the far side of the bridge. It must have been gone a long time. The book said that when the boat came in, the grass beside the post moved first. I'd never seen that grass. Half a green leaf had come down from somewhere above me and was floating out beyond the barrier, curling in at the tip. I watched it go for a little way. Then I couldn't find it again.

The man I'd spoken to was heading up towards the embankment by the time I came back to the top of the stairs. He raised his phone to me. He'd asked, he said. The man with the brush had told him to go up. The way I'd gone was only good for standing by the water a bit. I said that was right. How long had I been there? I said I didn't know, about as long as it took to read two pages. He laughed and said he hadn't even asked which side of the bridge his friend was on. He started to make a call as he walked away. I went back across in the other direction. In the middle of the bridge I thought there was no need to stop and check which way the water was going beneath me. I stopped anyway. The rail was cold. From there I could only see two trees a long way apart down below. Somebody behind me rang a bicycle bell. I put my back against the rail to give him room, and he was past the place I'd been standing before I could look around.

At home I folded the page in along its old crease. Half the river disappeared. Only the beginning of the fork still showed, just enough of the second line to look as though whoever printed it had tried and failed to rub it out. I heated the half cup of water I'd left in the morning and let my palm rest against the handle. A little later the foldout sprang up on its own, showing a sliver of white. I didn't press it down again. When I sat at the table later, I carried on reading from the page after the map. It turned out the writer hadn't gone very far once he got down to the water either. I knew what he'd thought of that part of the walk now. I turned the page all the same.
