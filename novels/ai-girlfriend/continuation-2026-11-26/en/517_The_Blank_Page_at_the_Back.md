# Chapter 517: The Blank Page at the Back

On Sunday I heated six of the dumplings left from the night before. I wanted a meal I could eat without clearing the whole table first, so I carried over my bowl and a pair of chopsticks and left it at that. The dumplings had stuck together in the fridge. I couldn't separate them with the chopsticks and had to work the back of a spoon down the middle. I saved the two with torn skins for last. There was a little filling in the bottom of the bowl; I hadn't made broth to go with it. One of the two apricots on the windowsill was ripe. There was a little give under my finger. A black speck on the skin wouldn't rinse off, but I rubbed it once with the pad of my thumb and it came away. I ate the apricot and moved the hard one away from the sun. I didn't touch the sheet of paper on the table. There was enough room beside it for me to sit and eat.

I'd meant to buy a light bulb when I went out, but the shop had a sign saying it didn't open until noon on Sundays. I had some time before then and walked on to the library. There were two folding tables in the lobby. Books ran in a row along the outer edges, with another row you had to reach over to get to. Everybody who'd just come in seemed to be waiting for somebody else to choose first. A member of staff called out that this wasn't borrowing; if you saw one you liked, you could bring it over to the scales. The scales weren't for weighing the books. They had no batteries. The base was heavy enough to hold the price sheet down under the cash tin. Everything was a pound a book. You could take one volume out of a set and that would be a pound too. I put my hand into my coat pocket and found the tailor's receipt from the other day. The money was in my trousers, mixed up with two coins I'd put aside for the laundry.

I picked up a book about place names. A little piece of blue thread showed at the front. It turned out to be a loose thread from the spine. I put it down and took the book next to it. Its cover showed two windows without any glass, and there was nothing outside either one. It had no jacket. The stamped title was so dull by then that I had to turn it sideways to read it: *Houses Along the River*. The first page was a map. I couldn't find the river. A few pages further on there was a picture of a house with half the roof taken off. Four words ran along the wall beneath the eaves, and the person who'd taken the photograph had got only one tyre of the car beside it. Somebody had written very small on the white margin that the car had been his father's. I tipped the page back towards me and realised the letter he'd written there wasn't the beginning of his father's name. It was the last letter on the plate.

I hadn't finished looking at it when a woman to my right asked if I could move the book underneath a little. I moved my hand and saw there wasn't a second book. The loose cover of the book in my hands had dragged across the table, exposing a corner of something printed. I told her it was all one book. She bent down to look. She knew this title, but there'd been an edition with a separate map tucked into it. I said there was a map at the front. The one she meant folded out at the back, she said, and the sheet was bigger than the book itself. It had the names from both banks of the river. Her grandfather used to talk about a landing that might be marked on it. She didn't expect it to be in one of the photographs. I handed her the book so she could see for herself. She almost missed it with her first hand and got the other one under it like I'd given her a basket of fruit that hadn't been packed quite tight enough.

There was a folded piece of paper at the back. She drew it out half an inch, but it wasn't a map. It was an old sale notice somebody had tucked away, for a television and a white child's bed. There'd been a phone number beneath the bed; somebody had cut off a whole neat strip to take the number away. She laid the notice across the book and told me to take the book if I still wanted it. I said I'd like another look. She gave it back and went to see what else was on the table. I took the notice out and asked the man at the till whether he meant to keep things like this with the books. I could have it if I wanted, he said. Otherwise there was a box for waste paper. The woman had heard. She touched the lower corner where an address had been printed. That building had been pulled down, she said. Would she like the paper, I asked. She shook her head. Her grandfather hadn't lived in that one. The words for the bed were quite large, though. That was how big you printed them if you really wanted it gone. She pressed the old fold with a finger and pushed the notice back towards me.

At the till I noticed somebody had torn a small corner off the blank back page. There was a thread of paper near the binding that hadn't quite come away. I held the page in the light by the scales. Did somebody still mean to trim this off? The man leaned over to see. By the look of it the last owner had needed something to write a note on. He could take my money and put the book in a bag. He couldn't put a corner back on the blank page. For an instant I thought of asking him to put it aside while I looked for another copy. There was a child behind me holding four books. The top one was upside down and he hadn't set it down once. I stepped forward and found a third coin beside the two meant for the laundry. I didn't take a bag. The man told me not to hold the book by the spine with one hand. It was coming apart. He told me to carry it from underneath.

I didn't go into the shop when it opened. I bought some salt on the way home. The light bulb I had still came on, and I usually waited for a bulb to go out before replacing it anyway. With one hand under the book, I put the salt in the empty pocket. It rubbed against my leg if I walked too quickly. Near the door of my building I saw the woman who'd wanted the map coming the other way. She had another book wrapped in old newspaper. She raised it for me to see and said she'd got something with a lot of photographs instead. I said my book hadn't had a map you could take out. There was most of a blank sheet left at the back, though. “You can put down the places you can't find on it,” she said. She pushed the newspaper a little further under her book and kept walking. Neither of us changed direction.

At home I put the book down in the free part of the table. The corner of the blank page was showing when I remembered that in the end I'd folded up the notice and brought it home. It wasn't in the book. I got it out of my trousers pocket, opened it up and ran my hand over it. A blue line curved away from where the previous owner's number had been cut out and made a zero in the price. Somebody whose name I didn't know might once have stood next to the bed and said there wasn't enough room, that this was the thing they wanted to get out of the house first. I picked up my pen to see how it would feel to write on the page at the back of the book. The nib touched down, and I didn't move it. I didn't have to give a blank page something to do on the first day. I put the pen back and brought a little saucer over to hold the fold in the notice flat. There was afternoon light in the window, falling only on one side of the apricot I hadn't eaten. The book lay open and wouldn't quite close. I turned back to the map at the front and let the cover rest wherever it came to rest.
