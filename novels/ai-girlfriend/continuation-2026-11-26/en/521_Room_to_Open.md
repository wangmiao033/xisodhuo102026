# Chapter 521: Room to Open

Not long after I got to work on Thursday, Mia knocked once on the cabinet beside my desk. Would I come to the cutting room? There was a trial booklet she wanted to put on an open-book stand. She'd found a wooden stand, but the bottom rail was too close in. She was afraid the book would shut in front of the apprentices as soon as she let go. I took half a glass of water with me. Sure enough, there was a thin booklet on the stand, with a piece of folded label stuck under one foot. It was open to page six. A black picture ran over two pages and half of it seemed to disappear into the spine. I put my water on the empty sill and felt under the stand. As soon as I touched the loose bit of label it went in farther.

“Won't it wobble without that?” I asked.

She held the wooden edge and told me to try it. I pulled out the label. The stand didn't move. It had an adjustable foot of its own, but the bottom was covered in felt the colour of the table. I drew the foot out half an inch. Mia tried taking her hand away from the booklet. It stood up briefly, leaned forward a little, and shut most of the way. All that remained of the picture was a black strip. The bit she wanted to explain was how much of an illustration you lose in the gutter when you print it over a spread. She didn't need to press the book flat. She did need the people standing out around the table to see how much space there was in the middle. I tried to straighten the bottom rail. It wouldn't go at first. I leaned down and saw it wasn't the stand. Whoever made the booklet had put a fresh fold in a card insert and tucked it in at the back. It wouldn't lie flat, and it was pushing up the whole thin stack of paper on the right.

I asked if we could take it out. Mia shook her head. “Where the card goes is part of the sample. There's only one of these.” I told her to keep it open while I went for a clip with a gap in it. I turned and nudged the glass on the sill. It didn't go over, but some of the water slopped onto the stone. I stopped to wipe it. Mia said from the other side of the table that we had time. If we damaged it trying to clamp it open, she wouldn't be able to show anybody. There was still nearly half a glass left. I took it back across the passage to my desk and left the cloth at the sink. When I came back she had lifted the book out of the stand and laid it on the table. The right side still curved up. The empty stand looked longer than it had a minute before.

There were two white boards outside the cabinet that we normally used to list the tools in use that day. We tried propping the book on one. The edge was flat and there was nothing to stop it closing past the angle we wanted. Mia crouched to look for rubber pads in the lowest drawer. I put both hands on the book to hold it there for a moment. Could she see the straight line in the middle of the black picture if she stood on my right? She stood up and came round to look from the right, without changing the position of my hands. She could see the inside end, she said, but my palm covered almost half the book. She walked around to my left without letting me move my hand. From there she couldn't see that the two sides belonged to the same picture at all.

“Have them stand on the right, then,” I said.

“There are only four of them. You could block them all with one chair.” She set the toe of her shoe against a table leg to gauge the space. “Or we could put the stand down the long side of the table. They don't all have to stand at one end.”

We carried the stand around. Once it was steady I pointed to the slot where the spine normally sat. Maybe we didn't have to open it the whole way. If one end were lower, the right-hand leaves could settle against the rail. She took the thin rubber pad she'd found, put it under the foot nearest the window and left the other end bare. I held the stand while she brought the booklet forward a little. She let go first. I waited before taking my own hands away. The folded card was still there, and the right side of the booklet rested just inside the rail. It could close a little more, but not far enough to hide what we needed. Mia didn't rush to say we'd got it. She told me to try the place where she'd been standing. I could see the line but not the very end. That last little length was exactly what she wanted to talk about, she said. She could reach across and point it out. There was no need for her to open up the entire line in advance.

We still had a quarter of an hour. Mia stood in front of the stand and said the first part of her explanation out loud. She stopped at “fully open” and asked if it sounded as if we were about to flatten the book. I said it had sounded that way to me. She tried again without looking at her notes. “See how far it stays open on its own.” She moved the top sheet with one finger and the card at the back didn't jump out. I didn't tell her I'd heard smoother sentences. I wanted to know what she'd say next and whether the two would belong together. In the end she tried “We'll let it open this far,” a little quieter, as if someone might already be in the room. There were two knocks at the door. She went to open it herself. I narrowed the gap in the window on my way out and went to get my half a glass of water. At the door she turned one shoulder aside so I could pass the short end of the table without pushing the stand farther back.

In the afternoon I came back for the pen I'd left on the sill, and it turned out to be somebody else's. It looked just like mine except the clip was cracked. Mia was putting the white boards back. She asked if I needed a pen. She could get me one from the drawer. I told her mine was in my shirt. I touched the cap through the cloth to make sure. The stand hadn't moved from the long side of the table. She'd already taken the book off. A white edge of the card stuck out from under the pages in the same direction as the fold we'd found that morning. I asked if anybody had stood in the wrong place. One of them had walked round to look from the back, she said. He hadn't known till he came round again which line they were looking at. She hadn't had to say the whole thing over; the person next to him had pointed it out. Mia lifted the boards a little as she told me this. I let her past, and she cleared the corner of the table without hitting the book stand.

There was still a strip of brightness outside when I left. I went past the newspaper stall near the station. The man behind it drew a magazine out from his side of the table and spread the whole thing open. Was this the one I'd been looking at? I didn't want to buy it, I told him. I'd been looking at the way he held it without letting half of it fall. He raised a hand from below the table and a few high pages folded over. “I've got my knee under it,” he said. I hadn't seen his knee at all. I made room for the person behind me who did want a paper and went home with my hands in my coat pockets. The used book on my table had only been touched at the spine. It was still where I'd put it. When I set the glass down, its base clicked against a few grains of sugar I'd missed. I lifted it, wiped the sugar away, and set it down on a clean patch. Then I poured. I didn't move the book over.
