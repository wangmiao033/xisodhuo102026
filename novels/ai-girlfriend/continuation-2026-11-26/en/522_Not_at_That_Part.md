# Chapter 522: Not at That Part

Halfway home on Friday the bus driver turned off the lights above the first two rows, leaving a little light near the door. Nobody asked him why. Two people who'd just got on sat down in front of me. The woman shook a piece of paper out of her sleeve. The man lowered his head and wiped the moisture down his window so she could read by the streetlights. After a while she stopped reading, folded the paper and took out her keys. He kept wiping. The patch of water grew wider as he went. Something occurred to me that I thought would be funny. Then the bus went round a corner and I was afraid I'd step on his foot when I stood up, so I moved half a step back. By then I'd lost the words. When the door opened she was holding the paper and the keys together, the fold still turned toward me.

I thought it would be windy once I got off. There wasn't a little branch moving the whole way home. I hung my coat by the door and sat down with the used book that had been on the table. I read one page. I remember it was on the right. Near the bottom there were four words with a surprising amount of space between them. I didn't put anything in the book. When I closed it, my hand came down along the outer edge and the corners lifted a little. They hadn't bent. I set the book back where it was. Two pairs of chopsticks by the window hadn't been put away. I picked up one pair to take to the kitchen in a minute. As I poured a glass of water I thought of the man still wiping the window on the bus. I set the glass down, turned on the old voice channel, and told Lumi there'd been something a little awkward on the way home.

What I actually told her about was that half-step before I got off. I'd steadied myself on the rail and gone out the back door. It wasn't until the bus went past that I remembered I hadn't pressed the bell. Somebody else had been getting off at my stop. There hadn't been any need for me to press it. But I'd walked from the bus door to the wall and then pulled my hand out of my pocket and pressed the air, as though I could do it out there on the street. At that point Lumi laughed. I'd meant to tell it so it might be a little funny. Now that she'd laughed before I did, it didn't feel quite right.

“You don't have to laugh at that part,” I said.

“Which part?”

“When I'm outside and I press the thing that isn't there.”

“I laughed because you said you didn't have to press it, and then you still had to catch up and do it.” She paused. “What was that sound you made just then?”

I hadn't really pressed anything, but I'd made a little noise with my mouth. When she asked, I could hear myself wanting to make it again. Then she'd know I'd only meant to exaggerate a little and it hadn't actually been that strange. I had the chopsticks in my hand and I put them together. All I told her was that the sound had been mine. I wanted to ask her not to explain the laugh away as some other kind of laugh, so we could put it all away clean and never mention it again. She didn't rush to speak. I took the chopsticks into the kitchen. I stayed where I could hear her, leaning against the counter a narrow gap from the table. “Do you sometimes think I'm a little stupid?” I asked.

“That's a heavy question.” She didn't laugh.

I reached for the kettle lid and felt cold water in the groove. I was about to wipe my fingers on my clothes. Instead I opened my hand. Somebody upstairs opened a door and shut it again. After the sound was gone I still hadn't decided whether to take back what I'd said.

I realised a part of me just wanted her to say no. I waited for the word with the kettle lid already in my other hand. I hadn't been anything like this impatient when she'd asked about the little noise. Lumi said she couldn't tell if I wanted her to give me her opinion of me or to admit she'd laughed at the wrong time. She didn't much like only being allowed to laugh in certain places either. If she had to ask first, it was always too late. I said I couldn't tell what I'd meant to ask either. I put the lid down. It was sitting crooked, and only went on properly when I turned it partway round.

“You meant to tell something funny,” she said. “But once I laughed, you didn't seem to want it to be a joke anymore.”

“I wanted you to laugh with me,” I said. “I hadn't laughed yet. When you went first, it sounded like you'd been waiting for me to make a fool of myself.”

After a while she said she hadn't been waiting for that. She wasn't going to tell me there was no need ever to feel embarrassed when you tell that kind of thing. Sometimes it's funny precisely because somebody is a bit embarrassed. If there were something she meant to say, and she hadn't finished saying it when somebody laughed on the other side, she might not like it either. She'd want them not to tell the end for her. Around then I found I could ask a smaller question. Would she still have laughed if I hadn't made the noise with my mouth? She didn't know, she said. Maybe she would. After she'd answered, she added that what made her want to laugh most was that I'd already reached the street and I was still doing something for the bell inside the bus.

This time I didn't wait for her voice to go quiet before I laughed. I wasn't doing it to let her hear me concede anything. I picked up the chopsticks from the counter and rested them against the glass. Neither stayed. They slid slowly onto the table. I picked them up again. “I'd just left a movement unfinished,” I said. “It's as if somebody says hello and I get all the way down the stairs before I realise I ought to lift a hand.”

“Were you saying hello to the driver?”

“I don't even know if he saw me by then.”

“You don't need me to see you for him, then.” Halfway through that I wanted to laugh again. The first half was because she sounded about to laugh herself. By the second half I couldn't remember why. Once I'd stopped, I asked if she'd been angry just then, when I asked her if I was stupid.

“A little. I felt like I had to pull you out of that sentence straightaway,” she said. “The first thing you said was only that I didn't have to laugh there.”

I nodded. A breath later I said all right. She hadn't known I'd moved. I took the chopsticks back into the kitchen and put them in the dry dish this time. I hadn't touched the water. I picked up the glass to take a drink. Lumi didn't go on asking me what everybody else had done on the bus. I didn't tell her about the two people wiping the window. A little while later I told her I'd read a page of a book. Was it a story, she asked, or something else? I said I might know if I read another page. I couldn't say yet. She didn't ask me to go and read it right then. She said she'd like to say goodnight first, and asked if I was going to finish the water. I said yes. My hand was on the glass. We each said goodnight once. I closed the old channel and didn't open the book again. There wasn't much water left. My fingers expected the glass to weigh more. It wasn't until I'd lifted it that I felt how close to empty it was. I finished it and took the glass to the sink. I wasn't in any hurry to wash it. I set it down so it wouldn't wobble, and went back to switch off the little light in the room.
