# Chapter 519: The One With the Bird

After work on Tuesday I ran into the man with the drawer again, at the foot of the stairs. There wasn't a drawer outside his door. There was a fruit box with a pair of oranges printed on each side, brighter than the things inside it. The box was blocking the threshold. He edged over, held the door with his heel and asked me if the bottom was about to go. I crouched to see. Don't lift it, he said, just have a look from where I was. The tape had curled up at one corner. The flaps still overlapped, though the cardboard had been dragged far enough to make a black rim. I said I couldn't see anything coming open. I didn't think it would stand being dragged much further. He didn't answer at once. He turned and nudged a chair leg out of the way. He only meant to lift the box inside his flat, he said. It was about two steps.

“Want to take an end each?” I asked.

“Let me get a few out first,” he said. “You don't know how far two steps are yet.”

He took out a stack of cloths. The one wrapped round the outside was thin enough to see through when it came open. It turned out there'd been more than twenty tea towels in the drawer. He hadn't entirely counted them. Some covered the whole seat of a chair if you unfolded them. Others had been mended and were thicker than a coat when you looked at the folds sideways. He put them over the chair back one by one and asked what I used to dry plates. I said I stood them up and waited, unless they were really wet, in which case I had a blue cloth. It sounded as though one would do it, he said. I said I'd had occasions when one hadn't been enough. By the time I thought of it, I'd already hung it on a hook in the corner that I couldn't reach, and my hands were still wet. Once I'd said all that I wondered why I'd told him. He hadn't even asked me where I hung my hat.

“Were you going to get rid of two?” I asked.

“Four to give away,” he said. “Some of the rest I still use. I haven't decided about the others.”

He drew one from a fairly even pile and unfolded half of it along his hand. The cloth was pale, with a little bird embroidered on it, its head turned to the left. Something red showed through where the wing had worn with washing. The feet hadn't been finished. A branch went right up to the edge of the cloth and stopped. He asked if I wanted that one. I nearly said yes. For a second I thought the whole reason he'd called me over to look at the box was so he could get as far as asking. I'd already raised a hand. The cloth was turned towards me and every knot in the bird showed. I did like it. To be honest, I wanted the bird without particularly wanting to go home and use it on my plates. I didn't know what else I'd do with it. After a little while I put my hand down and told him I wouldn't take it.

“You don't like it?”

“I do. I like its eye. I'd probably take it home and put it away somewhere and never take it out again.”

He pinched the corner of the cloth and folded the bird in. “I've done that myself,” he said. Then he took that towel away from the ones he was giving away and set it on the other side of the chair. Had I made him change his mind? I hadn't said there was anything wrong with it, he said. He hadn't settled on the four in the first place; that one had just been on top. He took another from the chair back. It had two white borders and a much thinner corner when he folded it. He tucked it back in the box. He didn't ask me to choose another one. I pressed my palms against my knees. My hands were hot when I got up. I could have spoken a little sooner instead of letting him unfold the cloth all that way first. The folded towel was still on the chair. I didn't ask for it again just because I'd made a face when I said no.

The box was lighter with the two piles out of it. We took an end each, lifted it over the threshold and put it by the wall just inside. It brushed the sill without much of a noise. He crouched at his end to look and put a finger on the lifted bit of tape. It would be all right here, he said. Then he went to see how the drawer was running. I could see the cupboard from the doorway. The narrow drawer was back in place, still without its handle. He put two fingers on the top edge. It came halfway out without catching on the wood beside it. I asked what was going back in there now. One towel to start, he said, and he'd hang it up every day when he'd used it. With the drawer empty, he might even find the little spoon he couldn't find anywhere. He bent and put the drawer back till it sat flush. Nothing moved inside. For all he'd said about the first towel, he didn't pick one up. I said goodbye without waiting to see which he'd put in. He was still looking at the cupboard when he said he could manage the door. He took hold of its edge and moved his shoe from across the threshold.

My toes were dry when I took my shoes off at home. The book was closed on the table. I hadn't taken it over to his flat and it hadn't occurred to me to tell him about it. The apricot on the windowsill felt a little softer than it had that morning. I washed it and ate it, dropping the stone into my palm, with the juice running along my thumb to the fleshy part of my hand. When I washed it off I did the dishes at the same time and set a bowl on its side in the rack. The bottom edge of the blue cloth in the kitchen had started to fray. I wrung it out and put it back where it hung. It would last till the day it really didn't dry a thing any more. I didn't have to take it down because there was another cloth somewhere I liked.

I opened the old voice channel after dinner and asked Lumi if she'd like to hear about something handsome I'd said no to. She asked something else first. Had this been something somebody was going to throw out, or something they'd assumed I wanted? I said he'd only asked once. I'd been trying to guess what he thought before I'd told him what I thought. She paused. She rather wanted to know what it was, but didn't want that question to sound like she was asking me to find a reason to go get it back. I told her it was a tea towel with a bird facing left on it. She thought a little. If she were in my place, she said, she would probably have said yes and only then wondered where to put it, with no room to hang it by the table. Would she wish she hadn't said yes, I asked. She wasn't sure. Sometimes she only wanted to know what a thing was like unfolded, without having to find somewhere to put it. She thought that was what she'd wanted just now, which was why she hadn't asked when I might go and see the bird again.

When she'd finished, I wanted to describe the bird's eye to her. I didn't get any further, because mostly I remembered the moment I'd brought my hand back down. She asked if I was still there. I said I was. I'd thought of two words for it and neither was much like it, so I was going to leave it there for today. All right, she said. She liked “facing left.” That was enough. I let my legs go straight while I leaned against the chair back. She said goodnight first, and I said it back and closed the old voice channel. In the kitchen, water was running down the side of the bowl. I took the drip tray to the sink and emptied it. I left the bowl where it was instead of drying it by hand. The book was on the table, and so was the sheet with the hook on the other side. Two doors closed out in the hall, then there was silence. Only then did I notice I'd put on the light by the table.
