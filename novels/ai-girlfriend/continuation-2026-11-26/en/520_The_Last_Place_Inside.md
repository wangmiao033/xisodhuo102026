# Chapter 520: The Last Place Inside

I went to the repair room by the square on Wednesday evening. It wasn't dark, but the glass already gave back the shapes of the people inside. If somebody bent forward in there, it looked as though somebody outside had leaned in over my shoulder. I hadn't brought anything to be fixed. Once I was through the door I looked along the wall. My neighbour hadn't come. There was a small brush on the table with the felt on it, but no one was using the place. Somebody opened a window a crack. Damp air came in and a corner of the newspaper under the brush started to rise. I stood and watched it swell and drop a few times. Then a man in a grey apron came around from behind me and asked if I was waiting to have a lamp fixed.

“No. I just came in to look.”

“You might be the right person, then. Could you sit here and make a note of who came first? I need to find some bulbs in the back. When I come back out I won't know how long anybody's been here.”

He gave me an old flyer. There were boxes someone had drawn on the back, with DESK LAMPS across the top and nothing underneath. I asked if he meant just this table. Just the lamps, he said; the other things weren't his. I put the flyer on my knees without moving his chair. The young man at the window had a pole tucked into his armpit, taller than the counter. I couldn't see the head of the lamp. Farther in, somebody had wrapped a desk lamp in a towel. The cable hung down to within a finger of the floor. Neither was looking my way. I held the pencil with the point up until one of them turned around. Could they tell me who'd got here first?

“Him,” said the woman with the towel, pointing at the pole. “I only need to know where to get another shade. Nobody has to take mine apart.”

The young man said he hadn't come about a lamp at all. That was a pole off his clothes rack. He'd put it by the window so nobody would trip over it. For a moment I thought he was making fun of me. He moved his hand. There were three empty plastic clips on the pole, and the top one had caught a thread from the edge of his hat. The hat was still on his head. When he looked down, the thread pulled. He freed it quickly and wanted to know who did clothes racks. I pointed to the table list on the door. When he'd read it, he tipped the pole sideways. A child near the passage took a step back and bumped my chair. I put a foot down hard. The chair legs didn't go quiet until the far end of the pole had made it through the other door. In the first box I wrote, Replacement shade, ask first. I didn't put a name.

A man came in later with an old green lamp base. His hands were red from the cold. When he saw my pencil he asked whether they could finish it tonight. He had to borrow a car at seven to take somebody somewhere and he couldn't hang around. He was still cradling the base as he spoke. There was a dent at the rim that looked brighter than the rest since it had been dropped. I told him there was one person ahead of him. I didn't know how long a shade would take or how many more lamps the repairer could get to today. Couldn't I at least open it up and have a look while we waited? I looked at my own hands. Gripping the handle of my umbrella on the way here had left a faint line at the base of my thumb. With the pencil turned sideways I could almost have rested it in the line.

“I wouldn't know how to open it,” I said.

“Keep my place, then. I'll go for the car. Ten minutes.”

I asked if he wanted to put the lamp down. He looked at the floor and said he couldn't leave it. And if the place was gone when he got back, he'd be in more of a hurry than he was now. He didn't set the lamp down, and I didn't leave him a place on the sheet. I asked whether he wanted to join the line now. He stood there a little longer, as if he were adding up two sets of traffic lights. Then he said, “No. That'll save me a trip.” He backed slowly toward the door so as not to put another dent in the base against somebody coming in. A person just arriving held the door for him.

I looked down and meant to straighten one of the box lines. The woman with the towel said not to cross him out. He'd never got in line. I said I hadn't put him down; I'd thought the bottom box was crooked. She turned toward me. After a moment she said, “Oh. Funny how long the table is, and you can still only do one lamp at a time.” She looked along the empty end. Then she put both hands into the gaps at the edges of the towel and lifted. The cable came up with the lamp without touching the floor. I asked whether she meant to go as well. She said she was going to wait for him to come out. The shade was larger than she'd thought. She'd bought two herself and got home to find neither would go on. She didn't have a car to collect, did she?

The man in the apron came back carrying a shoebox full of little bulbs wrapped in paper. He took the flyer and asked if there was only one. I began to tell him what had happened. Halfway through I stopped, in case he counted the green base as well, and said the man who'd left hadn't joined the line. How many more did he think he could take? There was time to open up one lamp at most, he said. He'd see first whether the woman's question called for tools at all. He put the shoebox down, went to the door himself, and turned over the sign that said STILL TAKING REPAIRS TODAY. The side facing out was blank. When he came back, he saw I still had the pencil and told me to leave it under the window. I put it in the little wooden trough beside the brush. The trough was too short. I found a slant that let the point stick out a little under the brush handle without catching the newspaper.

Before I went out I looked back. The woman had spread her towel. The neck of the shade lay there. They hadn't opened the lamp, and nobody knew yet whether another one would have fitted into the evening. Outside I pulled the strap tighter on my umbrella. A single drop tapped the pavement. It was so faint I had to look down to see it was from the umbrella, not the start of more rain. I went home by the restaurants. I'd meant to buy something for supper, but as I reached the door I saw the cook inside bringing out the last metal pan. I didn't go in and ask if I still had time to order.

I washed my hands when I got home. The line at my thumb was gone, though my palm was still cold. The book was where I'd left it. I touched the spine and didn't lift it. For hot water I had to empty what was left in the kettle first. I tipped it too sharply and the water splashed past the sink. I wiped it up and kept the cloth bunched in my hand while I moved the little bowl I'd already washed. While the man in the apron was looking for the bulbs, I'd wondered what was taking so long. I'd wanted him to hurry. Once he was back, I hadn't asked. As he put the shoebox on the table he'd flattened his palm on the lid, then lifted his fingers one by one. I rinsed out the cloth and narrowed the opening of the window a little. I didn't turn on the old voice channel that evening. Light from the streetlamp fell across the book I hadn't opened. It caught the two blank windows on the cover. When I moved the window, the patch of light moved too.
