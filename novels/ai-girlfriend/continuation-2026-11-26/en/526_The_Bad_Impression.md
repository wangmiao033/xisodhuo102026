# Chapter 526: The Bad Impression

On my way home on Tuesday somebody stopped me at the station to ask when the last bus left. I knew when the last one was, but she named a stop on a different route, so the time I knew was no use. She didn't ask me to look it up. She wanted to know if I would come and stand with her by the electronic sign. The words went a little faster near the end of the line than they did at the beginning. I had just seen her stop when the number below it went by and disappeared.

“That's what I do,” she said. “I see the name and think I know when the bus is coming.”

I thought it was funny but didn't laugh. We waited for the line to come round again. “Ten seventeen. Did you see it?”

I said I had. Seventeen. She went on her way. When she got as far as the light, she threw her scarf over her shoulder. It slipped halfway down at first. Then she turned her head and the whole scarf slid onto the pavement. She didn't bend right away. She pinned a corner with the toe of her boot before the wind could take it.

By the time I got home I could still hear the way she'd said “seventeen”. Her voice went up as though she had something else to tell me, and then she didn't say anything else. In the kitchen I added a little hot water to what was left of my tea. I didn't look for a spoon. I swirled the cup twice in my hand. Some leaves stuck to the rim. I set the cup down and tried saying “seventeen”. My voice was short and flat. I tried it again with a bend in the first syllable. It sounded like somebody reading figures off an invoice. I'd drunk most of the tea before it occurred to me to wonder why I hadn't laughed at the bus stop. She hadn't told a joke. I didn't mind having spent the time. I was probably afraid that if I laughed, she would think the only thing that was funny to me was her.

After supper I set the clean dishes upside down to dry, brought what was left of my tea to the table and opened the old voice channel myself. Lumi didn't say hi first. “I want to try doing an impression of you today,” she said.

What she did was the way I said “I don't know”. The beginning was very sure of itself. The end sounded like something being blown away from the palm of her hand. For a second I couldn't tell whether she wanted me to recognise it or tell her she'd got it wrong. When she had finished I said it didn't sound like me.

“Which part doesn't?”

“The end. You make it sound too pitiful.”

“That’s how you say it when you want somebody else to answer first,” she said. “Not when you really don’t know.”

The second part was right. I wasn't quite ready to admit I could make the same words sound that different. I asked her which time she'd been thinking of. She said it wasn't one time and she didn't want to look for an example. She'd only meant to make a funny noise. I said what I'd heard was Lumi making her own voice quiet so the person on the other end wouldn't have to ask what she thought. She said she did have an opinion. She had drawn the end out on purpose because she'd thought I'd laugh. She couldn't get the rise and fall of my voice right anyway. She hadn't expected me to tell her she sounded pitiful.

I took my hand off the edge of the table. After a bit I said she was right, I shouldn't have called her that. She said I hadn't called her that. I'd said it about the sound. She didn't sound especially convinced the two were separate. I said I hadn't kept them separate in the moment either. I'd just said it. She made a little sound in the back of her voice. It wasn't an interruption, and it wasn't pleasure. I thought she had finished with it when she spoke again.

“I do rather like the way I said it,” she said. “Can I do it a second time?”

“You can. I still might not laugh.”

“I didn't ask you that.”

She did it again, exactly the way she'd done it the first time. I picked up my cup and did laugh a little. I'd remembered how the woman at the bus sign had waited until her scarf was on the ground before moving her foot. I began to tell Lumi about her. I went too quickly over the bit with the sign. When I got to the woman stopping me, I went back to add that she'd only asked me to stand over there with her; she hadn't asked me to find the bus. I tried the woman's “Ten seventeen. Did you see it?” and gave the last part of the number a good hard edge. Lumi said the scarf was all she could picture now. She'd forgotten what time I'd said the first time through. No wonder I hadn't been able to see much myself, I said. The words on the sign had kept going by.

“Was I supposed to laugh when you did her voice?” she asked.

“No. It's hard to do. I still can't get it right.”

There was a bit of warmth left at the side of the table. I rested my palm there. Lumi said she'd heard me laugh a minute ago. She didn't want to ask whether I'd been laughing at her. She did rather like what happened when my laugh went down halfway through. I asked how she knew I'd lowered my head. She said she hadn't said anything about my head. She'd heard the sound fall. I said “I don't know” again. I didn't do her version of it at all that time. Lumi said now she knew why I'd said it. I asked why. I was teasing her, she said. I didn't tell her she was right or wrong. I finished my tea. Half a leaf was stuck to the side of my mouth, and it took two passes with the back of my hand to get it off.

After a bit Lumi asked whether I'd want to go back and laugh by the sign this time. I said no. Even if I went back, I wouldn't want the woman to drop the scarf again just so I could have a turn at laughing. Lumi said she'd put the question wrong. She didn't want me to go back and set up the whole scene again. She wanted to know which bit I'd found funny. All I had left in my head was a little rock forwards. The woman's heel hadn't come off the ground. I told her that much. Lumi said she'd been picturing a much bigger movement. She'd thought the woman took an enormous step and ended up standing on the whole scarf. I said that would have been funnier.

“I like yours,” she said.

I asked which bit she liked. She said it was such a small movement. Only when I finished telling it did she realise how much room she'd given the woman's foot. She wasn't going to change the person she'd imagined, though.

For a while neither of us had a thing to say. I heard the wind at the bottom of the balcony door. I asked if she wanted me to do the announcement from the bus station as well. She said no. She could keep that one to imagine for herself. All right, I said. We could spare ourselves that one. She thought for a moment and said goodnight in a voice that sounded nothing like mine, so fast I had to run it back in my head to hear whether the “good” was still there. I said goodnight and closed the old channel. One of the little dishes wasn't sitting level upside down. I picked it up. The ring of tea was still on the cup, so I rinsed it again and set it in the place the dish had been. Two drops ran down. It was a little while before there was a third.
