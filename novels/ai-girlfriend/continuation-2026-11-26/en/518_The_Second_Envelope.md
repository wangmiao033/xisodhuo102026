# Chapter 518: The Second Envelope

Mia came over before lunch on Monday to ask if I'd seen a brown envelope. She held her hands as far apart as the cutting mat on my desk. Not the kind we used for putting little things in, she said. You could put a cover sample in this one flat and still have room on either side. I said I'd seen a brown envelope in the entrance that morning. I didn't know if it was hers. Had it had her name on it? I remembered the courier holding a brown sheet of paper against his arm while he moved a pen to his other hand. I hadn't seen what was written. I looked towards the door. The courier was gone. There were a few coarse grains of salt the soles of his shoes might have brought in on the mat.

“Did he give it to you, then?” she asked.

“No. I walked past him.”

“When you said you'd seen it, I thought you meant the thing was already in our room.” She explained she'd arranged for somebody to drop off a separate cover sample today. This wasn't a receipt for the order from before. Around here we seldom said separate unless two things really would be easy to mistake for each other. Couldn't she just check what it said about delivery, I asked. The message only said “on the table.” We had five tables you could put an envelope like that on. Nobody liked to put so much as a drink on the one by the door, which in practice meant people used it to set things down all the time.

We tried the entrance first. There was an open magazine on the short table out front, with a leaf holding it down. The back of the leaf was wet. Just inside, on the long table, was a brown envelope hardly any narrower than the mat she'd shown me. Mia didn't pick it up. She bent to see the flap and asked if this was the one I'd seen. I remembered a distinct crease in one corner of the brown paper that morning. I couldn't see one here. Perhaps someone had smoothed it out when they put it down. There was no delivery slip. A surname had been written across the middle, but the paper was so damp the ink had run into a little bridge between two of the letters. Mia said that wasn't her name. Then she said her name out loud, and seemed to get less certain by the time she came to the end of it.

I asked if we should turn it over. She put her fingertips on one edge. “It's gone soft. There could be paper inside. Will you hold it up from underneath?”

I wanted something dry to slide under it. Somebody had kicked the flattened cardboard under a table leg out of place. I couldn't use that. There were a few empty trays propped against the wall. I drew out the top one and slid it under the envelope, bottom up. The envelope moved a little with it, but the soft corner didn't sag. We turned the envelope and saw a small label on the back with the name of the department on a different floor and the words DO NOT FOLD. Mia didn't open it. I set the tray down flat again. The paper shut out a little band of light at the edge, and a dark line of water began to show. I didn't know if the envelope had been out in the rain. The table was dry.

Mia rang the people upstairs from the entrance and told them there was something inside the door. She didn't tell the sender they'd received it. After that she went to look in the back room. I stayed by the tray, close enough to the door that two people bringing up a box of books asked if I was on the front desk that week. I said I wasn't and stepped aside. They put their box down in the spot that already had their names on it. One of them trod the curled end of the mat flat for me on the way out. It took me another half a minute to work out that whoever came down for the envelope might walk right by it without seeing the label on the back. I thought about turning the tray round. I didn't want the dark line to get any wider if I pushed the envelope. I left it where it was.

Mia came back with a thin grey envelope. A length of tape from the printer was still on the back. There was a board inside, she said, and there'd be room for a sample even in an envelope this size. She'd found it on the worktop on the inside of the stairwell. Somebody had written her full name across it in pencil. Was this the other envelope she'd thought was brown? She'd wait to hear from the sender before she opened it, she said. She'd ordered exactly one sample. If this wasn't it, opening someone else's would leave her with one more thing than she could account for. I thought about the way she'd held her hands apart in the air. The grey envelope upright under her arm was a good deal narrower than that. Half the space she'd shown me was space she'd meant to leave around it.

I asked if she had lunch. She did, cold noodles in the fridge. She'd been waiting half the morning and was hungry now, but didn't quite want to give up her place by the door. I offered to take the grey envelope upstairs for her, though I wouldn't wait for the person dropping off the sample. She looked at me. “Actually, I'd like to keep it with me and eat while I wait.” I left it in her hands. The person from upstairs arrived for the brown one. I told him we all used the tray and asked if he could leave it here once he was done with it. He put the department name written on the paper next to the one on his own note. He bent down to get hold of two corners of the tray and didn't ask me what was in the envelope. On the stairs he took it very slowly. He didn't prop it on the rail. When somebody came up behind him he stopped and let them by before going on.

When I packed up in the afternoon, Mia had a ruler holding the open grey envelope down, and a sheet with a raised pattern spread out beside it. The sender had answered. The tape from the printer was what he used to mark it. She sent him a photo and wrote that the new sample had arrived. She didn't bring up the earlier order. I didn't ask if she'd looked that one up while she was at it. The strip of tape she'd lifted from the flap had gone onto the back of the sample paper. A few little fibres stuck up where she'd peeled it away. She rubbed them down and asked me to come closer and see. The front felt a little smoother, she said. The back was like cloth. It took me two tries to tell the difference. Did I want her to turn it over once more? No, I said, I'd know it now. We were touching different corners. Our hands didn't meet.

It was as cold in my entrance at home as it was outside. The kitchen light came on at the first press and I didn't touch the switch a second time. The last apricot was still on the windowsill. I didn't eat it. I found an empty saucer and put it under the fruit where it was. The book was still open on the table, and the fold in the old sale notice was still under the shallow saucer. I pulled the notice out, folded it neatly and slipped it back under the saucer. Beside the book, the sheet I'd drawn my hook on was still face down. I closed the book and shifted it a little farther away, shadow and all. I didn't look for a pen. There was the whole evening left if I wanted to read. Having a hand free didn't mean I had to pick anything up with it straightaway.
