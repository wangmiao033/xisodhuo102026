# Chapter 176: Not to Prove Anything

Elena brought an empty basket.

On February 6, Adrian looked from it to her deliberately unoccupied other hand and asked whether she'd really brought no documents.

“One letter. In my coat.”

He raised an eyebrow.

“Not for you to deliver. I can do that. The basket is for oranges.”

Martha observed that the conservatory might not sell fruit. Elena stiffened. She'd plainly considered this outing carefully and missed something ordinary.

“Something else, then.”

Adrian offered to carry the basket without laughing at her imperfect preparations. They were visiting a public winter conservatory, with a sitting gallery beside the gardeners' heated sheds. It wasn't royal property. No borrowed invitation was needed.

Meltwater trickled between stones. Elena avoided one puddle and splashed her shoe anyway. Frowning at it, she didn't demand carriage delivery through the gate. Adrian offered a hand. She took it naturally until they crossed the narrow stones.

Not their first time holding hands. Precisely why she wanted it to mean something other than an explanation demanded by somebody else.

The elderly man at the gate collected ordinary admission and warned them against touching new grafts. Rosenberg prompted no special response. The basket scratching leaves concerned him more.

“Do you sell oranges?” Elena asked.

“No. Those two trees aren't old enough for picking.”

She lowered the basket beside her skirt. Adrian noticed and let her embarrassment pass quietly.

Inside, water clouded the glass. Damp earth smelled freshly watered. Flowers weren't uniformly abundant; leaves carried insect damage. Elena stopped before a severely pruned tree and asked whether it was dying. The gardener said it was conserving its strength. Look again later; he couldn't promise a blossom today.

“Nobody here guarantees things either,” she said.

“Were you shopping for guarantees?”

“Oranges.”

Along the narrow path, Adrian finally asked about the letter.

Instead of immediately producing it, Elena explained. She was asking a legal adviser who wasn't employed by her father about independently reviewing Adrian's work arrangements: pay, departure, housing, and whether private companionship had become ambiguously entangled with them.

“I don't mean to choose your adviser. I'm asking whether separate consultations are possible, and how whoever pays can avoid affecting what he tells you. You can choose someone else.”

Adrian stopped.

“Is yesterday's question whether I should leave the house?”

“No.” Too quickly. She lowered her face. “I was afraid you'd hear that. I didn't know how to begin.”

He waited without offering her an easier sentence. If she was going to say something difficult, finishing it for her would only give them another way not to hear it.

“I'm also afraid you'll never consider leaving because it might embarrass me, or seem to mean you don't like me.”

Water dripped from a leaf. She rubbed the basket's rim and withdrew her hand.

“Of course I want you somewhere I can see you. It isn't always a noble wish. Paying wages doesn't mean I've made every route equally easy.”

Adrian remembered the room where he'd first awoken, and people looking at Elena before calling him a servant. Speaking in council hadn't erased that relationship.

“I'd like independent advice. Changes to my work wait until I've read it. Don't dismiss me or keep me in advance.”

Only then did she nod and draw the unsealed letter from inside her coat. He took time to read it rather than offering approval because she looked anxious. His name was there, but it wasn't a signature she had provided on his behalf. It didn't announce a new employment arrangement. It asked whether the adviser could accept consultations and how conflicts would be avoided.

Adrian added that his response should come directly to him, not through her desk. Elena swallowed and agreed.

“Don't like that?”

“Not being the first to know.”

“Thank you for not saying you don't mind at all.”

She glared. “Still terrible at comfort.”

“I could say they'll sell you oranges.”

“That would be deception.”

They laughed. She put the letter away. She'd submit it normally when they left; acceptance and appointments would await a reply. No new contract, no immediate unemployment, no signature abolishing discomfort.

Passing white flowers, Elena unexpectedly admitted disliking their scent.

“They're often in your room.”

“Someone first said they suited me. Then so many people sent them that explaining seemed harder than changing water.”

She frowned after the light remark. Flowers weren't another grievance to place at someone's feet. They'd been kindly given and accepted. She hadn't said she disliked them. She could tell the person tending her room now, rather than expect every giver to guess an unspoken preference.

“What do you like?”

She walked slowly along the orderly rows of pots, looking for an answer of her own rather than choosing the nearest expensive bloom. At last she pointed to a low herb with very dark leaves. Nothing expensive; the label named a kitchen seasoning. With permission, she rubbed a single plucked leaf, smelled it, and offered it to him.

Adrian leaned too close and sneezed.

Elena stared, then laughed behind her hand. Laughing too, he admitted being an undignified admirer.

“Am I not allowed to like it now?”

“You are. Just don't put the whole pot beside my pillow.”

“I hadn't planned as far as your pillow.”

She blushed first. He didn't press her about whether she'd imagined it. Returning the leaf to the gardener's little paper, she said she still liked the scent.

“I know.”

It didn't sound dismissive. They walked on, basket empty. At least one preference needn't become identical for them to enjoy each other.

Two elderly women occupied half a gallery bench, arguing over whose curtains a flower resembled. Elena considered somewhere quieter. Adrian asked whether they required the entire gallery to themselves.

She gave him a look before sitting down beside him. There was room enough without asking strangers to surrender the pleasant part of their morning.

The women neither announced a romance nor departed at the sight of good coats. The empty basket rested at their feet, no longer pretending it would soon be full.

Elena looked at her gloves.

“I like you. You know that.”

“I know. I've said it too.”

“I don't need you to say it for the first time.”

She looked up. Their handholding, outings, and spoken affection hadn't disappeared. She was tired of every conversation about them beginning with a court, a family, or someone else's question.

“I want to know whether you want to keep seeing me seriously. Not to make gossip kinder. Not because we survived something together.”

Adrian didn't immediately offer forever. His pause tightened her chest. She resisted explaining it for him.

“Yes. And I want it understood that I didn't first choose you today. Every new crisis shouldn't take us back to not knowing how the other feels.”

She drew a slow breath. A crooked crease formed in the glove folded between her fingers. She had rehearsed the question far more often than she had imagined what to do after an answer she wanted.

“Why think so long, then?”

“Because yes shouldn't sound like a guarantee I'll never disappoint you.”

“I'm not that greedy.”

He looked at her.

“Not today, at least.”

Beside them an elderly woman burst out laughing. Her companion had called a flower by her husband's name. Elena laughed too, losing the solemn next sentence she'd prepared.

Adrian laid his hand on the bench. She moved closer and took it. They announced no engagement and didn't treat other people's feelings as resolved paperwork. He still owed serious answers of his own. Elena shouldn't have to reject everyone for him to prove their relationship.

“I'll get jealous.”

“I know.”

“I might say it badly.”

“Then we'll talk afterward. Not grant you permission now to hurt people every time.”

Annoyed, she loosened her grip slightly.

“And you can't give everyone the same hope to seem kind.”

That quieted him. He'd found it easy to call postponed refusal consideration, leaving hard answers for later. She wasn't demanding a public verdict on named people. She was asking him not to purchase convenience with her patience.

“I'll answer for myself.”

She nodded, less tense. For a while they remained where they were, listening as the neighboring argument about curtains finally came to an end. Elena didn't try to reclaim the solemn speech she had forgotten. Adrian didn't fill the silence with a larger promise. No larger crisis needed to prove their words worth keeping.

Before leaving, Elena asked to kiss him.

Her ears reddened first. Adrian smiled, said yes, and leaned closer. The kiss was brief, her hand still holding his, clear of the scar. No misplaced step, no startled audience, no later explanation of an accident.

When she drew back, her first observation was that it smelled of soil.

“Regret the conservatory?”

“No. I'd prepared something prettier.”

“That was accurate.”

She pushed him lightly without needing a serious rebuttal. One elderly woman, hunting her scarf, asked whether they were sitting on its edge. Both rose. It had slipped behind the basket. They hurried to return it. Her thanks spared little dignity for their solemn moment.

At the exit, oranges weren't for sale, but packets of hardy herb seeds and the gardener's dried fruit were. Elena bought seeds and asked whether inexperience would waste them. Read the season instructions first, he said. No need to plant immediately. Failure shouldn't be blamed on seeds lacking respect for nobility.

She put the small packet in the large basket. There was so much basket around the little packet that its emptiness had become funny. She no longer tried to conceal it against her skirt.

Adrian bought dried fruit with his own money and set it alongside. They emerged carrying far less than a full basket. Sunlight lay on wet paving.

As planned, Elena submitted her inquiry herself and collected her acknowledgment. Adrian separately left his contact details. There was still no confirmed appointment. Neither the person at the receiving desk nor the acknowledgment promised that the adviser would accept the matter. They had made an inquiry, not settled the questions in it.

Back at the house, the driver asked about preparing the carriage at the same hour tomorrow. Elena looked at Adrian first. He had another morning arrangement, so she said not for now. They would ask if needed. Today's affection wasn't a self-renewing daily itinerary.

Martha saw the basket and inevitably asked about oranges.

Elena showed the seeds and admitted the trees hadn't agreed.

“When do these get planted?”

“After checking the season.”

At least, Martha observed, nobody had commanded flowers to bloom in winter this time. Adrian lowered his face, laughing. Elena suddenly didn't want to argue either.

At an empty turn before the stairs, she lightly caught his sleeve.

“I'll still mean what I said tomorrow.”

“So will I.”

She let go. They returned to unfinished work. At her door she looked back and waved, not summoning him or adding a request. She'd simply found him looking back too, and was glad.

Downstairs remained a basket that had spent most of the day empty. Its two small purchases had decided nobody's whole life.
