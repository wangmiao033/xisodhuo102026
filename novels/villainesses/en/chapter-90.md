# Chapter 90: The Witch's Name Wasn't on the Stone

On the morning of December 6, the town's custodian of antiquities carried three books to the table. Half a dried leaf slipped from the thickest volume.

Adrian caught it. A borrowing slip lay beside the leaf, dated many years before he had arrived in this world.

“Does that count as a new discovery?” Viola asked.

“It counts as someone never coming back to renew their loan,” the custodian said.

She had brought the wooden bird with her. A carpenter had shaved away a little of the ring where it caught, allowing it to come off safely. A pale band remained around the bird's neck, as though it had worn a poorly fitting scarf. When the custodian noticed it, he moved his ink bottle farther away.

They began by checking the construction ledger found the day before. The threshold stone's angled notch, thickness, and surviving mortar had all been included in the site drawing. The stone itself remained in the shed under town custody. An earlier inventory of public property listed the contract registration pavilion before its demolition.

In the inventory's accompanying sketch, the long stone stood upright inside the pavilion. It had not been a boundary marker. Its inscription explained the registration of shared grain reserves and road-maintenance responsibilities. Grooves below held replaceable record plates. Each year's places and assigned contributions could be written on the plates instead of being carved into the stone anew.

“So originally, it was a notice that let people see the terms,” Adrian said.

“That's the use recorded here.” The custodian turned the page. “Whether it had been altered before that is a question for someone who knows how to read the tool marks. This is as much as I can say about the drawings you brought.”

Cecilia laid the drawing of the lining beside the book. The curved channels they had seen yesterday now had a verifiable explanation: they had marked the positions for paired registration plates, indicating both orientation and boundaries. Whether those plates shared their materials and surviving patterns with the lining from a different paper sleeve would still require a more detailed comparison of the craftsmanship.

An old threshold had not told them what anyone was thinking. But for the first time, those two unfamiliar curves looked like something people had once used in an ordinary way.

“This supplement has a clearer drawing.” The custodian brought out the third book.

It was a supplement on preserving old contract inscriptions, published three years earlier. Its pages were thinner than the inventory's. The author compared groove designs from several regions, taking particular care to warn restorers against joining two neighboring curves into a single closed line. Closing the gap would cause readers to misinterpret the original mounting direction.

Cecilia leaned closer to the name printed in the margin.

Luna Ashcroft.

“I've seen that surname in the Academy catalog,” she said. “But I've never met her.”

Adrian saw it too.

In *Crown of Fate*, that name had always come with the violet glow of experiments and a dangerous smile. He remembered players calling her the Forbidden Witch. He also remembered one route in which she had offered the protagonist a contract without giving them a chance to finish reading it.

As for how she restored a stone, what she ate for breakfast, or whom she had paid to print her books, the game had said nothing.

He returned his attention to the drawing.

“Can we find out where she is now?”

The custodian fetched a public directory of craft researchers. It listed her specialties as historical contract media and defunct seals. Her correspondence address was the East Ridge Forbidden Arts Research Tower, an institution for storing decommissioned implements and studying dangerous old arts, registered under the supervision of the regional academy.

“People in town say she turns visitors into glass bottles,” the custodian said. “But last year she returned an incorrect drawing we'd sent her, and enclosed the postage.”

“Would a glass bottle refund postage?” Viola asked.

“That's why I kept the letter.”

The address on the envelope matched the directory. Its contents concerned only the care of stone. After reading it, Adrian felt the witch in her game portrait step a little way aside.

At noon, Ivo's reply reached the inn as well.

The envelope had first been registered in the capital, then forwarded with verification documents to the regional post station. In his letter, Ivo explained that he had waited for the Academy to locate drawings of old specimens before completing his reply. All those days of correspondence made their little examination on November 24 feel a long way behind them.

Cecilia read his questions before looking for his conclusion. He wanted to know the light-source settings, the orientation of the reference specimen, and the original lining's condition in storage. He commended them for preserving the uncompleted drawing, then explained that his current equipment could not establish when the material had been made.

The expert he recommended was also Luna Ashcroft.

“I've checked her latest research registration. She's still at East Ridge,” Cecilia read. “The enclosed address is suitable for an ordinary consultation. She knows more about carriers of this kind than I do, though she has a habit of calling anything unexplained something worth trying immediately. Please read that last sentence to your traveling companions too.”

Elena looked up from across the table.

“I appreciate that last sentence very much.”

The letter did not claim Luna could solve the mystery of the old betrothal. Still less did it place them at a stranger's disposal. Ivo confirmed her area of expertise and provided the titles of two published papers so they could check her work for themselves.

Adrian set the two independently found leads together. The inscription supplement showed that she had studied similar structures. Ivo's reply established that she was still working in the field. The stone itself had never once supplied them with a living person's name.

“I'd like to meet her,” he said.

Elena did not agree immediately.

“Being well enough to sit in a carriage for two days doesn't mean you can spend all day standing at an experimental bench once you arrive. We could send the drawings first and wait for her reply.”

He looked outside. The winter sun had already moved past the eaves, and the new stone in the courtyard had not all been carried away. Waiting for another letter was a genuine option. His impatience did not entitle him to call it cowardice.

“I'd like to ask in person,” he said. “There are questions about the old registration I don't want other people to keep asking on my behalf. If she can only help with the stone, we'll start with the stone. I won't use the time spent traveling as a reason we're owed an answer.”

“We rest for half a day when we arrive.”

“I can agree to that.”

“And if her appointment times don't suit, we stay in town. No extra night of hard travel just to see her.”

“Agreed.”

Elena took out the carriage company's two quotes and pointed to the carriage with wider seats and a better-enclosed hood.

“Then I'm choosing this one. I have questions of my own for her.”

Once the carriage was settled, Elena brought out the scarf she had mentioned the night before. Yellow-brown and dark green intertwined like an autumn woodland stubbornly refusing to shed its last leaves. As promised, Adrian did not laugh. He ran his fingers seriously over its soft surface.

“Very warm.”

“I know.”

“And if I get lost in the snow, easy to spot.”

“I asked you not to laugh. That wasn't an invitation to find another way of saying it.”

Cecilia finally laughed aloud. Viola studied the colors and earnestly agreed that they would indeed stand out more than gray clothes. Elena set the scarf on the table, having concluded that no one in the room was currently capable of appreciation.

Adrian picked it up and asked whether she would sit beside him by the window tomorrow.

“Of course.” Realizing how quickly she had answered, she added that they would have a good view of the scenery.

“And I'd have a good view of you.”

She looked at him. Both retorts she had prepared arrived too late. In the end, she reached over, picked a short loose thread from the scarf, and left it in his lap.

Viola lifted her wooden bird off the price list and went to check the carriage. She did not insist on sitting beside the coachman to keep watch in the wind. Instead, she marked two post stations on the route where they could rest.

Cecilia did not take the map just yet. She went to the clinic first and checked the final three pages of the relief accounts with the local clerk responsible for supplies.

One injured carpenter's expenses were still in dispute. The previous form had left out part of the carriage fares for his dressing changes. The person taking over suggested they wait until she returned from East Ridge. Cecilia opened that page again.

“His next appointment is next week. We can't wait until I come back to discover the money still hasn't been arranged.”

She went with the clerk to the carriage company, verified the portion that remained unpaid, and obtained the receipts needed for payment. Those went to the office already responsible for processing them. The remaining disputed points were left with named contacts and response dates. They were not tucked into an envelope labeled “Until the Saint Returns.”

Only after inspecting the handover form did Dr. Shaw pour her a glass of water.

“You can go now.”

Cecilia held the glass. Something in her felt lighter, and a small space felt empty.

“You sound very keen to get rid of me.”

“I'm keen for people who've taught others how to do a job to let them finish doing it.”

Cecilia laughed, drank her water, and went to say goodbye to the nurses outside the ward. Martin was resting, still under neutral guard. She did not wake him simply to make her farewells complete.

Back at the inn, she wrote to Mira in the capital with her new forwarding address and asked whether she had finished knitting her scarf. Only after sealing the envelope did she move her own trunk beside the door.

Before leaving, Viola made another visit to the town offices. She gave the receiving clerk the locations of civilian houses suitable for sheltering wounded travelers along the escort routes. One household had moved and needed to be crossed out. They could not keep sending people there because it was marked on an old map.

The clerk asked if she would be leading a troop of knights again soon.

“Not for now,” she said. “But someday, I'd like to make it easier for the people willing to protect these roads to find one another.”

She did not give a name to a force that did not yet exist. She left only addresses she actually knew and the conditions under which the householders had agreed to receive people. Outside, her bag swung against her shoulder. She shifted it to her right and went to join the others waiting for lunch.

The express rider left that afternoon, ahead of them.

He carried an ordinary consultation letter, a copy of Ivo's introduction, and drawings whose respective custodians had authorized their sharing. There were no patients' addresses, no military deployments, and no long stone: that remained in Greymarch. The rider would change horses along the post road and was expected to reach the tower the following day. If she would not receive them, her answer could reach their intermediate post station the day after that.

At the same time, Viola reported the East Ridge tower and their receiving post stations to the regional liaison office. Her fourteen days of leave still began on the fourth. Changing her destination did not start the count again.

At dusk, Adrian walked to the bridge. The new planks had been nailed firmly into place, and only one ribbon remained from the mill market's celebration. The chestnut stall was still beside the bridge, its owner using a wooden spoon to turn the hot nuts up from the bottom of the pan.

Viola stood next to it with a small bag for tomorrow's journey.

“More reconnaissance?” he asked.

“Buying things for the road.”

“Doesn't the wooden bird count?”

“It eats very little.”

They walked a short way along the bridge. Last time, they had stopped here because Adrian was tired. This time, the road continued ahead, though tomorrow she would be taking a different one.

She was not disappointed.

“When I come back, I'd like to go behind the mill properly,” she said. “There's an old orchard. It blooms in spring.”

“An invitation for spring?”

“Something I'd like to do. I'll ask you then.”

Adrian nodded. “I'll remember.”

She handed him a warm chestnut and tasted another herself. They did not wait until the cold wind made his chest uncomfortable before returning. At the mouth of the lane, they turned back together.

At the inn, Elena was labeling the trunks. She had deliberately left space in her book chest so that new purchases would not have to travel stuffed inside a hat.

Adrian went to put the travel book away, but she stopped him.

“Leave that one out.”

“To read on the road?”

“We haven't finished tonight's reading yet.”

He sat beside her. Viola took the wooden bird upstairs, and Cecilia checked her letter to Mira one final time. Outside, the coachman led tomorrow's horses into the stable, their warm breath clouding in the lamplight.

Halfway through the reading, Adrian bent to pick up his fallen left glove. The old crescent scar lay quietly on the back of his hand. Nothing in the room lit up in response to it. He set the glove on the coat he would wear tomorrow, then turned a page for Elena, its paper softly yellow in the firelight.

The captain in the book had finally reached the sea.

Their carriage would wait until morning.
