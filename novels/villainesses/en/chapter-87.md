# Chapter 87: Her Name, Without the Word Traitor

On December 3, Viola checked her collar three times outside the hearing room.

The dark green ribbon was perfectly tied. Her collar wasn't crooked either. Elena had checked once, Cecilia had checked again, and eventually even the clerk carrying chairs felt compelled to assure her that no one inside would deduct points for it.

“I wasn't—”

She stopped. There was no particular need to explain.

Adrian sat on the bench in the corridor and offered her a cup of warm water. When she took it, she found a note from the innkeeper pasted to its base: Bring back the cup.

“She isn't worried about the verdict?”

“She said the cup had to come back whatever happened.”

At last, Viola managed a small smile.

When the door opened, she left the cup with Adrian. She didn't ask him to listen to the hardest parts for her. She went to the witness table, where the suspension order had once lain, and rested her fingers flat on the wood.

The supplementary questioning had lasted until the previous afternoon. The document examiner had checked Northroad's retained instruction copy: its paper, the order in which the ink had been applied, and the signatures. The custodians had separately explained which file compartment it had come from and when it had been sealed. Two objections raised by Morrow's lawyer were in the record as well. One payment had covered legitimate repairs and could not be counted toward the raid. One courier had seen only the sealed envelope, not the complete instructions inside.

The investigators had removed the repair payment from their calculation. They had not asked the courier to recite words he had never read.

The remaining withdrawal acknowledgment, carrier's register, instruction copy, and statements from the people who handled them still agreed within their respective limits. Plans to intercept wagons on the lower river road, burn the remaining grain, and report the loss as an enemy raid appeared in records made before the attack. None showed Viola participating in the payments or assignments. The grain total and November 4 annex previously used against her had already been shown, respectively, to contain double counting and a substituted document.

While Ward read the findings, Viola looked at a shallow mark in the tabletop.

She had traced it with her fingernail before, trying not to shake while she waited for questions. Now she could lift her head and hear what remained true even after every qualification.

“The criminal investigation into Morrow and the other participants will continue,” the colonel said. “Our ruling concerning Captain Blackwood today does not replace proceedings concerning anyone else.”

Morrow was not in the room. His case had gone to the special investigation, and his lawyer had received the relevant material. Giving Viola a fair hearing was no reason to deny another person the opportunity to answer allegations.

The questioning officer then placed the November 14 acknowledgment beneath the lamp.

It was not a document discovered that morning. Viola had written it beside the river road. The messenger had carried it back with the four riders she sent ahead. Its arrival time, the messenger's testimony, and the garrison duty register had already been checked during the earlier hearings.

“Return delayed. Wounded people remain on an exposed stretch of road,” the officer read. Did she still confirm that account?

“Yes.”

“You understood that the major had ordered the entire patrol back?”

“Yes. I did not bring everyone back within the original time required. I chose to keep the rear party with the last wagon until it was free, then escort it into town.”

Her adviser did not signal for her to make it sound less serious.

Major Voss stood and went to the map. The danger to the town gate that day had been real. When he issued the recall, he had not known that the last stretcher wagon was stuck on a narrow stretch of road without cover. After receiving Viola's acknowledgment, he had adjusted the gate defense. The four riders sent ahead had indeed joined the duty force.

“I still consider the recall to have been fully justified,” he said. Then he looked at another point on the map. “The reason she sent back was true as well.”

Transport records, the attendants, and the riders independently supported the wagon's condition and the timing of the evacuation. The committee did not assume there had been no other danger simply because everyone later reached town safely. It considered the road, the wounded, and the available personnel as Viola had known them when she made her decision, together with the partial return and prompt report she had actually arranged.

One committee member asked whether the wounded could have been left temporarily while all the riders returned.

Viola looked at the short blue line marking the river.

“That would have left people who couldn't move themselves on a road the attackers had just used. There was no replacement escort, and no building that could shield the approach. I can't describe it as an option equally safe for them.”

She did not claim she had known in advance that the town gate would not fall. She hadn't known.

Voss sat down and pushed the acknowledgment toward the clerk. His hand paused at the edge of the paper, as though he had meant to say something directly to her and had decided to let the actual record speak first.

The Alder Watch fire came next. The loss of the original books remained in the file. The real choice between trying to recover the hanging records and rescuing Martin was not erased. On the conditions established in the testimony, the committee found Viola's decisions in helping determine the rescue route and assisting the withdrawal necessary. The evidence loss still had to remain in the account; a favorable decision was no reason to remove it.

The written authority for the November 29 South Bridge assignment, her actions there, and the return of the borrowed short sword were recorded separately. They established that she had carried out a new assignment that night. They could not retroactively make every earlier dispute disappear.

The room fell quiet.

Ward lifted the final page. The other two committee members had signed it. After checking the commission's scope and the procedure, the royal judicial observer signed as witness, confirming that the decision would be delivered to the military and municipal registration offices.

“The allegations that Captain Blackwood colluded with the enemy, misappropriated military grain, and assisted the raid have no sustainable basis in the verified evidence. They are formally withdrawn. The corresponding treason review is closed.”

Viola heard her own surname. For a moment, she could not connect it to the words that followed.

The colonel continued. The delayed return on November 14 had been a necessary response to the specific emergency, promptly reported and accompanied by feasible measures to reduce the competing danger. No disciplinary sanction would be imposed. The November 17 suspension and the remaining case-related restrictions would end upon service of the decision. Her rank and good standing were restored; actual duties would be arranged separately. The public entries concerning her were to be corrected accordingly.

“You may obtain the complete decision and request correction of any transcription errors.” Ward put the page down. “Blackwood. Did you understand?”

Viola opened her mouth.

She had imagined this day many times. Standing perfectly straight. Answering calmly. Perhaps getting out of the room before anyone saw how badly she needed to let out a breath.

“Yes.”

Her voice was quieter than she had expected. Nothing in it needed to be said again.

The clerk brought her a copy. She signed the receipt, then stopped at the end of her name. Ink slowly gathered into a tiny spot beneath the nib.

Adrian did not get up to take the pen away.

She lifted it herself, waited for the ink to dry, and put the paper away.

In the corridor, Owen tried to salute and smile at the same time. The two intentions collided in a very peculiar expression. Viola nodded to him first, and his hand finally found the right place.

Voss came out behind her.

“I'll have the registry correct the notice today. You may collect your sword as well,” he said. “We'll discuss your next posting after your medical review.”

She looked at him without immediately offering to return to her old unit.

He did not hurry her.

“I received that acknowledgment on the fourteenth,” he added. “In the future, we need to bring the information from both ends together faster.”

“Yes.”

She accepted that much without using it to finish a conversation they had not yet had.

A few people were waiting outside. The old woman who had insisted on bringing her pot carried a basket of bread wrapped in cloth. She had evidently prepared quite a speech. When she saw Viola, all that came out was, “Have you eaten?”

Viola shook her head.

“Then eat. They're fresh.”

The first bite was too hot. Viola handled it rather badly. The old woman laughed and told her to slow down, then mentioned the draft under the door at the relief hall. Who should she ask about that? Viola pointed out the municipal repairs window instead of promising to take the door apart herself that afternoon.

The woman remembered the directions, left the basket with them, and went on her way.

People in the street recognized Viola and offered congratulations. Others were busy hauling carts and had no idea what had just been read in the hearing room. She had thought she might need the whole town to fall silent. Now she was glad the bread seller was still calling out his prices.

Elena suggested that she get into the carriage first, then went around the other side to discuss lunch with Cecilia. Adrian remained by the door and reached for her cup.

Viola looked down at the note on its base and suddenly laughed.

Halfway through the laugh, tears spilled down her face.

She turned away at once, searching her pocket with her right hand. Adrian offered his handkerchief. He didn't tell her she ought to be happy now. He didn't ask why she still hurt.

“I thought I wouldn't cry.”

“Would you like me to return the cup first?”

She shook her head. After a moment, she added softly, “You can stay here.”

So he stayed.

At the corner, a driver held his horse to let a wagon of timber pass. Viola dried her face and folded the handkerchief. Only then did she realize she had been clutching the decision against her chest.

“After today, will everyone just ask me about the next thing?”

“Possibly. You can tell them you need lunch first.”

“I want an afternoon when I'm not responsible for anything.”

“What would you like to do?”

She looked toward the distant mill roof and thought about it seriously.

“Sit down. Eat something I don't have to finish between patrols. And, if you'd like to, have you sit with me for a while.”

“I'd like that.”

Adrian steadied the carriage door while she climbed in herself. She leaned against the cushion. For once, she did not need to calculate how far she was from her duty station.

After lunch, they really did sit in the inn's back courtyard for a while. Elena ordered hot pies for everyone that evening. Cecilia first went to the clinic to inform Martin's adviser of the formal decision. The witness's protection would not suddenly end because Viola had been cleared, and Cecilia did not declare on his behalf that he was safe forever.

Viola left the decision upstairs. When she came down, all she carried was a little dish of apple sweets.

She asked Adrian whether he wanted the sourest one. After studying her expression, he cautiously chose another. That made her laugh so hard she could hardly stop, even as her own sweet made her eyes squeeze shut.

Toward the end of the afternoon, someone from the town office brought the corrected notice to the inn and asked her to check the spelling of her name. The innkeeper dried her hands and cleared a dry patch beside the door. The earlier notice still had to be retained under the archive rules. That did not mean its correction could remain hidden in a clerk's drawer.

Viola stood beside them as the new sheet was smoothed into place.

A young man who often delivered flour stopped and read it twice. Then he quietly admitted that he had repeated what people were saying about her.

She looked at him. He was holding his cap, its brim creased between his hands. Clearly, he hadn't prepared himself for an answer.

“If you hear someone say it again, please show them this.”

He nodded and picked up the flour. He did not ask her to smile at once and tell him it hadn't mattered.

Adrian put the nail box away for the innkeeper. Viola waited until the handcart had gone before asking whether she had been too cold.

“You told him something he could do.”

She looked at the date in the bottom corner of the notice. The paper was thin. Not everything she had been carrying would lift from her shoulders with it. But from today onward, she no longer had to defend every moment of unhappiness as part of proving her innocence.

The innkeeper brought out a bowl of freshly cooked chestnuts for them to take back into the courtyard. Viola accepted it without first asking whose share it was supposed to be.

When she collected her sword that evening, the armorer asked her to check the old scratches on the scabbard. She examined each one and signed. She did not immediately draw the blade to test it.

The sword was still hers.

Where she would choose to carry it when she woke tomorrow no longer had to be answered by naming someone she owed.
