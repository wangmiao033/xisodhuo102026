# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Three: The Knight Says She's Responsible for Me
### Chapter 66: Her Sword Didn't Follow the Flag

The alarm bell by the river rang early on November 14.

Adrian Reed was already dressed, but he still held a slice of untouched bread. The warning delivered the previous evening had kept the inn's lamps burning late. When the bell actually sounded, he spent half a heartbeat wondering whether he'd mistaken the breakfast hour.

A chair scraped downstairs. Elena appeared in his doorway, looked at his face, then at the bread.

“Bring it. Being frightened doesn't mean you don't need to eat.”

He put it in his coat pocket. Her injured hand remained wrapped in thin gauze. With the other, she fastened her shawl, leaving the heavy medicine chest by the stairs for someone else.

They reached the south gate as a scout pulled up his steaming horse. Blood dripped from his cuff onto the frost.

“Willowford. Two roofs burning at the eastern end. More than a dozen riders, blue flags with white markings. People have been cut. The villagers are retreating toward the high bridge!”

This was no uncertain smoke on the horizon, no secondhand rumor.

Viola handed a clean roll of cloth to a sergeant and told him to apply pressure to the scout's wound. Lieutenant Rusk had men bringing out the spare spear racks and closing the turn toward the warehouses.

“I'll hold the slope and the town gate,” he said. “The patrol you take won't magically replace itself here.”

“I know. Just the unit approved yesterday.”

Viola spread her map. Her finger settled on the thin line along the lower ground. The lower river road prepared yesterday had not been cut off. Yet people were being driven toward the damaged high bridge, beyond which the road narrowed between steep rock faces.

She had the duty officer record her numbers, then addressed the waiting civilian wagons.

“The collection point is still the old oil press. Wait until the scouts mark the cleared route with white cloth before advancing. If our line falls back to the courtyard wall, every wagon returns to town immediately.”

Adrian nodded. Declining a round-the-clock personal guard yesterday didn't prevent him from respecting a battle cordon today. He checked the brass whistle in his pocket without blowing it.

Elena confirmed where the medical wagon would travel before boarding their carriage. She left the curtain partly open, following the hoofprints disappearing past the frosted grass.

“Does your hand hurt?” Adrian asked.

“It's all right. How are you?”

“Rather close to being sick.”

She offered her uninjured hand, palm up. He took it. Both their hands were too cold to offer much comfort, but neither let go.

A low rise separated the oil press from the village entrance. The stone courtyard sheltered them from the north wind and hid much of the rising smoke. Dr. Miriam Shaw, the town physician, had already laid clean sheets beneath the eaves. She was in her forties, with her sleeves neatly rolled. Her first question concerned how many wagons could take patients lying flat.

Adrian was given a seat at a long table inside the entrance to register arrivals. Elena recorded the charges quoted by traders willing to rent out their vehicles. When the first stretcher wagon arrived, neither had time to look at a map again.

An elderly woman was carried down with a cut across her forehead and a brass key clenched in one hand. She asked three times whether anyone had locked her door. Dr. Shaw had an attendant answer her three times, without telling her to be quiet.

Next came a young man with a crushed foot. His father followed, his face bloodless, his trouser legs trembling. When Adrian asked his name, the old man said only, “He'll walk again, won't he?”

Adrian lowered his pen.

“The doctor is examining him. Sit here, please. She'll know where to find you when she needs to ask about him.”

He couldn't promise anything about the foot. The chair, at least, was real.

A driver offered to unload his bolts of cloth in the courtyard to make room for passengers. Once the cloth was gone, a protruding nail showed beneath the wagon boards. Adrian spotted it and stopped an attendant from laying bedding over it, then asked the driver to fetch a hammer. They fixed the board before putting down the straw mattress. Only then did an elderly man with difficulty walking climb aboard, holding the rail.

He kept a bread basket in his lap. There was no bread inside, just a small portrait wrapped in a flour sack.

“She hated it when I stuffed things away,” he said, touching the portrait. “Hasn't scolded me today.”

Adrian didn't ask where the woman in the picture was. He tucked the loose cloth back around it and entered the man's name in the boarding register. His handwriting was poor. He blotted an ink spot and carefully wrote the name again.

Hooves suddenly thundered outside. The gate sergeant kept the bar in place until Owen called the day's password, then opened the smaller gate.

“The western footpath is clear. The main road is blocked. The captain wants the second empty wagon moved farther inside. More stretchers coming.”

As Owen turned to leave, a woman clutching an old quilt caught his reins and pointed uphill.

“Someone said the army was at the bridge. Told us to go across!”

“Who?”

“A man in a leather cap. I don't know him.”

Owen didn't keep her there pointing. He backed his horse away and told the guards, “Hold the junction toward the bridge. Get her inside first.”

Adrian looked beyond the gate. A corner of blue flag appeared on the rise. A white shape flickered in the wind, then vanished behind the trees. From a distance, it certainly resembled a foreign army's colors.

He remembered the border raid in the game: a few lines of text. A devastated village, a knight who had failed, a verdict. Here, people fled with their house keys. Some had no time to put on both shoes. The ending he remembered couldn't tell him which footpath was still open.

Someone actually on that road had to find out.

Viola returned with mud across her horse's neck and a broken strip of cloth caught on her left shoulder plate. She stayed mounted, first directing the patrol behind her to bring the villagers inside. More than a dozen people followed, some carrying others, one woman holding only a wooden basin.

“The riders at the eastern end are burning the haystacks and driving everyone uphill,” she told the duty officer. “Two households are still by the river. We're bringing them out.”

A whistle sounded on the slope. A blue-bannered rider was withdrawing along the hill road with another man beside him. Owen tightened his reins.

“They may be running. We could cut them off on the left.”

Viola looked up. The standard-bearer wasn't far away. There were no visible reinforcements behind him. If she pursued, perhaps she could catch the person giving the orders. Perhaps she could find something that would help her own case.

Then someone cried for help beyond the low courtyard wall.

A cart carrying elderly and bedridden passengers had slipped into a ditch. Its driver held a broken shaft as the cart slowly sagged farther into the mud. Another rider had turned toward it, spear lowered.

Viola left her men no time to hesitate.

“Owen, protect that cart. The rest with me. Hold the western side. No pursuit up the hill!”

She wheeled downhill. Through a gap in the wall, Adrian saw a horse flash past, a brief gleam of sword, then heard the dull impact of wood against a shield. The gate guards blocked his view and sharply ordered everyone beneath the eaves.

He moved back. One sudden breath of cold air tightened his chest, setting off a cough. Dr. Shaw glanced up from a patient and pointed at the innermost chair.

“You. Sit. Hand that board to someone else.”

He gave the register to the driver without arguing that he could still help. Elena crouched before him and brought warm water. She waited until his breathing steadied before rising. As her fingers left the cup, they trembled.

This time, he noticed.

“I'm much better,” he said quietly.

“I know. The cup isn't that heavy.”

She knew he'd seen. Her lips pressed together, and she stayed where she was a moment longer. Adrian rested his free hand on his knee, offering her two fingers. She glanced at him and gently held them before letting go.

People ran past the eaves. Someone called for more clean cloth. They didn't shut out the sounds or search among them for a promise that everything would be all right. Elena simply made certain he could hold the cup himself, then returned to her table to keep recording wagon hire.

The fighting soon moved farther away. The two riders on the hill disappeared over the ridge without looking back. When the stranded cart finally came through the gate, mud covered an old woman's quilt. Two soldiers were supporting the broken frame with their shoulders.

Owen dismounted with a long slash through his right sleeve. Seeing Adrian stare, he waved.

“The sleeve got the worst of it.”

Dr. Shaw didn't smile. She beckoned him over. Owen obeyed at once.

Viola came last. Her soldiers had a grown man in custody, his right leg wounded and his short knife confiscated. A loose blue strip hung from his shoulder. Another sergeant carried saddlebags removed from the man's horse.

When they helped him into shelter, he forced out a name.

“Ellis.”

Nobody treated that as a verified identity. Dr. Shaw examined his leg, told the guards to keep him apart from the villagers, and cut away the trouser cloth around the wound.

A villager with a bandaged arm stepped forward.

“He charged us with a spear!”

A guard blocked him. Viola didn't tell him he was mistaken.

“I'll have someone record what you saw. Step back now. Let the doctor stop the bleeding.”

Her voice was hoarse. She remained between the wounded man and the angry villager, who drew several ragged breaths before sitting on the stone steps and covering his face.

Being rescued did not instantly make anyone forgiving.

The blue cloth removed from the prisoner was laid on an empty wooden tray. Owen pointed to the seam beside its white design. That corner had hung backward on horseback. Instead of a standard military sleeve, it had been fastened to a roughly trimmed pole with freight cord.

“A field repair, perhaps,” Viola said. She told the sergeant to sketch it. “Or perhaps they wanted us to believe the flag before we looked at anything else.”

Elena studied it from the other side of the table, then recognized the wrapping cloth beside the saddlebags.

“That double knot is used for bundles of long poles. It's common on the Greymarch trade routes.”

The inside bore part of a worn domestic carrier's mark. Only the words Northroad remained legible. Finding it in an attacker's bags warranted investigation. It didn't supply anyone's nationality.

Viola gave the cloths and saddlebags to the duty officer for separate registration, to enter joint safekeeping under Magistrate Ada Finch on their return. Elena didn't touch the material. She asked to compare civilian purchase records the following day.

A man with singed hair heard that the patrol would not return to the village and stopped Viola. Could she save his house? His woodworking tools were inside, collected over more than ten years. His cow was still tethered out back.

Viola looked at the rise. Wind was pushing smoke toward the river. The patrol holding the western side signaled that the road was clear for the moment.

“I can't send anyone back into the fire now.”

The man stared at her sword as though it should be capable of carrying an entire house home. He opened his mouth to curse her. Instead, he asked, “How am I supposed to earn a living?”

Viola had no easy future to offer. Elena came over, asked him to tell the town officials where the remaining animals were, and explained that shelter and supper were being arranged. He listened with reddened eyes and slowly released the reins.

By the time the final group arrived before noon, the courtyard's water barrel was nearly empty. Adrian took back the register, separated two duplicated surnames, and asked people to identify their family members.

At the gate, Viola looked toward the hillside. The blue flag was gone. The smoke remained.

“We didn't catch the standard-bearer,” Owen said softly.

“I saw.”

Her gaze returned to the old people aboard the cart and the soldiers making space for the wounded.

“Let's get these people back to town.”

Adrian handed her the list. Her fingers were still stiff from gripping her sword. He held down a corner the wind tried to lift, without searching for anything impressive to say.

Near the gate, a child cautiously asked whether they could sleep somewhere without smoke that night.

Viola crouched so he could see her face.

“We're going into town,” she said. “You'll ride with your family.”

She rose and ordered the wagons ready.

The flag had vanished into the hills.

Her sword stayed beside the convoy.
