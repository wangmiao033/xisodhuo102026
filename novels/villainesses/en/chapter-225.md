# Chapter 225: The Chair Without a Uniform

On March 24, Viola moved the chair nearest the head of the table for someone who didn't want to sit there.

Her assistant did the moving. With her left arm supported, she indicated the place with her right hand, then discovered the arrangement was more mistaken than the action.

“I've only come to hear what you want witnessed,” the ordinary works employee said. “I haven't agreed to chair anything.”

Viola immediately stopped the assistant.

“I arranged that too quickly. Please choose.”

He sat by the window. The head of the table remained empty. No presiding officer fell from the ceiling to occupy it.

They met in an academy reception room, not an experimental area. Invitations had gone through an ordinary contact office to adults able to answer for themselves. Nobody had copied names from meal lists, donation records, or old military orders. Their three visitors knew ordinary equipment maintenance, shop accounts, and public records respectively. None had promised to bear magical danger.

Viola wore no full ceremonial uniform. Taking off a coat wouldn't make people forget her position, but she didn't want a briefing to resemble a muster. She explained both her continuing knightly status and the command she didn't possess today.

One visitor asked whether witnesses would eventually appear on another list, since everyone called the women the five marked bearers.

“Listening today doesn't enroll you in an experiment,” Viola said. “Nor should our word be enough. The record will include commitments not made. You'll retain your own copies.”

The bookkeeper asked whether a copy needed a Temple stamp to be used.

“Your observations don't become forbidden because they lack that stamp.”

“Then my first question is what happens if you dislike what I write.”

Adrian answered this one. They could request a correction supported by reasons, not remove an original or turn a disagreement into a misunderstanding on the visitor's behalf. Pages containing private names required separately agreed custody. That didn't give researchers permission to erase unfavorable facts.

The bookkeeper wrote down his answer and showed him.

Being recorded was uncomfortable. Adrian nevertheless nodded, pointing out the missing words supported by reasons instead of polishing the whole statement into a declaration of brave transparency.

Before noon, they practiced with yesterday's paper workflow. There was no energy supply or human trial. Viola asked everyone to record observations independently before comparing them. Adrian moved the request slip; a visitor acted as its receiver. Luna wasn't present. Her assistant demonstrated only the ordinary steps she'd checked, answering no new technical questions.

The first three accounts differed.

The works employee counted receipt when the slip reached the table's corner. The bookkeeper counted the receiver's nod. The third visitor had missed Viola's question about the stopping point because he was asking whether he could use his own pen.

Adrian was disappointed. He'd expected independent witnesses to produce supporting accounts. Instead, they seemed to have divided uncertainty into three.

“Don't harmonize them yet,” Viola said.

They revisited the original slip and their viewing positions. One person couldn't see the corner. Another had heard an acknowledgment before the nod. Different definitions and viewpoints belonged in separate observations; a majority couldn't determine what happened. The person who missed the question added a note beside his original rather than claiming he'd heard it clearly.

For the second round, they defined the precise action meant by receipt and cleared the corner. Differences narrowed. One sequence remained uncertain and stayed that way, not polished smooth by three people's goodwill.

The works employee laughed. “So you didn't ask us to stand around saying we saw it.”

“A chair could do that,” Viola said.

“A chair wouldn't ask about payment.”

She smiled and explained the stated allowance for travel and today's time, subject to the ordinary application. Favorable opinions weren't a condition. Future attendance costs remained unconfirmed; she couldn't promise unlimited coverage. The bookkeeper required that uncertainty recorded too.

As they collected the practice sheets, the visitor experienced in public records didn't join the laughter. If the research failed and people blamed witnesses, who would explain that they hadn't approved the equipment?

Adrian almost answered that they would. Then he realized that making an accused witness find the researchers afterward still placed all trust in the researchers themselves.

Viola requested the ordinary public role-description form and removed the broad phrase assisting in confirming safety. Visitors could record who did what and whether stated procedures were followed. Engineering safety remained the responsibility of qualified reviewers. Any public notice naming witnesses must also state that limit, not append names as endorsements.

The visitor asked whether his address had to be published. It didn't; the office could forward communications. He asked for that answer recorded and declined to decide today about further participation. His workplace was small. He didn't want angry people blocking its door.

Viola let him take his copy without urging him to be brave for ordinary citizens.

At the door, he turned back to point out a loose nail in a table leg. He could decline future witnessing and still report a danger in front of him. An assistant noted the location for maintenance. Nobody treated the warning as proof that his refusal meant something else.

Looking at the nail, Adrian finally left the third prospective place on his list empty.

An earlier application brought permission to examine a historical document directory that afternoon. Viola attended only within her allotted short session, with an assistant handling documents. One visitor chose to observe the ordinary verification. The other two left without being labeled insufficiently committed.

No complete indictment had materialized on the desk. Limited parts of the noble register, fabricated oracle draft, compulsory escort references, and royal co-signature had already been checked separately. Today brought an index linking them and the protocol text authorized for examination. Custodians presented the originals; researchers couldn't carry them off privately.

Under one old registration number, the index listed five kinds of procedure. Its attachment numbers weren't consecutive. Adrian initially thought a missing number meant a torn-out list. The custodian produced the binding directory: that number identified a separately stored payment volume, not a vanished sixth person.

Adrian corrected the question. His palms stayed cold.

The protocol's worst feature wasn't a cruel verb. It was the orderly conversion of one step into another. Registration became an assigned position in the bearing order. Liturgical confirmation supplied the public explanation. Escort completion became reception. Dispute didn't automatically halt progression. Each handler could claim responsibility for only a small piece. Together, the pieces delivered an objecting person to a place without a box for their answer.

“The procedure is unified,” Viola said. “That doesn't mean every person's actual fate is established.”

The independent examiner compared the text's references with earlier archive numbers and corresponding entries in a separate royal counterpart. Wording, linked numbers, and the recorded date layer supported one another. The procedures weren't simply similar stories assembled afterward by investigators. One protocol had explicitly arranged their connections.

Old years stayed in their original notation, not forced into the current calendar. Missing private attachments remained missing. Unseen victims didn't receive invented endings.

But the shared structure behind September's execution template and later assignments now had a documentary link. They no longer needed to say only that the papers looked alike.

The observer asked whether this common protocol meant all current clerks could be listed as participants.

Viola shook her head. Who received which document, knew what, and did what still required investigation. The protocol provided routes for inquiry, not signatures beneath everyone's guilt. She had obeyed genuine orders and rejected unreasonable execution. She understood what all the same would erase.

Looking at reception, Adrian remembered opposing his removal in the former cycle and being transferred anyway. No new memory appeared. He didn't use this protocol to invent words he'd never heard. It explained how a system accommodated that transfer, not every thought behind every signature.

Elena was permitted only the limited extracts relevant to her. After reading, she requested continued examination of the version her house had received. She didn't ask the observer to record her distress as proof of present innocence.

Viola's time ended. She left first, asking the examiner to retain outstanding questions for the formal record sent afterward. A knight not sitting until the finish didn't make the findings lighter.

Back in the reception room late that afternoon, they addressed witness arrangements. Two visitors willing to continue set different limits. One would examine research records but not stand beside equipment. The other would observe preparations after reviewing safety conditions. The third offered no further answer today and could decide later.

Viola gave the group no name resembling a regiment. She sent provisional arrangements to the independent contact office, requiring separate future invitations. Two answers couldn't become an entire community's support.

Before leaving, the bookkeeper asked whether an experiment could proceed if a witness withdrew after it began.

The actual reviewers needed to establish minimum attendance conditions. Viola didn't say her own presence would suffice. If those conditions failed, yesterday's agreement couldn't occupy today's empty chair.

Bringing her coat, Adrian noticed a paper projecting from a pocket: the shoemaker's collection hours.

“Will you make it?”

“My assistant has gone. I never promised to collect them personally.”

“I thought you might put shoe collection on a duty roster.”

She glanced at him. “I might once have. Now I'd prefer to know whether they fit.”

The repaired pair of ordinary ankle boots arrived as they reached the level porch. The shoes that fitted well on March 14 still did; these were a different pair sent for new soles. Viola sat to try them, an assistant steadying the heel. She didn't strain her injured arm to avoid looking cared for. The right fitted. The left still pressed inside.

Adrian offered to return them. First she asked whether the detour would delay his arrangement with Elena.

“Yes, if I go.”

“Then don't. I'll ask the original messenger.”

He looked embarrassed. She smiled. Having spent the day listening to how much people agreed to, she couldn't pretend not to understand his time at the door.

He stayed until her carriage arrived. She said that once the shoes really fitted, she hoped to walk with him without discussing orders. She would ask when the time came.

He agreed to hear her invitation then, not turn an unspecified day into forever.

After the carriage left, an academy clerk brought Isabella the reception hours of local council delegates already in the capital. Witnesses could make events visible. They couldn't provide sufficient materials or assume distant communities accepted a capital-made design.

Folding the paper with her right hand, Isabella said she'd ask tomorrow what they were willing to examine. Today's two limited replies wouldn't become her claim that public opinion had taken her side.

Adrian glanced back into the reception room. The chair at the table's head was still empty. By the window lay opinions in two different hands.

It was a shorter line than he'd imagined that morning, and harder to lead away than a row of people who only nodded.
