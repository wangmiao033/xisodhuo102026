# Chapter 261: The First Layer to Break

An extra chair stood outside the academy isolation room on the morning of April 23.

Not an honored guest's seat. Luna's observation period was short, and leaving shouldn't require crossing the operators' space. The chair faced a window, padding ready for her right palm. Her assistant tested the view first to ensure the sill didn't conceal the necessary gauges.

Adrian arrived to find Luna criticizing its appearance.

“It looks as though it recommends retirement.”

“It's only meant to let you sit.”

“Your expectations of furniture keep falling.”

He handed the day's arrangements to the actual supervising technician rather than station himself at the main switch. Yesterday's permission specified two empty loads, independent newly supplied energy, and a limited total. Today's objects had to match. The replacement technician checked every mark without skipping his inspection because people waited.

Before supplying power, they rehearsed stopping. An observer called out; a separate technician opened the independent cutoff. Residual energy would go to an external absorber that didn't depend on the experimental node. Actions and indicators worked. The recorder entered a successful unpowered rehearsal, not successful stopping under load.

Then came the shield. Yesterday's certification hadn't prevented a fastening from sitting incompletely when installed today. The technician required reinstallation. Adrian looked at the clock and swallowed “only a little.”

Someone outside asked if volunteers could finally attend. No, he answered. Conditional intentions didn't turn people into test materials. The visitor looked disappointed: one adult had already taken half a day off. Adrian asked the explanation staff to contact them immediately, establish what impression had been given, and address lost time under the actual agreement. Nobody should wait outside for enrollment that wasn't happening.

During the repeated check, all five women briefly occupied the outer room. Being together didn't activate five marks. Their available time merely happened to overlap. Isabella opened a blank page for future arrangements. If entry to the core's perimeter was ever authorized, they needed to know who would do what.

“Not today,” Viola said.

“That's the first line.” Isabella turned the paper toward her.

Cecilia could request attendance to hear current ritual explanations, not replace technicians. Luna wanted to help design measurement methods, not be stationed closest merely because she could read patterns.

Elena glanced at Adrian. “I want to accompany him. That isn't a specialist qualification.”

“Does making him eat count?” Luna asked.

“You can do that too.”

“I usually make him pass me my portion first.”

The laughter eased the room. Adrian neither pretended to miss the affection nor claimed he couldn't live without any of them. If his old memories required explanation, he would contribute within the approved scope. If he simply wanted their company because he was frightened, he would call it a private wish, not a job requirement.

Viola considered her actual contribution: outer routes and withdrawal handovers, planned within current injury limits, not a sword-first charge. Isabella's role in applications and communication didn't grant core authority. Nobody won companionship on that page. Several possible positions awaited concrete permission.

Elena set down her pen. Was Adrian still free for dinner? No technical attachment accompanied the question; nobody competed for the same evening.

Yes. If delayed, he'd tell her rather than leave her waiting at a window. Luna sighed theatrically: the real advantage was knowing dinner time. Elena's ears reddened, but she didn't deny wanting it.

The fastening passed reinspection. Testing began.

Initial input was low. The two empty branches stabilized separately. Independent measurements and the node's readings broadly agreed. Successive withdrawals showed a measurable delay, but residual handling completed as planned at that setting.

Nobody cheered. Earlier single-branch results couldn't stand in for today's pair. Today's first setting couldn't certify the next one either.

The second stage remained within the permitted energy total. It shortened the interval between withdrawals rather than escalating to destructive input. Behind the observation line, Adrian watched two needles climb. The buffer changed more steeply. The supervisor required the original duration, not an extended look.

The first branch requested withdrawal. The second reached its scheduled endpoint.

A fine white line appeared inside the node's small window.

Adrian first thought it reflected light. The independent gauge, however, crossed the stopping threshold before the node's own indicator. The observer called stop. The responsible technician disconnected immediately. Nobody waited for Luna's explanation or Adrian's agreement.

The external absorber's indicator rose within its rated range. Input had stopped, but the new node gave a brief crack. The white line widened. One corner of the gray receiving layer collapsed from its support, sending fragments against the shield.

Not a loud sound. Enough to halt every breath.

An assistant took an instinctive half-step forward. The supervisor ordered him back. Independent instruments still showed localized residual energy; the shield must remain closed. Adrian had raised his own hand as though he might catch a falling cup. He lowered it and returned to his position.

Luna sat very pale behind the window.

“Nobody approaches. Let the people stopping it finish stopping it.”

They watched the independent indicator fall gradually. The node's needle had stuck. It could no longer certify calm inside. Elapsed time alone wasn't enough. The examiner waited until external measurement and residual reception met the approved opening conditions before allowing sequential inspection.

No human body had been connected. No fragment struck anyone. Two impact marks remained on the shield. The receiving layer was unusable; its support needed assessment. The external absorber had consumed part of its useful life. Wiping it clean wouldn't make it new.

The damage did not become imaginary because the shield had worked. They would have to replace, assess, or account for these objects rather than declare the room ready for the next hopeful attempt. No deaths did not mean nothing had happened.

Adrian heard himself ask whether the material might simply have contained an old crack.

The technician looked at him without anger, waiting for the rest of the question.

“We need to examine the material,” Adrian corrected. “And why these two branches overlapped here. We can't start by choosing whichever cause is easiest to replace.”

The recorder kept both statements. Luna didn't smooth away the first. Her assistant separated the node readings from the independent measurements. The latter had crossed the line while the former lagged. Whether that came from response delay, damage, or something else required examination, not Luna diagnosing it at a glance.

After the initial inventory, the supervisor ended testing for the day. Unused time on a permit didn't authorize changed configurations, replacement parts, or continuing with an unknown cause. Another test needed fresh review, not merely a declaration that they'd learned something.

The ugly chair prevented one act of stubbornness. Luna began to rise, steadied herself with her left hand, then stopped. Her assistant reminded her that her observation period was ending. Closing her eyes briefly, she asked that her witnessed record be sealed. Nobody could sign for her having seen the rest.

Could Adrian accompany her to the resting room? Yes, provided he didn't spend the walk promising success next time.

They moved slowly. Near the door, she admitted she'd also hoped the material alone had failed. Then they could replace one layer without reconsidering days of arrangements.

“You didn't report that first.”

“I still wanted it.”

She settled with her right palm supported and warned him not to use her admission to cancel his own responsibility. He nodded. He could understand why she had wanted the convenient explanation without making that understanding an excuse for either of them. Shared embarrassment was not mutual absolution.

Outside, Cecilia confirmed that nobody waiting had entered the operating area. The adult who'd taken leave received an accurate explanation. If plans changed later, they wanted direct notice, not merely an updated sign. They weren't angry at being denied danger. They didn't want another half-day's wages lost to a miscommunicated hope.

Adrian apologized in person. Staff recorded the communications and agreed expenses for handling through the existing authorized budget. He promised no money without a source. Before leaving, the person looked at the shield.

“Did it break?”

“Yes.”

“Then fix it first. Don't hurry to use it because we came.”

The ordinary sentence hurt more than the crack had. That person had not demanded a heroic demonstration, or told him to solve the kingdom before returning home. They had only asked him not to turn their arrival into one more reason to hurry.

Cleanup wasn't sweeping the floor after victory. Staff kept the area isolated and canceled another team's afternoon use of the room. That team arrived carrying tools. Hearing the explanation, one worker sat heavily on the corridor bench. They'd hired transport too. A failed experiment didn't erase their losses.

Adrian wanted to find another room. The supervisor reminded him that emptiness didn't guarantee equivalent equipment or permission. He went to the booking office, documented the delay, and established which costs could and couldn't be refunded. He didn't promise a solution in the corridor before finding one.

Returning, he saw Viola holding down the worker's windblown travel receipt with her right hand. She hadn't asked him to feel proud of a greater cause. Had anyone informed his colleague still waiting outside? No. She asked the duty liaison to go, remaining seated herself instead of turning an injured person's desire to help into another forced effort.

Adrian signed the delay acknowledgment. It contained no grand language. It connected the broken layer inside the shield to work someone else genuinely couldn't finish today.

That afternoon, damaged components and records were sealed separately. Initial readings showed withdrawal didn't simply make burden vanish. Residual loads from two branches had reached one receiving layer close together. A local peak exceeded that layer's capacity before the node's general indicator reflected it. Material behavior and response still needed review. Already, the evidence ruled out directly expanding the old summary's promise.

Should the result go to those reviewing necessity? Isabella asked. Adrian knew Oswin would see it. While they sought an alternative, this failure would strengthen his argument that time was running out.

Looking at the broken gray layer, Adrian said yes. The report must identify a limited nonhuman test. It couldn't prove all alternatives permanently impossible. Nor could fear of misuse justify concealing that their present configuration had failed.

Elena waited by the door with the restaurant's acknowledgment for tonight. He saw the paper in her hand before remembering what it represented. Among the broken components and sealed records, an ordinary promise was still waiting to be kept. Adrian remembered his dinner promise. She didn't wield it to make him leave immediately. Did the time need changing?

“Yes. Not canceling and never mentioning it again. I'll send this notice first, then give you an actual time.”

She nodded. He asked whether she could wait a little, rather than making love's supposed understanding into automatic consent.

The fragments inside the shield lay still. Material had broken first. Next came the comforting assumption that if everyone volunteered, the remaining danger would find somewhere to go by itself.
