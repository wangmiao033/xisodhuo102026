# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Two: The Saint Won't Let Me Get Married
### Chapter 51: The Saint Says She's Seen Me in Her Dreams

On the morning of October thirty-first, Adrian discovered he could eat a full breakfast on the very day something terrible was supposed to happen.

A month ago, that would have been unthinkable.

"Why do you keep looking out the window?" Cecilia asked.

"Checking that the bell tower's still there."

"It won't disappear just because of the date."

"I know." He picked up his bread. "I'm not sure my brain does."

Elena opened yesterday's interim safety ruling beside his plate. The ordinary memorial and blessings were authorized to proceed. The transfer ring, with its port for personal marks, was not. Independent certification remained incomplete, and nobody could be forced into the old registration procedure.

"You've read this four times," she said.

"Five might help."

Cecilia didn't laugh at him. She drew a sheet of paper from her notebook.

"Before we leave today, I'd like to tell you about the dream. I wrote down what I remembered last night."

Adrian set down his knife. "How much do you remember?"

"Only pieces. I've dreamed about a bell tower since I was very young. There's mist, a long flight of stone steps, and a man standing in front of me. He has a crescent-shaped scar on his left hand."

Adrian drew his hand back before he could stop himself.

Cecilia noticed. She didn't declare that she had identified him.

"I'd never met you then. At least, there's nothing in the records of my childhood to suggest I had. It was only yesterday that I began to think the man might be you."

She hadn't brought only a fresh account written after their recent experiences. To keep those experiences from reshaping her understanding of the past, she'd also obtained two copies of old diary entries preserved among her Temple coursework.

The first, written when she was twelve, mentioned only *stone steps in the mist* and *a bell ringing at night*. The second, from when she was fifteen, described *someone with a crescent mark on his left hand*.

Neither named Adrian.

"These don't prove my guess is right," she said, laying the faded copies on the table.

Hale checked the course instructor's seals. The registration entries confirmed that Cecilia had submitted the pieces before she and Adrian met. They established that she had written about these dreams years ago. They did not establish that the dreams depicted real events.

Elena turned to the bottom of a page.

"There's another line here. 'If he doesn't want it, nobody can sign for him.'"

"I never understood why the thought of someone picking up a pen frightened me so much," Cecilia said. "Whenever I woke, I felt as though I owed somebody an explanation."

Cecilia smoothed the faded page, written in her teenage hand.

"'He said, don't sign for me.'"

Elena looked up. "Who said it?"

"I don't know. A man in the dream. No name, no clear face. I used to think it was something left over from the stories my teacher told us about respecting a patient's consent."

Adrian rubbed the back of his hand.

In his fragment of memory from the old courthouse, someone had asked a witness whether she consented to the Substitute procedure. There had been a golden-haired woman in the witness box, but he had never heard her answer clearly.

"I have an incomplete memory of someone asking something similar in a courtroom," he said. "I can't make out everyone who was there. Similar words don't mean we witnessed the same event."

"I know. I'm not trying to use a dream to prove the historical betrothal was real."

"Maybe we remember the same place. Maybe we both heard stories like that." He looked at her. "Whatever we find out, I don't want you blaming yourself in advance because of a fragment of memory."

Cecilia's eyes shone a little.

"I'll try to remember that."

Elena sat quietly beside him. She made no announcement that their shared past had finally been revealed. She hoped she could regard what Cecilia had described with the same care: hold to what they could establish, and keep fear from filling the blank spaces.

Cecilia touched the edge of the diary copy.

"What I wanted to tell you was this. If that man refused to let somebody else choose for him, I'm glad he did."

It didn't sound like a declaration of love. Adrian's heart still beat a little faster.

"You told us you didn't want a record deciding how you feel today," Elena said to Cecilia. "The same has to apply to dreams."

"Yes."

Then Cecilia looked faintly embarrassed.

"My teacher also taught me how to express gratitude. Lately, I've been trying to learn a less formal way."

Adrian became wary. "You're not about to recite a witness's oath, are you?"

"No. I consulted a handbook of social expressions."

She produced a little slip of paper.

"It recommends telling someone, 'Through every hardship and every glory, let me walk beside you all my days.'"

Adrian's bread lodged in his throat.

Elena lowered her teacup. "That sounds remarkably like an old betrothal vow."

Cecilia froze, then hurriedly checked the handbook's cover.

"Oh. Yes. This is an older Temple edition."

She pressed her lips together. Her ears were turning red.

"I only meant to thank you for listening to me these past few days."

Adrian finally managed to laugh.

"Say that, then. It's clear. I like it."

Cecilia looked directly at him.

"Thank you for listening to me."

Elena glanced between them and gave a small smile.

"That version I can live with."

The memorial took place that afternoon in the old bell tower's outer courtyard.

There was no linked-mark transfer ring. No samples from living people. There were flowers, familiar names, and candles set out for the dead. Municipal safety officers had inspected the temporary platforms, and the clinic had arranged medical staff to assist anyone with limited mobility.

Cecilia was free to attend or stay away.

She chose to join Mira near the back of the gathering, helping an elderly woman carrying a bouquet find her way through the crowd. When the officiant invited any willing clergy to offer a blessing for the dead, Cecilia first checked that it involved no risk registration.

Then she went to the front.

The bell rang five times.

She didn't collapse. She didn't lose her memory. No unseen force dragged her into another world.

Some people simply closed their eyes and remembered someone they missed.

Beside Elena, Adrian finally released the breath he'd been holding.

"Apparently, a memorial service really can just be a memorial service."

"Of course it can. Standing beneath a bell tower shouldn't put you on a list of people to sacrifice."

A little girl whom Mira had once cared for had also come to the courtyard. She tucked a drawing of a wobbly sun into the book accompanying the floral tributes and asked Cecilia to say a blessing for her grandfather.

"I'd be happy to." Cecilia crouched beside her. "Or, if you'd like to say it yourself, I can stay with you."

The girl chose to read. She stumbled over two short lines, her voice so soft the wind nearly carried it away.

No pillar of light appeared. There was no miracle, and nobody announced that a danger had been transferred elsewhere. The girl wiped her eyes when she finished, then carefully retrieved her drawing and put it in her pocket.

A short distance away, Adrian remembered how he'd once treated the Temple in the game: a place to go when he needed to trigger a quest.

Now he understood that many people didn't come beneath its bell tower to learn whether they would die. They came because someone had already died, and they needed a place where they could say that person's name properly.

"Nobody should be using a service like this to experiment with risk," he murmured.

Elena took his hand.

"That's why we stopped the device from being used without consent. The remembrance deserved to go ahead."

Afterward, the technical inspector confirmed that the disputed ring remained in independent sealed custody. It had never been installed at the site.

"No personal marks were connected today," he said. "Whether the proposed demonstration itself posed a danger remains under technical investigation."

Adrian nodded. Most importantly, nobody had been forced to take on that suspicious risk in Cecilia's place.

Toward evening, the three of them walked beside the river. Cecilia fell behind for a moment, then went to buy three hot drinks. She didn't treat walking behind the others as a sign that they were excluding her.

When she returned, Elena and Adrian were holding hands, watching a painter at work.

"Is this part of a date?" she asked.

"More or less," Elena said frankly.

"Then I'll leave you to it." Cecilia handed them their drinks. "I want to ask whether he could paint a sun poster for the children's ward at Mercy Clinic."

Adrian smiled. "Mira would love that."

"I thought so, too."

She walked toward the painter with a lighter step.

Elena watched her go.

"Do you feel you have to return her feelings because she dreamed about someone who looks like you?"

"No. And she hasn't said she's in love with me."

"What if she does someday?"

Adrian thought about it.

"I'll listen seriously. I won't let an old contract answer for me." He gently tightened his hold on Elena's hand. "As for whose hand I want to hold right now, I already know."

She turned her face away, the tips of her ears pink.

"You're getting rather good at this."

"I've been learning from you, my lady."

They laughed together.

At dinner, Mira arrived with a new drawing and considerable excitement.

"A painter's going to help me redo my little suns. He promises they won't look like fried eggs this time."

Adrian looked up at once. "Who told him the old ones looked like fried eggs?"

"I did." Mira grinned. "The children have always said they like them, though."

Dr. Grace Winter also sent a brief note. Jonah was continuing his treatment as directed by his physicians. The message offered reassurance without disclosing unrelated details about his condition.

After reading it, Cecilia finally settled down enough to eat half a bowl of soup.

"Not every danger is gone," she said. "But people got help today. And people still got to choose what they would do."

Adrian nodded. "I trust that more than a record saying 'fate settled.'"

Later, alone, he went through his notes on the game's side stories again. Nothing told him who the man in Cecilia's dream had been. Wanting an answer badly enough didn't make the scar on his left hand supply another memory, either.

At last he closed the notebook. There was no point trying to force a walkthrough into existence.

That night, Hale showed the three of them the old registration rules. A historical two-party contract required each signatory's independent confirmation within its prescribed term. If that term expired before both confirmations were complete, the receiving custodian had to mark the archival transfer copy *lapsed* and notify both parties.

"If they weren't notified, would that affect the lapse?" Cecilia asked.

"It might constitute a procedural defect. It wouldn't automatically revive the old betrothal. We'd need to examine the applicable legal text."

Adrian nearly said that an expired betrothal ought to be like a canceled reservation: gone, with no further need to worry. Then he remembered the signatures written in advance and all those impossible-looking dates.

He would celebrate after he saw the original entry.

Cecilia was calmer than he expected.

"I'm not desperate to prove I never chose anyone back then," she said. "Whatever that choice might have been, I want the chance to understand my own heart today."

As Hale continued organizing the records, another extract arrived from the Temple's historical archive. It repeated the requirement for independent confirmation and showed the corresponding status field in R-074's transfer record.

The transfer was dated seven years earlier.

A gray-gold stamp obscured the status entry on the copy. They couldn't read what had originally been written beneath it.

"This may be the key to tomorrow," Hale said.

Cecilia studied the line without her old tension.

"Then let's find out what it says."

Adrian looked at her and realized October thirty-first was over.

She was alive.

And she still intended to decide for herself what tomorrow would bring.
