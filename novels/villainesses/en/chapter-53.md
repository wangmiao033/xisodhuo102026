# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Two: The Saint Won't Let Me Get Married
### Chapter 53: The New Inspector Didn't Bring Flowers

On November second, a notice arrived at the Rosenberg estate announcing a visit from the new inspector.

Adrian pictured black robes and an entire squad of Temple guards. The woman who appeared that afternoon carried a battered leather case and had mud from the station on her shoes. She looked to be in her thirties. Her short, dark brown hair was pinned severely back, and a nearly invisible repair ran along the cuff of her coat.

“Sabine Rowe.” She presented her credentials. “The independent oversight division for the outer district has assigned me to review the archival procedures involving R-074 and C-19. I'm not here to defend the Temple or to convict House Rosenberg.”

Adrian glanced at her. “What are you here to do?”

“Establish what can actually be verified about each allegation.”

That didn't sound threatening. Her next remark was less reassuring.

“I'll be reviewing everything you've submitted, including the evidence favorable to Miss Saintclair. Any exaggerations I find will go into the record.”

“Quite reasonable,” Hale said.

Sabine turned to Elena. “You're the principal outside supporter. The disclosure rules require me to establish whether you're funding Miss Saintclair's lawyer, and whether that funding gives you access to any privileged case material.”

Elena handed over the neutral legal-aid agreement without hesitation.

“We cover approved procedural expenses. We don't direct her representation or receive confidential material from her file.”

Sabine read it carefully. “The terms comply with the rules. I'll need the payment records to establish whether the arrangement operates as described.”

Adrian sighed. “Does every answer have to end with something else you need to check?”

“Yes. If someone promises to unravel ten years of secrets in an afternoon, I'd advise you to keep your distance.”

Elena laughed. Cecilia, meanwhile, seemed to find Sabine's rigor encouraging.

“Will you examine why Internal Oversight had the Administrative Duty Office revise the prognosis-completion summary?” she asked.

“Given appropriate authority and identifiable records, yes. But let me be clear: Miss Saintclair retaining her standing doesn't automatically make every investigator involved in the earlier case guilty of misconduct.”

“I wouldn't want it to,” Cecilia said.

They shook hands. Adrian had the sudden impression that either woman could turn a dinner party into a hearing, and together they might manage it before the soup.

Martha came in with the tea. Her gaze moved over the credentials on the table.

“Shall I bring another set of cups?”

“Thank you. Could I see the visitors' register first? I'd like to confirm the time I arrived.”

Adrian put a hand to his forehead.

Here they went again.

Having entered the time, Sabine moved her cup aside.

“There's an old ring here,” she said, pointing to the tabletop. “Your papers might stick.”

Elena looked down sharply. Adrian had left that ring while pouring her tea yesterday. The map for their trip out of the city was still partly caught beneath the papers.

“Those are private.”

She gathered them so quickly that a line on the back of the map flashed into view.

*A walk by the bridge. Just the two of us.*

By the time Adrian saw it, the damage was done. Sabine had seen it too, but returned her attention to her case without a question. Cecilia reached over to hold down a curling corner of the map.

“If you need to move your appointment with the clinic, I can help arrange it.”

“This isn't work,” Elena said, rather stiffly.

“I know. All the more reason not to let work take it away.”

Adrian had been bracing himself for a small dispute. For a moment he couldn't think of anything to say. Neither, apparently, could Elena.

“Thank you. We'll make our own arrangements.”

She put the map in a drawer, leaving the little note intact. Under the table, her shoe nudged Adrian's toe. Assuming she wanted him to stop slouching, he sat up straight.

She looked at him and nudged him again.

This time he understood. He held out the hand hanging beside his chair, and she took it for two seconds before letting go. Sabine was reading the agreement; Cecilia was pouring tea. Those two seconds never made it into the visitors' register.

Before the formal discussion began, Sabine established who could take possession of which documents. The three versions of the subsidy application belonged to the municipal office that held them. Independent custodians controlled the sealed originals of the Temple contracts. The estate had copies from authenticated sources. Mira's personal statement could be used only within the scope of her consent.

“Everyone seems to be following procedure,” Adrian said.

“Good. But following the procedures we have doesn't establish that every procedure in the old system was reasonable. Those are separate questions.”

She produced the outer district's standards for handling historical records. A demonstration involving old risk-sharing equipment had to document the source of its test medium, its purpose, and whether any living person's mark would be connected. Missing authorization couldn't be supplied by calling something a traditional rite.

“Does this concern the demonstration on October thirty-first?” Elena asked.

“In part. I've asked an independent artificer to preserve the transfer ring's equipment number and a diagram of its connections. Its internal workings haven't been examined yet.”

“Could it pass the risk to somebody else?” Adrian asked.

Sabine paused. “That's a reasonable concern. It remains unproven. Calling a device a transfer ring doesn't establish that it can transfer risk between living people.”

He nodded, swallowing the certainty he had been about to put into words. She had caught it before he could.

Later that afternoon, Sabine proposed checking the source of the high-risk observation record used on October thirty-first. She had authorization to inspect anonymized statistics that disclosed nothing about other individuals. Among those records she found something unexpected.

Under the same maintenance reference, some old entries marked *Risk reduced* were followed by another, linked reference marked *Burden pending verification*.

“What does that mean?” Adrian asked.

“The old system may have used linked entries to describe different parts of a single event. A risk-transfer mechanism is another possibility. Without the original media and approval forms, we can't choose between them.”

Cecilia leaned toward the reading board. “At least we need to ask how the risk was reduced.”

“Yes,” Sabine said. “And who took responsibility for what followed.”

Rather than keep the tables to herself, she signed a new application for independent examination in front of them. Hale and Vera each received it to check the proposed scope of access.

“I think there's a problem here,” Adrian said suddenly, pointing to the figures.

Sabine stopped writing and waited.

“You've grouped these entries by maintenance reference, but the units on these two pages are different. This one gives scale readings. That one counts items. Could somebody have copied the number of times a piece of equipment was serviced into the wrong column?”

He had no remarkable expertise. He had once, however, counted a dozen candles as a dozen cases while checking the estate's candlesticks. Martha had wanted to know which ballroom he proposed to keep blazing until dawn.

Sabine turned the sheet around. For the first time, she frowned.

“These can't be compared directly.”

She crossed out the line connecting the entries and noted why. Hale looked up at her, but she was already on the next page.

“You're right. Tomorrow I'll have the artificer establish what those readings actually measure.”

“You're not annoyed?” Adrian asked.

“I have other things to be annoyed about. Discovering I'm wrong, then spending three days trying to prove I'm right, for instance.”

Cecilia laughed and passed over two more pages.

“These use the same units. They're also from the same settlement.”

The three of them rearranged the sheets. Their remaining concern was narrower, but clearer. Sabine kept the excluded entries in the inventory too; she wasn't taking away only what supported her suspicions.

Adrian began to believe that her insistence on checking everything wasn't merely a way of shutting other people out.

As she was leaving, Sabine stopped at the door.

“One more thing, Lady Elena.”

Elena turned. “Yes?”

“The relief donation records your late mother was involved in show that House Rosenberg made a long-term maintenance payment to the Old Contract Records Office. Were you aware of it?”

For the first time that afternoon, Elena's expression visibly changed.

“I don't know exactly what the payment was for.”

“Then please help establish that. The donation may have been entirely legitimate. But if it financed risk demonstrations without consent, the payer will need to account for how the money was actually used.”

Elena's fingers tightened around her fan. She had been actively investigating the financial connections between the estate and the Temple. None of them had expected the new inspector to arrive with a lead involving her mother on the very first day.

“I'll ask my father to have the original accounts checked,” she said. “I won't ask you to stop because my family is involved.”

Sabine nodded. “That's the answer I was hoping for.”

After their visitor left, Elena remained seated for a long time. Adrian sat beside her.

“Are you frightened?”

“Yes. I'm afraid money my mother genuinely intended for relief was used for something else.”

“Then we start with what she intended and where the payments went.”

“I know.” Her voice dropped. “But I don't want to begin by assuming she knew nothing.”

He held out a hand. “Whatever she knew, we'll establish the facts before we decide who was responsible.”

She didn't take it immediately.

“When I was little, she brought me to a relief home. I complained about the smell of medicine. She didn't scold me. She opened a window and said pretending I couldn't smell it wouldn't make it go away.”

Elena looked down at her gloves. A repaired seam ran along one forefinger, the thread a little paler than the leather.

“These weren't hers. I had them mended last month. But whenever I see the stitching, I think of her.” She rubbed the seam. “Now I'm afraid I only remember the parts I want to remember.”

Adrian didn't tell her that her mother must be innocent. He moved his hand a little closer and waited.

At last she took it.

“Will you be there when we go through the accounts tomorrow?”

“Yes. And if you find something you don't like, I won't quietly replace it with a nicer version.”

“You'd better not.”

Her smile was faint, but her grip on the fan finally loosened.

Cecilia approached from the other side of the room, carrying a small sheet of paper.

“May I say something? The possibility that someone did wrong doesn't erase every good thing she ever meant to do.”

Elena looked up and nodded. “Thank you.”

Then Cecilia added, with a sudden change of subject, “I'm trying to learn to express myself more naturally. If neither of you minds, I'd like to tell Adrian that I admired his care with the evidence today.”

“You can just tell me,” he said.

She unfolded her note.

“I would share with you all glory and suffering—”

Adrian nearly jumped out of his chair. Elena turned toward her very slowly.

“Are you using that old Temple guide to social conversation again?”

Cecilia checked the cover.

“Apparently this page is a problem too.”

All three of them laughed.

This time, she didn't simply put the book back in her bag. She borrowed a strip of paper, tied off the offending pages, and began again on a blank sheet.

“What I liked today was that you weren't afraid to admit you weren't certain, even in front of the new inspector,” she read, more quietly. “And that you held out your hand for Elena without hurrying her to take it.”

Adrian couldn't hide behind a joke about betrothals this time.

“Thank you.”

Elena considered the two brief lines. An unreadable little smile touched her face.

“That version will do.”

Cecilia put the paper away, her ears pink. Somehow, leaving out an entire passage about glory and suffering had made the words harder to say.

The lightness didn't last the evening.

Checking the references Sabine had left, Hale found that the old maintenance payment was distinct from the B-17 transfer through the Royal Confidential Special Reserve. Two separate streams of money might have reached the same recipient.

“If both were paying for the upkeep of risk records,” he said, “we can't limit ourselves to the Crown's accounts.”

Adrian looked toward Elena in the lamplight.

“Then include House Rosenberg's,” she said softly.

Hale brought her the request for access to the family ledgers. She read it and signed her own name.

Adrian didn't steady her pen.

He turned up the lamp before it could go out.
