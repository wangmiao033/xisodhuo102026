# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Two: The Saint Won't Let Me Get Married
### Chapter 54: Please Don't Pair Us Off

On November third, Adrian had barely reached the Artificers' Testing Office when he spotted the wooden sign on the door.

*DO NOT TOUCH YOUR PARTNER*

He stopped. So did Elena. Behind them, Cecilia suddenly looked very serious.

“Does that restriction,” the saint asked, “apply only to formally registered relationships?”

The door opened. A man wearing thick protective goggles peered out and pulled away the scarf draped across the bottom of the sign.

*SPECIMEN OR ITS WIRING WITHOUT AUTHORIZATION.*

“Laboratory equipment,” he said. “Not that kind of partner.”

Adrian had a good feeling about today. He freed the left hand he had tucked inside his sleeve and held the door for the two women. Here, at least, a misunderstanding could be resolved by removing a scarf.

The man introduced himself as Ivo Kent, a licensed artificer, and showed them into the testing room. Sabine was already there. On the bench stood the historical linked-mark transfer ring whose demonstration had been halted on October thirty-first.

Two custodians checked its outer seals in front of everyone and recorded where they were opened. Only then did Ivo remove the casing. Permission had finally been granted to inspect the sealed device. Connecting it to any person's individual mark remained prohibited.

“Just one question,” Adrian said. “If somebody decides they need me to put my left hand on that thing, can we go straight home?”

“Yes.” Ivo held up his own hand. Heat-resistant leather covered his gloves, leaving none of the skin on their backs exposed. “My hands aren't laboratory supplies either.”

That did more to reassure Adrian than the three safety notices on the table.

Ivo showed them the hollow ceramic base. The ring had two sets of sockets. One could accept a sealed archival specimen; the other connected the medium that would receive the output. Today's test would use only newly made copper discs with artificial marks. One was labeled A on the back, the other B. Neither had any connection to a living person.

Cecilia helped check the labels and wrote *Pairing complete* on the record sheet.

“You've had plenty of practice writing that,” Elena observed.

“At least both parties agreed this time.”

“Copper can't agree to anything,” Adrian pointed out.

“Which is why we aren't marrying them,” Cecilia replied gravely.

Ivo studied the three of them through his goggles.

“Does examining a kettle usually lead to this sort of conversation?”

Adrian declined to answer. If he did, the kettle might acquire a complicated romantic history too.

For the first test, Ivo left the old ring out entirely. He fitted the discs to an ordinary base and supplied a small, measured magical charge from a standard storage stone. The gauge beside A slowly rose. Its wax indicator softened, while B stayed cold. Once the supply was cut, the remaining heat dispersed through the base. No additional energy appeared from nowhere.

For the second test, he connected the same discs to the transfer ring and used the same measured input.

A's gauge rose, then abruptly dropped.

The wax strip beside B bent.

Adrian stopped smiling. “The other one took it.”

“Don't touch anything yet.” Ivo adjusted the insulating divider. “We still have to rule out heat traveling through the wires themselves.”

He placed the two discs on separate insulated stands, retaining the runic connection. Then he exchanged their positions and repeated the test. The heat still shifted in the direction specified by the paired marks, rather than simply passing to the nearer disc.

During the third application of power, B approached the red line. Ivo cut the input immediately. The thin hum ceased.

He removed his goggles. Sweat shone at his temples, and the hand he reached toward his water cup was trembling slightly.

“That's enough. Holding the isolation field takes work. I need half an hour.”

Nobody asked him to try once more.

Cecilia drew a chair up behind him. Elena opened the window. Adrian stared at the bent wax strip and thought of yesterday's accounts: one reference marked with a reduction, another with a burden awaiting verification.

Sometimes a reduction only meant that something had moved off one sheet of paper.

“It didn't get rid of the load,” he said.

“No. Part of the input passed to the paired medium. The apparatus dissipated the rest.” Ivo took a drink. “The capacity is small, and exceeding it can damage the receiving end. What we've established today is that this particular ring can do that with this particular setup.”

“What if it were connected to an old mark belonging to a person?”

“I won't test it on people. And I can't turn a result from two copper discs into a conclusion about someone's death.”

His tone wasn't harsh. Adrian felt a chill down his back all the same.

This wasn't the answer. But for the first time, something he had spent so long calling fate had revealed a circuit somebody could take apart and inspect.

During the break, Sabine handed them the connection specifications they were authorized to see. The old operating guide did refer to *load distribution between linked marks*. The sections involving living subjects were sealed separately and required a further authorization to examine.

“The device has this capability, and the registers contain irregularities,” she said. “That's enough to investigate what was actually connected to it. It doesn't establish that it harmed any particular person.”

Elena studied the two copper discs.

“What if the receiving end doesn't want to receive it?”

Ivo looked up. “Copper can't refuse. Which means that when somebody applies this system to a person, the first question is who decided to connect them.”

Adrian thought of the signatures that looked far too much alike.

Cecilia had already put her pen away.

When the break ended, Ivo didn't switch the ring on again. He set its original casing on the bench and invited them to look at the gauges from an audience's position.

The glass window on A's side was wide enough to read from the door. B's window was hidden behind the base.

“If they set it up like this for a demonstration, the front row would only see the input falling.” Adrian crouched to look behind it. “Why would the receiving end face the wall?”

“Convenient access to the wiring, perhaps. Or some other reason.” Ivo rotated the base. “The design doesn't establish an intention to deceive. But an audience looking at the front would certainly miss half the result.”

Sabine had the custodians record certified images of the arrangement. She also requested an explanation of the original orientation from the maintenance staff due to arrive tomorrow.

When Adrian stood, Cecilia was still looking at the narrow rear window.

“What are you thinking about?” he asked.

“I've conducted so many blessings. Up on the platform, I always thought I could see more clearly than the people below.”

She measured the little window with her hands, taking care not to touch the equipment.

“Apparently, sometimes turning something around is all it takes to leave the person on the platform blind too.”

He didn't dismiss the thought by telling her she hadn't known. She was trying to look honestly at work she had done. Easy reassurance would have been another way of covering the window.

“If I notice we're missing a side of something, I'll tell you,” he said.

“You might not notice either.”

“Then we'll ask someone else to tell both of us.”

She turned. Slowly, her expression eased.

“That's more reassuring than a promise to protect me forever.”

“Easier to keep, too. I can't guarantee I'll catch someone every time they fall down the stairs.”

Elena opened the door just in time for the last sentence.

“Who's planning to fall down the stairs?”

“No one,” they said together.

She looked from one to the other, then set a lunch basket by the door.

“Good. Nobody has time to wash out bloodstains today. Martha packed the white napkins.”

Adrian laughed and took the basket. Before leaving, he glanced back at the little rear window.

What he would remember wasn't a miracle making danger disappear. It was the copper disc hidden at the back, hot enough to burn.

They ate in the neighboring courtyard. Elena unwrapped the waxed paper and discovered four apple pastries, with a note from Martha tucked beside them.

*Do not use your lunch as a control experiment.*

Passing the bench, Ivo paused to read it.

“This woman ought to run a laboratory.”

He accepted his share and left the three of them on a stone bench in the sun.

Cecilia tried to break her pastry in half and got sugar on her fingertips. Adrian offered his handkerchief. She reached for it, noticed the red rose embroidered in the corner, and drew her hand back.

Elena passed her a napkin from the basket.

“Use this. He lost that handkerchief once. Since he found it again, he's checked that it's still there every day.”

“Was it a gift from you?”

“Yes.”

Adrian was about to explain that it had only slipped into his coat lining, but Elena had already turned to him.

“So you're simply afraid I'll be cross?”

“No.” He returned it to his inner pocket. “I like it.”

She fell quiet, the color at her ears a little softer than the embroidered rose.

Cecilia wiped her hands. She didn't pretend not to have heard.

After a while, she asked, “May I give you things too? Ordinary things. Without instructions for their use or any historical relationship attached.”

Adrian considered it.

“Yes. But accepting a gift doesn't mean I've agreed to something you haven't asked me.”

“Then I'll say what I want to ask.”

She looked up with a smile. Somehow, that was harder to answer than any grand ceremonial blessing.

Elena broke off the last flake of pastry and ate it.

“Clean off the sugar first,” she said. “Otherwise every gift you give will be sticky.”

Cecilia looked down. Even her note paper was stuck to her fingers.

By the time they returned to the estate that afternoon, the family accounts office had delivered the original ledgers and donation letters.

Duke Edmund hadn't taken the seat at the head of the table. He pushed the lamp closer to his daughter and sat beside her, looking like another person waiting for answers.

Elena's mother had established the donation while she was alive, specifying winter heaters, lighting, and damp-proof cabinets for the relief homes' old registration documents. The first year's receipt listed the number of heaters and the addresses where they had been delivered.

As Elena reached the end, her breathing steadied. She recognized the short line her mother had drawn beneath *children's dormitory*.

But later renewal schedules carried only a broad description: *Long-term maintenance of historical contract facilities*.

“Was Mother still alive when they changed the description?” she asked.

The accountant found two documents from different years. The first payment under the new description had been made after her mother's death.

Elena's shoulders relaxed. She didn't close the ledger.

“Who authorized the continued payments?”

Her father looked at the final page. “I signed the standing family budget authorization. The accounts office cleared the individual purchases.”

He didn't dismiss the servants. He didn't suggest that a lifetime of charitable work made further questions an insult to her mother.

“Keep looking,” he said. “If we paid for the wrong things, I need to know what I signed.”

Adrian had spent the day preparing to comfort Elena. Only now did he realize she didn't need someone cushioning every answer before it reached her.

She needed enough light to read the papers on the table.

A long-established shop sent over its retained account copies later. One payment the receiving office had classified as cabinet maintenance corresponded to an order for a transfer ring support frame. The signatories acknowledging receipt still had to be checked. Even so, these were plainly not two different descriptions of the same piece of furniture.

After reading the documents, Cecilia moved tomorrow's volunteer work half an hour earlier.

“Sabine has asked me to testify about how these registrations were used,” she said. “I'm willing to go.”

“You don't know everything they'll ask,” Adrian said.

“That's why I'll review the scope with Vera tonight.”

She gestured toward the hand resting beside his papers. The scar had remained quiet, offering no explanations.

“I'll speak about what I actually did and saw. I won't answer for choices you may have made in the past.”

Another oversight official appeared at the bottom of tomorrow's interview list.

Dorian Mercer, the local Temple Internal Oversight investigator responsible for verifying ritual and relief programs.

Cecilia knew that name.

The supplemental notice waiving further medical verification had come from his office.
