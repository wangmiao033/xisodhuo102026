# Chapter 126: The Knight Reads the Whole Order First

When Viola Blackwood arrived at the palace, the gatekeeper looked first at her left arm.

She wore no armor under her cloak. There were no crates waiting for her to carry. She offered her consultation pass and proof of her restored military standing, not an order to take command of the gate.

“I'm assisting with the examination of historical military papers.”

The guard admitted her, then asked whether she needed directions.

“Yes.”

Adrian glanced at her.

Viola lifted her chin. “I see no reason to waste my strength finding the wrong corridor.”

“Good. The palace won't need a plan to search for a missing knight today.”

She tried not to smile and failed.

It was the afternoon of January second. Cecilia had returned to her office. Isabella was meeting a royal affairs official; Elena was handling her own papers in the city. Hartwell had reserved a seat beside the window, with armrests and a small cushion on the left.

Viola stopped at the sight.

“I didn't request quite this much attention.”

“I did,” Adrian said. “You can move it.”

She sat and settled her left arm safely onto the cushion.

“It's in a useful position. No need to waste it.”

The NR-42 copy was placed before them. Viola read the text, checked the issue date, then examined the small corner notation. Recognizing military stationery did not prompt her to explain a royal conspiracy to the room.

“This isn't an operational order to a garrison.”

Adrian studied the field she indicated. It named a forwarding destination, not a unit receiving orders, an execution deadline, or a commanding officer's acknowledgment.

“A distribution list isn't a chain of command. A liaison office can forward depot materials without having the authority to send a patrol anywhere.”

Hartwell asked her to identify the basis for each conclusion. She opened her extracts from the document regulations to the appropriate format. Her right hand held the pencil; her left arm remained supported. She spoke more slowly than she once had when confronted with false accusations at the border.

The date was clear: November sixth, four years earlier.

Adrian remembered a different November and the genuine recall delivered to her during the evacuation.

“Not the order you received on November fourteenth.”

“Of course not.” She looked up. “That recall was genuine. I signed the acknowledgment, explained the delay, and sent four riders back first. Finding another military paper today doesn't let us rewrite the past as though I never received it.”

She was not angry. She was placing a hard-won conclusion back where it belonged.

Adrian noted Four years earlier beside the date, so a later reader could not make the wrong connection.

They continued. The text directed that a decommissioned registration wrapper and maintenance papers go through the Temple's document office before transfer to royal storage under the receiving arrangements. Some office names had changed, but the route was consistent with the material they had seen.

One sentence was written very small: Not an operational instruction. Must not be connected for use.

“This concerns transporting papers and a wrapper,” Viola said. “It doesn't order soldiers to conduct a ritual or authorize the object to operate again.”

“So this paper cannot supply the summary's clearance for renewed use.”

“No.”

She was definite about that. Then she pointed to the signature field. This was the copy later appended to W-24. Its authorship and claimed issuing office still required separate verification.

Following the morning's findings, Hartwell had already requested a check of that year's forwarding record from the capital's Military Registry. His application was limited to NR-42's source, date, and dispatch scope, not garrison deployments. The reply offered a consultation of the retained counterpart in the neighboring registry room.

The original dossier stayed behind. Hartwell locked the cabinet and carried only approved copies and a reference list.

The registry room was warmer than the archive and contained two equally unhurried clerks. Viola gave her identity, explaining that she was not reopening her border case or invoking her rank to demand unrelated records.

The annual counterpart book contained entries submitted to the capital by the Northern Route Depot Liaison Office. It had been kept separately from W-24, with the original received page count and cross-page markings at the edge.

At first the clerk offered a prepared extract listing only the issuing office and the object.

“The short version for ordinary inquiries. With your military background, you should understand it.”

“I do. That's why I need the complete restriction.”

He looked uncomfortable. The omission was not intended to conceal anything, he explained. Most inquiries asked only where an item had gone. If every visitor read the entire volume, none of today's registrations would be completed.

Viola did not use her rank to force the issue. She pointed to the authorized scope: source, date, and nature of the dispatch. The prohibition on connection defined that scope; an extract could not replace it. Unrelated neighboring material could remain covered.

The clerk consulted his supervisor, then used a masking board to expose the relevant original entry.

While they waited, Adrian asked quietly, “Does this feel like they're making you prove you deserve to be heard again?”

She shook her head, then admitted that, at first, it had.

“But he hasn't accused me of anything. He has work to do as well. Being treated wrongly once doesn't mean everyone I meet afterward owes me repayment.”

Her right hand smoothed a corner of paper. She did not press with her injured arm. Adrian could see the tension still there, but she no longer had to make it into a battle waiting to begin.

NR-41 concerned an oilcloth quotation. NR-43 was a return notice for a damaged axle.

Between them, NR-42 recorded a material-forwarding notice issued on November sixth, four years earlier.

Adrian looked at Viola after reading the neighboring headings.

“Military affairs contain some very ugly axles,” she said.

“I imagined a solemn trumpet accompanying every page.”

“The trumpeter would request a transfer first.”

The clerk glanced up, smiled despite himself, and returned his finger to the correct entry.

The match involved more than the number. Issuing office, receiving window, description of the contents, and prohibition on connection corresponded. The retained entry also preserved an upstream reference from the old depot maintenance office.

The earlier original was neither within today's authorization nor in this room.

Viola did not demand that a nonexistent paper appear.

“We can trace this formal forwarding to the Northern Route Depot Liaison Office. We don't yet know whether there were earlier instructions, or who originally decided to decommission the item.”

The clerk entered that limit in the reply and supplied the appropriate desk for requesting the upstream reference. Nobody declared that the same people had forged every border order. Nobody used this document to challenge Viola's restored good standing.

As they left, a younger clerk rose and saluted her.

She returned the salute naturally with her right hand. He asked about winter road repairs near Greymarch. His sister ran a carriage business nearby and had written that the bridge still needed gritting.

Viola told him what she had seen before leaving, then directed him to the local roads office for later progress. She did not turn reports received afterward into things she had witnessed herself.

He thanked her and returned to the volume of oilcloth and axles.

Under the covered walkway, Viola paused.

“What is it?” Adrian asked.

“He asked about the road.”

“Yes.”

“Not the charges. Not whether I was sufficiently loyal.”

She looked at the melting snow with a slightly unfamiliar expression, as though discovering that after regaining her name, she might slowly regain moments in which she did not need a defense prepared.

Adrian did not tell her everything was over. Her arm still hurt. People at the border were still repairing their homes.

“Next time you can just answer about the road.”

She nodded, walked a few steps, then stopped again.

“There is a route I'd like to ask you about now.”

Adrian waited, prepared for a detailed safety recommendation.

She took a small sheet from her pocket. It showed the palace side entrance, two sets of steps, and a nearby chestnut shop.

“It's short. There are seats inside. No guard duty is involved, and you don't have to help with my rehabilitation exercises.”

“Viola.”

“If you have other plans, we can choose another day.”

“Are you asking whether I'd like chestnuts with you?”

Her ears slowly reddened. “Yes.”

“I have time.”

She folded the paper without turning the invitation into three pages of emergency procedures.

The shop's door curtain hung low enough that Adrian nearly struck his forehead. Seeing Viola's cloak and injured arm, the proprietor offered a chair near the stove.

This time she thanked him without explaining that she was not fragile.

They shared a small paper bag. Viola found peeling one-handed awkward. Adrian peeled a chestnut and placed it on the dish between them rather than pushing it into her hand. She took it herself, tasted it, and pronounced it sweeter than those sold outside the city gate.

“You've already compared them?”

“I tried a sample at the city-gate shop on the way in.”

“Reconnaissance?”

“I wanted chestnuts.”

She laughed at her own answer, relieved to say it without giving it the name of a duty.

Adrian tried another and broke the shell into fragments all over the table. Viola held the paper bag open with her good hand.

“You're better with archival originals than chestnuts.”

“The chestnuts don't have reference numbers.”

“Call that one Number One.”

“We've had too many of those today.”

Her smile widened. Firelight reflected in her eyes. The ease brought by the young clerk's ordinary question had survived leaving the palace.

While the chestnuts cooled, she asked why city celebrations required so many differently colored ribbons.

“So people who don't know what to buy can at least buy a ribbon,” Adrian said.

She looked at the blue and green strips beside the door. “I used to choose whichever showed the least mud.”

“You can choose something else now.”

“I might still choose the one that hides mud.”

“That's fine, too.”

She did not get up and buy a bright red ribbon she disliked, as though a change of clothes were needed to prove she was no longer the same knight. She simply looked at the colors and finished her chestnut.

When they returned to the outer reading room, Isabella had received the verification. She asked whether it established that the Crown had merely stored the material, without knowledge of its earlier uses.

Viola shook her head.

“It establishes the source of this transfer. It doesn't establish that no other orders existed elsewhere. Limited evidence cannot clear an entire institution.”

The princess watched her, then crossed out a line she had just added.

“All of you are very good at leaving me one sentence less.”

“Sometimes that lets the remaining sentences stand,” Adrian said.

Hartwell brought out the fourteenth leaf. The repair note recorded a replacement outer protective strip and retention of the existing lining. It contained no authorization to resume use. K-9's object entry, meanwhile, expressly prohibited removing the inner lining.

Words alone could not show what had happened beneath the gray-white repair strip.

Conservator Beatrice Wren had reviewed the application. Tomorrow she could conduct an ordinary optical examination without dismantling, wiping, or applying reagents. Luna could observe within her medical restrictions and explain the historical structure. The conservator would handle the object and equipment.

Viola read the arrangement and, this time, did not volunteer to guard the door.

“Then I'll take my scheduled rest tomorrow.”

Adrian looked at her. She put the remaining chestnuts in her pocket and added, perfectly composed,

“I've completed today's route.”
