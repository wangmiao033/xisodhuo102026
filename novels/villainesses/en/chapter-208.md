# Chapter 208: This Time, He Kept His Name

On the morning of March 7, Adrian packed an extra shirt beneath his coat.

Passing his door, Martha asked whether he was going away. He didn't know yet. She neither unpacked his bag nor hurried to fetch Elena. She merely pointed out that the shirt was missing a cuff button.

He turned it over. So it was. Apparently preparing to shoulder everything alone didn't automatically make a man presentable.

Yesterday's joint application had received a reply. The Temple repository would permit examination of its ground-floor catalog; underground access awaited engineering review. An enclosure offered another route. A party to the historical case could request individual reassessment, attend a private interview under the old procedure, and afterward receive a decision on access.

The messenger had called it quicker.

By the third line, Adrian was thinking of injuries among his companions, withdrawn political support, ordinary work still interrupted. If he went first, perhaps five women could stop explaining why they stood beside his name.

He packed the shirt. Then he reached the last page.

The applicant assumes all consequences arising from reassessment.

Whose consequences? When did reassessment end? Neither appeared.

Before noon they met the deputy custodian who had proposed this route. Old labels covered the cabinets behind him; his cuffs had worn white against desk edges. He brought no soldiers and claimed no warrant. He simply offered a form used for years.

“The person named in the file can proceed first. Joint applications involve several offices. Sign, and we can arrange a private interview today.”

“May anyone else see the interview record?” Adrian asked.

“It cannot be removed or discussed without our permission.”

“When can I leave?”

“Once necessary inquiries are complete. Usually not long.”

“I mean when I decide not to continue.”

The reply took longer this time.

Beside him, Elena kept her hands on her own folder instead of snatching the form. She requested the governing rule. Everyone should see that this was an optional historical procedure, not an existing detention order.

The deputy retrieved the register. The procedure existed. Its instructions also required the receiving officer to specify the inquiry's scope and departure arrangements. Both fields remained blank on today's form. Unlimited responsibility was already printed.

“We can fill those in,” he said. “Every blank needn't be treated as malice.”

“Then fill them first,” Elena replied.

Her composure nevertheless recalled the signature on the old handover slip. Adrian moved his hand away from the desk without pretending otherwise. She noticed and shifted back, leaving him to face the form himself.

Cecilia didn't reject private interviews altogether.

“Some experiences shouldn't have six listeners. If he chooses to answer defined questions privately, that can be arranged. Privacy can't become a reason he isn't allowed to leave.”

An assistant presented Luna's assessment. She remained in the rest room, neither turning pages with her injured right palm nor demanding that her left hand do two hands' work. Necessary inquiries failed to exclude bodily experiments. One interview signature couldn't authorize every later exposure.

Isabella held a paper corner with her right hand.

“My title cannot change this form or accept it for him. Separate each applicant's responsibilities.”

The deputy grew impatient. “If everyone acknowledges only their little portion, who's responsible when something goes wrong?”

Adrian stared at all consequences and understood why he'd brought a shirt.

He hadn't truly expected one interview to settle everything. He'd wanted to be kept somewhere so nobody else had to suffer for him. Then he wouldn't need to work out what to say to Elena, how to face Miriam, or how to eat and sleep while carrying an actual wrong.

A limitless declaration of responsibility had almost become his escape route.

“I answer for my statements and choices,” he said. “I can't guarantee unknown structures for the engineers or accept that every earlier accident was my doing.”

“You can append qualifications.”

“I'm not signing this form today.”

The deputy set down his pen. “Then you wait for joint review.”

“All right.”

Harder to say than he'd expected. No chains fell; nobody proclaimed him free. The quicker route closed. The underground chambers remained beneath stone. They might lose a useful opportunity through delay.

He pushed the form back anyway.

As the deputy collected the form, he also gathered the joint-application receipt clipped behind it. Adrian stopped him and pointed out the different application numbers. The man checked, separated the receipt, and returned it. On the individual form he wrote UNSIGNED.

A fresh dot of ink landed beside the empty signature field. Adrian waited for his own receipt to be checked before putting it away. Saying something decisive hadn't relieved him of following the papers through the rest of an ordinary exchange.

“I'll answer necessary questions and investigate my mistakes. But I won't go in alone, leave my departure to someone else, and call that protecting them.”

Viola looked up from the evacuation plan. Her left arm rested securely in its support.

“You needn't withdraw on my behalf. Today I can compare routes sitting down. That's still my work.”

Nobody followed with a vow to accompany him forever. Cecilia had duties at the meal service. Luna couldn't cancel her afternoon rest. Isabella had an unsupported proposal to revise. Working together didn't require surrendering every other part of their lives.

The deputy didn't end the discussion. He turned to the final field on the office's retained copy of the joint application. If inspection required closing occupied rooms, who would explain that to the copyists upstairs? Who would cover the disruption?

His unpleasant tone didn't make the question useless. Adrian nearly volunteered to pay before stopping himself.

“First establish the hazard and who can order the closure. My money or name can't purchase dangers other people haven't been told about.”

At Viola's request, the deputy produced the current room-use plan. Two western binding rooms still needed their paper-delivery passage. Her proposal preserved that route and began inspection in the disused eastern maintenance room, subject to engineering judgment.

Isabella offered no private guarantee either. Reviewers must identify the budget for temporary moves. If they couldn't, tomorrow's inspection needed a narrower scope. She insisted even though the condition might slow them further.

Finally the deputy wrote down the questions requiring answers. His small handwriting gave each problem an identifiable owner, unlike the form large enough to swallow every consequence.

Cecilia quietly asked Adrian whether he would still accept a separately arranged private interview he could leave.

“Yes. I'm not refusing to be asked.”

She recorded that without enlarging his refusal or reopening the rejected form.

In the gallery afterward, he discovered he was hungry.

Elena offered two pieces of bread. He chose the smaller one. She tilted the bag so he could see a dark piece of fruit inside it.

“That one has raisins.”

He exchanged it for the larger piece himself. After a bite, a delayed smile appeared. “I thought today I had to establish every ingredient for myself.”

“Lunch would take forever.”

They ate beside the window. A crumb caught his collar. She raised a hand, then asked whether she could remove it. He agreed. Her fingers took only the crumb, finding no excuse to linger.

“I packed a shirt,” he said.

Her hand paused against the bag.

“I thought I might stay there.”

“You'd considered signing this morning?”

“Yes. My old case. Perhaps if I stayed, you could all stop paying for it.”

She folded the bread bag without asking how he could do this to her.

“You wanted us hurt less. And you wanted not to hear each of us decide to stay. Have I understood the second part?”

He watched the yard before admitting she had.

“I've used arranging things for you as a reason not to hear your objections too.” She kept the old file separate from present responsibility. “Today I still wanted to seize that paper. I didn't.”

“What if I'd signed?”

“I'd tell you I disagreed and demand that your right to leave be stated. I wouldn't write your refusal for you.”

He disliked the hypothetical. Her good answer didn't erase the old waiting-room record. He merely took out his shirt, folded it properly, and returned it to the bottom of his bag.

“I want supper at home.”

“I'll be there. With me, or would you prefer—”

“With you. Don't make it another examination. We already did bread.”

She laughed at last, though her eyes were damp.

The afternoon's progress came through a different process.

Joint reviewers compared the ground-floor directory, the leads in Evelyn's surrendered log, and the independent works copy. The directory entry for the central comparison baseline table pointed to the Temple's old engineering records. Their storage directions identified an underground maintenance gallery. Engineers would determine whether it remained accessible. No applicant's promise could establish that.

Since yesterday's application, the works team had examined the surface vents and two existing stairways. Its afternoon supplement permitted an initial unpowered inspection of the outer level. Inner doors and unexamined structures remained closed. The Temple's chief custodian and independent reviewers signed a limited viewing order specifying participants, daylight hours, and custodial attendance.

The Temple wasn't generally unsealed. Isabella's lost authority wasn't restored.

Adrian read his own sheet before acknowledging receipt. Departure was explicit. Anyone could end their participation; an engineering or medical stop required withdrawal from the affected area. Unknown risks remained, but uncertainty couldn't stuff every decision into his body.

In the rest room, Luna asked whether there would be somewhere to sit. Only after receiving an answer did she elect to join the first short inspection period. Her assistant would continue if it ran longer.

“I'd hoped to design a heroic pose for you,” she told Adrian. “Now you'll have to sit on a stool and read.”

“At last, recognition of my talents.”

“Don't celebrate. You made the last chair creak.”

Isabella coughed delicately. The works team had checked all the chairs, she said. When Luna looked her way, the princess added that this was safety, not favoritism.

Viola didn't laugh aloud, but her shoulder moved. When that pulled at her support, she stopped and adjusted her posture. Laughter hadn't healed the injury. Adrian placed water within comfortable reach without taking over her diagram.

His shirt was still in his bag when he returned home.

Martha asked whether it needed its button after all. Yes, he said. He wouldn't be packing for an overnight stay tomorrow, but eventually he'd want to wear it. She accepted it without treating his return as an achievement requiring a household celebration.

At supper Elena served herself soup, then left the ladle within his reach. He filled his bowl too far and had to lift it carefully. She held back briefly before observing that a man who'd made so many decisions had finally been defeated by a bowl.

He laughed too. Tomorrow they would enter a genuinely sealed underground chamber. Refusing one form hadn't made its answers gentler.

Tonight, though, nobody needed his disappearance as proof that he cared.

He sat in the chair he'd chosen, waited for the soup to cool, and took his first spoonful.
