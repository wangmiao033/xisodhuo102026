# Chapter 247: The Uncolored Map

On the afternoon of April 12, Isabella placed a third wooden pin beside the map without pressing it in.

Adrian, arriving with the installation record, thought she needed a hammer. She pushed the pin away with her right hand. What she needed was a reply.

Inquiries dispatched days earlier along established postal routes were returning with different answers. Most of the map remained blank. Someone sorting papers suggested marking every district that had received the declaration as responding, then adding details later. At least the report would look better.

“Receiving my words isn't agreeing to work for me.”

Her left wrist remained supported. No seal of special coordinating authority lay on the desk. She could make requests through permitted channels and specify what she had authority to offer. Spreading a kingdom across a table didn't place it under the table's occupant.

A nearby district office would exchange public outer-station load bulletins within its existing duties and name a daytime contact. It explicitly declined unapproved new-node operation. Staff were already insufficient; it wouldn't accept additional round-the-clock attendance.

“They're still afraid of responsibility,” the assistant said.

“Or they're short of people,” Adrian suggested.

Isabella copied the offer onto the liaison sheet without removing the qualifications. No apparatus had been promised. But they needn't wait for a message to be retold three times in the capital before learning its original wording. That wasn't nationwide cooperation. It was one line with identifiable ends.

Another reply concerned wagons.

The abstract said two could be provided. The attachment offered to ask two owners. Whether they agreed, what their wagons carried, and when existing work ended remained unanswered. Remembering Elena's small, real arrangements, Adrian resisted entering two nonexistent wagons on the relief schedule.

Isabella summoned the abstract's writer without first accusing him of deliberate exaggeration. He'd assumed asking would be easy and wanted a shorter table. He corrected the original abstract, then personally retrieved the copy already at reception. Adrian waited for his return. I'll fix it wasn't proof that both had been fixed.

Outside, a driver called for dispatch. The returning afternoon mail wagon had other commitments. Isabella had wanted to send every question together. Now she selected the essential correction: inquiries weren't available vehicles; any actual offer needed confirmation from someone entitled to make it.

Other statistical questions would take the next departure. Tomorrow's material might remain incomplete as a result. She accepted that rather than invoking her title to demand a dangerous night journey.

Adrian tied the dispatch bag too tightly. Isabella looked from his hands to the letter requesting clear handovers.

“If they need an ax to open that, include borrowing instructions.”

He retied it. “An ordinary knot. No betrothal attached.”

“Good. The kingdom has enough complicated bindings.”

She laughed briefly, then let her assistant lift the bag instead of testing its weight on her left wrist. The joke neither made the letter romantic nor restored lost authority.

The thinnest answer was one page. A local liaison would receive authorized technical questions but wouldn't permit his name on a public supporters' list. That didn't establish a secret allegiance. Families there disagreed; he could answer for his liaison duties, not for all of them.

Isabella replaced supporters with confirmed liaison scope. The public map would show only publishable districts and routes. Personal contact information belonged on the controlled working sheet. She wouldn't punish the contact by declaring unnamed support worthless, or interpret one letter as informed agreement from everyone living there.

“What if tomorrow someone says you've obtained only a few ways of sending letters?” Adrian asked.

“I'll say he hasn't counted accurately. One receives only by day. Another doesn't dispatch until the next departure.”

He laughed, then looked at the blanks.

“I thought showing everyone the same evidence would make it easier.”

“The same evidence doesn't give everyone the same warehouses or children.”

She folded aside a sheet prepared for a fair copy. It had a generous space for a heading; the actual text didn't occupy half a page. Seeing her stare at the emptiness, Adrian asked whether she wanted to put it away until tonight.

“No. I'd just like, once, to produce something as large as people expect.”

She wasn't asking him to announce a victory. He returned the first reply to her hand and read aloud the sentence promising to exchange bulletins, keeping the qualification daytime.

Isabella released a slow breath. With an ordinary ruler, she shortened the line beneath the heading. She neither tore up the sheet nor ordered a smaller one to disguise her earlier expectation. The brief offer stayed where it was, surrounded by blank paper. They read on without inventing a grander name for that blankness. Adrian resisted telling her it was better this way. Better than what she had hoped for wasn't his decision to make. She had obtained a real answer; she was still entitled to find it smaller than she wanted.

She didn't reproach unanswered districts. Requests might still be traveling, sitting beneath other work, or approaching refusal. Her assistant would distinguish actual dispatch and receipt times rather than give silence a convenient expression.

Toward evening, the first confirmed channel delivered an ordinary daily bulletin. A nearby district's regular messenger, already coming to the capital on scheduled business, carried an authorized copy under the arrangement in the reply. He hadn't crossed the kingdom specially for them. It described observed outer load and current local passage arrangements, using measurements unlike the academy model's.

Adrian didn't attach those figures to Luna's trial curve. He asked duty staff to mark source, scope, and clock differences still needing checks. Road information went to those already responsible for receiving it. Isabella's liaison could bring news; it couldn't authorize her to declare a road safe.

“Am I supposed to come every two hours now?” the messenger asked. He'd heard coordination described as being available whenever called.

“No.” Isabella showed him the agreed daytime dispatch period. “Report exceptional conditions through your existing procedures. Don't wait for me, and don't come here first for my permission.”

He pointed to somebody's inscription on the envelope: Princess's Dispatch Order. Did it require him to visit the capital before returning to his own duty office?

Isabella's expression tightened. The person who'd labeled it explained that this wording might speed passage. He'd thought he was helping.

“By requiring whom to move aside?”

He couldn't name an applicable authority. People had used it before. Isabella wouldn't let the past sign for today. She replaced the envelope with an ordinary liaison cover and had the correction retained. Nobody would claim priority at a station under the discarded wording. The messenger would return to his scheduled office. If ordinary post took longer, the next material would arrive later. That cost couldn't be concealed in somebody else's queue.

Adrian passed her the blank cover. She wrote slowly with her right hand rather than asking an assistant to reproduce the old title more beautifully.

The messenger relaxed and took the corrected liaison sheet. A map hadn't swallowed his original job. Watching him leave, Adrian understood that obtaining agreement sometimes meant convincing people it wouldn't quietly grow after they'd given it.

The academy reviewers' acknowledgment arrived before dinner. Independent explanation and a site check could be scheduled the next morning, provided the installation changes, stopping and withdrawal procedures, separated duties, and individual consideration time could actually be accounted for. This wasn't an approval decision. It couldn't grant anyone the status of risk bearer in advance.

Isabella kept it separate from the regional correspondence. District letters couldn't approve the academy's work; academy intake couldn't consent for a district. Both stacks were real. Neither was a universal seal for the other's blanks.

Reading the reception requirements, Adrian remembered the two jobs separated that morning and fetched the final revision. The responsible person had checked it. One staffing position remained explicitly vacant. If it couldn't be filled before formal operation, that part couldn't operate. Possible signatures tomorrow weren't names for a shift roster.

He sealed the packet and delivered it to intake, where staff checked pages and outstanding content. Individual explanation and independent review still had to happen tomorrow. No liaison established today had become a voluntary risk contract.

At dinner time, Cecilia was disputing a chair beneath the accommodation hall's window, not a miracle.

A woman insisted she take it while the woman stood waiting for food. Cecilia asked whether her legs hurt. They did. She called the seating attendant and stayed at her own handover position. She neither seized the chair to demonstrate equality nor recorded the woman's courtesy as willingness to suffer.

Adrian had come to return a borrowed copy of the route instructions. He asked whether an ordinary dinner portion remained for purchase. Staff checked and reserved one. He waited for handover before bringing it over. Delivering a bowl didn't entitle him to interrupt Cecilia's work.

Her first act on sitting was to move her spoon to the left of her bowl.

“An anti-theft measure?”

“A reminder that tonight you needn't establish whether mine is too hot.”

He raised both hands and placed his bowl opposite. After tasting hers, she frowned. She'd expected milder soup. Could he get water? Yes. He checked available drinking water with the food staff rather than appropriating somebody else's kettle.

They spoke briefly about the woman and the chair. What Cecilia most wanted today was to sit for her whole break, not hear another person praise her inexhaustibility.

“I can refrain from praising you.”

“And from asking a third work question as soon as I put my spoon down.”

He swallowed the question already forming. She smiled and pointed at his bowl: perhaps he could try developing a stable relationship with his dinner. They ate, discussing why bread crusts always seemed to cool before their centers. Ordinary company didn't rescind a commitment to another woman.

Before the break ended, he asked whether she was willing to hear one arrangement for tomorrow. She was. Intake had received the revised packet; independent checks and personal explanations remained outstanding. She would help forward questions during her confirmed service hours. She wouldn't answer whether any visitor wished to agree.

While collecting dishes, a young worker asked whether today's visitors who'd left should remain on tomorrow's waiting list. Cecilia told him to establish whether each had requested it. People who hadn't agreed to return needn't acquire an expectant seat merely to make the room look supported.

Collecting his coat, Adrian passed Isabella's working desk. The map was rolled up. Three unused pins lay in their box. She'd confirmed only completed handovers. Districts that hadn't answered remained blank.

“It looks smaller than when you spread it out.”

“But tomorrow, when someone asks who agreed to that line, I can answer.”

He closed the pin box without coloring the gaps. Today, people in several places had begun exchanging messages they could take responsibility for.

The nation hadn't agreed. The apparatus wasn't running. Whether anyone could sign after adequate explanation tomorrow remained an answer the people at this table couldn't write for them.
