# Chapter 154: The Knight Is Not on Guard Today

Viola had drawn a chair on the map.

After studying it, Adrian asked whether it was a checkpoint symbol known only to knights.

“It's a chair.”

Afternoon light on January twenty-fifth crossed the support around her left arm. The chairback she had drawn right-handed leaned sideways. She considered correcting it, then didn't.

“My physician permits me to walk this far, rest, and return. Not stand at a door all day.”

“I hadn't asked you to stand guard.”

“I'm reminding myself first.”

He refrained from praising her and moved the ink away from her reaching hand.

Now that the hearing on the twenty-seventh was arranged, attendance posed a problem. Six named participants, companions, interested citizens. A narrow waiting passage and steps at the side entrance. Follow directions on arrival meant someone would have to argue in the snow.

Viola knew a duty sergeant. Instead of summoning him, she sent the building's access supervisor the notice and asked whether he wanted an ordinary route recommendation. He agreed to show them the external passages that afternoon, not unopened archive rooms.

Adrian expected instructions to carry copies. Instead, she asked him to ease her coat over the arm support.

“Where may I touch?”

She showed him, holding the front herself. He arranged the empty sleeve without tugging her shoulder. Close enough to notice a loose thread on his collar, she reached, hesitated, and said, “Your collar.”

“What about it?”

“Thread.”

He searched the wrong side. Laughing, she picked it off right-handed and deposited it in the scrap dish.

“Part of your work?”

“No.”

Her quick answer made her look away.

A carriage took them to the side street. No horse for Viola, no sword brought to prove she remained a knight. A building porter guided them to the supervisor.

He explained that the existing duty team would provide security. Suggestions were welcome; she couldn't change shifts or recruit armed personnel.

“I hadn't intended to.”

He apologized. Representatives of important visitors had recently demanded the area be cleared in advance.

Viola nodded without resenting the same warning. He could not know what she intended simply by looking at her name. She had come to inspect what ordinary people would actually encounter, not to demand a different rule for her own arrival.

They tried the shortest approach first. The steps were worn smooth, one handrail loose. Before Adrian touched it, the porter explained that repair was requested and the other side should be used meanwhile.

Viola examined two arrows on the plan.

“That isn't a second exit.”

“It leads through the storage yard.”

“Is the yard gate usually open?”

“For afternoon deliveries.”

Rather than dismiss the route, she asked to see it. Beyond the door, a delivery cart and stacked baskets left the narrow gate passable only sideways, one person at a time.

The porter suggested sending the cart away earlier.

“What if it breaks down?” Adrian asked.

The supervisor didn't treat that as needless argument. During the hearing, the route couldn't also be a loading area. Unless they could clear it, it mustn't appear as an available exit.

Viola faded the arrow and left a note awaiting confirmation. A tidy plan wasn't worth ignoring the cart behind its door.

Then she needed to sit.

It was exactly where she had drawn the chair, against a sheltered outer wall. The porter fetched one. Disappointment crossed her face as she sat, but her feet stayed still.

Adrian crouched beside her to inspect the remaining blank space.

“You've departed from the plan,” she observed.

“How?”

“I drew one chair.”

“My legs still work.”

She frowned. He stood and asked whether another chair was available. Her smile returned. Apparently she recognized that tone of bodily bravado quite well.

While the supervisor checked staffing, an argument erupted outside.

Two handcarts had met at the side door. One delivered paper; the other collected old wooden frames. The collector had an appointment and refused to retreat into the street. The delivery boy feared losing wages for lateness and wouldn't back up either.

A pedestrian squeezed past, slipped, and struck stacked thin boards. They clattered down. Viola's right hand clamped the chair edge.

Her body had begun the familiar movement before the rest of her caught up. The right hand gripping the chair could have pushed her to her feet; the supported left arm remained painfully absent from that old calculation.

Adrian didn't seize her. “The supervisor is there.”

She stopped.

He and the porter cleared people from between the carts, halted the delivery, and directed the empty collection cart toward space inside. A worker supported the elderly woman who had slipped. Her palm was scraped; she was more worried about her eggs.

Only after seeing someone help her did Viola release the chair.

No assassin. No battle requiring an injured woman to prove herself.

Adrian asked whether she would stay while he fetched a clean cloth. She agreed, pointing at the supervisor's keys.

“Ask him to open the empty room. Don't push the second cart into the pedestrians.”

He relayed the suggestion without presenting it as a knight's order. The supervisor checked the room, which held old tables rather than obstructing heavy goods, and opened it. The empty cart backed inside. Half the route cleared.

The woman covered her basket before accepting the cloth. Four eggs survived; one shell had cracked. Mistaking Adrian for a building employee, she complained that this happened every day.

“Every day?”

“The paper people say just a moment. So do the wood people.”

He asked her to tell the supervisor, without recasting her complaint as an incident endangering distinguished guests.

Back beside the chairs, Viola recorded both delivery appointments. She didn't draft disciplinary action. She marked the crossing where routine goods movements collided with visitors.

“The map looks worse,” Adrian said.

“The previous one had no eggs.”

He said she needn't draw them. She considered that seriously before lowering the pen. They laughed together.

The supervisor admitted he had calculated visitor numbers without checking existing deliveries. Paper could arrive before opening; the collector would receive a separately confirmed time. Regular workers would repair the handrail. An injured visitor needn't mend it herself.

Observers wouldn't all crowd the door. The waiting area would admit only its capacity, with the next group's time explained when full. Fine coats wouldn't confer priority. Anyone needing assistance could choose an ordinary companion, not necessarily a particular household's servant.

“What if someone insists on close protection?” he asked.

“Ask the person being protected.”

“And if he refuses?”

“Apply the actual safety requirements you can explain. Without grounds to detain him, don't decide he cannot leave.”

She glanced at Adrian. He didn't pretend he was unlikely to be overlooked in that sentence.

The supervisor separated workable measures from those requiring duty-team agreement or a repair acknowledgment. Written confirmation tomorrow. A recommendation wasn't a completed arrangement. Viola left the unconfirmed parts visible rather than smoothing them into a reassuring final drawing. The supervisor kept his own list of people to consult. Tomorrow they would know which answers had actually come back.

While he sent the final note, Viola put down her pen. Ink stained her right knuckles. Wiping only spread it. Adrian folded a clean corner toward her.

“Have I seemed interfering today?” she asked.

“The woman probably doesn't consider her eggs a trivial matter.”

“I'm not asking about eggs.”

He looked at her properly. Her gaze rested on the support's strap. She didn't demand an immediate return to duty. She said that when accompanying people before, she had always known where to stand. Now, once she sat and everyone moved away, she worried they wouldn't remember to invite her next time.

Adrian settled beside her without rushing to promise she would always be needed.

“I haven't asked whether that cat is attractive.”

“What cat?”

He indicated a wooden carving across the street. After squinting at it, she said it appeared to lack ears. Perhaps they would understand it better closer up.

She looked at her shoes but didn't immediately stand. Her rest wasn't finished. First she cleaned off the ink; when the tension eased, she chose to wait beneath the eaves. Adrian offered to have the carriage brought directly to the door. After considering, she preferred the short walk.

No inspection, no guarantee of safety. He slipped the map into a light paper tube and left it where the driver could collect it, freeing them from holding official business.

When they left, people could use the entrance again. The elderly woman sat in the corner shop while its proprietor boiled the cracked egg so it wouldn't leak all the way home.

Viola watched her.

“I nearly got up.”

“I know.”

“I didn't go over.”

“I know that too.”

She glanced back at the rolled map without asking to erase that moment. Someone opened a distant window. The afternoon air carried a faint smell of wood shavings.

They waited beneath the eaves. Close up, the carving looked like an earless fox. Catching Adrian's interest, its seller insisted the tail proved it was a cat.

“Foxes have tails,” Viola pointed out.

He turned it over. Cat was carved into the base.

She laughed until her shoulder moved, then carefully stopped.

“Clearer evidence than our map,” Adrian said.

“Not proof of a likeness.”

They didn't buy it. Unoffended, the seller offered his very definite cat to the next passerby.

Watching for the carriage, Viola said she still wanted to walk with Adrian.

“Not to find broken doors.”

“I understand.”

“Or to arrange a correctable problem at every corner.”

“We'd have to notify the whole street.”

She laughed, then said she would ask again when her physician allowed more. Today's journey wasn't a date he had already accepted.

When the carriage arrived, she asked for help with her coat again. He supported it where indicated, letting her manage the front with her right hand. The coat settled without pulling her shoulder. Neither joked about the thread, and she did not disguise the request as some service she could repay before the end of the ride.

At the house, she passed the recommendations to the relevant people, noting tomorrow's confirmation. Her name stayed among those providing advice, not on the duty roster.

A separate technical question had arrived: the reviewers wanted to distinguish ordinary protective accompaniment from contractual safeguards capable of transmitting risk. Reading it, Adrian remembered Luna's remark about spoons.

“Spoons may not suffice this time.”

Viola put the page aside for forwarding.

“Let her finish dinner first.”

Luna's right hand couldn't become instantly available because somebody else had finally agreed to rest. Adrian nodded and placed the question beneath tomorrow's papers.

By the window, the map was still drying. Nobody erased the crooked chair. Its legs still leaned, but the place where she had stopped remained perfectly clear.
