# Chapter 153: Breakfast Needs No Oracle

Cecilia had put the official reply beneath the breadbasket.

On the morning of January twenty-fifth, Adrian noticed a corner of blue cover, looked at her, then at the basket. She continued salting her egg with perfect composure.

“It arrived first. You needn't compete.”

“I wondered whether the Temple objects to crumbs.”

“Yes. I'll brush them off.”

She hadn't hidden the document or opened it the moment he sat down. Most of their agreed breakfast hour remained. She asked whether the captain in his travel narrative had finally boarded a ship.

“He has. Unfortunately, the wrong one.”

Cecilia considered this seriously. “How did that book earn a sequel?”

“He survived.”

Her quiet laughter lasted until another plate of toast arrived. She chose the darker slice for herself without asking whether that was sufficiently saintly.

Yesterday, children at the chapel had made boats from old lantern paper. One insisted every boat needed the same flag; another wanted a frog. The frog won, but only on its owner's boat.

“Did you adjudicate?”

“I found a green pen that hadn't dried out.”

Adrian liked the way she said it. Not everything passing through her hands had to become a test of faith.

Outside, a milk cart rattled by. Cecilia listened, then asked whether he would have heard her out when she first came to the estate if there had been no historical record.

He didn't pretend the question was casual.

“I'd have listened. Not necessarily believed you immediately.”

“That doesn't sound better.”

“I trust far more about you now than I did that day.”

Her fingers tightened on her cup.

“I'd like to keep that sentence.”

“It was for you.”

When breakfast ended, she didn't demand another date in exchange. Adrian helped clear their plates. Only when the basket moved did the Temple's answer reappear.

Both offices had replied on time. The registry acknowledged that individual technical assessments remained incomplete and stressed the temporary seven-day restriction. The Temple had supplied its full interpretation.

“You've read it?”

“Once. I dislike it.” Cecilia uncovered the page. “I want to establish precisely which sentence I dislike.”

Before noon, they reached a Temple reading room. Interested parties could consult the cited public texts and certified copies there, but not enter sealed archives. A doctrinal assistant brought two volumes and asked them to leave their hot drinks outside.

Cecilia complied. Her title didn't make books waterproof. She set the cup where instructed and returned to the long table. There was no point using her position to argue about what might happen to a page if someone knocked a drink over.

The oldest passage concerned people pledging mutual aid when calamity approached, bearing a defined burden together. Later registrations became evidence of possible transmission paths. The newest interpretation added that historically compatible relationships could inform priority candidate selection.

Finding his own name in the attached index gave Adrian a familiar sinking feeling.

He remembered the absurdity of first seeing the historical betrothal facsimile. Someone had held a past more complete than his own memories and waited for him to acknowledge a promise about his future. Finding his name again did not make that demand feel more familiar or easier to accept.

The assistant explained that the text didn't make historical betrothals immediately binding. An earlier connection might reduce uncertainty when establishing a new contract. That was the argument for considering it first.

“Might,” Cecilia repeated. “Why does that word disappear from the operative provision?”

“Individual assessment follows.”

“Of the path, or of whom someone should love?”

He didn't answer immediately. Perhaps he had expected an offended saint, not someone separating three layers of quotations.

Her fingertips whitened against the book. Adrian moved a loose sheet closer. She released the page and recorded its reference instead of marking the original.

“I'm not asking you to declare the belief false. How does willing mutual support become externally assigned candidates?”

He found a further passage about protective duties. She read it, then turned back two pages.

“The people making the pledge remain its subjects. It also says they must understand what they undertake.”

“Calamity cannot wait until everyone likes every choice.”

Cecilia fell silent.

He had not mistaken her calmness for agreement. He had seen how tightly she held the loose paper and how carefully she kept her hand away from the book. Adrian expected an immediate reply. Instead, she watched the bell tower through the window.

“Patients can't wait. Neither can a broken bridge. In relief work, I cannot first ask everyone which bandage they prefer.”

The assistant relaxed slightly.

“But that doesn't let me require someone offering bandages to marry the patient.”

A young copyist glanced up from the next table, then looked down again.

The assistant didn't laugh. Comparing the passages, he admitted that a possible historical path and a direction to submit to candidate assessment required something between them. He could record the objection, not withdraw an interpretation issued above him.

“Then send the question to someone who can answer.”

Adrian nearly asked how long they must wait. Seeing the assistant already reach for an official inquiry sheet, he stopped. Impatience needn't block the route they had just opened.

At the exit, an older priest recognized Cecilia. Without reprimanding her, he asked whether she had considered how public challenges might unsettle donors to the relief house.

“Have you received a withdrawal notice?”

“Not yet. I'm warning you of consequences.”

“I'll answer for what I've said. But I can't write a letter threatening myself on behalf of people who haven't spoken.”

He frowned. Adrian was ready to leave with her, but Cecilia asked whether the house still needed winter cloth.

It did.

“I'll examine the purchase request this afternoon as planned. This disagreement shouldn't cost anyone a blanket.”

She promised neither to pay every shortfall herself nor to make his support a condition. Outside, her composure finally cracked.

“I hated that.”

“I noticed.”

“And hated immediately wondering whether I should speak more gently.”

Wind lifted a page. Adrian held its corner rather than taking her whole folder.

“You asked anyway.”

“That doesn't make me entirely unafraid.”

He stood with her behind a column, out of the wind. After a moment, she asked whether he had expected her to tear off her holy emblem and throw it into the snow.

“Someone would have to fetch it.”

She looked down at the silver ornament and smiled.

“Besides, I still like it.”

Before visiting the review office, she stopped at the relief house's cloth room.

The woman at the door made her wipe her shoes. Uncut cotton covered the floor. Cecilia asked Adrian to hold her papers, crouched beside a folded corner, and measured it with her hand. The recorded quantity didn't cover the intended number of beds. Nobody had stolen it; several bolts were narrower than the old specification.

“Cut them the old way and they won't cover shoulders,” the woman said.

Cecilia didn't invoke her important business to demand compromise. They spread a sample over a bench and repositioned a seam. Still two blankets short. Two intact older blankets could cover the gap; damaged ones wouldn't be counted as usable. The next order must specify actual width, not just the number of bolts.

Adrian moved the bench clear of the doorway and found a wooden spool underneath. He returned it. The woman thanked him, then hurried them out of the porter's way.

“Were you bored waiting?” Cecilia asked afterward.

“I thought you might challenge the cloth's interpretation too.”

“It hasn't offered one. It's merely narrow.”

Laughing, she looked back. The porter was pinning their revised sample beside a measuring cord so the next shift needn't guess. The priest wasn't there. Nobody demanded she change her opinion. Her shoulders eased as she left the corrected request with its usual custodian. Continuing her work wasn't a bargain for forgiveness.

That afternoon, Cecilia submitted her questions as a person directly named by the order. She didn't place them beneath Elena's name or promise technical participation on anyone else's behalf.

The clerk read them back: Could historical material replace present consent? How did safe paths differ from partner selection? Would ordinary assessment proceed within a reasonable time after someone declined priority candidate review?

The wording mattered enough for her to ask the clerk to pause rather than nodding through a version that sounded close. This would be the question someone else received, without her standing beside it to explain her meaning. She corrected one phrase. She wasn't refusing every examination of risk. She refused to make accepting a particular person the price of entering that examination.

Both the original and correction stayed on file.

With the two offices' replies received, review would continue on the morning of the twenty-seventh. The responsible interpreter could reply in writing or send someone qualified to explain. Irrelevant family matters would be excluded from public questions, and technical discussion could restrict attendance. Accepting Cecilia's observations didn't withdraw the order.

Adrian noticed that the assigned waiting room was smaller than before.

“Which entrance will observers use?”

He asked before putting the notice away. Last time, merely waiting for their turn had filled an afternoon; this time other people would need places to stand and ways to enter too. The clerk indicated a side door. Its passage was narrow; the staff on duty would manage access. Interest had grown, so unnecessary attendants should stay away.

Cecilia noted the two steps by the door without volunteering Viola as a guard.

On their way back, the breakfast shop was displaying its unsold morning loaves at a side window beneath a changed price card. Cecilia chose a plain round loaf and asked whether Adrian would report on its flavor tomorrow.

“You watched me eat this morning.”

“Not this kind. It has no jam.”

“What if I dislike it?”

“I won't buy it again. I won't send it to an interpretation committee.”

He accepted, laughing. She bought one for herself too. Barely warm when torn open, it still smelled solidly of wheat.

At the house, she informed the people concerned about the hearing. Elena didn't ask whether Cecilia had finally joined her side. She pointed out that the twenty-seventh and Isabella's appointment deadline were different dates requiring separate arrangements.

Viola requested the entrance details and drew two doorways with her right hand. Luna asked for the full cited safety passage. Only after confirming an authorized copy existed did she have it brought over.

Cecilia didn't reenact the reading-room argument. She put away her papers, went to the window, and broke her loaf.

When Adrian passed the doorway, she called him back.

“I remember what you said this morning.”

He stopped and looked at her.

“So do I.”

She didn't search her folder for a sheet on which to preserve it. After he had gone, she simply finished the half loaf she had kept.
