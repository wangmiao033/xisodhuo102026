# Chapter 116: Her Present Was an Old Iron Box

On the morning of December 24, Luna Ashcroft surveyed the carriage outside the guesthouse.

“That looks more likely to slosh about than yesterday's soup.”

Adrian Reed held the door and looked inside. “You can face forward.”

“A research recommendation, or do you want to sit beside me?”

“It's to stop you seeing three copies of every document.”

With a disappointed sigh, she handed him her file and climbed in with her left hand free. Elena Rosenberg entered opposite and found Luna watching solemnly.

“What?”

“I surrendered a very important seat.”

Elena placed a hand warmer on her lap. “Your payment.”

“I hoped to charge more.”

“It's filled with hot water, not gold.”

Listening to their bargaining, Adrian took the short seat by the door. Cecilia Saintclair entered next. Seeing the documents clutched on his lap, she earnestly asked whether she was protecting their archivist today. Outside, Viola Blackwood reminded them to bring their feet in. The driver was waiting to close the door.

They hadn't raided the warehouse overnight or mistaken an old inventory number for permission to pick locks. The tribunal had received their application that morning and checked the local repository's inventory receipt. It authorized an unpowered inspection with a recorder and custodian present: provenance numbers, external condition, the accessible outer cover, and accompanying maintenance documents. Sealed internal components required another application.

Luna wasn't disappointed. Waiting had once been unbearable. Today, she accepted the half-day that courage could not properly save.

The eastern storehouse stood behind the church equipment repository, less than half an hour from the guesthouse. Snow formed two low walls beside the entrance. Workers repaired a courtyard pump. No sacred bells sounded at their arrival.

The gray-haired custodian checked their authorization, then requested a register of everything entering the building. Luna lifted her warmer left-handed.

“Does this count as a container?”

“Will it leak?”

“Probably not.”

“Then leave it on the bench. Probably not still doesn't belong on old papers.”

Luna obeyed. Adrian suppressed a smile and received a threatening glance.

Inside were shelves, iron boxes, and countless cotton covers. Cecilia studied a silver basin painted with two birds and recognized an old ritual implement. Luna suggested it let two people wash their hands together. Elena noticed drainage holes.

“You can't classify every object with two birds as wedding equipment.”

“Isn't that our principal occupation lately?”

Passing them, the custodian calmly explained that the basin strained impurities from lamp oil. The birds were its maker's mark.

Adrian bowed his head, hiding laughter in a cough. Elena looked at him and finally laughed too.

“Write that down,” she told Luna. “Today's first sourced conclusion.”

At the third row, the inventory led them to an empty wooden crate. Its lid carried the limiter's old category. Packing blocks remained inside, but nothing else.

Luna gripped her cloak. Adrian stopped too, without immediately asking who'd stolen the evidence.

The custodian knelt, examined the dust boundary, and opened the shelf ledger's relocation entries.

“The roof was repaired last month. Heavy iron pieces went to the back. The new location's underneath.”

A tag had folded into the crate's bottom, leaving one corner visible. The recorder first noted the empty crate's condition. Then the custodian retrieved the tag to show its date and signature. The iron equipment was at the back. It hadn't mysteriously vanished upon news of the investigation.

Adrian followed her to a gray iron box half a person's height. A new label called it an old flow-stabilizing base. The original side plate remained: East Storehouse Load Limiter No. 17. Its inventory system differed from C-17's. This was the device referenced in the load-table appendix.

“The name changed,” he said.

“Later staff reclassified by appearance. That's why I insist they keep the original numbers.”

Two similar bases stood behind it. A maintenance worker started toward one, following the newer label. Adrian asked him to wait. Turning the table's drawing over, he showed the recorder two holes in its lower corner. Both other bases had holes on the same side; the iron box's pair sat at different heights, matching the drawing.

The custodian checked the old plate's rivets again. There was no indication it had moved. The number engraved on the foot also matched the transfer ledger. Only then did she record a confirmed identification.

“Good thing you looked at the back,” Luna said.

“I'm becoming afraid of backs.”

Remembering yesterday's hidden units, she laughed. “A valuable research fear.”

“Please don't make it a project.”

“Too late. I've named it.”

The recorder looked up.

“Private joke,” Luna amended. “No funding.”

She didn't rush to call the box an answer. Gray paint blistered over it. Wooden blocks supported its feet. A narrow screwed-on front cover looked less sophisticated than a new control base at the tower.

Adrian asked to see the external power inlet. It was empty, its input connector missing, with an old lead withdrawal-from-service seal. After confirming that no energy plate would be connected, the recorder allowed removal of the authorized cover screws.

The warehouse maintenance worker did the work. Luna indicated positions on the load table. She neither cast magic nor steadied tools with her injured hand. Viola went to shift a lamp, discovered its stand was heavier than expected, and asked Adrian instead. She stepped back to give the worker room.

The cover came away without magical light or a voice proclaiming destiny.

Inside were two separate terminal bases divided by a ceramic break channel. An outer branch had lost its spring, with brown scorching beside a crack. A connection diagram sat behind a transparent protector inside the cover. Rather than one line interrupted by the main source, it showed two paths requiring separate breaks.

Adrian studied a marked yellow handle.

Its shape differed from the accident equipment, but its position recalled the sound of the ceramic neck shattering. In the drawing, a full pull lifted two contacts clear of their terminals, breaking the closed loop.

“This does more than stop sending power in,” he said.

Luna followed his finger. “Whatever remains in the side circuit needs somewhere to go. Or the path must be broken completely.”

“Did it work?”

“A drawing can't tell us that.”

She asked the worker not to pull the handle, even to prove it moved. Years out of service, the device might still hold unknown residue. Their curiosity wasn't worth testing that possibility.

The recorder documented the terminals, channel, and condition. Several structural labels matched the load tables. Two other features were later modifications requiring maintenance records. They hadn't discovered a machine that could save everyone. They'd found an old tool showing that earlier workers had taken precautions against a particular danger.

Luna watched too long. Her left hand began trembling. Elena noticed first and suggested resting outside.

“One more look.”

“How much will that look include?”

Seeing no mockery in her face, Luna slowly withdrew her hand.

“All right. Rest first. The drawing won't run away.”

Winter sunlight had warmed the outside bench a little. Cecilia retrieved the hand warmer, placed it on Luna's lap, and sat beside her. Adrian brought hot water. Instead of tending everyone, Viola sat and ate her own half-piece of bread.

“You're quiet today,” Luna observed.

“I'm resisting assigning everyone a position.”

“Difficult?”

“A little. Particularly when you choose the hardest places to leave.”

Adrian nodded. Luna glared, then laughed.

“Today, I walked out myself.”

“Yes,” Viola said. “So my practice is going well too.”

Blowing on her water, Cecilia asked whether Luna would continue if the device could only prove danger existed without offering a solution.

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“If someone says I invented all this because I burned my hand, I can at least ask them to examine that scorched terminal first.”

She fell quiet, then added, “And I want to know who recognized the danger, and where they left their warning.”

After resting, she didn't insist on returning inside. Adrian and the tribunal recorder completed the remaining external checks. Elena waited with Luna, without treating his absence as another seat to contest.

The warmer cooled. Luna rotated it left-handed. Seeing her glance at the warehouse again, Elena asked whether she feared they would overlook something.

“I'm wondering whether something might happen with him inside and me out here.”

Elena didn't immediately promise otherwise. She remembered his face after the accident too. Beautiful reassurances couldn't stop real light.

“The recorder's there. The custodian's there. Nothing will be powered today. He promised not to add inspections on the spot. We can trust those things, and ask clear questions.”

Luna looked down at her dressing.

“I used to think everyone was safer with me beside them.”

“I often think having him beside me ought to be enough.”

They met each other's eyes, then looked away.

“Apparently more than one device needs changes,” Luna said softly.

Elena didn't pursue the analogy. She divided the last half-piece of bread with her. Luna took it left-handed and bit into it, without covering her admission with a joke.

Inside, the custodian removed the maintenance pouch from the cover. Its old label remained intact. It held two relocation explanations and several repair appendices. Damp had affected the corners; the outer page was badly worn. A black triangular warning remained legible.

Adrian first saw a short sentence.

If discharge is incomplete, do not use repeated readouts to recover missing text.

More lines adhered to another damp page and couldn't be peeled apart. The custodian fetched a thin support board and laid pouch and papers flat for a qualified restorer. The recorder copied only exposed words, without rubbing the blurred portion.

“Who wrote it?” Adrian asked.

Part of a signature showed at the foot. Finer than the main writing, its last stroke ended in a small inward hook. The custodian shook her head. Recorders sometimes forwarded warnings to the old equipment office. A fragment of signature couldn't establish authorship.

The authorized attachments were registered and sealed in a protective folder for the recorder to return to the registry. The cover went back on. The device stayed in its warehouse. Adrian emerged with empty hands except for the field transcription they were permitted to see.

Luna looked up. “Any luck?”

“Found a very heavy present.”

“For me?”

“You may look. You can't take it home.”

“The first present you seriously pick out for me, and it's an old iron box.”

“You seem to like it.”

She opened her mouth. In the winter light, her gaze softened briefly.

“I do. Although saying so makes you remarkably difficult to tease.”

Elena handed him his scarf. If he didn't put it on, she warned, whoever liked old boxes would have to accompany him to the doctor. Luna immediately announced expensive escort fees. Cecilia offered to come free. Viola pointed at their waiting driver.

“Can we get in before discussing rates?”

On the return journey, Luna didn't read. The warning transcription rested on Adrian's lap, the unidentified final flourish visible at its edge.

They had found the old device, and a sentence more disturbing than the device itself.

Someone had known that looking once more didn't always mean merely learning a little more.
