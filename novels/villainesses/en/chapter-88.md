# Chapter 88: This Time, She Chooses to Come Along

Early on December 4, Viola fastened her sword at her waist and stood before the mirror for a long time.

The scabbard felt heavier than she remembered. As soon as the belt settled into its familiar notch, her shoulders wanted to straighten, as though wearing the right clothes meant someone would come through the door at any moment to ask about a patrol route.

Someone knocked.

“Captain,” Owen called.

She turned, one hand already resting on the edge of the desk out of habit.

But what the corporal handed her was a little bag of dried apples.

“My mother asked me to bring these. She said she couldn't send them into the hearing. They wouldn't make very good witnesses.”

Viola took the bag. Slowly, she smiled.

“Thank her for me.”

After a moment's hesitation, Owen asked about the new duty rotation. He knew she had been cleared the day before and her suspension lifted, but the officer overseeing duty assignments had not changed.

“Keep to the current arrangements,” she said. “I'm going to discuss what comes next today. When there's a decision, you'll receive official notice.”

He nodded, then finally ventured a look at the sword on her hip.

“It looks just the same.”

Viola touched an old mark on the scabbard.

“It is. I just need to think again about when to wear it.”

Her first stop was the clinic.

Dr. Shaw removed the thin bandage from her left forearm, asked her to flex her fingers, and checked the skin around the wound. It was healing well. Prolonged riding with the reins in hand and forceful parrying would still have to wait. Viola asked about a two-day carriage journey. The answer was to take breaks along the way, keep the wound dry, and seek medical attention promptly if redness or swelling developed.

“You can walk, and you can ride in a carriage.” The doctor put fresh dressings into a small pouch. “Don't translate those two statements into permission to stop a runaway horse.”

“I wasn't planning to—”

Dr. Shaw looked up.

Viola swallowed the rest. “I'll remember.”

Adrian happened to be in the outer room for his own examination. His breathing was much steadier today. The doctor had cleared him for ordinary, unhurried walks, but still advised against lifting heavy things or pushing on through cold winds while traveling. When Viola came out, he moved his hat from the seat beside him.

“I heard a horse being given special consideration.”

“I was only asking about the journey.”

“I only asked whether I could help carry someone's trunk. She suggested I start with my own cup of water.”

Viola laughed and sat down, opening Owen's paper bag. They each took a slice of dried apple. Neither tried to prove a full recovery by hauling off everything else in the room.

Later that morning, Ward met her at the council building. Two sets of official correspondence lay on the desk. One concerned an opening in a regional cavalry unit. The other outlined her options for an appointment after recuperation.

“If you'd like to stay on the border, you could apply for this post.” The colonel pointed to the map. “Escorting civilian transport, with separate authority and supply accounts. But I can't make promises on behalf of the receiving unit, and I certainly can't send you out injured to take up the post today.”

Viola studied the route. There were familiar ferries along it, and slopes that would be difficult in winter. She could imagine spending many years on that road.

“What's the other choice?”

“Recover first. You can tell us where you'd like to go and leave an address where notices will reach you. You'll still return to answer any further inquiries connected with the case.”

Outside, someone carried a new pane of glass into the courtyard. A window that had been missing for half a month was finally being replaced, though its old wooden frame had to be planed again. Watching a workman remove the hinges, Viola suddenly understood that restoration did not mean putting everything back exactly where it had been.

“I'd like to apply for recuperative leave,” she said. “And I'd like to travel for a while.”

Ward did not reach for her pen at once.

“Because of Reed?”

Viola's fingertips stopped at the edge of the map.

His name made her want to dress the answer in something more impressive. For the investigation. To repay a debt. To safeguard an important witness. Any of those explanations would give her firmer ground to stand on.

“Partly,” she said. “And partly because I want to know what kind of knight I can be away from here. I was happy yesterday. Today, I don't want to pretend every old arrangement still fits.”

Ward regarded her for a moment, then slid the application form across.

“Write that, then. Send us the detailed route when you've settled it. I'll approve fourteen days of recuperative leave for now. Have another medical examination before it ends, and we'll discuss the next step.”

Viola took the pen and wrote the inn's mailing address. If she moved on, she would report the receiving post station and an address for forwarded correspondence before departure. Her former unit's duties, issued equipment, and reports still needing her explanation would receive their final handover that afternoon.

As she was leaving, the colonel stopped her.

“Blackwood. Yesterday's finding recognized that your decision on the fourteenth was necessary. It doesn't require you to choose the hardest road every day for the rest of your life.”

Viola nodded and slipped her copy of the application into her pocket.

A servant wearing Baron Harcourt's colors had been waiting outside the inn for half an hour.

The baron was prepared to recommend her for a place in an aristocratic cavalry unit, provided she resumed her former social obligations and appeared with him in public. This letter made no further demand that she alter her testimony. Its phrasing was quite courteous. But at the end, it pressed for an answer that day and warned that, should she miss this opportunity, the next offer would not be so generous.

She finished reading and folded it.

“Please thank the baron for his invitation. I won't accept the arrangement.”

The servant noticed her sword, as though only then realizing how much had changed since his last visit.

“You could meet with him before deciding.”

“I've decided.”

She asked the innkeeper to witness receipt of the letter and wrote a brief reply. Only after the servant left did she notice how tightly she had creased the paper. A white line remained across her fingertip.

Elena came downstairs and glanced at the letter still in her hand.

“He ought to have given you at least three days to consider it.”

“I wouldn't have gone even if he had.”

“Good. Three days you can spend on something more interesting.”

Elena had removed the outer layer of gauze that day. The new skin on her hand still needed protection from friction. Turning the pages of a travel book with her other hand, she asked Viola if she could borrow a bookmark. Viola searched all her pockets and produced only a blank duty slip.

Elena accepted it with a barely suppressed smile.

“When you get back, I'll give you a box of proper ones.”

The afternoon's handover involved more small details than Viola had expected. She had to explain where water had collected on an old patrol route, which lamp had a loose hook, and who had personally counted a delivery of horse feed. The officer taking over was young, but no raw recruit. When she started explaining something for a second time, he looked up and reminded her he had written it down.

Viola stopped and slowly drew her hand back from the papers.

He pointed to a narrow path on the map. Temporary boards had been laid there the night before, allowing wagons carrying wounded people to avoid a short detour. Her first instinct was to mention the unstable old bridge pier. Instead, she asked who had inspected the work.

The young officer named a town foreman and produced a load-rating statement confirmed by all three responsible parties.

She read it, then dated a note beside the route on her old map. The road had not waited for her return to improve. It would not stand still while she was away.

“That's good,” she said.

Only then did the younger man seem to relax. He asked whether he could send the lamp with the loose hook for repair, rather than continuing to wedge cloth beneath it.

“You should. I kept thinking I'd deal with it alongside the next batch of repairs.”

“Then I'll send it today.”

Viola found its original issue number for him and resisted explaining how to hold the lamp steady. Once she had signed the final page, she pushed her usual red pen across the desk. He accepted it without hesitation.

He took the map to check something with the stores, without waiting for her to accompany him. Owen emerged with her personal cup. A ring of tea stains still clung to the bottom.

“You left this in your desk.”

“I thought it had been thrown out.”

“No one dared.”

She wrapped her hands around it. Suddenly, all the days she had spent here seemed to have become something ordinary she was allowed to take with her.

Toward evening, Adrian found her in the inn's back courtyard. She had put her sword away in her room and wore only her loose-sleeved coat. She was watching a carriage struggle to turn around.

“I heard you applied for leave,” he said.

“Fourteen days. Then another examination and a discussion about my next post.”

He stopped half a step away.

“Where do you want to go?”

“I haven't decided everywhere yet.” She paused. “First, I wanted to ask what you're planning to do about that old lining.”

“Wait for Ivo's reply. Look through the town records. We might have to find someone who knows more than we do.”

Viola looked at her empty hands and took a deep breath.

“Then I'd like to come with you.”

At last, she looked at him.

“I'll protect you as well as I can, tell you about any dangers I know of, and listen to what you decide. If I disagree, I'll say so. I mean this next part of the journey. I'm not asking you to owe me your whole life because of what's happened.”

Adrian did not answer immediately. A wheel scraped past the courtyard gate, and the coachman muttered that someone had built the gatepost entirely too solidly.

“What would you choose if I weren't here?” he asked.

She gave it serious thought.

“I'd still recover first. Visit the regional unit, perhaps go home for a while. I have places I could go.”

“And right now, you want to come with me?”

“Yes.” Her voice was steady this time. “I want to see you. And when I meet other people who need help, I want to do something with what I've learned. I don't want to hide either of those things inside a duty someone else wrote for me.”

Adrian smiled.

“I'd like us to go together. But you have to let me look after you too. Tell you when it's time to get your dressings out, for instance.”

“I may be better at that than you.”

“I could start by reminding you to have lunch.”

She hesitated, then looked a little embarrassed.

“That might help.”

He offered his right hand, palm up. After a moment, Viola placed her right hand in his. There was no kneeling, and no one recited an ancient oath. His palm was warm. She held it far more gently than she held a sword.

“Welcome aboard,” he said.

“There's something else I'd like to say.”

“Yes?”

She did not let go, but her cheeks slowly colored.

“When we're resting on the road, you can just call me Viola. I'd prefer it if you didn't always begin by asking what the captain has planned.”

“All right, Viola.”

The next sentence she had prepared vanished from her mind.

Adrian waited, then asked, fighting a smile, whether she needed to issue him a permit to use her name. She looked up, finally laughed, and drew her hand back.

Before dinner, the four of them discussed their plans. Cecilia first needed to confirm with Dr. Shaw who would take over the local relief accounts. She also owed Mira a letter in the capital. An independent bookkeeper was continuing Elena's audit of the trading station. The original records would remain locally, and she would receive progress reports on the dates already arranged.

Viola explained that her leave allowed her to travel with them, though her left arm could not yet sustain a forceful block. Elena laid a carriage company's schedule on the table.

“Then we'll hire something comfortable. Since we aren't chasing a fugitive, surely we can let the wheels bounce a little less.”

“I can inspect the axles first,” Viola said.

“Tomorrow morning.” Adrian served her soup. “This is an extension of the lunch-reminding duties you just approved.”

“It's dinner.”

“I'm still working out the scope of the position.”

Their laughter made the innkeeper look up from the lampshade she was repairing beside the window.

After dinner, Elena and Adrian sat by the fire to read. She pulled a carriage-fare receipt from between the pages and held it in front of him without a word.

He took the slip, admitted his guilt, and asked whether she would like to see the winter celebration's ribbons with him tomorrow.

“Yes. But promise you won't give the whole evening to the accounts too.”

“I'd like to save the evening for you.”

She lowered her gaze, her hand pausing a fraction before it turned the page.

“Then let's start tonight.”

Beyond the courtyard, wagons carrying bridge-repair timber passed one after another. The workers said they would be taking new stone to the mill market in the morning, where the cracked drainage channel had to be lifted and repaired. Hearing them from upstairs, Viola packed her dressings into her traveling trunk and left half of Owen's dried apples on the table.

Just before closing the lid, she remembered something and opened her little pouch again.

She carefully placed the two ring-toss tickets for tomorrow's celebration right on top.
