# Chapter 97: The Saint Brings the Pay Sheet

On the afternoon of December 11, Cecilia did not begin by asking Luna how she intended to publish her research.

She put two sheets of paper on the desk.

The upper sheet was next week's instrument-calibration schedule. Beneath it lay a wage receipt. The same brass clip held them together, and both had a space for a signature in the lower right-hand corner. At a glance, they looked like two parts of the same form.

“Someone downstairs wants to know whether she can still collect last week's pay if she doesn't take part in the new observations.”

Luna looked up. Surprise cut through the weariness in her face.

“Of course she can. She's already done that work.”

“She wasn't sure.”

The woman waiting in the doorway was older than Adrian had expected. She introduced herself as Tessa Quinn, twenty-seven. She had spent three years repairing the tower's fine brass springs. A worn little file poked out of her apron pocket. She looked at Luna rather than at any of the visitors.

“I'm not saying I won't repair the instruments,” she hurried to explain. “I just don't want another finger-steadiness observation next week. My sister's staying with me, so the evenings will be noisy. My numbers won't look very good.”

“You don't have to produce good-looking numbers,” Luna said.

Tessa smiled as though she had been offered a little encouragement.

That smile stopped Luna's next words.

Cecilia brought over a chair and set it where Tessa wanted to sit, without putting her in the middle of a circle of onlookers. Elena closed the papers they had been discussing that morning. Adrian moved his own chair toward the window.

“We can leave,” he said.

After a moment, Tessa nodded. She asked only Cecilia to stay. Before the door closed, Luna had already removed the brass clip and handed her the wage receipt separately.

In the corridor, Viola rested her right hand lightly on the railing and looked back at the door.

“She stayed on her feet the whole time.”

“She was waiting to be allowed to sit,” Elena said quietly.

Adrian remembered arriving at the Rosenberg estate and watching people's faces before choosing even a chair. Nobody needed to lay a knife on the table to make a request difficult to refuse. Knowing who paid the wages could be quite enough.

A little later, Tessa came out alone. She still had her pay sheet, along with a separate copy of her ordinary working hours.

“I'm taking this to the administrator,” she said. “And I'll still repair that spring this afternoon. It won't mend itself just because I'd rather not hold my finger out.”

This time, her smile belonged to an actual joke.

Luna invited the others back in. A thin notebook had appeared on the desk, but she left it closed.

“Tessa has agreed that you can hear what we've decided, not the private reasons she gave me,” she said. “She won't join next week's observations. Her pay and repair work are unchanged. I won't ask her to make any other decisions today.”

“What's the notebook?” Adrian asked.

“Work records from adjusting the anti-tremor clamps. Operator fatigue, numb fingers, mistakes. I wanted to use them to show how the instrument behaves with different people.”

“Did they know you would use the records that way?” Cecilia asked.

Luna pressed her fingertips against the cover.

“They knew we were recording things to improve the clamps. They hadn't heard about the paper I intended to write.”

She did not add that she already had the data anyway.

Outside, a brass hammer struck three times, paused, and struck again. Downstairs, people continued doing work that required no grand story to justify it.

“I thought removing names would be enough,” Luna said. “But Tessa pointed out that she's the only person here who repairs fine springs left-handed. Anyone who saw the description would know.”

Elena slid a blank sheet toward her. “Sometimes hiding only the name gives people more confidence than it should.”

“I understand. Those pages won't go into the application.”

“You won't simply give her a number and put them back?”

“No.”

Luna's answer came out sharply. She rubbed the space between her eyebrows.

“I don't particularly enjoy admitting this many mistakes in one day. But I have heard the question.”

Cecilia did not press her advantage. She picked up the teapot and refilled Luna's cup.

“Records of injuries and defects still need to be kept,” she said. “We shouldn't make numb fingers disappear on paper just to reassure everyone.”

“They'll stay in the restricted maintenance and safety ledger, for investigating problems, arranging care, and fixing the instruments. I'll stop using the research extracts. I won't pretend I asked a question in the past that I didn't ask. Any new study needs a separate explanation and separate volunteers.”

Luna looked at Adrian.

“And giving your result a prettier name wouldn't turn it into something I can lend to anyone I please.”

“Then we've finally found a very ugly, very accurate name for it.”

“Which is?”

“Something That Can't Be Lent Out Yet.”

Viola turned her head and laughed. Luna laughed too. Her little pen made half a turn between her fingers before she set it down firmly.

She called in the administrator and first asked him to check whether any other pay sheets had been clipped to the wrong forms. Then she made the distinction explicit: earned wages and normal work assignments were not conditional on voluntary observations. Future invitations would be sent separately. Replies could go to the administrator, so nobody had to refuse her to her face.

He wrote that down, then reminded her that he also assigned working hours.

“Disputes can go to the academy's registry office,” Luna said. “Put the address on the invitation. An office name nobody can find isn't much use.”

Cecilia fetched the public directory from downstairs and located the address for visitor and research complaints. Viola checked the postal route to make sure ordinary letters could reach it. At last the elegant words on the desk became an envelope someone could actually put in the mail.

They went down to the little room where the maintenance ledgers were stored. An open wooden chest stood under the shelves, discarded gears sharing a layer with papers awaiting binding. Luna crouched beside it and took out the attachments for her application. She checked the page numbers and withdrew the sheets concerning the operators.

“Nothing was sent?” Elena asked.

“Nothing. You can check the outgoing register by the door.”

The administrator brought it over. It listed materials sent to the academy, but the most recent entry concerned replacement bearings. The research draft had not yet been assigned a dispatch number. Elena checked each relevant entry and closed the register. The fact that they had argued was no reason to accuse Luna of a disclosure that had not happened.

Luna put the withdrawn pages in an envelope, marked it with the date, and explained why they were no longer to be used. The administrator would retain the original repair records. Anyone consulting them would leave a name and a reason on the access sheet. Luna separately copied the clamp dimensions and material defects, which contained no personal information. Those problems still needed fixing.

“Is there enough left to write about?” Adrian asked.

“Not enough to support the conclusion I wanted.” She crossed out a heading. “Enough to tell the academy what we don't know.”

It was not a neat correction. A heavy clot of ink remained on the page.

Cecilia did not ask her to cross out her disappointment too. She simply steadied a box of rulers that was slipping off the shelf while Luna put the papers away.

Before they left, the administrator asked whether future volunteers could receive extra pay for supplying new data.

“For their time and travel, yes,” Luna said. “Not a payment they lose unless they finish the last session. If they stop, we still pay for the time they've given.” She paused. “But don't recruit anyone on that basis yet. We don't have a new study suitable for people.”

Adrian noticed that the recruitment notice beside the door had been turned over. The side facing the corridor now bore a single line.

Repair work continues as usual.

By the time they finished, they were late for tea. The pear tart from the kitchen had been cut into six pieces. Luna counted them and discovered that hers was the narrowest.

“Is this the fee for an ethics review?”

“The knife slipped,” Adrian said.

“I prefer to believe someone's persecuting me. Otherwise I must admit I can't even cut a tart.”

Cecilia exchanged her slightly larger slice for Luna's.

Luna considered it. “Will this compromise your independent judgment?”

“No. I've already judged that you need something to eat.”

She sounded entirely serious, which only made Luna laugh harder.

When Tessa came to collect the spring, Luna asked her to check a short account of their decision. The woman read it line by line and pointed out that it described her as right-handed.

Luna corrected it at once and asked her to look again. She did not wave the error away as close enough. Tessa took her copy, thanked her, and went to return her tools. Nobody required her to remain and listen to an apology about how much the researcher had learned.

Watching her go, Adrian thought that removing a few pages from an ambitious application did not necessarily mean the tower had accomplished less that day.

Toward evening, Cecilia sat on the bench outside the guesthouse, sorting her own visit notes. A thin rim of ice clung to the courtyard bucket. She tucked her skirt out of the way, leaving a small, dry space beside her.

“May I sit?” Adrian asked.

“Yes. I'm trying to work out how to avoid making something kind sound rather awful.”

“That usually starts with telling me what you meant to say.”

“I wanted to say that I'd always ask these questions for you.”

“That doesn't sound awful.”

“But today I realized Tessa doesn't have a duke's daughter beside her. Her name isn't in a saint's old record.” Cecilia looked down at her notebook. “If I only take this seriously when someone I like is sitting in the experimental chair, it isn't quite the fine promise I thought it was.”

Adrian sat down. He did not hurry to assure her that she was already good enough.

She opened the draft of a public monthly report for the relief station and pointed to an account of a patient's experiences. It omitted the name, but the profession, street, and family's circumstances would allow the neighbors to guess. Cecilia marked the passage for revision. She would write to Mira and ask her to help check it. Good intentions did not mean everyone wanted their life made into an example.

“I wasn't only examining Luna today,” she said.

An evening breeze rounded the wall and stirred the hair beside her cheek. Adrian held down a page before it could turn.

“I like that you're willing to ask yourself the same question.”

Her pen stopped in midair.

“Does ‘like’ mean approval of a particular action here?”

“It also means that I'm glad to be sitting here with you.”

She considered that, then nodded gravely. “Then I can be more specifically happy.”

Adrian laughed. Color rose in her face, but she did not look away.

“Would you have tea with me tomorrow afternoon? No medical forms. No future you're required to take responsibility for.”

“Yes. After we've finished preparing the workshop.”

“Then I'll choose ordinary cups,” she said. “Otherwise Luna might think there's something to measure.”

The witch happened to be passing the doorway in time to hear the last sentence.

“Only how much hot water you use.”

“Do you need our permission for that?” Adrian asked.

“If it affects the washing-up bill, I need the kitchen's permission.”

Elena stood behind her, tapping the travel book lightly against her palm.

“I've saved your passage for tonight,” she told Adrian.

“I remember.”

“Good. I've found someone the captain ought to ask for directions.”

“Has the author agreed?”

“The author isn't here. I'll give you my opinion first.”

She held out her hand. Adrian rose and took it. Cecilia gathered her notes and followed them inside. Nobody needed a new invitation to erase an evening already promised.

Luna stopped beside the door and opened another drawing.

“I'd like to move the equipment into the west workshop tomorrow. Assembly and empty-rig checks first. Nothing attached to a person.”

The drawing showed two specimen holders with a removable bridge between them. They would use blank material recreated from public drawings of old instruments, not anyone's original betrothal record.

“The table upstairs is too narrow, and there's no suitable heat sink,” she said. “Trusting one another won't teach heat to be polite.”

Viola took the plan and followed the line to the western exit. It crossed a square labeled temporary storage, then disappeared at the door.

“I'll look at that door first thing tomorrow.”

“It opens,” Luna said.

“I want to know what's on the other side.”

Adrian followed her finger. Outside the window, a coal cart was coming to a halt beside the western wall. Its wheel slid an inch across the thin ice.

Tomorrow, clearly, the experimental bench would not be the only thing worth guarding.
