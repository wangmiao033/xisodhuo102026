# Chapter 159: Fairness Doesn't Mean Dividing Me by Five

The broadsheet misspelled Adrian’s name.

It was remarkably certain about his marriage.

On the afternoon of January 27, two coppers bought him a sheet proclaiming the imminent restoration of the saint’s old union and a noblewoman’s interference. Its Adrian had acknowledged a marriage while accepting five women’s competing claims. The man apparently had no need for sleep.

Elena finished the final paragraph.

“They’ve got your name wrong.”

“Unfortunately, they haven’t merely mistaken me for someone else.”

The vendor looked up, suddenly realizing who stood before him. His mouth opened. Nothing emerged.

Adrian asked where the batch came from and whether this was the complete edition. The vendor indicated the printer’s imprint. The deliveryman had left no manuscript. He hadn’t attended the hearing; someone had taught him the headline to call out.

“What supported your announcement of a restored marriage?” Elena asked.

He pointed at the large type, flushing. He couldn’t ask the Temple about every story.

Elena breathed in and lowered her voice.

“We don’t expect you to know everything. Please don’t present unchecked claims as something you witnessed.”

He lowered the sheet he had been about to display. He made no promise to retrieve the dozens already sold.

They kept their complete copy. The bottom credited today’s public inquiry and informed sources, naming none and reproducing no restoration text. The headline called Cecilia his current wife. The article offered half a sentence about a historical registration.

Adrian folded it. Mentioning the Temple did not prove an internal archive leak. Public answers could have been cut into this shape; someone might also have supplied more. For now, they knew what had been printed.

Two more copies and seven interview requests awaited them in the small sitting room.

Cecilia sat behind them, looking ready to preside over a funeral.

“Whose?” Adrian asked.

“Our quiet afternoon’s.”

Luna pushed a page forward with her left hand.

“She’s scheduled the arrangements.”

It was a rota. Elena had the first slot, Viola the second, Luna the third. Isabella and Cecilia followed. Across the top: Accompany Adrian. Twenty minutes each.

Five gazes converged on Cecilia.

“Receiving interviewers,” she added immediately.

“The heading works very hard to conceal that,” Luna said.

“I wasn’t dividing his dates.”

Adrian turned it over. Sources and questions were listed on the back. Three interviewers wanted him present with another person, so someone could take responsibility for the discussion.

“Why do I need someone responsible for me?”

“They’re asking who will take responsibility for your marriage,” Isabella said. “The question has reserved a space for its answer.”

She held a note in her right hand, keeping her burned left wrist clear. It requested confirmation, in her royal coordinating capacity, that the hearing had recognized a historical betrothal. She marked the expired title. Her reply would state that she held no such authority.

Viola indicated her slot with her right hand.

“My dressing is changed then. And I shouldn’t be counted as a visitor’s guard.”

“I can move it,” Cecilia said.

“First ask whether it needs moving,” Elena said. “I won’t answer questions about who deserves to marry him.”

“Neither will I,” Luna agreed. “I only want to know whether they called me an apothecary to make the five of us look more complete.”

Cecilia examined her heading, visibly embarrassed.

“I was afraid one person would end up speaking for everybody. I wanted us each to have the same opportunity.”

“To answer our own questions, yes,” Adrian said. “You don’t need to manufacture equal numbers of interviews. And I don’t need to attach someone to my time before using it.”

After a pause, she nodded.

“We discussed that on the twenty-third. I remember. Today I mixed handling rumors with what’s between us.”

She didn’t tear the schedule up. She marked it unconfirmed, removed its top line, and replaced accompanying him with choosing individually whether to respond.

“Disappointed?” Luna asked, watching the deleted twenty minutes.

“A little. I thought knowing my turn would come might make me less afraid of missing it.”

Nobody laughed.

Adrian sat down. He didn’t want that fear turned into a ticket he must eventually honor. Cecilia put down her pen, looked at him, and finally said she understood.

Viola returned one request. She could discuss her access recommendations, not testify to someone else’s relationship. Luna declined interviews that day, replying only that her public technical paper contained no romantic ranking. Isabella corrected her status. Elena kept an invitation concerning noble exemptions, pending confirmation that ordinary registration applicants could attend.

No replacement five-way division appeared.

Isabella handed Adrian her reply to check only the two sentences concerning him. It claimed no shared position among six people and promised no royal order silencing gossip. He changed unanimous opposition to her own rejection and let her decide whether to accept it.

She read it and signed.

“That sounds rather powerless.”

“You can’t give the printer orders,” Viola said.

“I know. I’m complaining.”

Luna laughed, shifting her cup away from her right palm. Cecilia relaxed and sorted requests by their actual subjects. She set an unverifiable letter aside rather than labeling it reliable because it favored her.

Martha arrived with tea. Watching everyone reclaim their papers, she asked whether the table might finally serve pastries.

“Very soon,” Cecilia said.

“Last time someone said that, another stack arrived.”

She placed the plate in the cleared space. Nobody dared cover it. Adrian bit into a pastry and shed crumbs over the line declaring Cecilia married.

She considered them solemnly.

“It withstands evidence better than the reading room.”

He nearly choked. Elena slid him water, laughing herself.

Then they chose their corrections. Adrian had no current valid betrothal, had accepted no rotating arrangement with five women, and had chosen no future wife at the hearing. Cecilia confirmed that the old record created no present marriage. Elena didn’t explain his feelings; she corrected the claim that her application sought a family exemption.

Each signed their own text for the printer. Cecilia kept the replies separate, with each person’s name attached only to the words that person had actually approved. A wrong profession did not oblige Luna to join a public emotional declaration. Viola and Isabella weren’t added to make a collective statement.

Adrian and Cecilia took the corrections. At the corner, she stopped.

A hat shop displayed the sheet. Two young women discussed it. One said a noblewoman would never really marry a servant. The other suggested the saint simply wanted to keep what was already hers.

Cecilia’s hand tightened.

“We can go in,” Adrian offered.

She shook her head. “First, somewhere that can change the printing.”

She was hurt. Her steps quickened past the window. Adrian followed without promising that everybody would eventually understand.

The print shop smelled of warm ink. The man behind the counter examined them, the sheet, then their signatures. He hadn’t written the headline. He took printing orders; a contributor had delivered the article, claiming to have attended the public inquiry.

Did he have complete, authorized notes to quote?

He did not.

He would accept the correction, but wouldn’t immediately admit deliberate fabrication. He could forward their objection and attach a signed correction to the next printing. Stock already distributed would not all return at once.

“Please don’t describe this as two sides disputing a current marriage,” Adrian said.

He produced the publicly releasable hearing page, not the private attachments. The printer read twice, holding the corner down with a ruler, then acknowledged that the headline’s present-tense claim had no support there.

They settled the correction, retaining the original sheet and a receipt. The printer agreed to post it in his sales window first, then distribute it with the next batch to existing outlets. Further questions about the contributor still required investigation. No convenient list of criminals emerged.

The printer asked who would pay for additional loose copies. Correcting his original batch wasn’t advertising for nobility, but extra quantities required discussion. Cecilia almost volunteered to pay. Instead she glanced at Adrian.

They chose only the agreed correction today. Urgency did not require an immediate financial promise. The relief office’s purchasing funds weren’t spare change because a street story hurt her.

The printer wrote down the quantity. Adrian checked it and kept a copy. The paper arrangements were clearer. Conversation outside had not stopped.

An assistant posted the correction too low, behind a basket of booklets.

“Could it go higher?” Cecilia asked.

Then short people wouldn’t see it, the assistant complained. Adrian asked permission to move the basket, checked that it wouldn’t block the way, and shifted it half a step. An ordinary passerby could finally read the notice.

A customer read it and asked whether Elena had therefore won.

“No,” Adrian said.

The man left, disappointed.

Cecilia laughed at his retreating back.

“We’ve ruined quite a few splendid stories today.”

“They still have my misspelled name.”

“That needs fixing too.”

She returned to the counter to supply the correct spelling. A strand of hair slipped against her cheek as she bent over. Adrian remembered the sentence she had wanted to keep at breakfast. It weighed less than a supposed marriage advertised throughout the street, but it was something real between them.

On the way home, she bought roasted chestnuts and offered him some. They didn’t debate whether this was consolation or add it to a rota. Her first chestnut was spoiled; she threw it away.

“That one has a historical record too,” Adrian said.

“Which doesn’t support present consumption.”

Her solemn answer dissolved into laughter. They ate the rest one each at a time. The bag stayed in her hand, slowly losing its heat.

When they reached the lit house, Elena had received a reply. Tomorrow morning, a neighborhood bookshop would host a limited public discussion in its courtyard, with the owner moderating. It was no noble tribunal. Ordinary applicants could come. Some intended to ask whether she had purchased her servant’s obedience.

She showed Adrian that line without inventing a paid provocateur to explain it.

“I’ll go. You needn’t come to prove I’m good.”

“I’ll consider what I want to say.”

Reading the invitation again, Adrian noticed that the moderator specifically allowed them to decline private questions. He ran a finger along that sentence and asked where she wanted him to sit.

“If you come, somewhere you can hear. Don’t stand behind my chair.”

He pictured that position and nodded. If he stood there looking as though he awaited an order, the arrangement everyone could see would speak before the best statement he could prepare. Taking his own seat was a small thing, but he wanted to do it.

“I won’t reserve you a place in the front, either,” she said.

“Then I’d better arrive early.”

“Have breakfast first.”

She nodded, leaving the letter on the table. Nearby, Cecilia filed her revised response arrangements. The top line remained empty. She did not refill it with anyone’s name.
