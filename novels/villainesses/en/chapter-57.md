# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Two: The Saint Won't Let Me Get Married
### Chapter 57: Get Them Out First

“Don't go down that passage.”

Ward stopped Adrian with an arm across his chest. There was no time for a lecture. She pointed to the thickening smoke above the doorway.

“Tell the crew outside: changing room, second window on the right. They can reach it from the outer passage.”

Adrian ran.

He wasn't the sort of hero who could hold his breath for half an hour. The little smoke he had inhaled already made his chest tight. Outside, he found the municipal rescue crew carrying pry bars and led them around the wall.

Elena handed the main entrance over to another attendant and followed.

“How many?”

“We don't know. We have to move the cart.”

A scenery cart lay crooked across the stone passage, loaded with wooden frames. After admission, someone had pushed it there to block the audience's view of the lighting rig. A wheel had caught in the drainage channel.

A rescuer slid a lever underneath. At his direction, Adrian and two attendants pushed while Elena cleared loose timber from the wheel. Nobody touched the increasingly hot door.

The wheel finally climbed over the stone lip.

They could see the bar now. Someone had added a padlock. The rescuer switched to cutters. The first bite failed. The second broke through with a sharp metallic crack.

Smoke rolled out above them when the door opened. Everyone withdrew behind the line the crew had marked.

Ward called directions from the other side. The responders entered the still-passable outer corridor low to the floor and shifted a fallen garment rack jamming the changing-room door. The coughing suddenly sounded much closer.

A woman clutching a coat emerged first, followed by a hymn seller and a hall attendant. A fourth person came out leaning on the wall, scarcely able to stand.

“Two more!” the attendant said. “One uses a walking frame. Her sister stayed with her!”

Cecilia had reached the outer entrance. She stayed outside, crouching to meet the woman with the coat.

“Can you walk into the courtyard?”

The woman nodded, then shook her head. Tears ran down her face.

“We'll take it slowly.”

Cecilia let the woman lean on her while another volunteer supported her other side. Elena cleared the clothing table out of their path and called for clean blankets in a sheltered part of the yard.

Adrian started toward the fourth evacuee. His chest clenched. He doubled over coughing.

A hand caught his arm.

“This way,” Elena said.

“I can still—”

“I know you can walk. So walk. Leave the stretcher clear.”

He looked up. She had gone very pale.

This wasn't his employer giving orders. She had heard him cough and was frightened enough that her voice had tightened.

He nodded and let her lead him farther into the fresh air.

“Sorry. I was trying to lend a hand.”

“Lend it to me.”

He put his hand in hers. She held it hard, relaxing only when the rescuers brought out the last woman, who could not walk unaided.

All six were out.

The crew checked every room. The hall steward compared the sign-in book with the clothing register and found the two residents who had left separately through another entrance. Nobody said everyone was “probably fine” before the count was complete.

The fire crew isolated the remaining fuel and extinguished the fire. It had consumed the curtain and part of the lamp frame without reaching the neighboring dormitory. Ward closed the backstage area and prohibited anyone from returning for possessions.

Mercer still held his sealed heresy complaint.

When Cecilia passed, he reached toward her.

“The Temple's inquiry isn't over.”

“She's going to see a doctor,” Sabine said, stepping between them.

A hole had burned through her own coat sleeve. She didn't raise her voice.

The municipal officers required Mercer to remain for questioning. His clerk could submit papers. A complaint could not become an arrest order merely because its author wanted one.

Across the courtyard, Adrian saw Cecilia nod to Sabine before sitting at the temporary treatment station.

Her hands had begun to shake.

They had been steady while she supported the woman. Now she couldn't undo her collar. Adrian started forward, but Elena touched his hand.

“The medics are with her.”

He stopped.

Sometimes the hardest part of worrying about someone was admitting he wasn't the person who could help just then.

Dr. Grace Winter arrived with a team from the nearby clinic. She assessed breathing, alertness, and injuries, sending two residents with heavier smoke exposure for hospital observation. Rafe's burned arm was cooled, constricting items removed, and a clean dressing applied before he was sent for further treatment.

No one offered prayer in place of care.

Cecilia was still coughing. Grace kept her for observation. Adrian received follow-up instructions and a particularly blunt warning.

“More coughing, chest tightness, feeling unwell: tell me immediately. This isn't a competition to see who can carry on longest.”

“I wasn't competing.”

Elena and Cecilia both looked at him.

“I was considering it.”

By the time they were allowed to share another bench, their lunches were cold. Elena took out the bag with the fork, opened it, then closed it again.

“Martha will be furious.”

“Because I didn't eat?”

“Because you're considering eating that.” She inspected the sooty edge and handed it to an assistant disposing of contaminated items. “We'll get something fresh.”

A hall attendant brought clean food and warm water. Cecilia took a careful sip before noticing the navy scarf.

“It's dirty.”

“It'll wash,” Elena said.

“I thought you'd be upset.”

“I already was.”

She was looking at Adrian. He abruptly forgot whether he had meant to pick up his cup or his bread.

Cecilia lowered her eyes. Her mouth twitched.

“I've learned that one too.”

“Not today,” Elena said quickly.

“Why?”

“He needs rest. We can't keep shocking him.”

Adrian laughed once, then stopped when his throat hurt. Both women offered water. Both paused.

He chose the cup in front of him.

“I can still pick up a drink.”

“Good,” Cecilia said. “I couldn't, a little while ago.”

The humor settled gently between them.

She studied her hands. “When I pushed the lever, I was afraid I had the wrong one. Then he shouted about the holy object, and for a moment I wondered whether I had any right to touch it.”

“What happened then?” Adrian asked.

“Rafe had gone white with pain. I thought he needed an answer more than the lamp did.” She looked up, eyes reddened. “If I'd stopped to think any longer, someone might have been burned.”

“But you didn't stop,” Elena said softly. “You're allowed to be frightened now.”

Cecilia leaned against the bench and cried. There was nothing luminous about it. She covered her face with a handkerchief, sniffed, then lowered it when it made breathing uncomfortable, as the doctor had instructed.

Adrian offered a clean spare.

“No fork on this one.”

It took her a while to smile, but eventually she accepted it.

“A shame. The fork seemed useful.”

Afterward, she asked to borrow a mirror. Elena took a small case from her bag.

Cecilia examined herself in silence, then rubbed her nose.

“Other side,” Adrian said.

She rubbed farther away.

“Not that side. It's—”

“Mr. Reed,” Elena interrupted, “this is exactly how you help me look for hairpins.”

With a clean corner of cloth, she indicated the mark. Cecilia finally wiped it off. They studied the mirror together, then turned toward him.

Adrian developed an unpleasant suspicion.

“You have some too,” Elena said.

“Where?”

“Other side.”

Cecilia covered her mouth. Tears still shone in her eyes, but her shoulders shook with laughter.

He decided to borrow the mirror.

The woman they had helped came to thank them, still gripping the coat. Her sister had been sent for observation. She kept meaning to hand the coat over, she explained, but all she could think of was how easily her sister got cold.

Cecilia asked the transfer assistant to take it and urged the woman to check with the medics about her own care. After she left, Cecilia quietly asked Grace whether today's emergency had disrupted Mercy Clinic's regular work.

“The duty staff are there. Jonah's treatment doesn't stop because this happened. Elliot's own records are still under his corrected identity.”

Cecilia nodded. At last she stopped trying to stand.

“Is it hard to leave things to other people?” Adrian asked.

“I'm afraid someone will be overlooked if I'm not there.”

“Then let the people responsible know you trust them,” Elena said. “I'm learning too.”

She put the cup she'd been holding back within Adrian's reach.

“Starting with letting him drink his own water.”

Later, Ward admitted an independent technician to the cooled backstage area. Sabine and a municipal recorder witnessed the examination. They sketched everything in place before numbering the removed components.

Inside the apparently solid lamp base was a second set of lenses and a high-output oil jet. A thin copper wire ran around the frame to the operating lever and controlled a projection shutter. The hand and wheat on the screen had been painted with material that glowed briefly after illumination. Opening the shutter let the concealed strong light make the image appear almost suspended in the air.

There was no personal-mark connection and no transfer ring. It was a stage effect presented as a divine reply.

The danger came from the excessive flame, the drape added over the exhaust hood, and the locked exit.

Sabine didn't invite them backstage. She brought the releasable findings into the courtyard and showed them a scale drawing of the mechanism.

Adrian followed the wire to the lever.

“So the hand didn't vanish because Cecilia said anything.”

“The light weakened after the fuel was cut. The shutter also slipped out of position. The operator's account matches the components.”

Rafe had been questioned separately at the treatment station. He admitted operating the projection. He had originally been told it was a symbolic image accompanying the prayers. Before the display, Mercer had demanded brighter light and additional blackout cloth, and forbidden him to call it a demonstration in front of the audience.

Rafe had written a warning about the exhaust on the back of the running order. The clerk had signed for it. The paper was in the folder Sabine had removed, its oily edge scorched yellow, the warning legible.

“His account alone won't settle everyone's responsibility,” Sabine said. “But it isn't standing alone.”

“Who ordered the door locked?” Elena asked.

“The attendant names Mercer. We'll compare his account with the spoken instructions, the addendum, and the outer-passage duty record.”

At last, some questions could be followed back through human hands instead of thrown at the sky.

The folder also held a C-19 completion summary prepared for the reassurance speech. Its reference led to a later supplement. Sabine sent the number to independent records staff, requesting the corresponding dispatch receipt from the sending office rather than relying on the hall copy alone.

Near evening, Vera arrived with notice of an urgent review the following morning. It would address the validity of Mercer's referral and the threat of unlawful detention. Responsibility for the fire would be investigated separately.

Cecilia's first question was where tomorrow's winter-clothing distribution would happen.

“The school's covered playground,” Elena said. “I've spoken to the person in charge. The volunteers will move everything.”

“What about the chestnut cake?” Adrian asked.

“You finally remembered it?”

“I promised I'd come back for it.”

“The cake can wait. Have your follow-up first.”

Cecilia put down her empty cup.

“So you do listen sometimes.”

“Very occasionally. Treasure the experience.”

She smiled and reached out with a hand that still trembled slightly, brushing a scrap of ash from his shoulder. That was all. She didn't turn the lingering fear into an embrace or tell him she owed him her life.

“I'm glad,” she said, “that you didn't go down that smoky passage.”

“So am I,” Elena added.

Their voices agreed completely.

Coming back alive, Adrian discovered, wasn't necessarily a magnificent declaration of victory.

Sometimes it simply meant someone would still be waiting tomorrow for him to finish a piece of cake.
