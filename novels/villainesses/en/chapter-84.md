# Chapter 84: Drawing Swords Won't Tell Them Who the Enemy Is

On November 29, Owen waited outside the inn for a long time before finally taking off his right glove and knocking a second time.

When Viola came downstairs, he straightened, then remembered that she was still suspended. His hand froze halfway to his forehead.

“You can just say good morning,” she told him.

“Good morning, Captain.”

“Good morning.”

The awkwardness made Adrian smile. Then Owen noticed the two scarves in his hands, and a look of despairing comprehension crossed his face, as though he had chosen the wrong place to stand somewhere even more dangerous than the supply warehouse.

“I can come back later.”

“This one dried upstairs, so I brought it down.” Adrian lifted one scarf. “The other one's mine. Neither of them requires you to leave.”

Viola took hers. The previous day, she had gone to the council building to give information about the duty personnel she knew, and rain had soaked her on the way back. The landlady had hung the scarf beside the stove outside Adrian's room. That was all the explanation needed, but Owen was already giving the carpet his undivided attention.

“Actually,” Viola said slowly, “if there ever is something you should leave us alone for, I'll tell you.”

The scarf in Adrian's hand slipped several inches.

This time, she was the one who looked at the carpet.

Owen finally remembered why he had come. He brought an account of the previous night's confrontation at the warehouse. Two of Ward's guards had orders to secure the relevant dispatch registers. The garrison duty officer, however, maintained that the originals also contained routine military supply arrangements and that no one could take them without Major Voss's personal approval. Captain Morrow had never appeared. He had only sent a clerk to say that the ledgers needed to go to West Slope first for checking.

“Our people think the outside committee wants to carry off the whole supply warehouse,” Owen said. “The review team thinks we're hiding things for Morrow. Someone's already said they can't come in unless they surrender their swords.”

“Did anyone draw one?”

“No. The colonel eventually ordered both sides three paces back and sealed the doors until morning. The major was inspecting the night defenses at the north gate. He's only just returned.”

Viola asked whether delivering this statement was part of Owen's assigned duty. He nodded and produced the delivery sheet. After reading it, she pointed toward the council building.

“Take it there. My name shouldn't be a shortcut around your current commanding officer.”

His lips moved.

“But if you were still—”

“I'd still need to find out what was inside the warehouse and what orders the people outside had received.”

She pointed out his upturned collar without reaching to fasten it for him. The young corporal straightened it himself. At last, he no longer looked like a deserter secretly seeking out his former commander.

By that morning, the knights outside the council building were already standing in two groups.

Some were there because they knew Viola. Some disliked the committee. Some simply thought outsiders should not be questioning their fellow soldiers. Others had personally seen the false transfer order the night before and believed that if the supply office were allowed to keep delaying, they would not even be able to protect Martin in his hospital bed.

Both groups spoke quietly. Somehow, that made the steps seem narrower.

As Viola walked between them, someone called her rank. She did not stop to inspect them, or mistake the gap between their familiar faces for a place that belonged to her.

Adrian walked beside her. The cold wind tightened his chest, and he slowed. She slowed with him, until someone behind them impatiently asked why they had not gone inside yet.

“Because the stairs can't do his breathing for him,” Viola said over her shoulder.

The man fell silent.

“She has learned to skip the lengthy explanations,” Elena murmured to Adrian.

“At the cost of one man on the steps being afraid to speak.”

“A cost I wholeheartedly approve of.”

Inside, Major Voss and Colonel Ward sat facing each other. Each had signed orders on the table. Neither intended to put the papers away merely because the other raised their voice.

“The warehouse also handles tonight's rotations and provisions,” Voss said. “I won't let an investigation shut it down.”

“My preservation order specifies three dispatch registers, the stubs for blank passage permits, and the N26 settlement attachments.” Ward pushed a copy toward him. “Why does the version your people received contain only the part saying no documents may be moved?”

The duty officer looked down at his copy.

It was not a matter of missing the fine print. He had never received the second page.

That page required routine supply work to continue at a separate desk. Garrison personnel and the review team were to jointly record the removal of the relevant originals. Military deployment records unrelated to the case were to remain where they were.

The delivery clerk from Morrow's office was summoned. He said he had received only the first page. The receiving office, however, had recorded two pages fastened together. Red-eyed, the clerk insisted that he had not removed one. Ward did not let the room's anger supply the next name for him. She ordered his copy retained and the timing of the delivery investigated separately.

But the confrontation at the doors had already changed its shape.

Voss read the full order twice. Then he personally signed instructions to assist with retrieving the records, while maintaining restrictions on who could hear discussion of the military supply information involved.

“My men will keep guarding the north gate,” he said. “Anyone invoking the regiment to block access to those three registers can explain how that squares with this new order.”

A long breath was released outside.

It did not make everyone like one another. An older knight passed Owen and muttered that he had bowed to outsiders. Owen flushed scarlet. His hand went to his sword hilt, then dragged itself away.

“I went to the clinic and saw that carriage and the damaged courtyard gate,” he said. “That carriage wasn't there to protect our honor.”

Viola heard him from beside a column. She waited until the older knight had gone before approaching.

“You didn't draw.”

“I thought about it.”

“I know.”

Owen looked down at his hand. “I always thought that when it really took courage, someone would tell me which side the enemy was on.”

Viola looked toward the warehouse. Among the people emerging were comrades she had once shared meals with and investigators who had asked her difficult questions only yesterday.

“Perhaps today the first thing is to hold back. To avoid making enemies of people before we have to.”

Retrieval of the records finally began that afternoon. With Dr. Shaw's permission, Martin's follow-up questioning also went ahead, divided into two sessions with ample rest between them. He confirmed that after the thirteenth, someone had brought him an empty-rack settlement form to sign after the fact. The visitor had worn a supply-office coat, but Martin did not know Morrow personally. When the investigator showed him several ordinary uniform styles, he said the cuff buttons resembled two of them. He could not narrow it down further.

Bellamy recorded the inconvenient answer exactly as he gave it.

Meanwhile, the carriage firm's bookkeeper produced another reservation ledger. The carriage used in the false patient collection on the twenty-eighth and two box wagons booked for today had all had their deposits paid by the same supply-office errand runner. Today's destination was not West Slope Depot. It was the freight shed at the old mill beyond the south bridge.

That road led directly toward the county boundary.

Ward sent word to the south bridge checkpoint to register the vehicles' identifying numbers and verify their passage permits. Regular defense of the north gate remained Voss's responsibility. At last, neither side was handing control of every gate in Greymarch to a vague rumor simply to investigate one supply officer.

While they waited for news, Elena brought lunch into a side room.

Viola looked at the hot meat wrap on her plate and first asked whether any had been provided for the knights on duty. Elena tapped the edge of her plate with the handle of a fork.

“Theirs are downstairs. This is called lunch, not a test of loyalty.”

Adrian had already picked up his own. Juice promptly dripped from the far end onto the paper.

“It fights back.”

Holding in a laugh, Viola showed him how to fold the bottom closed. He followed her instructions and finally stopped feeding the table first.

“I used to eat these on horseback,” she said.

“I'm not managing very well on a chair.”

“You don't have to be better at everything than I am.”

She said it naturally. Only afterward did she realize that her hand had stopped beside his paper wrapper, so close that the slightest movement would bring them into contact. She did not use the folding lesson as an excuse to touch him. Instead, she withdrew her hand and went back to her own lunch.

But Adrian asked, “Could you show me again? Next time, I'd like to wrap one for you that doesn't leak.”

She looked at him, her lips slowly curving upward. “That depends on whether you remember this time.”

Elena leaned back in her chair and sighed deliberately. “I knew bringing lunch would give someone else an opening.”

“You can take mine.”

“Who wants something you've bitten?”

Before he could answer, she took the clean napkin in front of him and used it to wipe her uninjured hand.

“This will do.”

Cecilia arrived a little later, when only half a warm flatbread remained. She sat, first finished the water she had forgotten to drink earlier, then took a half-written letter from her pocket.

Mira had asked whether Greymarch had any pretty little things, preferably something other than another stack of papers to check. Cecilia looked around the table and earnestly asked for suggestions.

“Walnut shells?” Adrian offered.

“If you can put them back together, I'd be happy to send them,” Elena said.

After a moment's thought, Viola mentioned the little wooden bird whistles sold at the mill market.

“Can you play one?” Cecilia asked.

She nodded, then added, “I used to.”

The landlady's son arrived just then to collect the dishes. Hearing the conversation, he took a whistle he had bought from his pocket and asked her to try it. Viola wiped it clean with a cloth and blew a tentative note.

It sounded like an extremely angry duck.

The room went silent for half a heartbeat. Adrian covered his mouth. It did not help. Elena turned toward the window, laughing, while the letter shook in Cecilia's hand.

Viola stared at the whistle. Finally, she laughed, too.

“I did say I used to.”

“You could use it to organize a duck evacuation,” Adrian said.

“Then you'd better get somewhere safe first.”

She returned the whistle to the boy. Her face was still red, but embarrassment did not make her sit up like a suit of armor again. Later, when they discussed the best food stalls, she even managed to mention that she liked her candied apples a little tart.

Adrian remembered that without taking out a pen.

After lunch, Viola walked with him to the sheltered courtyard behind the building. He was looking for a bench where the wind would not chill his chest. She noticed the meat juice on the end of his scarf first.

“Apparently it never learned to surrender completely,” she said.

He looked down, regarding the dark stain with considerable regret. She took a handkerchief from her pocket, asked his permission, then turned over one end of the scarf and gently dabbed it with the slightly damp cloth.

He neither leaned closer nor moved away. With her head bent, Viola abruptly realized that this made it harder to breathe than being asked her name before the hearing panel.

“It doesn't matter if you can't get it out,” he said.

“I know. I just… wanted to try.”

Only when she finished did she realize how long they had been quiet together. A horse snorted beyond the courtyard wall. Upstairs, a chair scraped across the floor. He looked down at her, and there was no impatience in his eyes, no expectation that she provide another useful opinion.

“Why have you been looking at me so much today?” she finally asked.

Adrian blinked. “Because you've kept smiling since you played that whistle. I haven't seen you like this very often.”

Her face warmed again, but she did not excuse it by saying everyone else had been funny.

“Then you can look a little longer.”

Adrian forgot what he had been about to say. For once, Viola seemed to have won something between them. She pressed her lips together and tucked away her handkerchief.

A moment later, she could no longer hold back her own laugh. He laughed with her. When his breathing grew a little short, he sat on the bench. She sat beside him and let that small stretch of afternoon remain undisturbed, with no one calling either of them away.

At dusk, a rider from the south bridge hurried into the courtyard.

The two wagons with the registered numbers had not stopped at the freight shed. At the checkpoint, one had been found carrying an undeclared document chest and told to wait for verification. Its six armed escorts insisted on crossing the bridge that night. They had already rammed the first movable barrier crooked.

Worse, a line of civilian wagons delivering bedding to the temporary housing was trapped at the bridge approach.

Ward sent reinforcements at once. The regular sergeant at the south bridge had injured his leg in a fall that morning. His temporary replacement knew only how to admit vehicles according to the duty schedule. Faced with supply escorts and civilians crowded together, he had sent two contradictory requests in succession.

Viola stood in the doorway, looking at the civilian road map with the south bridge marked on it.

She knew which side of the approach had room for wagons to back away. She knew the small riverside gate could let people on foot get clear. And she knew it had been many days since she had been allowed to give any knight an order.

“Colonel,” she said, “if you need me, I can hold the bridge approach. But I can't lift a shield with my left arm, and I can't lead a pursuit.”

Ward looked at her, then asked the clerk for a blank order sheet.

“Then let's start by writing down exactly what you can do.”
