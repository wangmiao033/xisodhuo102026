# Chapter 89: Today, the Knight Just Wants to Win a Wooden Bird

On December 5, ribbons for the winter celebration went up again over the mill market.

Three faded strips of red fluttered beside one bright blue one. The roofers had borrowed the ribbon that used to hang there, so the stallholders had searched their trunks for anything they could spare. This year's celebration would end half a day early. Once the stalls closed, the square was needed for wagons hauling timber.

At the end of the bridge, Viola handed Adrian two ring-toss tickets.

“What are these?”

“Your assignment for today.”

He looked at her.

“An invitation,” she amended at once.

“Does the assignment pay?”

“If you win, there's a wooden bird.”

Adrian surveyed the stall ten paces away. The largest bird perched on the highest shelf, its painted plumage looking extremely pleased with itself. The wooden rings beneath it were so small that he suspected a feud between the ring maker and the bird maker.

“I think some reconnaissance is in order.”

“I already asked. We aren't allowed to inspect them up close, or use a sword.”

“You asked about the sword?”

The tips of Viola's ears reddened. “The stallholder saw me and volunteered that part.”

She wore dark green civilian clothes today, with only a coin pouch at her waist. Her left arm still needed protection from sudden strain, so she had chosen something she could play with her right hand. She had also given serious thought to Adrian's need to avoid standing too long. But once she reached the stall, the first thing she forgot was the relaxed expression she had practiced.

Her first ring struck the wooden bird squarely on the head and bounced back.

The second sailed past its beak and caught on the stallholder's cuff button.

He unhooked it and spread his hands. “That one's not a prize. My wife would come looking.”

Adrian laughed so hard he had to brace himself on the counter.

“Excellent aim. Your choice of target needs work.”

“Your turn.”

His ring missed the bird by a considerable margin. Instead, it landed neatly around the earthenware money bowl at the corner of the table.

“Choice of target?” Viola asked.

“Professional instinct.”

This time she made no attempt to hide her laughter. People nearby joined in, and no one ordered her to fall into formation or asked her to explain an evacuation she had already described far too many times. She bought two more rings and chose a smaller bird on the shelf below, its head tilted to one side.

Her final ring dropped over it and stuck around the widest part of its body.

The stallholder picked up the bird, ring and all, and handed it over. “I'll count that. You can work out how to get it off at home.”

Viola cradled it for a long moment.

“It looks as though it's been taken prisoner.”

“Perhaps it's just wearing the wrong size,” Adrian said.

She put the bird in her bag with half its head peeking out. When they went to buy hot wheat cakes, Elena was at another table checking timber delivery prices with two merchants. Cecilia was retrieving a roll of paper the wind had stolen from the clinic's charity stall.

Elena looked up and saw the bird's head.

“Is that today's escort?”

“I won it,” Viola said.

“Then tell it to keep a close eye on him. He sometimes uses receipts as bookmarks.”

Adrian opened his mouth to object, remembered that Elena had caught him with that carriage-fare receipt in his travel book just last night, and went to buy a fourth cake instead.

They carried their food over to a ribbon-draped post. The elderly woman selling the cakes gave Viola an extra pinch of sugar for hers, then asked if she could read a letter. Her younger sister, who had married and moved to another county, had sent it with a coachman. There was no military intelligence inside. Only a recently sold pig, a pair of shoes bought too small, and the question of whether she might come home for a few days that winter.

Viola read it more slowly than she would an order. At the shoe size, she paused and asked the woman to fetch the wooden foot measure she kept at home, so they could be sure what to put in the reply.

“So you can read this sort of thing too,” the woman said.

“This is the best sort.”

Then she noticed Adrian smiling and handed him the pen to write the answer.

At the end, he asked whether they should add that she missed her sister. The old woman wiped her hands on her apron twice. Her voice grew suddenly quieter.

“Tell her if she can't come now, spring will do. It's a long way. Don't rush her.”

Viola looked at the letter, worn along its folds, and pressed the reply's seal closed for her. The woman tried to give her another two cakes. Viola held up the wooden bird to demonstrate that her hands were already full, and all three of them laughed.

The musicians were beginning to tune when a dull blow sounded from the mill's grain shed behind them. Someone shouted for the workers to stop prying.

Viola turned. Two masons inside the construction rope had laid down their iron bars. A drainage channel cracked by the frost two days earlier needed relaying, and they had tipped up one side of the long threshold stone, whose dark edge had always shown beneath the shed door. Its underside was not the smooth-cut surface they had expected. Fine grooves covered it, packed with mud.

The foreman had already moved the onlookers back. Viola stopped outside the rope and asked whether they needed someone from the town offices.

“Whoever looks after old things.” He pointed at the stone. “If that counts as an antiquity, I'd rather not owe anyone a temple next year.”

Adrian stayed outside too. Along the narrow exposed edge, he could see part of a curve. A small gap separated two grooves, as though someone had left a space on the stone for something else to fit.

He fetched Cecilia.

She crouched beyond the rope. After only a brief look, she asked the foreman to stop using the wet brush.

“There may be loose flakes in the mud. Let's have whoever will be responsible for its safekeeping look first.”

“Will this keep us from opening today?” the grain-shed owner asked.

The custodian who arrived from the town offices offered no comforting promises. He asked the masons to secure the raised stone and have goods carried through the side entrance until they could inspect its edges and decide where to move it. The foreman calculated the longer carrying distance, frowned, and went to have two empty barrels shifted.

The celebration did not stop for them. A violin played a wrong note in the street. The honey seller kept calling to customers. A timber wagon waited by the bridge for the road to clear.

Adrian went around to the side door and found a young worker trying to lift a sack of wheat alone. He could not help carry it, so he held the door and called another worker over. As the two passed him, husks spilled onto his shoes.

Cecilia was still studying the stone.

The catalog of antiquities the custodian had brought offered no ready answer. The shed owner, however, remembered his grandfather saying the threshold had come from a contract registration pavilion demolished long ago. Some of the same stone might also have been used in the west wall.

“That's all I remember him saying,” he added. “I couldn't tell you what year they pulled it down.”

Viola looked toward the mill, which had been reroofed three times. She had passed it more often than she could count, without ever wondering what words lay beneath the doorway.

Adrian asked the custodian to record the owner's recollection first, then have someone look for the building accounts. An oral account could give them somewhere to start. It could not turn a grandfather's story into a demolition record.

By early afternoon, the town offices had sent over a construction expenditure ledger more than forty years old. Between the entries for demolition and purchased materials, a clerk found the pavilion's name. There was also a long stone delivered to the grain shed: approximately the right length and width, with an angled piece missing at one end.

The masons checked it with a measuring cord. The length differed by half a finger's breadth. Examining the layer of mortar added later, the custodian decided to include the discrepancy in the drawing and measure again after cleaning.

“Worth preserving, at least,” he said.

Before they moved it, one mason asked what they were supposed to use for the new threshold. The stone he had brought was a little too short; he had planned to reuse the long one. If it was now being preserved, the grain-shed doorway could not simply remain unfinished.

After conferring with the foreman, the custodian arranged for a replacement from the town's surplus road-repair stone. The extra work would be logged and paid for at actual cost. The owner agreed to stay that evening to help, and some of the tension finally left his face.

Viola realized that when they had gathered around those few carved lines, none of them had first asked how grain was supposed to be unloaded here tomorrow. She borrowed a broom and, using her right hand, swept loose stones aside at the side entrance. Adrian held the dustpan. They worked slowly, but a handcart could finally pass through the doorway without catching.

“If every word you decipher costs me another day with a blocked door,” the owner said, “I'll have to charge that stone rent.”

“It must have considerable experience avoiding payment.” Adrian glanced at the forty-year-old ledger. “It's been lying in your doorway long enough.”

The long stone was slowly turned upright on a wooden support. A large section of writing had been chiseled from its reverse. The few surviving words lay in two groups, one above the other. The upper fragment still read “border mutual aid.” Below were “linked registration” and part of the word “certificate.”

Cecilia took out the drawing of the lining she was authorized to carry. Keeping both sheets on the table, she compared it with the new sketch of the stone. The outer curves ran in a similar direction, but the inner notches differed. Two thin channels crossed the middle of the stone, and their dimensions did not fit the fragment of lining they had.

“Are they the same sort of thing?” Viola asked.

“They may belong to related craft traditions,” Cecilia said. “I'd need the full historical instructions.”

Adrian studied the surviving words. Mutual aid ought to have been reassuring. Repairing a bridge together in winter, shifting grain, leaving a lamp lit for someone unable to get home: all of that belonged under those words. Yet now his first thought was of an old registration assigning risk to a particular name.

He did not share that fear with the stone.

“Does the catalog list any surviving documents from the pavilion?” he asked.

The custodian promised to check the old index in the morning. Today, they could only record where the stone had been found and how it had been turned. The masons would take it to a dry shed borrowed by the town offices. It would remain there, rather than joining some future wagon journey of their own.

Adrian wrote down the time they could return the following day and passed the note to Cecilia. When he looked up, Viola was gazing at the wooden bird peeking out of her bag.

“Have we used up the whole day again?” he asked.

“Not yet.” There was a rare note of stubbornness in her voice. “They haven't lit the lanterns.”

He glanced at the stone, now safely secured, and said goodbye to the custodian.

The square was quiet in the dusk. Repair timber awaiting collection stood in front of several empty stalls. A woman pointed out one beam to her companion: it would become the frame for her new front door. What had been lost was still lost. But someone had brought out hot soup, and at last there were enough seats.

Viola and Adrian sat on the same bench. She placed the bird between them and tried to work off its ring. It was firmly stuck. After three attempts to turn it free, all she had managed was to turn the bird's head toward him.

“It looks as though it's blaming me.”

“You could ask whether it wants to stay.”

“It hasn't answered.”

“Then perhaps hold off on assigning it a patrol.”

She laughed and decided to take it to a carpenter tomorrow. Adrian moved his soup to his other side, leaving the bird somewhere it would not get wet.

The musicians began a slow tune. In the center of the square, people joined hands and turned in a circle. The steps were simple; anyone who went wrong followed the person beside them. Viola glanced over twice before she finally asked, “Would you like to dance?”

“One slow round, yes.”

She offered her right hand. He took it, and they joined the edge of the dancers. She did not count out every step. When he turned the wrong way, she gave him a gentle reminder. Then she made a mistake herself, and they nearly spun together into the apple stall.

“I thought you'd be good at following commands,” he said.

“There aren't any commands here.”

“Should I call some?”

“No.”

She answered so quickly that they both laughed.

At the end of the tune, he said he needed to sit. She went back to the bench with him, though her foot kept a soft, reluctant trace of the rhythm. Elena brought over hot drinks, handing one to him and the other to Viola.

“How was the dancing?”

“We nearly bought some apples,” he said.

Elena glanced toward the stall at the edge of the square. “That sounds like your footwork.”

Cecilia arrived with two small cakes left from the charity sale, which the four of them shared. She had already arranged her handover with the clinic. For the rest of the day, her teacup could stay where it was; she would not move it aside to write another receipt.

When the final paper lantern was lit, Viola took the wooden bird from her bag and settled it on her knees. Looking at the ring that refused to slip free of its neck, she suddenly pressed her lips together against a laugh.

“What is it?” Adrian asked.

“I just realized the only prisoner I'm taking home today is one I paid to capture.”

She laughed for a long time. In the lantern light, the little bird with the tilted head seemed at last to have accepted its lot, resting quietly in her hands.

Back at the inn, Viola went to find a box that would keep the bird from getting crushed. Adrian brought the travel book to the fire and waited for Elena to change out of her outdoor clothes and come downstairs.

“I remembered our plans for tonight,” he said.

She sat beside him and rested one hand between them, palm up. He gently took her uninjured hand, and the pages began turning a little more slowly.

“That bird is very sweet,” she said.

“I'm more interested in whether you found anything you wanted to buy.”

“A scarf. Dreadful colors, but it felt wonderfully warm.”

“You didn't buy it?”

“I did. I'll show you tomorrow. No laughing in advance.”

He promised, though the corners of his mouth had already begun to rise. She smiled too and pulled the book half an inch closer. By the same lamplight, they went on reading about how the captain lost his second compass.
