# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Three: The Knight Says She's Responsible for Me
### Chapter 67: No One Was an Extra Passenger

Before they left the oil press, Adrian Reed turned over the tag on the last wagon.

Six on one side. An elderly woman's surname on the other. She insisted she couldn't board it. Her grandson was in wagon three, and the family's cooking pot was in wagon five.

“We can bring your grandson here,” Adrian said.

“What about the pot?”

He looked at the conspicuous black pot. Before the driver could speak, Elena had asked wagon five to move closer and called two workers over.

“The pot comes, too. When you make soup, please put in a little extra water.”

The woman hesitated, then agreed. Adrian drew a short line beside her name and registered grandmother and grandson in the same wagon.

He couldn't run or carry heavy things today. Fortunately, the main difficulty in counting people rather than sacks of grain usually had little to do with weight.

Tobin Quill, the town transport clerk, was waiting at the south gate to receive the convoy. Here, the drivers followed the arrangement he'd drawn up beforehand. The seriously wounded who'd arrived first were already at the front, with walking relatives in the middle. The final two wagons took later arrivals, elderly people and patients who needed a slower pace, watched over by Viola's patrol. The damaged high bridge lay behind them on another branch of the road. Nobody needed to gamble on how many more people it could hold.

Adrian took an outer seat on a wagon in the middle, where he could see everyone who kept looking back for family whenever the road curved. Resting his register on his knees, he told the driver he'd hand it over if his cough grew bad. They wouldn't need to stop.

“I can read,” the driver said. “My writing's ugly.”

“That should be enough today. I haven't time to practice my calligraphy, either.”

The driver eased his reins and gave a quiet laugh.

Beyond the stone courtyard, the wind was colder. Tree shadows striped the road like shallow ditches. Less than fifteen minutes into their journey, the lead wagon stopped on a narrow slope. People behind immediately tensed. Had the enemy ridden around them?

It was a goat.

The animal refused to leave a roadside bush. Its owner refused to abandon the rope tied behind the wagon. Between them, they had immobilized the entire column. Adrian was about to ask someone to untie it when a woman with a walking stick produced half a carrot and waved it in front of the cart.

The goat moved.

“Always easier to persuade than my husband,” she said.

Few people laughed aloud, but some loosened their grip on the wagon rails. Elena glanced over from the carriage window on the other side, a small smile in her eyes.

The next stretch wasn't so easily managed.

A young man discovered his father wasn't among the relatives walking with him and turned to run back. Adrian stopped him, asked his name, and remembered an older man who'd boarded one of the patient wagons alone. He found the surname on the first page and sent word forward.

The young man kept staring at the smoke behind them, refusing to believe it.

Then a voice from up ahead shouted his childhood nickname, followed by “Stay where you are, and stop running off!” He bent over the wagon rail. Adrian let him wipe his face before showing him the tag.

“Number two. Second wagon from the front. A sergeant will take you there when the road widens.”

The young man touched the words again and again, as if afraid they might move.

After noon, the convoy reached the narrowest part of the lower river road. Water lay on one side, and autumn rain had softened the foot of the slope. The patrol dismounted to guide everyone toward the inner edge. A rear stretcher wagon's left wheel sank half an inch. Its driver dared not force the horse forward. Viola had two soldiers lay short timbers while the others escorted the mobile wagons ahead, rather than trapping the whole column there.

That was when Major Voss's messenger arrived from town.

He dismounted on wider ground and presented a sealed written instruction. The duty officer witnessed Viola accept it. When she'd finished reading, her eyes remained on the wagon still stuck in the mud.

Her patrol was ordered back to Greymarch to reinforce the gates.

Rusk's defenders were preparing for riders who might circle around. The major couldn't know whether the attack on the village was a diversion. His concerns had weight. An entire town stood behind them.

The two patients on the wagon couldn't even sit up.

“Evacuation was already underway when the order arrived,” Viola told the officer. “Record our location and the wagon's condition.”

She didn't tear up the paper or ask the messenger to pretend he'd arrived later.

“Four riders from the forward group will return with you. I'll bring the rear escort and the last wagon. Once it's free, we'll withdraw by the same road. Tell the major our return is delayed because wounded passengers are still on an exposed stretch.”

“The major said the whole patrol.”

“I understand.”

She signed her explanation on the back of the receipt and handed it over. The messenger didn't argue again. He left with the four riders. After the hoofbeats faded, she remained still a moment before crouching beside the bad patch to inspect the timbers.

Adrian didn't ask whether it was worth it. She knew perfectly well whose desk that paper would reach.

At last, the wagon shifted. The wheel creaked against the timber with a sound that set his teeth on edge. One of the young patients clutched his bedding in pain while an attendant quietly assured him they would soon be across.

On wider ground, Adrian asked Viola whether he should move to the inner seat beneath the carriage hood.

She looked at the northern slope and nodded.

“There's an exposed stretch ahead. Would that be all right?”

“Yes. You don't need to make it a twenty-four-hour arrangement.”

Understanding came, and her shoulders eased a little.

“Only as far as the gate.”

This time, they understood one another.

At a rest point, Viola finally dismounted and drank a cup of warm water standing up. A little boy who'd been crying earlier approached with a dusty chestnut.

“I only have one,” he said.

She crouched to accept it and thanked him with all the gravity of receiving a contribution from the town council. The boy looked puzzled when she simply held it.

“Don't you know how to peel it?”

Adrian tried to hide his smile behind his cup. Viola glanced at him, placed the chestnut in her palm, and attempted to remove the shell with her gloved fingers. She failed.

“Her gloves are too thick,” Adrian explained.

The boy reached out again. “I'll help.”

The chestnut turned in his small hands. The hard shell cracked. He put the kernel back in Viola's palm, then returned to his mother, satisfied. Viola ate it. At last, the faintest smile touched her mouth.

“That part doesn't need recording,” she told Adrian.

“Too late. I'll remember it for a long time.”

She looked up. He meant to add another joke, but he saw the weariness in her eyes and stopped. Instead, he nudged the low stool beside him toward her.

She sat for a little while as the groom watered her horse. No one asked her to make a decision in those few moments. Adrian didn't produce a fresh question.

“You handled that man well,” she said quietly. “Having him stay where he was and wait for his father.”

“I happened to remember the name.”

“You cared enough to remember.”

It was quieter than her earlier praise of his retreating skills. Before remounting, she remembered to return her empty cup, rather than automatically stowing it like equipment in her saddlebags.

Elena made room for him on half her cushion.

“What were you laughing about?”

“Captain Blackwood encountered a particularly difficult chestnut.”

“So I missed a fierce engagement.”

Her voice was light. Her gaze lingered briefly in the direction Viola had gone, then moved away. Adrian settled into the seat and deliberately set the register on his other side.

“Will you sit with me for a little while when we're back?”

“I'm sitting with you now.”

“With no lists, no patients, and no cooking pots waiting for me to relocate them.”

She studied him. Her fingers slowly relaxed.

“Yes.” After a pause, she added, “I may fall asleep first.”

“I'll make sure that doesn't go in the record.”

As they passed through the final stand of willows, someone began quietly crying beside the convoy. It was the woman with the wooden basin. She took out a fire-cracked spoon and tried over and over to fit its broken edges together. Her hands shook harder each time.

A neighbor began telling her that at least everyone was safe, then stopped. The woman's house, bed, and the cloth she'd saved for years remained somewhere in the smoke. Surviving had not made them unimportant.

Elena offered a dry handkerchief through the window. The woman didn't take it. After a while, she carefully put away the spoon and reached for it.

Adrian looked down at his register. There was an empty line waiting. He didn't turn what he'd seen into an encouraging note about high spirits. He simply added another mark beside the stopping arrangements, somewhere an attendant would notice it.

Greymarch's south gate finally appeared ahead in the afternoon light. Adrian had expected relief. Instead, the sight of the hot-water buckets waiting beneath the arch nearly made him cry. He blamed the cold wind and said nothing.

Tobin sat behind a long table, his injured hand on a cushion. His assistant checked the passengers wagon by wagon. When they reached the grandmother and grandson's changed seating, he didn't complain about the untidy alterations. He reconciled the two entries.

The spare guildhall Elena had rented already had its fires lit. The cost went to her personal account. Magistrate Ada Finch assigned local attendants and night watch staff. Elena paid the rent; the accommodation team decided who went in which bed.

One man assumed clean clothes meant she was assigning rooms and stopped her to request a private one. She took him to an attendant. Once they understood he coughed at night and feared disturbing others, she asked him to let a doctor examine him. Then she stayed to help find a bed near the aisle, where carers could reach him easily.

Her hand was aching. After signing the last rental slip, she put down her pen. Adrian noticed and gathered the unorganized papers, sorting them by destination. She thanked him quietly without insisting on working another fifteen minutes.

A merchant tentatively asked whether they should hang the Rosenberg crest over the entrance. Elena glanced at an old man leaning against the doorway to catch his breath.

“A notice saying whether the rooms are warm would be more useful.”

The merchant didn't ask again. He went to help carry benches.

Ellis was taken to a separate side room at Dr. Shaw's infirmary, with guards at the door. Wounded soldiers and villagers used another entrance. Someone recognized the blue cloth removed from his shoulder and demanded that they drag him out. A wounded villager raised a hand to restrain his companion.

“Let him live. I've got questions.”

Dr. Shaw closed the door, keeping only the necessary attendants inside. Questions would wait until she judged that the patient could bear them.

Owen and the duty officer then delivered the blue cloth, freight wrapping, and saddlebags to the storeroom designated by Ada. With his assistant's help, Tobin checked the item count against the records kept on the road. Villagers described separately what they'd witnessed. Nobody asked them to make their stories agree simply to produce a tidier account.

Adrian listened briefly, then yielded his place to people who'd actually seen the attack. In his own notes, he distinguished sounds heard behind a courtyard wall from things he'd witnessed himself.

As daylight faded, the final stretcher wagon completed registration. The names of those whose relatives hadn't been located went on a separate list for the town to check against earlier shelters. A pleasing total wasn't allowed to conceal them.

Viola handed her horse to a groom and went to submit her receipt and patrol report. Passing the guildhall, she was stopped by the woman with the cooking pot, who offered her soup.

She looked about to say she still had work.

The woman pointed at the table by the door. “I'll leave it there. Come back when you're finished.”

Viola nodded.

At the inn, Elena never managed to open their book. She sat by the window, her head slowly drooping, still holding an unfolded rental receipt.

Adrian took it, smoothed it on the table, and sat beside her. She woke for half a moment to ask whether the bell was still ringing outside.

“No. The landlady's clearing the plates.”

“Good.”

She leaned a little closer. He left his shoulder where she could rest against it and listened to someone carrying dinner in downstairs.

The cooking pot should be on the fire by now, too.
