# Chapter 100: Even a Witch Can Be Wrong

On the afternoon of December 13, Luna found a chair outside the laboratory door.

Adrian occupied it. He had no experimental consent form on his knees, only a plate of turnovers fresh from the kitchen.

“I thought you were waiting for me,” she said.

“I am.”

“Well. A welcome like this might deserve—”

“You left halfway through and forgot the rest of your lunch in the dining room.”

Luna studied the pastries as though they were unusually difficult testimony to dispute. “I ate some bread.”

“Half a slice. The other half is underneath your wiring diagram.”

“At least it's been useful as a paperweight.”

He passed her the plate. Her fingertips brushed his right hand. He didn't pull away; she steadied her own hand first, then lowered her head and took a bite. Hot jam waited inside. She started to speak and discovered that, for once, patience was unavoidable.

Elena watched from the doorway with considerable satisfaction. “So the kitchen really can make you quiet.”

“How much did you invest in this discovery?”

“He bought the pastries.”

The witch turned to him. Adrian kept the last turnover for himself. He had bought her lunch, and her being beautiful did not oblige him to promote the gesture into a heroic rescue.

“We'll start after you've eaten,” he said. “We need a break too.”

Luna looked down at the plate, then finally sat in the neighboring chair, which had no diagrams on it at all.

The morning's paired-conduction result remained on the observation sheets.

Two inert plates, bearing neither names nor personal obligations, had passed measurable heat through a particular connection. That showed how an old contractual structure could provide a channel. It did not establish that pain, accidents, or anything called fate could simply be moved along it.

The afternoon's question was narrower. They would apply one limited pulse, lasting no more than six seconds, to the source and see whether the receiver's response could be measured independently. This was not a second examination of Adrian's left hand. No person's body would be connected to the circuit.

After finishing her lunch, Luna left the empty plate outside and washed her hands before returning to the bench.

Both plates rested beneath a transparent hood on bench W-3 in the west workshop, connected through a limiting ring. The brass-cased R-4 gauge provided the main reading. Beside the receiver stood a sealed liquid thermometer. Its red liquid rose as it warmed, independently of the gauge needle.

“What if they disagree?” Adrian asked.

“We stop and find out which is wrong.”

“And if it's your favorite?”

Luna tilted her head. “My favorite is holding the stop handle.”

Cecilia looked up from the record desk.

“My experimental partner,” the witch added.

“Friend,” the saint corrected gently. “He's helping you watch the readings today.”

“Friend and temporary gauge reader,” Adrian said. “No plans to work overtime.”

“Your preferred titles are more complicated than the wiring.”

Elena reset the calibrated seconds pendulum. “You can correct a title afterward. The starting time is less forgiving.”

Luna smiled and lifted a hand in acknowledgment. She checked the closed hood, the unloaded needle's zero position, and the seal on the limiting ring. It was unchanged since morning. As arranged that morning, Viola had gone to post the confirmation of her follow-up examination. She would not take part in the afternoon procedure. The administrator stood by the master supply control beside the door, watching the passage and intercepting a kitchen helper attempting to bring in an entire tray of tea.

Adrian surveyed the cups and asked Luna whether she had ordered one for every notebook.

“That would bankrupt me.”

“I thought they at least deserved refreshments while working overtime.”

“I've decided nobody is working overtime today.”

Their laughter broke up the room's excessive solemnity. When Luna asked whether everyone was ready, no one seemed eager to prove their courage. Each simply answered.

After checking that the exit was clear, the administrator set the master supply control to its timed position. Faint violet light glowed inside the power cell.

In the first second, the brass needle rose from zero. In the second, the thermometer’s red column rose past its first mark. Luna watched R-4 while Cecilia called out the liquid level. Elena timed the procedure against the seconds pendulum and recorded both sets of readings. Adrian did not look at his hand. The old scar remained beneath his glove and had nothing to do with today's circuit.

At the fourth second, the needle had not crossed the second mark.

The red liquid had already reached the third mark.

“Stop,” Adrian said.

Luna's pen froze. “The thermometer might be picking up heat at the edge. Let me—”

He pressed the handle.

The ceramic bridge slid out, and the spring-loaded shutter closed. The power light went out. Residual energy drained along the discharge connection into the dry sand-and-stone heat dump. There was no explosion, and nobody had to throw themselves in front of anything. A small crack sounded in the tray. Its thin ceramic heat wafer had fractured exactly as designed.

Luna remained bent over the bench.

The red liquid rose a little farther. The needle slowly caught up with the position it should already have reached.

“I said stop.” Adrian kept his hand on the handle.

She looked up at him, then back at the belated needle.

“I heard you.”

“I know you did.”

That left her less room to hide than an argument would have. All she had wanted was one more second. She had even believed she could keep that second under control. But their agreement existed precisely so she could not rewrite it alone at the moment she most wanted to continue.

Luna set down her pen.

“You were right to stop. We'll wait for the discharge to finish.”

She checked the independent indicator. Only after the residual energy reached zero did the administrator open the hood. Neither inert plate had cracked. A new discoloration marked the receiver's surface. The power cell had spent almost half its charge; the ceramic heat wafer needed replacing, and both measuring instruments would require fresh checks. Nobody was hurt, but the afternoon had still cost money, materials, and the use of an instrument.

Cecilia checked the final timed entry with Elena. She did not describe an explosion that had nearly happened.

“The thermometer could be wrong,” she said. “So could the needle.”

“Yes,” Luna answered. “Which means we can't use today's measurements.”

“What about this morning's observations?” Elena asked.

“There's still independent support for conduction itself. But these numbers can't tell us how much passed through, or how quickly. Those are separate claims.”

She reached for a fresh sheet, paused, then turned over the page on which she had written the success criteria.

On its back, Adrian saw her write: First quantitative pulse trial. Aborted.

She added the reason. She did not rename it a preliminary completion.

Once the administrator had left with the out-of-service notice, the room grew quiet. Back from posting her letter, Viola came inside, wrapped her unfinished pear in clean paper, and asked Luna whether she wanted some air.

“In a minute,” the witch said. “I need to dislike myself first.”

“Then sit down.” Viola pushed a chair toward her. “Standing won't make it more sincere.”

Luna laughed once. The amusement soon faded.

She held out the pen she had taken from her hair, offering it to Adrian in her open palm.

“You can check whether I've secretly blamed you in the report.”

“I'll read the report,” he said. “I don't need to search you.”

“A shame. I had a very good line prepared.”

“Save it. Your jokes don't have to make this go away.”

Her fingers curled slightly. Finally, she put the pen on the table.

“I wanted to keep going. It looked as though it was about to work.”

“I know.”

“It wasn't because I thought you had no right to stop.”

“But you nearly acted as though I didn't.”

She met his eyes. This time she did not try to find an elegant route around the point.

“Yes.”

At the far end of the corridor, someone called that the kettle had boiled. The small, ordinary sound kept their silence from turning into a tribunal. Adrian picked up his empty cup and asked whether she wanted to come downstairs.

“You still want to?”

“The tea isn't connected to that gauge.”

Her smile returned slowly. She made no attempt to make him blush.

On the stairs, Elena fell half a step behind with him. Her hand brushed his sleeve. When he looked around, she quietly asked whether he was angry.

“A little.”

“You're allowed to be more than a little.”

“I'm still deciding. But I don't want to trade forgiveness for her finding an answer faster.”

Elena nodded. The narrow window at the turn in the stairs lit his face. After watching him for a moment, she said, “You looked rather handsome when you pressed that handle.”

He missed a step in his rhythm.

“Really?”

“Don't stand in the middle of the stairs waiting for me to say it twice.”

He hurried on, but there was no hiding his smile. On level ground, she let him take her right hand, allowing the compliment a little room to linger.

In the dining room, Cecilia was explaining that friends could also refuse to stay up all night with someone.

“And you?” Luna asked.

“I refuse too.”

“He hasn't asked you.”

“I'm providing a gentle example in advance.”

Viola sat down with the hot water and earnestly asked whether everyone ought to provide one. Elena pulled Adrian's chair beside her own.

“He has plans with me tonight.”

“Reading,” Adrian clarified.

“I didn't suggest anything else.” She glanced at him. “That was a very quick explanation.”

Luna laughed over her cup. She didn't ask him to cancel, nor did she use the title of experimental partner as though it came with first claim on his evenings.

Halfway through their tea, the kitchen sent out a small, chipped honey jar and asked them to help finish what remained. Viola took the spoon and carefully compared the amounts in everyone's cups before discovering that Luna had already added sugar.

“I can do without,” the witch said.

“Then you can have two pieces of bread.”

“Which military code is that from?”

“My experience of lunch. People who say they can skip it usually end up forgetting it entirely.”

Then she realized she sounded as though she were training Luna, and the tips of her ears reddened. Adrian turned the breadbasket toward the witch, sparing Viola an explanation. Luna took two pieces, spread honey on one, and set the other beside it. Apparently, she was prepared to let someone include her afternoon in the meal schedule.

Cecilia produced her perpetually crooked knitted strap. After several attempts to unravel and repair it, it had become short enough to resemble a bookmark.

“Perhaps it's chosen its calling,” Adrian suggested.

“It hasn't consulted the flask.”

“The flask can have a better-fitting strap.”

She considered this, then held the unfinished strip beside his book. Instead of slipping it between the pages, she asked whether he would use it once it was finished.

“Yes. Don't pull out my old bookmark yet, though.”

“Of course. I only wanted to give you somewhere to think of me.”

She said it so calmly that Adrian took a moment too long to answer. Elena lowered her gaze to her tea. Her cup made a slightly crisp sound when she returned it to its saucer.

Luna brightened. “That result doesn't need an instrument.”

“Or your records,” Elena said.

“I only envy how clearly she can say what she wants.”

No one continued teasing. Cecilia returned her needle to its cloth bag and told her, quite seriously, that being clear did not mean the answer would always be yes.

Luna glanced at Adrian.

“Then I'll practice with something small. After we finish with the maintenance records tomorrow, will you walk around the courtyard with me? No diagrams.”

He considered it. “After lunch. I can't promise to find the answer you want.”

“I said no diagrams.”

She lowered her head to her bread and got a little honey at the corner of her mouth. Adrian touched the corner of his own lips to let her know. She picked up a napkin, wiped the wrong side first, then caught him smiling. For once, she did not pretend she had done it on purpose.

Viola set down her cup. It was time for the gentle movement her physician had permitted. She asked whether anyone wanted to join her. Elena accepted first. They waited by the door for Adrian to put away his things, without turning the short break into a contest that required a winner.

During the evening shutdown inspection, the administrator brought the brass gauge's maintenance card. Its most recent calibration had genuinely passed. The listed checks, however, covered static endpoints, not the speed at which it followed a short pulse.

Luna ran her finger beneath a small line partly covered by the retaining strip.

Treat as fitted with historical buffering attachment. See preceding volume.

“Where is the preceding volume?” Adrian asked.

“The old equipment store's maintenance ledger. The attendant has gone home. We can request access tomorrow morning.”

He copied the volume number and returned the card.

“I'd like to come.”

“To inspect me?”

“To find out why the needle was late. And you did say I could read the full records.”

Luna looked at the number in his hand and nodded seriously.

That night, Elena reached the part of the travel memoir in which the captain decided to trust a slow clock.

Adrian looked up. She stopped reading. For a moment they stared at each other, then both laughed.

“At least he can still ask someone,” she said.

Outside, darkness had settled completely. The old notebooks would wait until the cupboard opened in the morning. The pastry plate was clean, and the witch had agreed to eat her supper.

Adrian moved a little closer so he and Elena could read the next line together.

One slow needle had not cost them the evening they had promised each other.
