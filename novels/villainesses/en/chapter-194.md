# Chapter 194: Leave That Box Blank

The station manager had already filled in the form.

Adrian sat down on February 21 and saw the words beside his name: Caused alarm among passengers while carrying an unusual device.

He didn't take the pen.

“Have you finished asking questions before I arrived?”

The manager, a middle-aged man with dark circles under his eyes and coal dust on his cuff, drew the sheet back. It was merely the reason for the appointment. A signature would confirm attendance.

Smoke rose from the brazier. Outside, someone kept tapping the window to ask when the next coach would leave.

Viola sat near the door with her left arm supported. She wore an ordinary dark coat. Her caregiver had a small hourglass on her lap.

“Use a separate attendance record,” Viola said. “Leave that box blank.”

“Miss Blackwood, since you've come to clarify—”

“My surname can't testify for that box either.”

He glanced at her swordless waist and produced another sheet. Then he summoned the ticket seller, whose steps slowed when he recognized Adrian.

The seller reported complaints about a metal object and an argument during inspection. A young station boy interrupted. He'd heard that a gentleman had flown into a temper and nearly stopped the entire coach from leaving.

Adrian's chest tightened. That version could travel much farther than two streets. He laid his ticket stub on the desk, fingers white.

“Who lost his temper?”

“That's what everyone says.”

“Where were you?” Viola asked.

“In the yard. Loading coal.”

“Then record who told you, not what you saw.”

The boy reddened. A delivery worker had told him; the worker had gone. The manager frowned, apparently preparing to dismiss youthful carelessness. Adrian asked that the correction be retained.

“I understand you're afraid of delaying coaches. I also need you to remember whose voices were actually raised.”

The seller admitted that two passengers had argued. Adrian had named his destination without threatening anyone. The driver had inspected the clasp and admitted it.

Viola requested the driver. He was preparing a departure and couldn't immediately come. Continuing like this would delay today's service too, the manager complained.

“Write down when he can attend. The departure time can't answer for him.”

The room quieted. Adrian could hear sand falling.

This was why he'd asked her to come. He wanted to crush the form and shove it under the desk. He wanted to tell the seller he knew the truth and had written this anyway. Any outburst would finally give them the angry man they'd described.

The manager went to arrange things, leaving water. Viola moved her covered cup to her right. Adrian unscrewed the stiff lid before her caregiver could.

“Your medicine-bottle business wasn't approved,” she murmured.

“This is water. Separate license.”

Her mouth twitched. He released his grip on the ticket.

Someone shouted outside.

It wasn't aimed at them. A narrow trolley piled with coal sacks rolled from the yard into the shelter. The station boy chased the handle and tried to stop a wheel with his foot. His shoe slipped on wet brick. One knee struck the ground.

Viola saw the raised edge of the wheel chock.

“Stay out of its path! Clear the right!”

She remained seated rather than sacrificing her injured arm. Adrian moved an empty chair from the doorway so the boy could scramble aside. Two workers caught the handles from the side. Another braced a long block behind a wheel. The trolley stopped several paces from the waiting bench.

The boy had scraped his knee but could sit up. The manager emerged, demanding how anyone could mishandle a coal trolley on a day already this disorderly.

“The chock split,” Viola said.

She indicated it and asked that the damaged piece be set aside while somebody found a sound replacement. A waiting passenger was already asking whether that man had brought something dangerous again.

“The rear wheel wasn't secured,” the coal worker called. “I saw it.”

He didn't look at Adrian. Perhaps he didn't know him. He simply refused to let another story take possession of the trolley he'd just stopped.

After checking the boy's scrape, Viola's caregiver handed him over to station staff. Viola settled into her chair, breathing faster. The hourglass was half empty. Adrian asked whether she wanted to leave early. She would sit first; if the pain increased, she would go.

The manager returned with the driver and a repair slip for the chock. After dealing with yard safety, the driver described the inspection two days earlier. An ordinary metal clasp, permitted under the rules. The argument hadn't prevented departure.

“We left two minutes late,” the ticket seller said.

“So did the loading. Don't give one passenger all two minutes.”

This time the manager didn't dismiss the distinction. He crossed through the original entry while keeping it legible and recorded the driver's account. Adrian acknowledged naming his destination and feeling impatient that others had to wait for the inspection. He didn't describe himself as composed.

“But I carried no prohibited object and demanded no exemption because of my position.”

“Did you tell the ticket seller to shut up?” Viola asked.

He looked at her. The question hurt, as though she too had begun hunting for something suspicious.

“No.”

She asked the seller, who agreed. Only then did she request that the point be recorded. Adrian understood: the rule she'd protected also had to admit questions he disliked.

Who had approved the handwritten warning?

The seller explained that several regular customers had asked whether travel was safe after the temple circular arrived. The manager was absent. He'd wanted to give them an answer and put up the notice himself. On returning, the manager had thought caution sensible and left it.

“So this letter was your decision,” Viola said.

The manager admitted it. His fears were specific. If something happened, everyone would ask why he'd admitted a man named in a warning. If sales fell, his superiors would only say he managed badly.

“I never meant to arrest anyone.”

“Then don't make people obtain a certificate of innocence from you before they buy another ticket,” Adrian said.

After studying the stub for a long time, the manager fetched the current dangerous-goods regulations. No clause authorized exclusion by a name in an old history. Threatening conduct and dangerous objects had their own provisions. An undefined word like unusual couldn't substitute for either.

He withdrew his recommendation that Adrian stay away, recording that the station had established no prohibited item or other grounds preventing his travel. The temple circular remained posted. The correction application awaited its publisher. The manager could change his own letter and the addition on his own wall.

He set the fresh sheet aside to dry, then picked up the first form.

“This one's void. Let's throw it away before somebody reads it out of context.”

Adrian nearly agreed. He hated his name beside alarm and unusual. If the sheet vanished, there would be one less thing to explain.

Viola requested a copy with the reason for correction attached. The seller looked frightened again.

“Are you going to have me disciplined?”

Adrian recognized how readily he'd accepted destruction of an unfavorable record. It could hurt him. It could also establish how that harm had been written.

“I'm asking for correction. I haven't decided your punishment. Put down what you relied on. I don't want tomorrow's story to be that you changed your mind for no reason.”

The seller began writing. Could he name customers who'd asked? The manager told him to name only those who actually had, without adding everyone complaining in the shelter today.

Each read back his own account. Adrian marked two points of disagreement before signing the attendance record. Viola signed only for what she'd witnessed, not for his entire past.

The station boy brought a new cup, limping slightly. He quietly asked the knight whether a worker's claim that the chock should have been replaced earlier also needed reporting higher up.

“Have the person responsible record when it's replaced. If they use the cracked one again tomorrow, take today's record to the supervisor.”

He nodded and hurried off. The caregiver called him back to a walking pace. Viola watched him take smaller steps, and her breathing eased.

The seller picked up a paste brush, then set it down. The old handwritten notice had dried fast. He scraped it away bit by bit, revealing the original rules beneath. The replacement required inspection of actual objects and referral of disputes to the duty supervisor. The manager signed it.

Nobody applauded. One passenger complained about too many words. Another wanted to know whether the coach was ever leaving.

Viola's sand was almost gone. Adrian wanted her to stay for the final stamped copy, but she was already preparing to rise. He swallowed the request and told the manager he'd wait for it himself at the window.

“I'll buy my own ticket home.”

“I know.”

She stood carefully, her caregiver on her left. Outside, a gust blew the tie at her collar across her face. Two attempts with her right hand made it worse. Adrian shielded her from the wind and asked whether he could lower the knot.

“Yes. Though this isn't required by uniform regulations.”

“I'll charge less, then.”

“You're running too many businesses today.”

She seemed to worry that it sounded like a complaint about his kindness and hurried to add thanks. He had already stepped aside for her carriage.

Before entering, she asked whether he might accompany her to a shoemaker next week. She wanted ordinary walking shoes. Whenever the man heard knight, he produced riding boots.

“No escort duty. I'd like someone to look at the heels. My caregiver can consider the medical requirements.”

“I can. Tell me when you've confirmed a day.”

She accepted that answer at its actual size. After the carriage left, Adrian paid the ordinary fare at the window. The seller remained awkward but didn't ask him to open his coat.

A woman moved her vegetable basket from a seat, then shifted herself slightly toward the window. Adrian took the space without asking her for any guarantee. At a corner, an onion rolled out. He stopped it with his shoe so she could retrieve it.

“Thank you,” she said quietly, and looked outside again.

Through the window, the split chock lay on a repair bench while somebody measured a replacement. Today, one danger had finally been left where it had actually occurred.

That evening Elena listened as promised. When she guessed the driver's next words incorrectly, Adrian raised a finger to his lips. Leaving the correcting pen behind didn't entitle her to revise the story aloud.

She leaned back in surrender. After a moment she still asked what had happened to the boy.

“He could walk. Somebody went with him to change the dressing.”

She exhaled. Adrian discovered that he wanted to tell her that ending too.

Cecilia's note was less comfortable. The meal house confirmed he could enter under ordinary visitor arrangements. The deacon leading prayers had prepared a passage for communal recitation. It called the catastrophe in the old history a predestined sin.

Adrian read it and passed it to Elena.

Apparently tomorrow's empty bowls would hold something more troublesome than an ordinary quiet morning.
