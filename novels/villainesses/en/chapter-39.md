# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Two: The Saint Won't Let Me Get Married
### Chapter 39: The Patient in the Prophecy Was Someone Else

On October nineteenth, Adrian received three different schedules.

The first was from Hale.

*Morning: verify conflicting patient reference numbers at Mercy Clinic.*

The second was from Cecilia.

*Courtesy and Forms of Address During Joint Investigative Meetings.*

The third was from Elena.

*Seven this evening. Dinner, if you still have the energy.*

Adrian immediately moved Elena's note to the top of the pile.

Cecilia, however, was already standing in the doorway.

"I prepared the etiquette guide because several people yesterday referred to you as the male party to a Temple betrothal. That is not an established identity."

"Then what should people call me?"

"Mr. Reed in formal proceedings. Adrian in ordinary personal conversation, if you consent."

Elena flipped to the last page.

"Why have you scheduled a 'three-person tea and conversation' after the investigation?"

"To prevent misunderstandings if someone leaves early."

Elena crossed out that line and wrote:

*Attendance optional.*

Adrian studied the page.

"I'm beginning to like this guide. At least it guarantees nobody can force me to eat a fourth meal."

"Have you often been forced to eat?" Cecilia asked.

"It's a new hazard I've discovered in your company."

Elena hid a smile and handed him the actual case notes.

"First the clinic. We'll discuss your personal freedom over dinner."

When they arrived at Mercy Clinic, Dr. Grace Winter had already obtained limited written authorizations from the two people whose registration numbers were in dispute.

They could examine identity information and the history of the amended reference numbers.

Private medical details remained with the treating physicians.

"I agreed to help figure out why somebody changed my name," Elliot Morris told them. "I did not agree to see my old injuries in tomorrow's newspapers."

"We only need to verify your identity and the time you were registered," Adrian said. "Anything else stays with your doctor."

Elliot rested his walking stick against the chair and showed them the cloth identification band around his wrist.

**C-91.**

Beneath it was a nurse's signature from two days earlier.

"This is what they gave me when I registered. Later somebody from the Temple attached a C-19 card to the bed and said the original entry was wrong."

"Did you receive a Temple blessing?" Cecilia asked.

"A junior priest read something. I thought it was an ordinary visit."

"Did anyone tell you that you were the seriously ill patient named in a prognosis?"

"No. I would have asked the doctor if they had."

Cecilia closed her notebook.

"Thank you. We don't need to ask you anything about your private condition."

Grace placed the two original registration cards in different-colored sleeves.

"The numbers exist to manage beds and supplies. A patient isn't a number. We check names and identifiers precisely because a card can be attached to the wrong person."

Elliot looked genuinely offended.

"Then why did someone put another man's name above my bed?"

"We're finding that out," Grace told him. "But you have an examination scheduled. Anyone who wants to display you as proof of a prophecy can ask your permission first—and you may refuse."

Elliot thought for a moment.

"If I authorize the medical supervisor to examine my wristband, will that help Jonah?"

"It can establish that you were not the person identified by the original C-19 registration," Hale replied. "Finding Jonah will still require us to check the transfer documents."

Elliot nodded and wrote down the narrow limits of his consent himself.

Adrian noticed he didn't ask for compensation or publicity.

He just wanted his own name back.

In another room, Jonah Payne's mother held a work certificate stained with mud.

She explained that Jonah had originally received care in the east ward. Then someone told her he'd been transferred for observation.

Nobody could clearly tell her where.

Grace arranged for the treating physicians to continue locating him and checking whether his care had been properly transferred.

She wouldn't allow the paperwork investigation to delay medical work.

Hale laid out the two sets of identity records they were authorized to examine.

The original clinic entries were straightforward.

Jonah Payne: **C-19**.

Elliot Morris: **C-91**.

On October sixteenth, a *Corrected Oracle Patient Notice* from the Temple's Administrative Duty Office had switched the two identifiers.

Then an October seventeenth report stated:

*"C-19 patient stable following completion of Temple blessing."*

"But the patient in the bed marked C-19 was me," Elliot said, tapping his wristband. "And my original number was C-91."

Adrian felt cold.

Someone might have used a person who was already stable to make a Temple forecast appear to have been fulfilled through a blessing.

The patient originally assigned C-19 had meanwhile been moved under somebody else's reference.

"Does that prove the Temple's prophecy was fabricated?" Adrian asked.

"It establishes a serious identity problem," Grace said. "We haven't been authorized to see the original prognosis. We don't even know exactly what it predicted. But moving a patient's identity without physician review has already interfered with care."

Cecilia lifted her head.

"I've helped audit blessing registers. A patient reference must include an identifying initial and admission date as well as the number. If those details don't agree, the rite cannot simply be filed as completed."

She produced an unused example of the old Temple form.

There were two spaces for independent witnesses.

On the altered document in front of them, neither space held a personal signature.

Only a department stamp remained.

"Who authorized it?" Elena asked.

Grace produced the original nursing ledger.

"No signature from the clinic director. We've asked repeatedly for the correction to be withdrawn."

"Do those requests have receipt numbers?" Adrian asked.

"Yes. We submitted another one yesterday."

He nodded.

If someone later claimed the clinic had never objected, the dated receipts would say otherwise.

Cecilia asked Grace what the phrase *blessing completed* actually meant in the clinic's ordinary accounting.

"A Temple visitor came in, read a prayer, and asked reception to record the visit. If the patient wants a blessing, that's fine. It doesn't replace conventional treatment."

"That's how I was trained too," Cecilia said. "Prayer can comfort someone. It doesn't make medical care unnecessary."

"Then why does this report make it sound as though the patient finished treatment?" Adrian asked.

"Because the person writing it wasn't a physician," Grace said. "They used the same completion field for a religious visit and for medical care. A reader coming along later could easily confuse the two."

Cecilia studied the report.

Then crossed *oracle fulfilled* out of her own draft.

In its place she wrote:

*Administrative record states that the blessing was completed. Medical outcome has not been independently verified.*

"That should stop us from turning a patient into an advertisement for some office," she said.

The nurse responsible for the ward, Ada, hesitated when Hale requested the lawful records-preservation procedure.

"Someone from the Temple showed me a confidentiality order yesterday. Said giving you these forms would count as leaking a prophecy."

Elena didn't lose her temper.

"Then don't hand them to House Rosenberg. Seal the originals and transfer the identity-related records directly to the municipal medical inspector who's authorized to examine them. All we need is the filing reference."

Ada visibly relaxed.

Cecilia added, "The oversight order restricts unauthorized public speculation. It shouldn't prevent patients from accessing their own treatment identifiers. If someone has used it that way, I'll challenge the instruction with my committee."

Ada nodded.

The records were secured without anyone taking private medical files into a noble household's custody.

By noon, Grace had located a witnessed temporary-transfer entry.

On the evening of October sixteenth, Jonah's name had been struck from the east-ward list and entered as:

*C-91 — Temporary Observation, Graybell Relief Station.*

"That suggests he might still be at Graybell Street," Hale said.

"It establishes that someone wrote down that destination," Adrian corrected himself. "We need the receiving station to confirm he actually arrived."

Grace immediately had a physician send a medical-transfer query asking Graybell to identify the patient received and describe whether appropriate care was in place.

No answer came.

Adrian found Cecilia in the hallway.

She was standing beside the wooden door on which Mira had drawn little suns for the children.

"Thinking about Mira?" he asked.

"She might have discovered this exact problem two days ago. She wanted to make sure one patient hadn't been entered under another person's name. And I was the one who sent her out."

"You didn't order her to put herself in danger," Adrian said. "You gave her limited instructions. You told her to return if the route changed."

"She hasn't returned."

He didn't rush to reassure her.

"If we let Jonah stay under the wrong name because we're frightened, I suspect Mira would be furious."

Cecilia looked at him.

"How can you tell?"

"She draws suns for children and argues with people over hospital records. She doesn't strike me as the sort of person who'd approve of leaving someone without medicine."

Cecilia took a slow breath.

"You're right. She'd say exactly that."

When they returned to reception, Grace had received a municipal medical inspector's acknowledgment.

Graybell Relief Station had registered a patient under C-91.

It refused to confirm the person's name, citing Temple confidentiality.

Grace stared at the response.

"Which regulation permits a receiving station to hide a patient's identity from the physician who transferred him?"

The municipal inspector's reply was clear. When the original clinic remained responsible for continuity of care, the receiving station had to confirm the patient's identity and treatment handover.

Religious confidentiality didn't override basic patient safety requirements.

"Then we have grounds to request an on-site medical inspection," Hale said.

Elena asked that a licensed municipal physician accompany the application.

Not private soldiers sent to force open a door.

She also wanted to know what would happen if Graybell refused entry.

"The inspectors will present their authorization," the medical supervisor explained. "If delay places a patient in immediate danger, the doctor can seek emergency access. Any compulsory entry is for the authorized municipal personnel to decide."

"So we don't order the Rosenberg guards to break the door down," Adrian said.

"Correct."

"Then the physician goes first. Guards protect the party. The witnesses keep the records."

Cecilia tucked her Temple investigator's badge into her coat.

"I don't want people to see a saint arriving and assume I'm bringing an oracle they have no right to question."

Elena met her eyes.

"Then today you're a volunteer trying to help."

Cecilia smiled faintly.

"I'd prefer that."

Just then, a boy in a rain-spattered coat rushed into the clinic.

"Someone told me to give this to Dr. Winter!"

He held out a folded supply slip.

Two lines had been written on its reverse.

*Jonah is alive. East-side sickroom, Graybell. Don't let them change his name again.*

There was only one initial.

**M.**

Cecilia caught her breath.

"Mira?"

The boy shook his head.

"I didn't meet whoever wrote it. The paper was in a medicine wagon. The driver asked me to bring it."

A little old-style sealing wax clung to the slip.

Hale had the nurse and the boy separately describe how it had been delivered.

Nobody called it Mira's handwriting yet.

But it gave them somewhere to look.

The east-side sickroom at Graybell Relief Station.

Adrian turned to Elena.

"This isn't about finding something that will embarrass the Temple."

"I know."

"It's about making sure Jonah gets a doctor. And giving Mira a chance to come home."

Elena handed Hale the inspection application.

"Then we start with the people. Preserve the evidence while they're being helped."

Cecilia folded the note.

"I'll go with you," she said. "But not as a saint who gets to decide what's best for the patients."

"What, then?" Adrian asked.

"As a friend who promised to return with medicine, and still owes someone an explanation."

Rain began ticking against the windows.

Adrian had never liked prophecies about fate.

Today, he liked even less the people who changed the name of a living patient until their prophecy appeared to have been correct.
