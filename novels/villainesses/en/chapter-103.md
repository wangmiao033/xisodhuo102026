# Chapter 103: Hide It Where Everyone Can Find It

Adrian did not grab the glass case.

He drew the empty tool tray half an inch toward himself. The wooden clamp struck its rim and stopped short of the lid.

“Move your hand,” the custodian said.

“Put down the clamp first.”

His voice was quiet. His knuckles were white. The object behind him would not repair itself when this argument ended. One careless squeeze could destroy something no victorious speech would restore.

Luna steadied the opposite edge of the tray without magic.

“Will you be listing me as an obstruction to transport?”

“Miss Ashcroft, I am carrying out a transfer.”

“Then carry out the suspension that hasn't been lifted.”

Cecilia began recording their exact words. She asked the porters to step back so nobody mistook the case for cleared cargo. One released his carrying strap immediately. The other looked toward the administrator, who had arrived in answer to Viola's bell.

The older man checked that the inner lid remained closed.

“This cabinet has a fitted dust hood. I am covering the specimen so no more tools reach over it.”

He brought out a rigid cover lined in black. Its feet rested on the outer wooden frame, beyond the original support. Luna indicated the contact points while Cecilia watched. There was space between hood and glass; nothing touched the silver-gray lining.

The fragile surface disappeared from sight.

“You're hiding it,” the custodian said.

“In front of you,” Adrian answered. “Same cabinet. Same tray. The administrator's key. Watch where it goes.”

The entire support slid six inches backward along its level cabinet rails. No second box appeared. Nobody slipped anything into a coat. Cecilia read the cabinet location aloud and noted the time, hood, and intact inner cover.

At the door, Viola released the bell cord with her right hand.

“The stairs are clear. The lead inspector can come straight up.”

The custodian glanced at her sword. She left it sheathed and pushed an empty transport case out of the passage with her foot.

“We aren't preventing you from leaving.”

When the lead inspector arrived, Elena was beside him with the companion preservation instructions. A strand of hair had escaped across her forehead.

“Where is the specimen?”

“Original cabinet, under its hood. Inner lid unopened,” the administrator replied.

Adrian released the tray. Shallow crescents marked his palm. Only then did he notice how carefully he had been holding his breath.

The inspector read Cecilia's record, then checked the cabinet rails and original seals without lifting the hood.

“I instructed you to maintain the existing condition,” he told the custodian. “Preparing a pressure seal was not permission to proceed.”

“The general instructions say—”

“When specific preservation requirements conflict, bring them to me. The easiest page to follow does not win by default.”

The companion instructions allowed fragile objects to remain sealed in place pending technical review and a suitable carrying cradle. They did not cancel the academy's authority to request C-17. They did keep it off today's carriage.

Luna exhaled.

“That does not authorize you to break an inspection seal yourself,” the inspector added.

“I have no plans to.” She indicated her crooked cuff button. “I've reached my limit for delicate handiwork.”

Nobody laughed at first. Then the inspector looked at the button, and his mouth moved slightly.

The custodian's conduct went into a separate execution complaint for review. Elena obtained a copy without demanding an instant finding of deliberate destruction. The missing technical attachment and the transcription of the December 12 list still required investigation.

The inspector sealed the outer cabinet himself. The administrator retained the key; access required a designated inspection witness. No pressure wrapping, no separation of the lining, no departure before the preservation review.

The clerk read the restrictions back while everyone was still present. Luna checked the word lining, and Elena checked that the location had not been replaced with a vague reference to local custody. The porters signed only for what they had actually witnessed: no transfer had taken place. Their ropes remained empty. Adrian watched the administrator put the key back on its ordinary ring, between the kitchen storeroom key and another whose purpose he did not know. For now, protection looked remarkably like knowing exactly where something belonged.

Outside, the carriage finally rolled away with an entirely ordinary crunch of ice.

Luna turned to Adrian.

“Your hand.”

He opened his right palm. No broken skin, only the marks of his own grip. Her intended joke disappeared.

“You shouldn't compete with a clamp.”

“I blocked the tray, not the jaws.”

“Next time, call me.”

“I did. You were right there.”

For once, she had no ready answer. Elena arrived with warm water and put the cup into his hand.

“Drink. You sounded like a cat caught in a bellows.”

“A cat with a second occupation?”

“This particular cat.”

Luna laughed, briefly but properly. People began moving away from the cabinet.

While the clerk checked the duplicate seals, Luna fetched a small teaching frame. Two copper leaves fell shut under their own weight. Only pressure on both handles exposed the white card between them.

“Not approved for the cabinet yet,” she explained. “This uses window light. The specimen is still asleep.”

Then she looked at Adrian, recovering a little of her usual confidence.

“It is, however, excellent for establishing whether two hearts act as one.”

“I thought it established whether two fingers move on time,” Elena said.

“You dismantle all romance into components.”

“You brought the components.”

Adrian checked that the leaves could not strike his hand, then took the opposite handle. Luna instructed him to press on three. If either released, the light would close off.

“One. Two—”

“You can look at me while you count,” he said. “I won't cheat.”

She looked up directly into his smile. Her finger loosened. One copper leaf snapped down before the third count arrived.

Only half a stripe of sunlight reached the card.

“Synchronization remains unproven,” Cecilia observed gravely.

Even the clerk failed to conceal his laugh.

“Deliberate interference,” Luna said, resetting her grip. A faint pink colored her face.

“You wanted our hearts involved. I checked whether you were looking.”

“Of course I—”

She stopped. Elena took over counting, and this time the full card emerged on three. The administrator released one handle and confirmed that the leaves closed.

No magic had occurred. Nobody's feelings had been measured. They had, however, found a way to block the observation light without touching an old object.

“Next time,” Luna murmured as she passed Adrian, “no smiling before three.”

“What about afterward?”

She walked faster.

On the stairs, Viola paused. Her left shoulder had stiffened from sitting. She accepted Adrian's offer to wait while the others went ahead.

“I wanted to get up,” she admitted.

“That wouldn't have improved his understanding of the lining.”

“I know. I'm used to letting people notice my sword first.”

She looked down at the sheathed weapon with an unfamiliar satisfaction.

“Today he heard the bell first.”

“And you kept the stairs clear. I saw the empty case.”

“Hardly something for a battle report.”

“Your diary, then.”

She considered this. “May I write that you praised me?”

He blinked. Her ears reddened, but she did not withdraw the question.

“Yes. Include the part about the sword you didn't draw.”

“It won't compete with me for the page.”

When they resumed walking, her shoulders had loosened. Hot soup waited below. An argument that had not become a fight required neither a victory feast nor the cancellation of supper.

In the dining room, Luna was attempting to relocate her carrots.

Elena moved her plate away. “My dinner isn't part of your research.”

“I haven't proposed anything.”

“Your fork already has.”

Cecilia suggested eating one piece and leaving the other as an expression of intellectual freedom. After negotiation, Luna ate the larger one. Adrian pushed her water closer.

“You won't rescue me either?” she asked.

“I can eat with you. I can't enjoy it for you.”

“What a cruel arrangement.”

Her gaze lingered on him before she turned away. With the afternoon's fear receding, the ordinary bickering seemed to leave her less certain of herself than the confrontation had.

After dinner, the deputy inspector staying at the tower delivered documents for the next morning's preservation review. Luna took a packing diagram and automatically folded its corner inward.

“That fold is wrong,” she murmured. “With the silver-gray surface outward, the flap brushes—”

She stopped.

Cecilia, collecting cups, stopped too. The old Temple Instrument Office stamp appeared at the top of the sheet. This envelope design had not been in general use for years.

“You've used this diagram?” Cecilia asked.

Luna slowly unfolded the corner.

“Something like it.”

She did not claim to have seen it in a book. That hesitation was clearer than the answer.

Elena looked at her. Luna looked out at an apron billowing on the courtyard line, like an empty person trying to leave.

“Tomorrow morning,” she said at last. “I have something to show you. I don't want to improvise another explanation.”

“About the Temple?” Adrian asked.

“About the Temple. And me.”

Cecilia set the cup down carefully.

“Tomorrow, then. Don't change any old documents tonight.”

A suggestion of a smile touched Luna's mouth and vanished.

“I won't.”

She took away her cold cup. Adrian did not follow her up the stairs demanding the rest. A question they could ask tomorrow did not need to grow into a monster through tonight's guesses.

Elena waited at the guesthouse door.

“You said you had something better to tell me this evening.”

For a moment he stared. Then he remembered her note about keeping his soup warm.

“I remember.”

“I suspect you remembered just now.”

“Would you prefer the honest version or the impressive one?”

“Honest.”

He faced her properly.

“I remembered just now. But I didn't just invent what I wanted to say. When you came back today, I noticed your hair was untidy before I noticed you'd found the instructions. I was glad you were there because of your own judgment. And glad that afterward, I could walk back to the same porch with you.”

The reply she had been preparing faded from her face.

“Only the porch?”

“Tonight there's a sofa and a book too. I don't want distant promises to make those seem small.”

She took his hand carefully, mindful of the new skin on her palm.

“You may come closer.”

He did. After he agreed, she rose slightly and kissed his cheek. It was not a medal for bravery.

“Because I wanted to,” she said.

Upstairs, Luna shut a window. Elena kept hold of his hand and led him inside. Their lost captain was still waiting; tonight he had finally found an inn willing to lend him a lamp.

At the instrument cabinet, the temporary seal remained intact. C-17 lay hidden beneath its black hood. Everyone who knew it was there also knew how it could properly be brought back into the light tomorrow.
