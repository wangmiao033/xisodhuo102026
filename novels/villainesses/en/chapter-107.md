# Chapter 107: A Footstep He Seemed to Take Twice

On the second count after the light closed, Adrian saw a narrow white edge rise along D-17.

No lamp had come back on.

Sunlight from the side window struck the angled receiving frame, overlapping the date's remaining double image. From his position at the outside, looking through that thin surface, the white edge suddenly seemed to stretch into distance.

A corridor.

Stone walls. A door half open. A slanting strip of light across the floor.

A shoe crossed the strip. He heard a heel strike the ground. Then the same shoe returned to its former position and crossed again.

The same sound.

He could not tell whether it was his foot. There was no face, barely any clothing, no sentence that explained anything. Only a movement too brief to recognize, and the sudden conviction that he had already waited for that footstep.

“Stop. I'm seeing something else.”

His right hand pressed the mechanical handle as he spoke.

It was already locked. It could not disconnect power twice. Luna did not use that fact to dismiss him. She immediately closed the receiving frame's outer hood. The restorer covered the side window; the administrator confirmed separated power contacts.

The white edge disappeared.

So did the corridor.

Cecilia stopped counting and recorded the observation's termination before four seconds had elapsed. By her timer, Adrian's experience lasted less than two breaths. Nobody reopened the cover to use up the permitted six seconds.

“Step away from the frame,” Luna said.

She did not touch his left hand. Adrian stepped backward onto real wooden flooring. His heel made one quiet sound. He stared at his shoe and waited.

No second sound came.

Elena moved into his line of sight without pushing him.

“May we sit by the door?”

He nodded.

She accompanied him and left room for Dr. Hensley, who was already downstairs for ordinary medical support. The administrator summoned her without announcing an established magical injury.

Luna stayed at the apparatus long enough to verify the closed inner lid and covered receiving plate. The power cell went into its isolation box. C-17 was hooded and sealed again. Only after everything had stopped did she join him.

“I saw a slanting edge of light,” she said. “I didn't see a corridor.”

He looked up.

She was completely serious, not asking who might have stood behind the door.

“Tell the doctor how you feel first. The rest can wait.”

Hensley asked about headache, pressure in his chest, nausea, and lingering images. He answered each question. Fear remained, but the doorway was steady and the floor did not repeat itself. Before removing his glove, he asked whether it was needed. She explained the ordinary visual examination. The crescent scar was not warm; the skin had not changed.

He could not decide whether that was reassuring or more bewildering.

“Finding no new injury doesn't tell me what happened,” Hensley said. “Rest. I'll ask again later. No return to the observation position today.”

“All right.”

Elena had remained beside him. Only now did she sit, laying an open hand on her own knee.

“May I hold it?” he asked.

“Yes.”

He took her unhurt hand. His fingers were colder than hers.

“The real floor made only one sound.”

Her eyes tightened slightly, but she did not insist it must have been an illusion.

“I heard it. I walked only once too.”

Outside, Viola remained seated by the wall. She handed Cecilia her biscuit tin to put on the drinking table. Nobody mistook biscuits for medicine. The small ordinary clink still gave the room something besides unanswered questions.

After a while, Adrian was ready to talk.

Luna put down her pen so he could describe it without her choosing the words first. Corridor, half-open door, shoe, repeated movement. Near the end he stopped, wanting a name large enough to explain that familiarity.

A game image. An old memory. Another ending that had already happened.

He had not seen enough.

“I felt as though I'd waited for that footstep before. Only a feeling. I don't know the place. I don't know whether the foot was mine.”

Luna took up her pen, then read each sentence back for confirmation. She did not turn his account into a discovered earlier timeline. The others' sighting of a bright edge did not become a shared vision.

The deputy inspector asked to retain a brief record within the authorized safety-event scope. Adrian read it and objected to memory recovery, which the clerk had added. He had never said that.

The words were struck through and replaced with reported a brief image.

Luna watched the correction, lips pressed together. Yesterday she had explained how a summary could decide a fact's fate. She did not let convenient wording do it again today.

“Preserve D-17,” the restorer said. “No further power or opening today. Examine the external optical path and decide on subsequent handling first.”

“And C-17?” Elena asked.

“Original cabinet. An abnormal receiving plate does not authorize illuminating the original and a person's hand all over again.”

Adrian's grip finally eased.

At the late-morning recheck he reported no new symptoms. Hensley still prescribed rest and immediate contact if anything changed that evening. It was time for Viola to collect confirmation of tomorrow's follow-up appointment. At the doorway, she hesitated instead of taking her cloak.

“I could go later—”

“Don't,” Adrian said. “I have company. Your appointment still matters.”

She checked with Hensley that he could rest at the guesthouse, then nodded. Before leaving, she lent him the biscuit tin and warned that the lid was stiff. It opened with the right hand; he was not to let Luna dismantle its spring in the name of investigation.

“My academic reputation has already run out of spare parts today,” Luna said, raising both hands.

Viola smiled and left. She did not make delaying her own care proof of greater concern.

Adrian descended slowly. Every stair remained real and steady. Elena brought him to the guesthouse window and set down their travel book without opening it.

“Quiet, or the unfortunate captain?”

“Tell me about the letters you planned to write.”

She blinked. “They're boring.”

“I'd like to hear about your original day.”

She fetched the folder and explained how a merchant had combined three different freight charges into one figure, then discovered supposedly lost receipts when she asked questions. Halfway through, she laughed.

“I thought important people handled enormous matters. Now I spend half my time asking where a piece of paper actually is.”

“At least their paper doesn't turn September nineteenth into the twentieth.”

He stopped at his own joke. Elena did not tense with him. She looked over her pen.

“You're still allowed a bad joke today.”

Slowly, he smiled. She moved her chair closer, knees brushing. They did not need another question about the vision to remain close.

That afternoon, Luna returned his washed and dried handkerchief. Before handing it over, she indicated a faint surviving ink mark.

“It wouldn't wash out. I didn't keep trying with anything that might damage the fabric.”

“I accept the result.”

She remained standing. Elena finished the page she needed Adrian to check, then moved to the ordinary writing desk in the next room, within calling distance. She was not surrendering anybody. Her letter also genuinely needed finishing.

Luna sat down with an expression of calculated ease.

“Well? Has the beautiful researcher finally frightened you away?”

“Are you asking whether I still want to talk to you, or trying to make me say you're beautiful?”

Her mouth opened.

“Can't I ask both?”

“Yes. First: I do. Second: you know the answer.”

“Say it.”

“You're lovely.” He meant it. “But are you going the long way around because you care what I think of you now?”

Her effortless smile missed a beat.

“Researchers naturally care about participants' opinions.”

“I wasn't your research material today.”

She looked down at her empty hands.

“I care. I haven't worked out how to say it without making it sound like a more persuasive application.”

Adrian did not finish the sentence as a confession for her.

“You don't have to work it all out. Would you sit on the porch and look at the sky with me?”

“Only the sky?”

“You can look at me too. Don't count the seconds.”

Pink ears made indifference difficult. She checked that Hensley's instructions allowed a few quiet steps, then accompanied him outside. Without a lamp or instrument, she seemed less certain where to put her hands than she had been at the shutters that morning.

Cecilia brought hot tea. She plainly noticed and chose not to comment. Instead, she described Mira's letter: the extraordinarily wide bedsheet had finally become two sheets, with enough fabric left for pillowcases.

“A more complicated career than my bookmark's.”

“A good ending,” Adrian said.

“I hope today has one too.”

Luna held her cup without supplying a grand guarantee.

Cecilia asked whether he would like to sit in the southern passage tomorrow, away from instruments. She did not promise it would erase an unpleasant image. A small tree outside that window simply refused to lose its leaves.

“I don't know why. But not every unknown needs an answer today.”

Adrian watched the tea move gently in his cup and agreed. Then he admitted that what frightened him most was not somebody hiding in the corridor. It was the possibility that everyone else would decide what it meant faster than he could.

“I nearly asked whether you'd remembered the past,” Cecilia said. “I want to know where that old record came from.”

“You didn't ask.”

“I need to remember that sitting beside me doesn't mean you owe me an answer about a betrothal.”

Luna's fingers paused on her cup handle. She nodded, without turning the sentence into a lesson she had already mastered.

A carriage entered the courtyard. Viola, back with her confirmation, waved her right hand before taking the December 18 appointment slip inside. She did not wave her left arm to demonstrate recovery. Adrian did not spring up to demonstrate that he needed no rest.

When she returned, four ordinary cups surrounded the bird-decorated tin. Luna tried its lid and failed on the first attempt.

“No dismantling the spring,” Adrian reminded her.

Everyone laughed.

Toward evening, the administrator brought the restorer's preliminary opinion. D-17 was a newly formed external tracing plate with an observable decay trace. Neither the original specimen nor a human body would participate further. A limited powered examination of the receiving assembly's decay would require separate checks of the isolation control ring, discharge route, and stop mechanism before approval tomorrow.

Luna left the opinion on the table. She would operate the external ring if authorized. Nothing had been switched on, and she did not require Adrian to attend.

“If I'm still symptom-free tomorrow and Hensley agrees, I'd like the outer mechanical stop position,” he said. “Only that. I won't enter the observation light path.”

“You can change your mind.”

Elena returned with her sealed business letter. She confirmed that tomorrow still required independent review. Today's uneventful recovery could not guarantee the next operation.

That night they read a few ordinary pages. Adrian occasionally glanced toward the doorway. The real corridor repeated no footsteps. Elena noticed and simply held the book steady, leaving the last line easy to find whenever he wanted to return.

Upstairs, C-17 rested under its original black hood. D-17 was separately sealed, its cell removed, every handle in the stop position.

The brief vision had not given him an answer.

It had left one question that tomorrow would still need to ask carefully.
