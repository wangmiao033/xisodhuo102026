# Chapter 264: This Time, They Waited for Zero

The last hammering on April 25 ended at dusk.

Outside the isolation room, Adrian held cold meat pies. The craftsman emerging from inside collected his coat before looking at the food.

“A pie won't buy another shift.”

“I came to say somebody's taking over tomorrow morning.”

The man smiled and accepted his portion. They ate beneath the window. Nobody explained how many lives another hour might save. After removing the old support that morning and installing its replacement that afternoon, his hands trembled too much for fine pliers.

Both small buffers had actually arrived. One lining ring had been the wrong size, costing half a day while it was replaced. The broken receiving layer remained in the evidence cabinet, not crushed into new material. The damaged support was gone; the external absorber had been replaced by an inspected unit with sufficient service life. Payment left a larger hole in the research budget than anyone had expected.

Elena didn't disguise it. She canceled the planned display platform and checked wages with the bookkeeper. Isabella retrieved a copied sheet claiming promised components had already arrived, returning that evening with genuine delivery signatures. Exhausted, they could only argue over the last half-pot of hot water.

“Patients first,” Elena said.

Isabella raised her supported left wrist. “You've finally acknowledged my advantage.”

Elena put the pot in her right hand and went to find another.

Luna didn't attend the nighttime review. During her short daytime consultation, she'd examined the near-branch measurement arrangement, requiring it to bypass the node's sluggish general indicator. Two duty technicians then checked the physical equipment. A question mark remained on her page. Someone else supplied the actual answer; her signature wouldn't cover what she hadn't seen.

By the morning of April 26, a verified measurement appeared beneath that question mark.

Luna was reading it when Adrian arrived. She'd abandoned her old wide laboratory sleeves lest loose fabric catch on her support. Her hair was loosely tied behind her neck; her right hand remained padded.

“Distrust screws I haven't tightened myself?”

“I was thinking your ribbon looks lovely.”

She glanced at him, then down. “No distracting the reviewer.”

“I'll say it again afterward.”

At the bottom of the page, she forgot to turn it. Suppressing a smile, her assistant placed the next sheet before her. Her ears pinkened. Anyone recording this incident, she declared, must also record the assistant laughing at the wrong line.

Fresh permission had followed yesterday's examination of the actual configuration, staffing, and residual-handling limits. This morning the site supervisor checked identities again, examined the distance between buffers, and required a demonstration that one branch's withdrawal wouldn't force the other to receive its burden. The independent stop chain remained outside them. Worst-case total residual energy stayed within its capacity.

Seated by the outer door, Viola inspected an ordinary route, not magic. A delivery cart had to move so Luna could leave. Site staff actually guarded the entrance; Viola's left arm never left its support.

Cecilia noticed an extra observer's chair. Staff hoped the candidate who had withdrawn would see their new achievement and feel reassured.

“Ask whether he wants to watch first.”

They removed the chair and rewrote the invitation so refusal was welcome. Nobody would sit at the test bench today. Hard covers visibly sealed the empty loads' rear connections.

The unpowered handover was untidy. A replacement technician habitually approached from the right; his sleeve brushed the record stand, obscuring a nearby gauge. Viola stopped him and asked him to try again with his own height in mind. Nobody scolded him. They moved the stand.

Its new position covered Adrian's carefully mounted instructions. He immediately peeled them away. The paper tore; he didn't make everyone wait for an equally attractive sheet.

“You spent all evening drawing that,” Isabella said.

“People's arms aren't built to match mine.”

Smiling, she held a curling corner with her right hand. Together they remounted the instructions lower on the opposite side, where even seated Luna could read them. She noticed a missing word in the stopping instruction. Adrian supplied it in front of her, rather than saying everyone knew anyway.

The delay meant nobody would have to protect themselves by remembering one organizer's habits.

Before either branch started, Adrian looked through the little window. Fresh material occupied the place where the white line had appeared. His memory hadn't been replaced. He heard the faint crack again; his stomach tightened.

Elena joined him without promising it couldn't recur.

“Where do you want to stand?”

He chose somewhere farther from the window, with a view of the independent gauges and clear of operators. “Here. If I rush forward, say my name.”

She stood where he'd chosen. Passing behind them, Isabella suggested that anyone intending to catch another basin should first ask whether the kitchen would lend a third.

He laughed, his shoulders easing.

Initial input was low and steady. Both needles rose; each buffer retained spare capacity. The first branch finished. Its residual load entered the first buffer while the second continued its scheduled operation, growing no heavier because its neighbor stopped. Until the first buffer cleared, the next stage's entrance stayed closed.

Waiting lasted long enough to make Adrian want to reach out.

Someone coughed beyond the window. The operator didn't look up from his gauge. Even after the first buffer reached zero, independent confirmation preceded the next stage. Adrian could feel how much he wanted that next movement to happen. The impulse had not disappeared merely because he understood it now. He stayed where he was, letting the people reading the instruments finish their work instead of offering his impatience as a reason to proceed. They weren't waiting until everybody thought enough time had passed. They were waiting until the residual burden was actually gone.

Adrian followed the recorder's pen. The interval erased from his handsome arrow now possessed sound, sweat, and an awkwardly long pause.

The next run shortened the interval between withdrawals, still using only the approved small total. This was where the earlier configuration had broken.

The first branch finished. The second followed.

Both buffers' readings rose. Adrian's fingers tightened; his eyes searched the window for a white line. The independent near-branch gauge gave its preset signal first. The operator cut upstream supply. Each residual load stayed in its own container; the external safety absorber remained ready.

No crack sounded.

Silence wasn't yet a result. They waited through the measured delay, then watched both readings fall and reported each buffer clear. Technicians checked that nothing flowed back into the other stopped branch. The old shared layer's local concentration hadn't reappeared.

Adrian finally realized he wasn't breathing.

“Breathe,” Elena murmured.

He obeyed, nearly coughing. Holding back a smile, she placed water within reach without making his embarrassment a public attraction.

The approved plan also required a check of an unexpected stop. The operator didn't know when, within the permitted window, the observer would call out. He knew only that stopping couldn't wait on anybody's pride.

The call came earlier than Adrian expected.

The technician acted immediately. The next stage remained closed. Residual energy entered the buffer with capacity already reserved for it; the other wasn't commandeered. The external chain recorded the whole event. Nobody had to wait for the node's own instrument to acknowledge danger.

At last both needles fell, physical checks finished, and the apparatus was disconnected.

“Repeated results were stable under today's conditions,” the technician said.

Not the kingdom is saved. Adrian still felt the words deserved his full attention. A small part of the gap formerly reserved for a person had genuinely been bridged by materials and actions instead.

Luna smiled at the window—not her usual calculated teasing smile. Her left hand moved toward the record. Her assistant raised it to a comfortable height, and she relaxed, reading the lines she had actually witnessed.

“I wasn't in there,” she said suddenly.

“I know.”

“It worked anyway.”

Adrian didn't tell her that made her unimportant. She looked happy and a little bereft, like somebody watching a pupil finally leave the doorway. What she'd taught, he said, could now protect her too.

She turned away. “You're choosing unusually difficult things to argue with today.”

“I can return to normal tomorrow.”

“Preferably not.”

Hearing the result, Viola first asked whether moving equipment remained necessary. Her wish to help stayed a question; people with the strength performed the lifting. Outside, Cecilia gave a visitor the simple facts: no human connection, stable withdrawal of two small loads, further assessment still required. Could he tell his wife there was still a way forward? After considering, she said he could tell her they had accomplished one small thing today that they couldn't accomplish before.

Lunch covered a long table in the outer yard: bread, apples, and reasonably hot soup, no wine. Isabella forbade reviewing the experiment with the soup basins. She was hungry enough to oppose everyone publicly over it.

“Her Highness has lost patience,” Luna observed.

“I retain my appetite.”

Adrian asked how many pieces Viola wanted before cutting her apple. Four, she said, then specified it wasn't a tactical arrangement. He laughed and complied. He neither fed her nor required her to demonstrate recovery for the sake of an apple.

Elena sat beside him and quietly exchanged her larger piece of bread for his. He noticed. Was this a reward?

“I can't finish it.”

“You just took two pieces.”

She glared. Accepting the bread, he murmured that he liked this favoritism she refused to admit. Her gaze rested on him briefly. She didn't take the bread back.

Cecilia tapped her cup with a spoon. A personal proposal: each could state one wish unrelated to testing, and nobody would make it a task. Isabella wanted to wake without an alarm. Viola wanted a dress that didn't have to accommodate her support. Luna wanted to finish the novel whose foolish lovers nevertheless avoided plugging holes with their bodies.

Elena wanted to walk down a street that didn't lead to a courtroom.

Adrian looked at her. He wanted to go with her. His answer excluded nobody from the table. The others merely heard him leave one genuine wish undiluted instead of dividing it into five equal portions.

Soup cooled. Work continued. Independent afternoon examination found no new damage within the tested range. Stable results went to the review office beside the failed results. The new configuration was secured for further assessment. Its success opened none of the five old locks.

The returning messenger wasn't smiling.

An urgent notice invoked Oswin's authority to preserve disaster facilities. By the following morning, he demanded the outer core console's duty token and selector component be handed to the Temple, with local observation copies suspended. Uncoordinated interpretation of readings, he argued, might obstruct final action.

Adrian put down the cup he'd just washed.

The two steady needles still seemed visible. The notice would take away their window onto the real danger.

Elena's first words weren't a curse.

“Who's on duty there now?”

They needed to find that person first. Today they had finally waited for zero. Tomorrow somebody wanted them to hear only an answer announced by someone else.
