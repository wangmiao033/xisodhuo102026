# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Three: The Knight Says She's Responsible for Me
### Chapter 82: The Knight Asks If He'll Come Along

On the morning of November 27, Viola entered the assembly building and glanced at the small storage cabinet.

She quickly looked away.

Her sword wasn't there. It remained in the garrison armory, and nobody in this room would give her permission to reclaim it today.

She knew that. She'd looked anyway.

Adrian Reed stood behind her without warning her against false hope. He simply accepted the copy of the decision from the clerk by the door and waited for her to approach the table herself.

Colonel Ward had taken off her traveling coat. The cup at the corner had changed, but the table was the same one she'd had leveled on the nineteenth.

“Listen to the decision first, then ask about each provision,” she said. “You needn't sign the receipt as soon as you hear something favorable.”

Viola rested her right hand on her knee while the clerk read.

The request to place her under guard at the garrison was denied. The verified inspection authorization, rescue sequence, and independent supply records did not support a finding that she posed a risk of destroying evidence or coordinating testimony merely because the registers had been lost. The destroyed documents remained a genuine loss. The rescue judgments and procedural questions would continue to be examined; the decision could not erase what had happened.

Second, the restriction preventing her from leaving Greymarch would be adjusted.

During registered daylight hours, she could use open civilian roads to visit the town's southern outskirts and specified relief accommodations, returning to report the same day. Ordinary journeys within that area required advance registration rather than a special investigative permit each time. Travel beyond the defined area, entry into military facilities, or participation in other investigations would still require separate approval.

This time, Viola raised her head.

“I can leave town?”

“Within the area and hours in the annex,” Ward said. “Look at the map.”

The clerk pushed a civilian road map toward her, containing no defensive deployments. South Bridge, the mill market, and the riverside relief houses were circled in ordinary ink. Willowford's damaged roads lay outside the boundary. So did Alder Watch.

The black line wasn't particularly attractive. But, for the first time, the town gate wasn't the end of her permitted road.

The command suspension imposed on the seventeenth remained in force. Her issued sword would stay in storage. She could not summon her former troops or use her rank as captain to issue orders to soldiers along the way. Neither the treason allegation nor the dispute over her deployment decisions on the fourteenth had received a final ruling.

One officer asked her to state the distinction as she understood it.

“My ordinary freedom of movement has been expanded,” Viola said. “My command hasn't been restored, and the case isn't closed.”

Her voice trembled slightly. She did not swallow the final sentence.

Her adviser confirmed the reporting location and how notices would be served, requesting written reasons for any additional restrictions rather than quiet reversals through verbal messages. Ward signed the record and explained that any new, specific risk would be examined through the proper procedure.

Viola read every page before picking up the pen.

She didn't write that she submitted to every possible arrangement.

She signed her name.

In the corridor, Owen was handing a duty response to the clerk. His eyes brightened at the paper in her hand.

“Captain, are you back?”

Viola stopped.

She wanted to say yes. The word was already on her tongue. If she said it, Owen might run to fetch her horse as he once had, and the entire corridor could return to its familiar shape in minutes.

“Not yet. I can go a few more places now. The command arrangements haven't changed.”

His face fell, then he tried to smile.

“Someone's selling roast chestnuts by South Bridge. Fresh delivery today.”

He was clearly searching for another piece of news worth giving her.

Viola looked at him and smiled.

“Thank you. I'll have a look.”

He saluted and went back without summoning an escort for her. She didn't call him back and turn that welcome enthusiasm into an extension of old orders.

Outside, Elena was having the carriage brought out of the wind. When Viola emerged, her first question wasn't about the decision.

“Did you sleep last night?”

“A little.”

“Then eat first. We've been waiting since this morning, and you're about to substitute a sheet of paper for lunch again.”

Viola looked down at the decision as though discovering it was unsuitable for consumption.

Lunch wasn't a victory banquet. The innkeeper gave her an extra egg and asked whether she'd be staying in the same room that evening.

“Yes.”

“Good. Being allowed out doesn't mean forgetting to bring your damp cloak back to dry.”

Viola agreed and tapped the egg gently against the table. The crack went crooked, leaving fragments stuck to her fingers. Adrian held the plate while she peeled it slowly with her right hand. Somehow, getting an ordinary egg into her mouth had become more difficult than she'd expected.

Elena didn't press her to offer any reflections. Cecilia was returning to the infirmary that afternoon and checking that the clean cloth had all been delivered. Everyone's affairs continued to exist. That finally allowed Viola to lean back in her chair.

Halfway through the meal, she looked at Adrian.

“I'd like to go to South Bridge this afternoon.”

“For chestnuts?”

“That too, perhaps.”

She realized she was once again finding other explanations for a simple request. Her right hand closed briefly on her lap before she tried again.

“I wanted to ask whether you'd come with me. I don't need you to help organize documents today.”

Adrian looked at her.

“I'd like to. But I can only walk a short distance, and if my chest bothers me, I'll return to the carriage.”

“All right.”

She almost launched into the entire route, then stopped to ask first.

“Would a carriage to the gate, then a walk to the bridge, suit you?”

“Yes.”

Elena moved her teacup to make space for the registration paper.

“I'm reviewing accounts with the trading house this afternoon,” she told Adrian. “I'd like to continue the travel book after dinner.”

“So would I.”

They exchanged a smile. Viola returned her attention to the map. His agreeing to go out with her had not invalidated anyone else's plans.

Before leaving, she met Elena by the stairs.

Elena was struggling to tie her shawl one-handed, keeping her injured hand still. Viola asked whether she could help and waited for consent before threading the loose end through.

“This is harder than fastening a uniform.”

“It didn't used to be this difficult.”

“I'm very grateful to you today.”

Elena looked at her, understanding that she wasn't talking about the shawl.

“Protecting Bell also protects his ability to say things we don't like,” she replied. “Some of those accounts may still cause me headaches.”

“If they uncover something I need to explain, I'll answer.”

“Good.”

The shawl was finally secured. Viola stepped back, but Elena stopped her once more.

“If he says he's tired, don't hide where you wanted to go. You can ask another time. Don't make him guess.”

Viola paused, then nodded.

“I'll tell him.”

No ceremony awaited her at the south gate that afternoon.

The guards checked the registration and asked when the accompanying carriage would return, then let her through. Behind her, someone hurried to deliver goods. The gate hinges creaked as it was pushed wider.

She couldn't help looking back once.

No one ordered her to stop.

Outside, a woman pushed a handcart carrying two removed window panels. Recognizing the knight who'd stayed beside the last evacuation wagon, she asked when she could go back to Willowford to repair her house.

Viola looked at the broken boards. She didn't promise a date she couldn't know just to make this day happier.

“We still have to wait for the road and housing safety arrangements. I can show you where to register your need.”

The woman sighed, asked which desk she needed, and pushed her cart onward. She neither blamed anyone in disappointment nor felt less urgency about her lost roof because the knight could finally leave town.

Viola watched her go and withdrew the hand she'd almost offered to steady the cart. She could care about other people and still remember her own injury.

Adrian looked back as well, following her gaze to the gate. Then he continued beside her, without explaining the moment into something larger than it was.

The thin roadside frost had melted. Wheels pressed tracks of varying depths into the wet ground. South Bridge wasn't far from the gate; the mill market lay beyond it. At the bench they'd agreed on, Adrian stopped to check that the wood was dry.

This time, Viola didn't ask him to manage a few more steps.

She sat at the other end and removed her right glove. Her cloak covered the empty place at her hip. The bandage on her left arm was still inconvenient, but it didn't prevent her from watching a duck monopolize an entire stone by the water.

“It won't let the others past.”

“It may not have received the handover notice.”

“Should I write one?”

“Check whether it understands first.”

She laughed, almost forgetting to keep her voice down. A woman carrying baskets across the bridge turned, recognized her, and left them uninterrupted.

The chestnut stall was only a few steps away. Viola went to buy two separate bags. When she returned, one was particularly full.

“He said that one's cheaper today,” she explained, a little helplessly.

“Did you ask why?”

“He said because he felt like it.”

Adrian accepted his bag without demanding a more formal justification from the vendor. Steam rose through the paper. He placed the hottest chestnut near the edge to cool.

Viola didn't hurry to peel hers either. She looked toward the market across the bridge. A tinker called out, a family chose wooden spoons, and children clustered around a spinning toy windmill.

“I'd meant to walk as far as the mill,” she said.

Adrian turned.

“It's all right if we don't today. I just wanted to tell you.”

He touched his chest. There was no discomfort, but he was already tiring. He could probably push himself to reach the mill. That wouldn't shorten the return journey.

“I'll stop here for today.”

“All right.”

She answered simply, neither so disappointed that he felt obliged to change his mind nor withdrawing her wish as though it had never mattered.

“If you'd like to ask another day, I'll give you a real answer then too,” he added.

Viola looked into her chestnuts. The steam brought a little color to her ears.

“Then I'll save the question.”

A broken chestnut dropped onto the paper. She started to offer him a whole one, then stopped and asked whether he preferred the larger ones or the softer ones.

“The softer ones.”

She handed him what he'd asked for and ate the other herself.

They remained until the shadows moved across half a plank. There were no escort reports, and no one required them to fill the silence with gratitude. The carriage waited by the road. Having finished lunch, the driver patiently untangled a stem of grass from one horse's reins.

Viola gathered the shells. When she rose, she didn't immediately take the position in front of Adrian. She walked beside him and discovered that letting another person choose his own steps didn't make the road disappear beneath her feet.

Back in town, she reported under the revised conditions. The clerk stamped the time and returned her copy. Whether she was late would depend on the actual hour. Nobody moved the clock backward as a gesture of kindness.

Looking at her name and the recorded return time, she finally believed that the short journey hadn't been a momentary indulgence she had imagined.

That evening, Dr. Shaw sent the arrangements for Martin. His condition permitted a limited supplementary interview the following morning, under the existing protection, with his adviser present and the doctor able to stop it at any time.

The commission had only a few proposed questions. One asked whether anyone had returned to the outpost with a settlement form after the cargo left on the thirteenth and required him to acknowledge it afterward.

Adrian read the copy and returned it to Bellamy rather than leaving it on the communal dining table.

The relevant military supply offices had received the preservation and production notices. By the next morning, they would have to answer where the originals were and who held them. The questions now had a particular road to follow toward people who had not been asked before.

Viola came downstairs in a coat with loose sleeves. She placed the remaining chestnuts in the middle of the table and asked who wanted some.

Elena took one, Cecilia another. Adrian moved the travel book beyond the reach of the soup bowls.

Night slowly gathered outside the window.

At the infirmary door, the neutral guards changed shifts as recorded. Inside, Martin rested, still unaware how many people would want him to remember tomorrow, and how many would want him never to remember at all.
