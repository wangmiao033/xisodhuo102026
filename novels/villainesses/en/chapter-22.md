# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume One: A Betrothal Written in Blood
### Chapter 22: No More Playing the Hero Alone

A fine rain was falling over the capital on October second.

Adrian finished the last bite of toast and made an announcement.

"I'm going out to buy a new pair of boots this morning."

Elena looked up.

"Aren't those boots you're wearing?"

"The soles leak."

"Yesterday you said your bedroom window leaked."

"The capital has a terrible damp problem lately."

She set down her spoon.

"Which shop are you going to?"

"South Street."

"Didn't the bootmaker move to North Market last month?"

Adrian closed his mouth.

At the far end of the table, Duke Edmund slowly folded his newspaper.

"Young man, geography is an unfortunate subject to choose for a lie."

Adrian considered hiding his face in his soup.

The trouble had started with a public maintenance diagram he'd found the night before.

According to the old courthouse accounts, the monthly Fate Maintenance Fee was reimbursed through the B-17 Treasury clearing reference.

But the building plans also showed an underground equipment room closed to the public. Someone had been replacing preservation equipment there for the last seven years.

If the workers kept an access register, perhaps it would reveal what the money had actually purchased.

Unfortunately, the equipment room stood beside the sealed judicial archives. Visiting it required another permit.

Adrian had been thinking of asking one of the old guards whether the room had a secondary entrance.

Just asking.

That part was important.

After breakfast, Elena intercepted him in the corridor.

"How are your boots?"

"Miraculously cured."

"Wonderful. Then let's talk about something else in the study."

She walked ahead.

Adrian followed with the sinking feeling of a man approaching a second breakfast interrogation.

On the desk lay yesterday's risk register.

His name occupied the first substitute position. Four other names remained redacted.

Beside it was a hand-drawn plan of the old courthouse.

He stopped.

"Where did you find that?"

"You left it in the tea room."

"I was just sketching."

"Your sketch says 'avoid guards here.'"

Adrian looked out the window.

"Geographical research."

"Does your geographical research usually include a return carriage marked 'Do not inform House Rosenberg'?"

He had no reply.

Elena didn't raise her voice.

"You promised you wouldn't investigate dangerous places alone."

"I wasn't planning to go inside."

"Your map appears to disagree."

"I only wanted to keep you out of trouble. You barely escaped an execution. Now there's another list with my name on it."

She studied him.

"Have you forgotten what you told me in the greenhouse yesterday?"

He hadn't.

He'd said he was afraid.

He'd promised not to mistake silence for protection again.

Not even a full day had passed.

Adrian bit his lip.

"I need to find out what's in that equipment room. If too many people show up, someone could empty it before we get there."

"If you go alone and they detain you, we won't even know where to look."

"You're right."

"I know."

"...Could you occasionally let me finish arguing before you win?"

She'd been trying to remain stern.

A laugh escaped.

"Go ahead. Make your case."

He looked at her.

His smile faded.

"I hate the idea of anyone getting hurt because of me. Especially you."

Elena straightened the map.

"I feel the same way about you. But I dislike being excluded from the decision even more."

She didn't demand a promise that he'd never face danger.

She didn't propose locking him in his room under guard.

"You can choose not to go," she said. "Or apply for permission and go with witnesses. Just don't lie to me."

Adrian nodded.

"Then I'd like to invite you to come with me."

"Where?"

"To Hale's office. We should start with the paperwork."

This time, her smile needed no effort at all.

The old courthouse's maintenance section was managed by the municipal facilities office, not the Judicial Office's archive division.

Once Hale established the proper authority, he submitted an application for an on-site inspection of public maintenance records.

The facilities supervisor was reluctant.

"That section's been closed for years. Nothing worth seeing."

"We're asking for public equipment maintenance records," Hale explained. "We won't touch sealed court files or any Temple-owned apparatus."

The supervisor examined the Rosenberg authorization and the independent notary's credentials.

At last he opened his register.

"I can take you to the outside inspection area. Beyond that door is a section held in trust for the Temple. I can't authorize entry."

Adrian was disappointed.

But a certified document would help more than breaking into a room and being arrested as a thief.

The inspection station sat below a flight of stone steps. It smelled of wet plaster. Brass dehumidifying lamps stood beside stacks of worn wooden crates.

The supervisor brought out an equipment replacement list.

October first: replacement preservation lamp cover.

September twenty-fourth: two rolls of moisture-resistant waxed cloth.

September seventeenth: one set of testing wires.

Adrian stopped at the last line.

"Do these need replacing every month?"

"Lamp covers every three months. Cloth as needed. The wires are checked routinely."

"Who pays?"

The supervisor showed him the bottom of the page.

"The municipality pays in advance, then charges the Old Records Preservation Office."

That name again.

Elena compared the maintenance list with their B-17 account extract.

"But these materials cost far less than the monthly Fate Maintenance Fee."

"Because we're only paid for equipment upkeep," the supervisor said. "There's another fixed transfer for Temple archive maintenance. That doesn't go through us."

"Is there a transfer acknowledgment?"

He hesitated.

"I can't show you the restricted payment entries. But the institutional recipient on the handover receipt is public information. You may copy that."

He brought out a municipal receipt index.

No secret runes.

No magical sigils.

Just a row of month-by-month transfer numbers and an acknowledgment stamp beside each one.

The Treasury had paid the monthly fee to the Old Records Preservation Office.

That office then forwarded money to a Temple archive custody desk.

"Who receives it?" Adrian asked.

The supervisor pointed beneath the stamp.

"Registered custody contact."

The name was:

**Evelyn Vale.**

Adrian read it aloud.

"A Temple records officer?"

"That's the name on the contact register. I've never met her. I can't say whether she personally handled any money."

Elena carefully noted the limitation.

They also examined the plan of the equipment room.

A second doorway connected it to the old court's inner courtyard. Access was controlled by a separate Temple register.

Adrian stared at the diagram.

His proposed route *around the guards* was, in fact, a marked emergency exit.

The place he'd nearly risked breaking into yesterday had an official access procedure if you asked the correct department.

Elena glanced at him.

"How do you feel now?"

"Like I nearly got myself arrested because I can't read an architectural plan."

"Much more honest."

They had the maintenance index copied under the notary's supervision. They didn't request restricted files or touch the locks.

They were about to leave when a maintenance worker entered carrying a toolbox.

He stopped on seeing visitors.

"Boss, are we doing today's wiring inspection or not? Someone from the Temple told us to stay out of the inner courtyard."

The supervisor frowned.

"I haven't received a stop-work order."

"Said it was an emergency instruction."

Hale asked whether the worker had anything in writing.

The man produced a short note marked *Suspend Work*, with no signature or reference number.

"If the inspection is canceled and something breaks, who's responsible?" Elena asked.

The supervisor looked distinctly displeased.

"Under our contract, the ordering party must sign a suspension. Otherwise the fault falls on us."

Adrian's first instinct was to use the distraction to slip toward the inner door.

He took a step.

Stopped.

"We can record the discrepancy," he said. "We shouldn't use their contract dispute as an excuse to enter without permission."

Elena glanced back.

Something in her expression softened.

The supervisor inserted the note into the day's work log, recording that a verbal stop-work instruction had been received but not verified. The notary witnessed the entry.

The worker, who'd been afraid of being dismissed for disobedience, relaxed.

"There's something else," he said. "That Fate Maintenance Fee isn't our pay. Last month a man wearing a gray Temple records armband came to count the old lamp covers. He didn't touch the equipment. Just read the numbers on each crate."

"Did you see his face?" Adrian asked.

"Not properly. Hood stayed up."

"Did he sign anything?"

"Yes. A sealed-transfer book. Not the repair register."

The supervisor explained that equipment maintenance and sealed records transfers were managed by different offices.

That might explain the different amounts.

It didn't establish what the larger fee was purchasing.

Hale recorded the worker's statement separately and asked him to check the wording.

Adrian didn't demand that he identify a face he hadn't seen.

Instead, he asked whether any Temple employee could wear such an armband.

"Not generally. The Old Contract Records Office uses gray. But I've only seen it before at handovers. Don't put me down as an expert."

"Of course."

Adrian was beginning to understand why investigations in games always seemed so easy.

In a game, the right non-player character simply announced the villain's identity.

Here, a useful witness was someone who could explain what he'd seen—and what he hadn't.

Elena thanked the worker.

The man bowed awkwardly.

On the steps, Adrian asked, "Does it strike you that everyone we meet is teaching me how to do my job?"

"You said you wanted to learn."

"Why does every promotion come with more things I don't know?"

"Because the responsibilities increase."

"Then surely my salary should increase too?"

She laughed.

Before they left, Hale requested a copy of their original inspection authorization.

"Why?" Adrian asked. "We're already in the visitor register."

"Proof you were authorized to come and proof you actually came are two separate matters. If someone accuses you of trespassing later, you'll want both."

Adrian carefully copied the authorization number and signed where instructed.

It cost him a few minutes.

It might save them hours of defending themselves one day.

By the time they stepped outside, the rain had stopped.

Adrian closed his file.

"Do you find me terribly troublesome?"

"Yes."

He sighed.

"But today you invited me, waited for permission, and let other people do their jobs. That's better than yesterday."

"Only yesterday?"

"Better than the day we first met, too."

"You nearly sent me to the dungeon that day."

"You nearly ran away."

They laughed together.

The carriage waited at the curb.

Adrian opened the door, but Elena didn't get in at once.

"You told me yesterday you were afraid some dangers would fall on you alone."

"Yes."

"I can't take those risks away from you. But if someone tries to take away your right to choose merely because you're investigating, I'll stand beside you."

Adrian looked at her.

"Not in front?"

"No. And not behind."

He held out his hand.

"Then side by side."

She took it.

On the way home, Hale sent a supplementary note.

Evelyn Vale was indeed listed as a Temple archival contact. Establishing which documents she actually received, and on what dates, would require formal transfer records between the Temple and the Judicial Office.

Adrian looked at the copied name.

Royal confidential money.

The Old Records Preservation Office.

A Temple records contact.

Evelyn Vale might have been an innocent name on a form.

Or she might not.

Either way, he knew one thing.

The next time he planned an investigation, he'd leave the map where Elena could see it.
