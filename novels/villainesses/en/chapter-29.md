# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume One: A Betrothal Written in Blood
### Chapter 29: No Engagement Required

On the morning of October ninth, Adrian checked his bow tie before leaving for court.

Not because he cared what Prince Leon thought.

Elena had warned him that the newspapers were perfectly capable of interpreting a nervous tug at his collar as proof that another engagement was about to be announced.

"How do I look?" he asked Martha.

She inspected the knot.

"At least you don't look as though you're trying to hang yourself."

"Is that a compliment?"

"From me? The highest."

Adrian turned.

Elena stood in the doorway in a simple, dark-blue dress, carrying the evidence schedule Hale had prepared. She wore none of the jewels the royal family had given her.

A small silver rose, the crest of House Rosenberg, was pinned to her collar.

"Still frightened?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Of what?"

"That we've spent weeks investigating this, and in the end a forged document will be enough to win."

"Then bring the genuine documents."

She handed him the case file.

Her smile was slight.

It helped more than he wanted to admit.

The independent review court convened at nine.

Every public bench was occupied. The Archives Preservation Society had sent two independent observers, with Viscount Harding in the second row. The South Street stationer, the warehouse gatekeeper, and Matthew Holt waited to testify.

Duke Edmund sat near the front with the expression of a man obliged to attend an exam in which he expected every candidate to fail.

Prince Leon was there too.

He sat behind the Crown's counsel, not beside the judge.

When Adrian met his gaze, the prince didn't smile.

The clerk read the scope of the hearing.

The court would review the source and chain of custody of Evidence Item 714—the evidence used to accuse Elena Rosenberg of disclosing the capital's eastern defenses—and determine whether the Crown wished to continue the prosecution.

It would not decide the meaning of historical case R-074.

It would not pronounce judgment on the Temple's entire archival system.

Adrian welcomed the distinction.

The point was to finish the case that could be finished. He wasn't going to allow a seven-year-old mystery to keep Elena under a treason accusation indefinitely.

The Crown's advocate spoke first.

The disputed letter had entered the palace through an established registration channel, he said. A date entered incorrectly did not, by itself, prove the contents were false.

Hale stood.

"We agree that a clerical irregularity is not proof of forgery. That is why we ask to examine the evidence's provenance rather than speculate about motives."

The first witness was the stationer.

Under the notary's supervision, he produced the original ledger from September sixteenth. Six boxes of blue sealing wax had gone to the Rosenberg warehouse. Three more had been collected by Henry under a royal loan order. The payments appeared independently in the merchants' guild's monthly books.

"I can tell you what I sold," the old man said. "I can't tell you what anyone did with it afterward."

The Crown didn't challenge that answer.

Henry testified next.

He admitted misusing a purchasing advance. He also confirmed handling the wax transfer requested by Clay's purchasing staff.

"I did wrong," he said. "But I signed that receipt. I saw the royal purchasing officer load the boxes."

Elena neither coached him nor looked away.

House Rosenberg's investigation of the missing advance would continue. His willingness to testify didn't erase a separate wrongdoing.

Matthew's voice still trembled when he took the stand.

Hale showed him the preserved tracing of the R-074 transfer sheet, recovered yesterday under independent supervision.

"It said 'temporarily held, pending verification,'" Matthew testified. "I was later told to change it to 'receipt complete.' I refused."

"Can you identify the superior who gave the order?" the judge asked.

"No. Someone passed it down verbally. There was no signature. I won't name someone I can't identify."

The clerk recorded his words exactly.

Next came the issue register for a historical judicial seal.

On September seventeenth of this year, an old circular stamp template had been checked out under a request citing Evidence Item 714.

The review court had authorized emergency production of the current-year tool register. A certified copy had arrived that morning.

The borrower field contained an internal code assigned to the Judicial Office's evidence department.

According to the office's own authorization table, that code belonged to—

**Edwin Clay.**

Whispers erupted through the gallery.

Adrian remembered the clerk who'd recorded evidence using tomorrow's date at the palace.

At last, the issue register identified something more concrete than a man who merely looked suspicious.

But the judge didn't declare Clay guilty.

"Whether this loan was authorized, whether Mr. Clay took possession himself, and what the seal was used for remain matters for investigation. The formation of the evidence, however, plainly requires independent scrutiny."

Two blue jewelry cases were placed on the examination table.

The one from the Rosenberg estate had an uninterrupted preservation record. A shallow mark remained where its hidden compartment had been opened.

The case registered at the palace as Item 714 had no such mark.

Two craftsmen examined both under supervision.

The design was the same.

The wood grain and joinery dimensions were not.

"They are not the same case," the examiner concluded. "That fact alone cannot establish who placed the letter inside either one."

The judge ordered the conclusion entered without embellishment.

The palace gate officer testified as well.

He confirmed that a sealed blue case had entered the ballroom through the side passage on September seventeenth, and that Edwin Clay had been present.

"Did you see a letter inside?" the judge asked.

"No. The case was closed. I wasn't permitted to open it."

"Did you observe anyone take the case out and bring it back?"

"Not that I saw. But I wasn't watching the passage the entire evening."

Adrian was oddly grateful.

The guard hadn't suddenly acquired perfect vision merely because his testimony helped their side.

A materials examiner then presented magnified images of the sealing wax.

It contained traces of silver-colored powder consistent with the stationer's special batch. Similar wax could have been made elsewhere with the same formula.

It wasn't a unique identification.

The Crown's advocate seized on that.

"If the material isn't unique, how can the respondent claim our letter is false?"

Hale didn't raise his voice.

"Because the Crown's original account was that the letter was discovered in the jewelry case sent to House Rosenberg. We now have two physically different cases, neither of whose movements has been fully explained. We're not substituting a wax formula for an argument. We're asking when, where, and by whom the letter entered the evidence case."

The advocate turned several pages.

He could not produce a complete transfer record.

At the judge's direction, the Crown formally acknowledged that this critical portion of the provenance could not presently be verified independently.

Elena's grip on her fan loosened.

After so many days, someone had finally put the problem into an official transcript.

Not dismissed it as a noblewoman refusing to accept her fate.

The examiner also compared the historical seal template against impressions from the letter. A small notch occurred in a similar position.

That pattern was potentially identifying.

But a full comparison of the actual seal and original wax impression would be necessary to establish whether the same tool had been used.

"What can we safely conclude today?" the judge asked.

Hale answered.

"The physical items contradict a central part of the asserted provenance. The original evidence ledger was destroyed, and the surviving transfer accounts are inconsistent. Mr. Clay was the registered holder of a historical seal associated with this matter. We do not yet have evidence establishing who physically wrote the letter."

The Crown's advocate fell silent.

Prince Leon stood.

He requested permission to add a statement as a royal witness.

The judge allowed it.

"Before September seventeenth, I received a warning from the Temple's archives committee," Leon said. "At the time, I believed Elena Rosenberg should leave the capital. I can no longer establish that the warning was supported by reliable evidence."

He paused.

"I withdraw my support for any attempt to pursue a treason charge against her on the strength of Item 714."

Elena regarded him without triumph.

"Your Highness, that doesn't erase your public accusation."

"I know."

Leon offered no excuse.

The Crown's counsel conferred quietly with the prosecutor authorized to act on the Crown's behalf.

After a brief recess, the prosecutor submitted a formal application to withdraw the charge. The evidence had been materially undermined, he said, and no independent evidence presently supported proceeding with the treason accusation against Elena.

The judge verified the prosecutor's authority and turned to Hale.

"Any objection?"

"None to withdrawal. We ask that preservation orders and the independent inquiry into possible evidence tampering remain in force. This matter should not disappear merely because the treason charge ends."

"Granted."

The gavel fell.

"The present prosecution of Lady Elena Rosenberg for treason is formally terminated. The disputed evidence shall remain available for independent investigation. No finding is made today concerning the personal guilt or liability of those connected to its handling."

A low murmur spread through the courtroom.

Adrian didn't stand immediately.

He looked at Elena.

On September twentieth, all they'd achieved was stopping an immediate punishment.

Today, she no longer had to defend herself as a treason suspect every time an unverified letter appeared.

Elena turned and held out her hand.

"That part is finally over."

Adrian took it.

"You can leave the house without carrying a summons."

"And perhaps you can stop treating breakfast like an interrogation."

He laughed.

The duke approached and thanked Hale and the witnesses individually.

Only then did he turn to Adrian.

"Well done."

"Thank you, Your Grace."

"Your salary will remain unchanged next month."

"...Could I enjoy the victory for five minutes before we discuss wages?"

Even the duke smiled.

After the hearing, Viscount Harding congratulated Elena.

"You let the record speak rather than asking the observers to take your side. That takes discipline."

"The record wouldn't exist without ordinary people willing to sign their names to what they saw," she replied.

Harding nodded toward Adrian.

"And your young aide. I heard he didn't know a clearing reference from a wine label when he started."

"I still don't pretend to understand every account," Adrian admitted. "But I do know a sensational headline can't replace a ledger."

"Will you remain in the duke's service?"

Adrian looked at Elena.

She didn't answer for him.

"I'd like to stay," he said. "Not because I have to."

She turned her head slightly, ears already pink.

The duke noticed and sighed.

"I suppose I must get used to an extra chair at breakfast."

"Father," Elena protested.

"I said a chair," he replied. "What did the two of you hear?"

Adrian suspected his probation would be more difficult than the treason hearing.

Once the gallery had emptied, an independent investigator brought news.

Clay's unsupervised access to evidence had been suspended pending inquiry. His handling of historical seals and transfer records would be examined separately.

Evelyn Vale, however, was no longer at her Temple office.

Her workroom had been cleared.

"She fled?" Adrian asked.

"We can establish that she's currently unavailable and the room has been emptied," the investigator replied. "We cannot yet establish that she fled to avoid responsibility."

Adrian nodded.

There was still a long way to go.

Outside the courthouse, reporters waited on the steps.

"Lady Rosenberg! Now that the charges have been dropped, will you celebrate by announcing your engagement to Mr. Reed?"

Elena stopped.

"I don't need an engagement to prove I'm innocent."

"Then what is Mr. Reed to you?"

She glanced at him.

For once, he didn't rush to answer for her.

"We worked together to establish the facts," she said. "What we become after this is for us to decide."

She took his hand and walked down the steps.

Adrian thought it felt more like a promise than any magical contract could have.

That evening, a formal letter arrived from the Temple of Fate.

A special investigator from the Old Contract Records Review Office would arrive in the capital on October tenth.

The document bore a name Adrian remembered from the Temple's correspondence weeks earlier.

**Cecilia Saintclair.**

Hale laid it on the table.

"She'll be here tomorrow."

"The saint?"

"Yes."

"Is she investigating Evelyn Vale?"

"The letter doesn't say," Elena answered. "But she's bringing historical betrothal records associated with R-074."

Adrian's smile froze.

"Another engagement?"

Elena's voice became alarmingly calm.

"Didn't you tell the reporters that we could decide our future for ourselves?"

"Yes."

"Then tomorrow, let's ask the saint why an old document is trying to decide it for us."

Adrian had won a court case.

Apparently, that hadn't removed all danger from his life.

The danger had merely changed its name.

From treason to betrothal.
