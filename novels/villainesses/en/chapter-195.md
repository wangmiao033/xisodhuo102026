# Chapter 195: The Saint Would Not Bow to Fate

Facing a hundred bowls, Cecilia declared Adrian's work more important than hers.

“At worst I mispronounce a word. Knock over this table, and nobody gets lunch.”

Steam filled the meal house on the morning of February 22. Adrian supported a stack of washed pottery more carefully than he'd handled yesterday's questions. The woman in charge had agreed to his visit, but his apron almost reached the floor. Two knots still left him looking like an apprentice in stolen kitchen clothes.

“Could you shorten the ties?”

Cecilia stepped behind him and folded them twice, deftly. Her ceremonial sashes were considerably more demanding.

“What kind of blessing is this?”

“One that prevents you from tripping over yourself. Usually effective.”

She patted the knot and gave him a bowl. People were already waiting beyond the windows. Some recognized him and whispered. Others wanted to know whether today's soup contained beans.

The deacon leading prayers arrived with a newly copied text. Cecilia dried her hands and read: May those stained by predestined sin acknowledge the debt incurred by their birth.

She turned the page over to check the context.

“Which liturgy is this from?”

“An addition prompted by the recent warning. People need reassurance.”

“Who established that anyone was born guilty of this?”

He looked past her at Adrian, who was separating chipped bowls into another basket. People at the front of the queue noticed.

“The historical sources are clear. You've read them too.”

“What I've read has missing pages and attributions still under examination. What are you asking everyone here to acknowledge?”

He lowered his voice. They could discuss it privately. Prayers were about to begin; a public argument would only increase anxiety.

Cecilia placed the sheet on the table and held its curling edge.

“Then don't begin with that sentence.”

A woman near the front, clutching a cloth parcel, interrupted. If danger remained, shouldn't they ask for divine protection? Her husband had injured his leg when their roof collapsed last winter. He still couldn't stand for long. She knew nothing about old history, only that before some disasters, everyone good at talking insisted there was nothing to fear.

Cecilia stepped out from behind the table onto the same floor.

“Of course we can ask for protection. We can also ask whether the roof was maintained. Your husband's birth date shouldn't answer for the person who failed to repair it.”

The woman didn't immediately agree.

“But will he do it again?” She looked at Adrian.

He set down the bowls and approached. Cecilia turned as though to spare him answering, then let him speak.

“I can't promise I'll never make a mistake. Today I brought no historical object and I'm conducting no experiment. I'm distributing bowls. If I do something dangerous, you can identify it and I'll have to answer.”

“I don't want an argument.”

“You don't have to sit at my table.”

An undignified answer, perhaps, but her shoulders eased. She asked the cook about taking a portion home to her husband. The ordinary collection arrangement applied. She moved aside.

The deacon began gathering his prayer.

“You're reducing faith to separate little matters.”

“Eating and being accused belong to people's lives,” Cecilia said.

Some listeners agreed; others muttered. A young man by the door laughed and asked whether the saint forbade criticism because she wanted to marry Adrian. His voice carried across the table. Several bowls clinked together.

Cecilia flushed. Adrian felt anger sooner than she displayed it and stepped forward. She raised a hand, asking him to let her answer.

“I do like him.”

The man opened his mouth as though she'd delivered the evidence he needed. She didn't leave him the rest of her sentence.

“That's precisely why I cannot use my position to pronounce him innocent. Nor can you use my feelings to declare him guilty on the strength of an unfinished historical inquiry. If you have testimony, submit it where it can be examined. I'll make room.”

The room quieted briefly. Adrian watched her profile. Her directness didn't demand a public response from him. She endured the heat her own words had caused without looking to him for rescue.

The man muttered and retreated. The deacon asked whether she believed destiny meant nothing.

Cecilia removed her small prayer token and placed it on the table, leaving her hands empty.

“I once thought that repeating the title in an old record clearly enough would tell me whose side I belonged at. Now I know it can stop me hearing a person say no today.”

She glanced at Adrian, then back at the deacon.

“If my faith requires someone to be born guilty, it needs questioning. It doesn't need him on his knees.”

A spoon struck a pot in the kitchen. The soup was catching.

The woman in charge stepped between them and took the prayer. Meals would proceed. Communal recitation would use the familiar thanksgiving. The new passage would go to the office responsible for liturgy. Anyone wanting additional prayer could stay afterward; people waiting for lunch wouldn't be compelled to join it.

The deacon signed his objection beside her written decision and withdrew. He had no authority to close the kitchen.

Cecilia didn't follow. They resumed arranging bowls. Adrian found a hairline crack in the seventh and replaced it. An elderly woman complained that she'd eaten from worse in her youth.

“Hot soup could leak onto your legs.”

She examined it and took the replacement. “No need to be nostalgic about that.”

The first pot finally reached the tables. People who'd discussed destiny began discussing salt. The kitchen evidently considered salt cheaper than philosophy and had been generous. Cecilia tasted hers with the solemnity of finding another dubious prayer.

“May I request a small miracle?” she whispered.

“Add water.”

“How modest miracles can be.”

The cook brought a jug and told them not to drink beside her workbench looking like that. Cecilia almost choked laughing. Adrian moved her bowl, and they were sent behind the door to finish their half portions.

Not everyone became friendly. The woman collecting for her husband avoided them. The young man finished his soup without apologizing. But a child who'd only dared push bowls from the edge caught one before it fell and asked Adrian where the next stack belonged. He indicated the dry cloth. Together they set it down. The moment ended with unbroken pottery.

At the doorway, the woman with two portions turned back. Her parcel held a lidded jar, its carrying cord softened by hot water and beginning to slip.

Adrian started for a cloth, but she held it toward Cecilia. Cecilia supported the bottom and requested an old basket. She didn't insist the woman give it to Adrian to prove her earlier explanation had worked.

“My husband doesn't want visitors,” the woman said suddenly. “If you're thinking of coming to say something, I'm telling you now.”

“Nobody's coming today. Ask the person in charge about collecting for him next time.”

The woman looked at the empty cord on Cecilia's chest. Had she put down her token because she was angry with the divine?

“A little angry with people.”

For the first time, the strained face showed a faint smile. She lifted the basket and asked whether she owed for it.

“Return it next time,” the cook called. “Replace the cord before using the jar again.”

A clear arrangement. The woman finally left. Cecilia watched her cross a puddle, then wiped her token dry. Adrian didn't pursue a verdict on whether she was still afraid. The rain was ending, and the last run from the eaves fell loudly into an empty bucket.

After the rush, a message arrived from the deacon. Cecilia's invitation to lead next week's recitation was suspended pending discussion of her objection. She took the appointment slip from her book and folded it herself.

“You've lost next week's place,” Adrian said.

“Yes.”

“Will there be more?”

“They'll have to tell me. This is what I know now.”

She replaced her prayer token, winding its cord around her fingers twice before releasing it. He wiped the last bench beside her and asked whether she wanted some air.

In the rear courtyard, damp cloths brushed together on a line. Cecilia suddenly covered her face.

“I said that too loudly.”

“Which part?”

She peered through her fingers. He knew, but waited for her to lower her hands.

“The part about liking you. I meant to be more dignified.”

“You sounded dignified.”

“You mustn't pretend it wasn't awkward for you merely because I spoke for a principle today.”

Adrian leaned against a dry section of railing.

“I heard it. It matters to me. I don't want gratitude to give you an answer that isn't true.”

She watched him and slowly nodded. The breeze disordered her hair. She tucked it behind her ear herself.

“Then that's enough today. I still have next month's choir to practice for. Losing one recitation doesn't entitle disappointment to borrow my voice.”

“What are you singing?”

She sang the first phrase softly. He tried joining in and went flat on the second note. Cecilia wrinkled her nose and firmly requested silence. Even a saint's patience had musical limits.

“Apparently I'm also a danger to choirs.”

“We have eyewitnesses for that one.”

After laughing, she didn't make him try again. He stayed to hear the whole phrase. Her voice wound around the hanging cloths without needing the entire city to believe in her before it could reach the other side of the wall.

As they returned their aprons, a middle-aged woman waited beside the empty table. Binding paste marked her fingertips. She gave no name, only asked Cecilia where someone unwilling to tell an old story at a temple window might inquire about confidentiality.

Cecilia neither pulled her into a room nor asked Adrian to listen. She wrote the independent receiving office's address and inquiry hours. Whether to make a statement remained the woman's choice. If she feared revealing her residence, she could first ask how contact details were sealed.

The woman folded the paper twice and tucked it deep away. Before leaving, she looked at the public circular on the wall.

Adrian waited by the kitchen until she was gone.

“Do you know her?”

“No. She delivered some repaired account books.”

He studied the empty doorway without following. Before someone agreed to speak, there ought to be a road that didn't close around them.

Back at the house, Luna's assistant brought a new diagram. Would Adrian permit Beatrice to reexamine the technical traces on the sealed trial plate? Separate gray segments had been carefully drawn apart. Only observation periods were marked. There was no brass handle, no face from his recollection.

He agreed to the stated scope and asked whether he'd need to sit at the frame again.

No. Tomorrow used an ordinary light path and comparison material to establish how much the previous observation could actually support.

Adrian held the diagram beside the window. Light passed through the spaces between the lines. He'd heard enough people name empty places today.

Tomorrow, perhaps they could first let the gaps remain.
