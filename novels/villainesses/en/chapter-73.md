# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Three: The Knight Says She's Responsible for Me
### Chapter 73: She Left the Signature Line Blank

On the morning of November 20, the first thing Viola noticed in the statement the noble delegates handed her was her own name.

It had been written in a beautiful hand, followed by a blank long enough for her signature. She hadn't even taken off her gloves, and they had already arranged the day's most difficult decision into a movement of her wrist.

“Only a suggested draft,” Baron Harcourt said. “You're free to amend it, of course.”

The assembly building's stove burned fiercely. Representatives from several border estates sat around the long table, their coats free of mud from the countryside. The legal adviser Viola had chosen sat to her right and first asked the clerk to record that this was not a military interview and could not constitute an admission of guilt.

Adrian sat in the place allotted to him as her authorized companion. He too had been given tea, though no one had reserved a place for his name on the paper.

The draft acknowledged an error in the grain-requisition annex but attributed it to an overlooked transcription mistake at a lower level. Losses during Willowford's evacuation were ascribed to residents' failure to accept the prescribed resettlement arrangements promptly. As for the purchases and transfers involving Northroad Freight, the draft recommended leaving these to an internal inquiry and refraining from repeating them in public.

The last paragraph was the gentlest. If Viola would help restore the border's reputation, the houses would submit character references to the review board and consider continuing their support for the Blackwood family's place in the knightly order.

She read that paragraph for a long time.

The baron didn't hurry her. He even had a servant replace the cold tea.

“Your father served on this border as a young man,” he said. “We know what sort of person you are. We can't let one problematic annex burn everyone's good name to the ground.”

“The annex was substituted,” Viola said. “It wasn't a transcription error.”

“There's no need to describe something that's already been corrected in the harshest terms possible.”

Adrian looked at the exceedingly thin slices of lemon on his plate. Heard only once, the remark could almost have passed for kindness. He looked up and asked whether the clerk might retain the suggested draft in its original form, rather than keeping only the version Viola amended.

The baron glanced at him.

“Mr. Reed, we're trying to help a knight.”

“Then keeping both should make it easier to see what help was given.”

The legal adviser nodded and asked the clerk to note the time the draft had been delivered. Adrian didn't answer the remaining conditions on Viola's behalf. Nor did he pretend that a visiting administrative officer could shoulder the consequences for her if her family lost its backers.

A female delegate across the table put down her cup.

“This isn't all about reputations. Carters along the road are raising their prices, and two merchant caravans now refuse to take the north road at all. The country rents aren't coming in, and we still have bridge repairs to pay for. If people outside decide the entire border is involved in smuggling, a great many people won't survive the winter while you hunt down the next ledger.”

Viola looked at her. “How many evacuees has your estate taken in?”

“Forty-seven. And a few sick animals.”

“Receipts should be kept for those resettlement costs and submitted for verification. I'm willing to give a clear account of what I've seen.”

“So you do understand that an investigation can't go on indefinitely.”

“I understand that forty-seven people need to eat,” she said. “But this paper makes the villagers responsible for failing to evacuate promptly. We haven't finished investigating who told them to take that road.”

The woman said nothing more. Her fingers continued working the edge of the handkerchief in her lap. She hadn't suddenly become someone who cared for nothing but a lie.

The baron pushed a second sheet across the table. It was a recommendation letter, already drafted, stating that Viola sincerely acknowledged that her judgment had been rash and was willing to make maintaining order her foremost duty.

“Nobody is asking you to admit to treason.”

This time, Viola's gaze stopped on the words “recommend reinstatement.”

Adrian remembered the morning she had taken away the chipped cup. She had spent years earning the right to have those riders wait for her to speak in the morning light. Now someone was telling her that the road remained open, provided she first learned to keep quiet on another sheet of paper.

She took off her gloves and laid them on the corner of the table.

“If I sign, will you stop supporting the investigation into Northroad Freight?”

The baron didn't answer directly. “Unnecessary speculation should naturally cease.”

“And if tomorrow's investigation leads to a warehouse you fund?”

“You needn't bring every worst possibility into the room.”

Viola slowly breathed out.

“Then I can't accept this recommendation.”

A log cracked in the stove. She didn't tear up the paper, nor did she place every noble's name among her enemies. She laid the draft back on the table, pointed to three passages, and asked the clerk to record her objections.

The document from the fourth was a substituted annex. Delaying her return to town on the fourteenth had been her own decision, and she was willing to have it examined. What the residents, merchants, and lords had each done should be established from their respective records.

“I'll sign my own statement. Whoever is willing to recommend me doesn't get to decide what I saw.”

The baron was quiet for a moment, then took back the recommendation letter.

“You may lose some people who would otherwise have been willing to help you.”

Viola's fingertips touched the wooden button in her pocket.

“I know. Please don't make the shelters pay for it with the grain you've already promised them.”

That made the baron look up. Finally, he signaled to the clerk that the planned grain donations for resettlement would stand. The personal recommendation, however, would not be submitted for the time being.

There was no applause at the end of the meeting. No one followed her out to congratulate her on her courage. On the way out, Viola put one glove on backward, then silently took it off and tried again.

Adrian held the building's door for her without smiling.

“I really did want that letter,” she said.

“I saw you read it twice.”

“Do you think I wasn't firm enough?”

“I think you knew what you were turning down.”

She stood on the steps, her shoulders and back slowly relaxing. A cold wind blew along the street. Passersby detoured around a patch of frozen mud, making a winding path. None of them paid any attention to the negotiation that had just taken place upstairs.

Viola watched that path for a while, then suddenly asked, “Would you come somewhere with me this afternoon?”

“Tell me what for first.”

“To sit for a while. No reports, and I don't need you to witness anything.”

She sounded quite serious this time. Adrian agreed to half an hour, provided there was a seat out of the wind and he wouldn't have to undergo any physical fitness tests.

“Of course you won't.” Her answer came a little too quickly.

On the way back to the inn, he accompanied her to see the legal adviser first. A copy of the day's suggested draft was retained with its provenance recorded, and her refusal of the personal endorsement went into the meeting account. The adviser reminded her that character references were not a prerequisite for the review. One fewer letter wouldn't directly deprive her of a chance to present her defense, though the question of her family's funding could indeed have separate consequences.

After listening, Viola borrowed a sheet of ordinary writing paper.

She wrote home slowly. She didn't paint the baron as a villain or say that she had upheld her honor with ease. She explained which statements she couldn't sign and which forms of help she might lose, and asked her family to read the original text she had enclosed before responding to any inquiries.

Adrian waited for her in the corridor. A pot of herbs stood in the window, almost frozen through. He pushed the window a little closer to shut and heard the pen fall still for a long time inside the room.

When she came out, the envelope was still unsealed.

“The hardest part to write was that I can't promise they'll understand.”

“You can give them some time.”

“You can reconsider this afternoon, too,” she said quietly. “You don't have to feel you ought to accept my invitation because of what just happened.”

“I've already considered it. I want to go, and I really do need to sit down.”

Only after assuring herself that he wasn't forcing himself did she allow a small smile. The legal adviser took the letter and agreed to send it as ordinary family correspondence, with appropriate care taken over the passages involving material that hadn't yet been made public. The letter hadn't solved her family's difficulties. At least, though, it wouldn't leave her relatives to learn who she had been today from someone else's recommendation.

After lunch, Adrian finally understood that hesitation.

She had brought him to the civilian training ground inside the town.

Caravan guards and residents could rent the grounds. It had nothing to do with the military command she had already surrendered. At the far end of the open space, a wooden practice post had had its head knocked crooked. Bracers hung over the fence to dry in the sun. The elderly groundskeeper recognized her, brought over two earthenware cups, and quite naturally asked whether they wanted to borrow two wooden swords or one.

“Neither,” Viola replied.

The old man looked at Adrian with an expression of even greater understanding. “I see. You demonstrate, he watches.”

“No demonstrations, either.”

“What have you come for, then?”

Viola opened her mouth. Adrian had already sat down on the sheltered bench.

“At present, it seems we've come to be questioned by you.”

The old man went away laughing. The tips of Viola's ears were red. From her bag she produced a packet of roasted chestnuts, two clean cloths, and a cushion that had been meticulously brushed off.

“It gets the sun in the afternoon. No one will suddenly arrive with a military duty book.”

“Do you know anywhere else that gets the afternoon sun?”

She thought for a moment. “Outside the eastern stables.”

Adrian bowed his head, his shoulders shaking with the effort of not laughing. At last she laughed too and pulled over the other cushion herself, without angling her seat to guard the entrance.

The chestnuts were hot. He shifted one from palm to palm. Viola started to peel it for him, then stopped halfway through reaching out and asked, “Would you like help?”

“Yes. You can have the two hardest ones. I'll do the rest myself.”

She selected two as though accepting an assignment with an exceptionally clear scope.

A guard on the training ground kept practicing the same sidestep, his feet wearing a shallow hollow into the earth. Viola watched him for a while. Her fingers moved against her knee, but in the end she didn't get up to correct him.

“You want to go over,” Adrian said.

“He's going to turn his ankle.”

“Then tell him. Sitting with me doesn't mean pretending you've never held a sword.”

She looked at him, momentarily taken aback. Then she went to the fence and first asked the guard whether he would like a suggestion. He tried planting his foot in the new position and thanked her with a smile.

Her smile was brighter when she came back to the bench.

“So you didn't bring me here just because you had nothing else to do,” Adrian said.

She put the peeled chestnuts on a cloth. After a while, she answered, “This is somewhere I truly like. I wanted you to see it.”

She hadn't hidden the words inside a protection plan. He didn't treat them as a slip of the tongue to be casually teased, either.

“I'm glad you brought me.”

She lowered her head to drink and discovered the cup was empty only when she tried. Adrian passed her the teapot and very considerately declined to point it out.

Back at the inn, Elena was waiting for someone from the town office to collect a draft comparison of the commercial transport records. She accepted the remaining chestnuts with her uninjured hand and asked where they had been. Hearing that it was the training ground, she looked from Viola to Adrian.

“You've come back on your feet. The invitation must have gone well.”

“I stayed seated the entire time, which made it an especially great success.”

Viola had meant to explain. Hearing them laugh, she instead readily admitted that the tea there wasn't very good and they ought to bring their own next time.

Elena didn't make any decisions about that “next time” for them. Beneath the table, she lightly touched the side of Adrian's knee. He turned and smiled at her.

The arrival of the town official interrupted their brief merriment. Edith Bellamy, who accompanied him, belonged to Ward's outside review team and was responsible for investigating civilian evidence. She placed a fuel waybill on the table, noting that the original had already been secured separately.

Northroad Freight had delivered fuel to Alder Watch at the beginning of the month. The old post had long since been decommissioned. Its ground-level storerooms were legally leased for storage, and the town retained authority to inspect how the rented premises were being used.

A property clerk from the town office had visited that day to verify the lease term and been stopped at the entrance. From inside the yard, he had heard someone order the old paper cabinets and every delivery ledger cleared out by tomorrow evening. Even after the clerk presented the registration requirement, the person at the entrance had refused to give a name.

“This doesn't prove they're guilty,” Edith said. “But it's enough to apply for emergency preservation measures. We can't send a visit request first and wait patiently for a reply.”

She looked at Viola. “Do you know the roads around the old post?”

Viola nodded, then asked, “What authorization would be required?”

The lampwick on the table gave a small flicker.

This time, she hadn't first reached for the sword she had already surrendered.
