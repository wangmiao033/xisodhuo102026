# Chapter 106: The Second Experiment Finds an Old Date

On the morning of December 17, Luna handed Adrian a card bearing the date September 19.

His chest tightened. Then he noticed Newly made teaching control on the back. The ink was much fresher than the writing in the old files.

“A teaching control,” she said. “Made before bed last night. A memorable enough date?”

“Yes.”

Seeing his expression, she stopped smiling.

“I didn't know it meant anything to you. Shall I change it?”

He laid the card down.

“Not necessary. I wasn't expecting it at breakfast. Next time, tell me first that you wrote it yourself.”

“I will.”

Elena pushed the bread basket toward him. She did not ask about the execution document or require Luna to explain his history over breakfast.

“Teaching cards aren't edible. Distribute the actual controls first.”

Luna examined the two differently browned plates of toast and selected the softer batch. Viola accepted the jam with her right hand. She would watch until late morning, then collect confirmation of tomorrow's follow-up appointment. The final day of convalescent leave would not become a guard shift.

“If your experiment needs a knight who stays until supper, reschedule.”

“I only dare recruit friends who finish breakfast now,” Luna said.

“I'll volunteer for that,” Cecilia replied.

Their laughter did not erase Adrian's alarm. It let him return the card and keep eating.

While they cleared the table, Viola presented a flat tin decorated with two rather crooked birds. Inside were biscuits in clean paper, without chalk dust.

“I found one.”

He recognized the purchase she had considered unworthy of a military requisition.

“Very imposing.”

“The shopkeeper said it was for children's lunches.”

“Clearly capable of greater responsibilities.”

She smiled and closed the lid. If tomorrow's examination still required restrictions on her left arm, she would report them honestly. Fourteen days passing was not proof of healing.

She sounded steady, though her right fingers traced the tin's corner twice.

“Afraid of being left behind?” Adrian asked.

“A little. Everyone has important things to do. My important task is arranging for someone to look at my arm tomorrow.”

“Important indeed. It's the arm you'll want to hold a sword with.”

“A shield,” she corrected. Then she smiled at him. “But I'll accept the intention.”

He apologized for military knowledge that had not eaten enough breakfast. She gave him a biscuit to remedy the deficiency.

Cecilia remarked that she was glad she would not have to count readings while worrying whether a knight was secretly treating her arm as a repaired door hinge.

“You're all acquiring bad habits,” Viola said.

“From whom?” Luna asked from the doorway.

They looked at her together. She retreated, declaring the conclusion insufficiently verified.

In the corridor, Adrian asked before placing the tin in Viola's accessible right pocket. He did not take over tying her cloak. She fastened it herself, ears pink, and confessed that she had chosen the birds; they had not been the only design available.

“They reminded me of the wooden bird at the mill.”

“I remembered that day too.”

Her smile lasted.

Before going upstairs, Adrian paused on the porch with Elena.

“Do you want to withdraw from today's task?” she asked.

He considered it seriously.

“No. I can stay at the outer stop handle without leaning in to look. And agreeing doesn't mean I have to remain.”

“Then I'll sit where you can see me.”

“No business correspondence today?”

“This afternoon. The letters won't improve themselves because I sit here, or fall apart because you look at me.”

“That sounds more reliable than the captain's plans.”

“He has yet to discover planning.”

Upstairs, the instrument room had been cleared. W-3 remained out of service below; R-4 had not arrived under a different name. The new arrangement was labeled C-17 Preservation Observation. A fixed filter stood before the light source, with an external receiving plate held in its own black frame. Nothing touched the specimen.

The administrator and deputy inspector checked the seals. They opened the outer inspection seal, not the glass inner cover. C-17 stayed on its level cabinet support. The restorer confirmed that yesterday's hood had not pressed against the glass.

The restorer checked the hood’s feet one by one before asking the administrator to lift it. Their shadows moved across the cabinet floor, never across the fragile lining. Luna watched closely but kept her hands away. Yesterday they had fought for this small undamaged space; today, restraint was the only useful way to enter it.

“Controls first.”

Luna placed her newly written September 19 card in a separate frame. It had an ordinary afterglow coating behind the ink. Another frame held a clean blank. Neither path involved C-17 or the sealed receiving plate from Adrian's earlier observation.

Under the weak light, the teaching date appeared clearly on a fresh receiving surface. After closure, its characters faded together within the known interval. The blank produced only a brief edge glow, no second date.

Elena recorded it and let Luna check. Cecilia counted without rushing. Viola sat by the door, her left forearm on its small cushion.

“No one's heart has been proven this time,” she observed.

“Materials are heartless,” Luna replied.

While the teaching plate finished fading, Cecilia noticed Adrian's thumb brush his coat pocket. His small book was there, a length of bookmark thread showing.

“You can touch that if you're nervous. It has no special properties.”

“I wasn't expecting it to switch off the lamp.”

“Good. I only want it to remind you there are still tea invitations outside.”

“And dinner,” Elena added.

Luna regarded her empty cup, then asked whether she could join an ordinary walk afterward.

“Depending on the weather,” Adrian said. “Not the result.”

She smiled and returned the cup to the drinking table outside.

The teaching frame was removed. Luna and the administrator checked the actual receiving plate: unused, inert, numbered D-17 at its edge, from a registered batch. Ordinary slanting light revealed no writing. Elena and the deputy inspected it separately before the administrator fitted it to the outer frame.

Adrian stood farthest from the apparatus, both gloves on. His left hand hung beside him. His right held the mechanical blackout handle. He supplied no power and brought no scar near the specimen.

“All right?” Luna asked.

“All right.”

“You look more solemn than that teaching card.”

“I don't fade uniformly.”

She laughed, easing her shoulders.

The administrator posted a Please Wait notice, leaving the ordinary corridor clear. Downstairs, deliveries continued and a knife tapped against a kitchen board. Adrian found himself grateful that nobody had required the whole tower to hold its breath for an answer.

Before the reading, the outer shutters needed alignment. Luna and the administrator held one handle each while the restorer examined the slit. His side was slightly dark. As Luna bent to adjust it, a lock of hair slipped forward.

“Your ribbon,” Adrian said.

“It's not in the light path.”

“I know. I meant that it looks lovely today too.”

Her fingers paused. Half the shutter closed early.

The deputy inspector looked up. Luna shut her eyes and took a breath.

“Not powered. Realigning.”

Cecilia covered her mouth.

“The heart control apparently needs more practice.”

Elena glanced at Adrian. He raised his empty left hand and promised no unannounced interference once the test began. Laughter moved the room's attention back from Luna's pink ears to the mechanism. Nobody needed to make a day's entertainment of her lapse.

Realigned, both leaves closed cleanly. The restorer checked the unpowered action. The deputy read the four-second exposure limit and the six-second post-closure observation limit. The administrator fitted the single sealed miniature cell and moved the spare-material boxes away.

“Second round of inert-structure research. Noncontact reading of the original,” Luna dictated.

It was a newly proposed and separately approved examination after the aborted replica pulse of December 13, not another attempt at that failed heat measurement. Adrian's body was not being returned to its earlier twelve-second agreement.

He nodded, keeping hold of the outer handle.

The light came on.

Filtered illumination fell obliquely through closed glass onto the silver-gray interface. The copper sleeve did not move. During the first second, D-17 acquired a faint gray veil, like mist across a morning window.

During the second, the gray drew inward into several misaligned strokes.

Luna touched no adjustment knob. Elena watched the timer. Cecilia called three.

Adrian began to recognize the end of a date.

It was neither bright nor complete. Fine lines hesitated apart, refusing to join. The corners of D-17 remained dark. Only the lamp's reflection showed on the specimen's cover.

“Four. Close,” Cecilia said.

The administrator released his handle.

“Stopping. Light closed,” Luna repeated.

Copper leaves fell together; the power contacts separated. Adrian pressed his independent blackout handle as well, confirming its mechanical lock without waiting for her judgment.

D-17 did not immediately darken.

The separated strokes settled one after another in the remaining glow. A short date became legible.

September 20.

Adrian felt as though something cold had touched the inside of his chest.

He knew that day. The document that should have taken Elena's life had named it when they first met, marking an ending that had not yet happened. They had prevented that ending. The date had not left his memory.

But this plate bore no name, no execution seal, no sentence connecting the two objects. A date could not testify for another document all by itself.

“I read September twentieth,” Cecilia said first, without looking at him.

The deputy wrote his observation before comparing it with Elena's. They did not all wait for Luna to supply the answer and then claim independent agreement.

Luna's lips parted.

“I read the same portion. No year.”

The September 19 teaching card lay covered on a distant table. Nobody altered it or suggested that old materials had learned arithmetic. The surfaces, light paths, and positions were different. Ordinary residual ink, reflections, and earlier inscriptions still required investigation.

A second pale edge began separating from part of the date. It was not a new sentence, merely portions of one image fading at different times.

Luna's hand stayed beside her locked control instead of reaching toward the power box.

Elena looked at Adrian. He remained steady, but could not quite pull his eyes from the date. She did not announce his history to the room. She placed her free hand quietly on the table where he could see it.

“We're here.”

The words proved nothing. They still let him release a longer breath.

“I know.”

Cecilia continued counting the seconds after closure. They were watching an external plate already disconnected from power. The original remained beneath its glass, the silver-gray surface receding into darkness.

No new betrothal appeared. No voice declared that someone had once belonged to someone else.

Only an old, unbearably familiar date lingered on a plate first used today, offering far less explanation than its few strokes seemed to promise.
