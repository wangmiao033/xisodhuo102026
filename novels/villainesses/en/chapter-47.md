# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Two: The Saint Won't Let Me Get Married
### Chapter 47: The Altered Prophecy Comes to Light

On the morning of October twenty-seventh, Adrian found two breakfasts waiting for him.

To his left stood the warm bread and apple preserves Elena had asked Martha to prepare. To his right were porridge and honey cakes, courtesy of Cecilia.

“Why have you both brought me breakfast?”

“You only ate half a pie during yesterday's recess,” Elena said.

“You said nerves upset your stomach in the morning,” Cecilia added.

He looked from one place setting to the other. “This seems less a betrothal dispute than a nutritional one.”

“You can choose,” Elena said.

Cecilia nodded at once. “I haven't applied for exclusive dining rights.”

Adrian took some bread and a spoonful of porridge. “Both. That should keep me from being called as a witness in the breakfast dispute.”

Martha laughed so hard by the door that she nearly dropped the teapot.

An hour before the hearing, Vera arrived with three documents that rather outweighed breakfast.

The first was a certified image of the municipal medical subsidy office's third version, received at 10:26 a.m. on October seventeenth. The second was the third-version image submitted by Temple Internal Oversight, bearing an internal compilation stamp timed at 11:08 that morning. The third was a statement from the municipal custodian: both carried the same catalogue number, and no fourth revision had subsequently been registered.

“So someone must have forged it after eleven?” Adrian asked.

“No.” Vera shook her head. “A receipt or compilation stamp tells us when a document was received or compiled, not when its words were written. But if two copies labeled as the same version contain different wording, each copy's history needs explaining.”

Cecilia set down her teaspoon. “What exactly does the extra sentence say?”

“We can't read out a restricted passage over breakfast. It will be examined under the proper authority before the reviewers today.” Vera returned the copies to their sealed bags. “You are also entitled to know precisely what material they're using to accuse you.”

The hall was quieter than the day before. There were no reporters from the society papers outside. The authorized audience consisted of three observers from the Association for Archival Preservation and several representatives of medical relief organizations.

Sister Anna announced no conclusion in advance. She called on an independent document examiner to present a redacted comparison.

The municipal third version ended:

*Following the blessing, the patient identified in the prognosis is stable. The associated measures have been completed.*

The supposedly identical Temple version added:

*The fate record permits exemption from subsequent medical verification. No further signature is required from the original care provider.*

Dr. Winter pressed her lips together.

“An authorized physician must decide whether clinical care is complete. Wording used to close a religious aid project cannot replace medical instructions.”

Anna asked her to confine her testimony to the applicable medical rules, without evaluating Temple doctrine. She did.

Before beginning the detailed comparison, the examiner explained an easily overlooked distinction. The municipal office held an image of the document received and processed when the payment was settled. The Temple record derived from a paper transcription later submitted to Oversight. Both had been certified, but by different institutions.

“Could copying methods account for the difference?” Adrian asked.

“Spacing can change. Ordinary ink bleed cannot add an entire sentence.” The examiner overlaid the corresponding areas. “We can confirm that text has been added. We cannot yet establish who added it or on what date.”

“If the additional explanation was lawful,” the Oversight representative asked, “where is the wrongdoing?”

“A lawful addition should have authorization and a reference connecting it to the original,” Vera replied. “We are asking you to produce verifiable procedure, not to confess to forgery.”

The clerk carefully recorded that the addition's authorization remained unverified.

Adrian noticed how far the dispute had shifted from whether the saint had damaged the Temple's reputation. Now it turned on a question neither side could sidestep: who was accountable for the extra sentence?

The examiner pointed out differences in the spacing, ink tone, and visible seal impressions at the foot of the two records. The Temple document might have been remade later, or a further annotation might have been introduced during archival transfer.

“Can you identify who added it?” Anna asked.

“No. We have lawfully produced images, but haven't directly examined the original paper. What we can establish is a material difference between the documents currently presented as the same version. They should not be treated as identical, unaltered texts.”

The Oversight representative repeated that it might be an authorized internal supplement.

Vera let him finish. “Then please provide its supplementary reference, the approving officer, and its connection to the original receipt record.”

He hesitated. “The archive is closed. Those materials are awaiting transfer.”

“Until that is verified, this sentence cannot be treated as part of an original prognosis that Cecilia disclosed.”

Anna instructed the clerk to record the point accurately.

Cecilia rose.

“I acknowledge warning physicians about the identifier error on October sixteenth. I did not issue a statement that the patient no longer needed medical review. Nor do I accept anyone invoking the Temple's authority to tell a clinic to stop providing care.”

She looked at each of the three reviewers.

“If someone truly believes fate records protect patients, they should not close off the possibility of a doctor taking another look.”

The medical reviewer nodded slightly. “That is an opinion, but the conduct it concerns can be checked.”

Anna then prepared a procedural bulletin for public release, with private information removed. A third-version document used to settle a religious relief subsidy contained an unexplained addition in one official custody channel. The added passage concerned subsequent medical verification. The person responsible and the reason for the change remained subject to independent investigation.

Before publication, Dr. Winter asked the committee to obtain separate consent from Jonah and Elliot for their circumstances to be cited anonymously. Both agreed that the confusion of identifiers could be described. Neither wanted details of his condition disclosed.

“Won't that weaken the bulletin?” Oversight's representative asked.

“Patients' privacy is not publicity material,” Anna said. “This committee will not display other people's suffering to win applause.”

Cecilia looked up at her.

She had come to think everyone in Oversight cared only about preserving the Temple's dignity. Anna, however, was not tearing up the procedure to suit either side. It gave Cecilia a little hope. People still existed who would actually read the records.

The observers reviewed the text word by word, checking that it disclosed no patient names, medical histories, or protected passages of the original oracle.

The bulletin did not declare that the gods lied. It did not prove the entire Temple fraudulent. But for the first time, it publicly established that a supposedly unquestionable record of a fulfilled prognosis bore human alterations that required an explanation.

Hearing the phrase *differences introduced into the text*, Adrian felt solid ground beneath his feet.

A month ago, he'd needed his memories of *Crown of Fate* to warn Elena. Today he needed no prophecy to point out that two records with the same number contained different sentences.

Oversight asked that publication be delayed. Anna answered that the committee could publish its own procedural findings provided protected personal information remained confidential. She also ordered both institutions to preserve the originals and every copy.

During the recess, Cecilia stood before a portrait in the corridor for a long time.

“Do you regret it?” Adrian asked.

“I don't know.” She studied the saint in the painting. “I used to believe a Temple record meant someone had done their best to take responsibility for a patient. Now I have to acknowledge that somebody interfered with this document. And others may not even have known it was changed.”

“What do you want them to do?”

“Find out why. Give the patients an explanation. Then change the rules, so the next Mira isn't locked up for refusing to sign a false report.”

Elena brought her a cup of hot water.

“That's your goal. Even if people stop calling you a saint, there will be people who value what you did to reach it.”

Cecilia thought before answering. “I want to remain a saint. Of course I do. But I want to keep that title without denying the words I just saw.”

She didn't cry. Her hands tightened around the cup.

That afternoon, Vera returned to the first allegation. Dr. Winter had never received the restricted original. What Cecilia had communicated under the medical-safety exception was the conflict between the identifiers. Oversight remained free to submit specific new evidence of excessive disclosure.

Anna scheduled the panel's private deliberations for that evening. The formal disciplinary decision would be announced the next morning.

Adrian expected Cecilia to relax. Instead, she approached the young Oversight clerk still standing by the door.

“If you find a patient's identifier differs between two documents,” she asked, “will you notify a physician?”

He hesitated. “I'd ask my superior to confirm it first.”

“All right. Just don't let ‘my superior hasn't replied’ become a reason a patient doesn't need their medicine.”

The clerk lowered his head and bowed.

As their carriage passed through the lower district, they found Mira back outside Mercy Clinic, asking a nurse to help repaint the little suns on the wooden door. She hadn't heard that the bulletin was out. She only wanted to know whether Cecilia could come again tomorrow.

“If the committee permits it, I'll keep volunteering,” Cecilia said. “And if I can't for the moment, I can still donate supplies through the proper channels.”

Mira smiled faintly. “Jonah's mother said that when he woke yesterday, the first thing he asked was whether somebody had changed his name again. I told her that this time, at least, I wouldn't be the one signing the mistake.”

Adrian's chest tightened.

He watched a nurse packing a medicine case. This, he thought, was the day's most important result: somebody needn't fear losing his name, and with it his existing care, because of a ceremony.

Back in the carriage, Adrian finally had time to consider dinner.

“We have a public record now. Surely that deserves a celebration?”

“Yesterday you said we weren't discussing betrothals,” Elena observed.

“I said dinner. Not a betrothal.”

Cecilia looked up from the opposite seat. “I could bring two kinds of dessert. There's some left from breakfast.”

Adrian sighed. “This time, I'd like to approve my own portion in writing.”

Elena slipped her hand into his. “You're already qualified to decide how much you eat.”

Cecilia glanced away, her cheeks warm. She couldn't tell whether she envied the ease with which they held hands or felt grateful that an old agreement no longer had to decide what closeness meant for her.

At the estate, Duke Edmund heard their account, then asked Cecilia what she intended to do if she kept her title.

“Go back to the clinic.”

“And if you don't?”

“I still want to go. I'll need to find another way to participate, with the proper qualifications.”

The duke inclined his head. “Then at least your ambition isn't a badge on your collar.”

“I value it very much.” She smiled. “But I'd like to deserve it, rather than let it make my decisions.”

Beside her, Elena reached to refill her cup.

Nobody here, Adrian realized, was treating Cecilia as a historical betrothal that might steal away the hero. Through her own choices, she was becoming a friend they respected in her own right.

At six that evening, Hale brought news of another application. An independent archive reviewer had been permitted to inventory some of the sealed records in person. The approval field for the additional sentence in the October seventeenth third version contained no named acknowledgment.

“So someone slipped it in?” Adrian asked.

“It establishes a gap in the approval procedure. Whether there was malicious intent still needs investigation.” Hale closed the file. “But when the committee decides tomorrow whether Cecilia broke the rules, it can no longer pretend that document has only one version.”
