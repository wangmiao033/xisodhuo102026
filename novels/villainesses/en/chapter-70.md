# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Three: The Knight Says She's Responsible for Me
### Chapter 70: Without Her Sword, She Didn't Know Where to Put Her Hands

On the morning of November 17, Viola straightened her gloves twice outside the garrison.

The first time, she was tidying her uniform. The second, Adrian noticed her fingers lingering over the same seam.

The local legal adviser she had chosen the night before was already inside, confirming the scope of the day's proceedings with the duty clerk. Elena handed the guard their visitor passes, which permitted them to observe the portions concerning the civilian evacuation, then returned to Adrian's side.

“Sit if you want to,” she murmured.

“I'm all right.”

“I meant me. This wind could turn a person into a flag.”

She led the way to a seat beneath the covered walkway. Viola glanced over. The tension around her mouth eased a little, and she finally stopped adjusting her perfectly serviceable gloves.

The map was still spread across the meeting-room table. There were more wooden pins beside Willowford. An empty teacup rested over Greymarch's south gate.

Major Garrick Voss didn't keep her waiting through a reprimand. He signaled the clerk to open the record and offered her adviser a seat.

“Today we'll hear your account of the fourteenth, then decide command arrangements and the conditions under which you'll remain in town during the review. The treason allegation will not be settled at this table.”

Viola nodded.

The clerk read out the recall order issued on the fourteenth. When the messenger had delivered it, the evacuation along the river road was already underway. The last stretcher wagon had a trapped left wheel and was still on the dangerous stretch. The order required Viola to recall her patrol to reinforce the town's defenses.

It was a separate document from the grain supplement dated the fourth, which they were still waiting for the original issuing office to verify.

“You didn't return immediately upon receiving it,” Voss said.

“No. Four riders from the front escort returned with the messenger. I signed my reasons for the delay on the receipt and stayed with the rear escort to bring in the last wagon.”

“Why?”

“Neither of the two wounded passengers could walk, and the wheel was still trapped. We were still receiving reports of attackers along the road. If I took the rear escort away, they wouldn't reach the gates.”

“Do you think I didn't know there were people outside the walls?”

Voss moved the cup away from the south gate. The marks there were crowded together.

“I had reports of riders on the northern track. I couldn't tell whether a second attack was coming. When the rest of your people didn't return in time, I had to draw men from elsewhere. Wounded people on one road don't make it the only road on the map.”

Viola met his eyes.

“I understand.”

“I need more than that.”

The room fell quiet. Outside, a horse pawed the ground. Once, then again. Adrian remembered the last wagon jolting along the riverbank, its driver afraid to use the whip because every bump made someone cry out.

That wagon wasn't a little wooden pin that could be moved at will.

And there were people behind the town gates who needed protecting, too.

Lieutenant Rusk was called in to explain the change of watch. He wore the same uniform with its faded collar and stood close to Viola.

“Four riders returned first. We still had to draw guards from the depot to reinforce the south gate,” he said. “The handover was disrupted. No grain was lost as a result, but there was additional risk.”

The clerk asked about the prisoner's arrival.

“Weapons confiscated, separate guards. He wasn't put among the villagers' beds. The infirmary isn't a military prison. I requested additional sentries. We still haven't established his identity.”

He stopped there. He neither added an accusation that she had endangered them all nor erased the difficulties that had genuinely occurred.

Viola's adviser asked the clerk to include the bridge inspection, the evacuation permission, and the delivery receipt for the recall order. Voss agreed, then asked whether she had anything further to say.

“I should have given a fuller estimate of the time the evacuation would take. At first, I thought the wagons could move faster. That was my judgment.”

She paused.

“After receiving the recall, I still chose to bring the last wagon home. That decision was mine as well. Mr. Reed did not ask me to alter a military deployment, and the wagons Miss Rosenberg hired were not under military command.”

Adrian watched her profile. Her voice was steady. Her hands were clenched in her lap.

He didn't interrupt to declare that anyone would have done the same. That would turn a choice she had made into something as ownerless as the weather.

Voss glanced at the map, then rose to turn the coat drying beside the stove. Its hem was still wet from his inspection of the walls the previous night. Water dripped into an iron tray.

“If another appeal comes from outside the walls today, I have to send whoever I can spare. I also have to know when they'll come back,” he said. “Blackwood, I'm not sitting here waiting for you to make a mistake.”

“I know, Major.”

She spoke more slowly this time. Neither yielded. But Adrian could suddenly hear another kind of exhaustion in the room.

Voss closed the book before him.

“I accept that you faced real danger. Whether it justified all your actions remains for the independent review.”

The clerk presented a new document.

Dated November 17, it suspended Captain Blackwood's remaining provisional patrol command. The troops assigned to the south gate and nearby evacuation routes would come under another duty officer appointed by the garrison. During the review, she was to deposit her issued sword, refrain from assembling troops, and remain in Greymarch.

Having heard the proposed residence and security arrangements, the major did not order her confined to a cell. She could stay in town, report as required, meet her legal adviser, and submit evidence. There was no verdict. She had not been stripped of her rank.

Voss signed in front of her.

It was an ordinary stroke of the pen. The last part was a little faint where the ink ran thin.

Adrian gripped his own pen without writing a word. The clerk had already moved the patrol handover book to the other side of the table. The major had the authority to sign this suspension. From that moment, she could no longer lead her patrol.

Viola read the order and signed to acknowledge receipt.

“Received.”

Her chair scraped softly against the floor as she stood.

No one knew what to say after that small sound.

She deposited her sword in the room next door. The old armory sergeant spread felt on the counter and asked her to lay down the weapon in its scabbard.

Viola undid the fastening very slowly. The leather had a repaired section and edges polished by wear. Adrian had watched her check it almost absently beside the supper table. Now the buckle opened, and the metal settled onto the felt with barely a sound.

The sergeant recorded the condition of the sword and scabbard, then offered her the receipt.

She didn't take it at once.

“The leather at the mouth of the scabbard was replaced last year,” she said. “Don't dry it by the stove if it gets damp.”

“I know, Captain.”

He placed it in a dry rack and offered the receipt a little farther forward. This time she took it.

Outside, her right hand drifted to its customary place at her hip.

There was nothing there.

She still had to collect her personal belongings from the duty room. The cabinet wasn't large: a chipped cup, two spare hair ties, half a bar of soap wrapped in paper. The clerk waited by the door while she stood over this tiny collection, unable to decide what to pick up first.

A hair tie rolled under the table. Adrian retrieved it and dropped it into her open palm.

“Does that need a receipt, too?”

“No.” She noticed him glancing toward the excessively solemn clerk. “Not yet, at any rate.”

The clerk lowered his head to conceal a smile and handed over the paper bag from her desk.

Viola packed her things. Last of all, she picked up a piece of sugar in a scrap of paper. It was for the patrol horses. She left it beside the shared cabinet.

“Remember to divide that when it's their turn.”

Then she stepped aside, leaving the way to the cabinet clear. Her replacement hadn't moved into the room yet. Her cup was already gone from the desk.

In the courtyard, Owen was waiting for the new officer's roll call. He took an instinctive step toward her, then heard his name and stopped.

“Fall in, Corporal,” Viola said.

She caught herself.

“Go and hear your duty officer's instructions.”

Owen pressed his lips together and saluted. She returned it, then walked past the formation alone.

The soldiers didn't follow. Somehow, the boots staying obediently in place hurt Adrian more than any rush forward with declarations of loyalty could have.

Life continued in the street. The bakery had just produced a batch of coarse wheat cakes. A wheelwright lay beneath a cart, swearing at a rusty nail. Outside the infirmary, a woman waved to Viola, called her Captain, and asked whether she could come and look at a leaking window in the temporary lodgings.

Viola almost agreed at once. Then she looked toward the clerk accompanying them.

He checked her conditions. “Ordinary visits are permitted. If you take part in relief work, it must be arranged through the town office. You may not deploy the patrol.”

“I'll ask Magistrate Finch first.”

The woman hadn't followed all the conditions. She simply pushed a cloth-wrapped bundle into Viola's hands.

“Kept these from yesterday. Chestnuts. Sorry they're cold.”

Viola stood holding them for a moment.

Elena didn't promise that a letter from her father would get the sword back. She gave the adviser the page references she had noted, arranging for Viola to decide later what to add, then asked where she wanted to go.

“Somewhere quiet.”

“All right. Nobody's unloading in the inn's back courtyard just now.”

Elena took them away from the watching street, then went to ask the landlady for hot water. When Adrian stayed behind, Viola was sitting at the far end of a bench, holding the receipt as though she had forgotten that paper could go into a pocket.

He didn't sit right against her.

“Is anyone using this bit?”

She shook her head.

He sat. The bench gave an alarming groan. They both looked down.

“Loose on the left,” Viola said.

“Should I evacuate immediately?”

“Move a little to the right.”

He did. That brought them closer. She looked at the reduced space between them and finally managed the faintest smile.

“It really is the bench this time.”

“I believe you. It hasn't put dinner on an escort schedule.”

The smile stayed briefly. She looked down, folded the receipt, then opened it again.

“I thought I'd be angry.”

“And now?”

“When I took off the sword, all I could think about was whether they'd still ask me which road to take tomorrow.”

Wheels rattled beyond the courtyard wall. Listening, she rested her right hand at her hip again, then returned it to her lap.

“I worked for so long to be allowed to lead them.”

Adrian watched her hands. He didn't say it was all right. What she had lost had a name, and weight, and people who wouldn't knock on her door tomorrow morning.

“Would you mind if I stayed here a while?”

“You don't have to comfort me. I don't think I can help you with much today.”

“I know.”

She looked up.

“I knew before I sat down.”

That silenced her longer than anything else had. At last, she put the receipt in her pocket and the chestnuts between them, slowly unfolding their cloth.

“They're very cold.”

“I'll bite carefully.”

She took one and worked her thumb into the split shell. This time, her gloves were off. The shell parted, revealing a whole chestnut.

“That child was right,” she murmured.

Adrian remembered the rest stop by the river. He didn't tease her again. She collected the shell in one corner of the cloth and opened another. Gradually, her hands grew steadier.

A little later, she offered him a whole chestnut.

“This one's yours.”

“As spoils of war?”

“As a chestnut.”

He took it with a smile he couldn't suppress.

When Elena returned with the water, she noticed two pieces of shell in his hand and set a small dish between them. She sat on Viola's other side without asking what they had talked about. The adviser, she said, would send over the material that could be submitted tomorrow for Viola to review herself.

Before dusk, a messenger arrived from the town office.

The rider sent on the thirteenth to verify the supplement dated the fourth had checked in at the preceding relay station. The original issuing office had agreed to make its retained original available for examination. A clerk familiar with the dispatch register was accompanying it, along with the consecutive register entries and retained delivery receipts. They were expected in Greymarch the next morning, November 18.

No one here had opened the sealed materials.

Viola pressed her fingers against the receipt in her pocket.

“May I go tomorrow?”

Elena checked the notice's list of attendees.

“As a party to the case, with your legal adviser. Yes.”

Viola nodded. She didn't pick up an order book or call for Owen to have a horse saddled.

Adrian watched the hand against her pocket relax.

The sword she had surrendered today might not return tomorrow.

But the order that had first set her on the road to this bench was finally coming to Greymarch as it had looked when it left its issuer's hands.
