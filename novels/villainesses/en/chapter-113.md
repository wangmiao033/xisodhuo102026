# Chapter 113: The Knight Draws No Sword Tonight

“Ask him to wait. Don't let him upstairs,” Viola Blackwood said.

The porter glanced at her waist. No sword hung there, only a neatly tied coat belt.

“I'm asking for your help,” she added. “Not giving orders.”

He nodded and left. Adrian Reed stayed to ask Tessa Quinn, “Will you let the watch see the note?”

Her hand still pressed against her maintenance copies. She looked from the note to Luna Ashcroft's bandaged palm.

“They said it could affect my job.”

“They can't dismiss you on my behalf,” Luna said. “And I can't buy testimony with your wages. If you're frightened, you can say so.”

Tessa bit her lip, then released the paper. “Yes. But I don't want to ask them alone.”

“Then you won't go tonight.” Viola brought the lamp closer, examining a faint impression. “First, we'll fetch the people who actually patrol this street.”

She asked the proprietor to summon the night watch and stayed to learn where the guesthouse's doors led. His face darkened on hearing that someone was using official business to lure a guest down the street. He put away the spare back-door key and sent his son to bolt the coal entrance. The ordinary courtyard exit remained accessible. They weren't cargo to be locked in a box.

Elena Rosenberg accompanied Tessa upstairs for her coat and belongings. Cecilia Saintclair stayed with Luna. When Adrian and Viola entered the hall, the messenger was rubbing his cold-reddened hands.

The messenger was a grown man in a short wool coat and muddy boots.

“Miss Quinn's agreed to come?” he asked.

“Who sent you?” Adrian asked.

“A man in a gray hat. Two coppers now, another for the answer.”

“You know him?”

“No. Found me when the market closed. Said he was from the office.”

Viola asked where he'd stood and whether anyone accompanied him. She asked only for what he remembered, and his knowledge soon ran out. He had no name and had seen no seal. The supposed office was merely a door the stranger had indicated.

“I didn't force anyone,” he said suddenly.

“Then sit and tell the watch what happened.” Viola offered him the bench. “You can ask the proprietor to stay.”

He stared at her empty right hand, then sat.

The watchman took time to arrive. While they waited, Tessa missed a stair. Viola moved, her right hand braced against the table. Adrian reached the stairs first. Elena had Tessa firmly supported; neither was hurt. He raised a reassuring hand.

Viola withdrew hers.

“You can call me,” he said.

“I thought I'd be quicker.”

“I know. But the stairs didn't demand that you face them alone.”

Her tense mouth softened. She sat again.

After examining the note, the watchman went to verify it at the tribunal's night receiving office. During his absence, Luna pushed a biscuit toward Tessa, explaining that mouths still worked when people were frightened. Tessa didn't smile, but she took a bite.

The watchman returned with registration papers. No nighttime interview had been requested. The address wasn't a tribunal office. He took the note as evidence of an impersonated summons and a threat to employment. Tessa, the proprietor, and the watchman confirmed when it had arrived before the original went into a paper sleeve. The messenger agreed to explain his hiring. A gray hat alone could not establish that his employer belonged to the church or academy.

“What happens to that place?” Tessa asked.

“We'll investigate. You needn't identify the door. If anyone else comes tonight, have the porter call us.”

Viola asked the proprietor to record the watchman's name and station, then suggested moving Tessa to a room overlooking the inner courtyard. With her agreement, he escorted her upstairs. Her new room number wasn't displayed in the front hall.

Adrian carried her case. It was lighter than it looked: one change of clothes, a comb, tools wrapped in old cloth. Suddenly he understood why a threat to her job could move her more readily than a naked blade.

It wasn't a brave statement that needed protecting, but a person who still had to earn tomorrow's meals.

At the new room, Tessa wouldn't release the case clasp.

“If the tower can't operate, what if they say they don't need an assistant anymore?”

Luna didn't promise everything would stay the same. She didn't know how long the closure would last either. She leaned against the doorway and let Tessa finish.

“You'll receive the pay we agreed on. If there's no more work later, I'll tell you. I won't send a letter demanding an obedient answer.”

“I'm not asking for extra.”

“I know. You repaired that retaining spring better than I could. I knew that while we were haggling too.”

Tessa smiled down at the case and finally put it beside the bed. Viola inspected the window catch and the distance to the courtyard without moving the heavy cabinet. Someone passed below with a broom. She established that it was the proprietor's daughter before drawing the curtain.

“Should we block the door with the table?” Tessa asked.

“No. You must be able to leave if something happens. The working bolt is enough.”

Viola had her test it herself, then stepped outside. Tessa closed the door and opened it a crack again.

“Do you think I'm being troublesome?”

Remembering her left arm and the doctor's repeated warnings, Viola answered slowly.

“I often need help too. I'm still learning how to ask.”

Silence lingered in the doorway. At last, Tessa's fingers loosened.

No second visitor knocked that night. Trouble instead reconvened beside the hot-water pot.

Viola wanted to wait for the next patrol before sleeping. Luna couldn't sleep anyway and offered to listen for disturbances. Adrian saw their empty cups.

“Then I'll—”

“If you three take turns proving you're awake, the proprietor can open an invalids' teahouse tomorrow,” Elena interrupted.

She laid out his watch arrangements. He and the porter would alternate at the entrance. Watchmen patrolled the street. Upstairs, bellpulls could summon help. Their sleeplessness would add nothing.

“I only want to sit awhile,” Viola said.

“Then sit awhile.” Cecilia turned a small hourglass. “Fifteen minutes. Then bed.”

“Even the saint's companionship comes with a timer,” Luna observed.

“So do your experiments.”

“Mine usually cost more.”

Adrian laughed, then coughed. It rarely happened now, but he accepted Elena's warm water rather than interpreting a reminder from his body as a qualification for heroism.

Luna proposed investigating whether the kitchen had hot porridge. Cecilia escorted her away. Elena went to check whether Tessa could reach her bellpull, leaving Viola across the table corner from Adrian.

“I didn't help much tonight.”

“The proprietor wanted to go down the street himself. You got him to fetch the watch. Tessa was going with the messenger. You asked what she wanted. What else should we count?”

“I'm used to standing in doorways.”

“You can still see people sitting inside them.”

Viola looked down, fingering her coat belt. She didn't insist that she must always stand in front of everyone.

“Did you stay because you thought I'd overdo it?”

“A little,” he admitted. “I also wanted to finish this cup with you.”

She immediately looked at it. The remaining mouthful of tea offered little support for a heroic vigil.

“I could refill—”

She stopped, her ears reddening.

Adrian smiled. “There'll be tea tomorrow.”

She looked up without quite hiding her embarrassment. “Then don't spend all tomorrow with the papers.”

“All right.”

Elena returned before the sand ran out. Both were preparing to rise. She looked slightly surprised but asked no questions. At the door, Viola earnestly thanked her. Tessa was willing to put out her lamp now.

“She decided herself,” Elena said.

“Yes. I know.”

The next morning, Tessa appeared early, her hair neatly combed and her copies in her arms.

Elena came down a little later, a plain ribbon in her hair. Adrian was slicing bread. He saved her the window seat.

“You didn't wait for me to read last night,” she said as she sat.

“I thought you'd be tired afterward.”

“You could have asked.”

He put down the knife and nodded seriously. “Shall we read tonight?”

Her prepared reproach suddenly had nowhere to go.

“Yes. Just one passage.”

“The captain can finally continue his journey.”

“First, he can learn to wait for people.”

Across the table, Luna raised her half-buttered bread. “When he's finished whispering sweet nothings, could you pass the knife?”

Elena set it beside her without changing expression. “Will that go in your research report?”

“No. This requires a separate notebook. With a lock.”

“Buy a larger one,” Cecilia advised. “I have something to say too.”

Adrian dropped his bread onto his plate. Watching him scramble to recover it, Tessa discovered that the people who'd kept danger outside last night could be cornered over a little butter in the morning. Her grip on the papers relaxed. She reached for her own slice.

After eating her bread, Tessa finally asked about the day's arrangements. She didn't ask whether they'd identified whoever sent the note. She asked whether she could stop for water while testifying.

“Yes,” Luna answered. “If they object, I'll argue about that first.”

“Less arguing. Your throat needs water too,” Tessa reminded her.

At last, they both laughed.

Thin frost covered the streets on December 22. A watchman accompanied them to the registry and formally transferred the night's records. Tessa's safety arrangements went on a separate sheet. Further questioning would go through the tribunal; her employer couldn't approve her account beforehand. She could still state facts unfavorable to Luna. She could also say she didn't know.

Her first words to the technical recorder erased Luna's smile.

“Before the eighteenth, I thought disconnecting the main source was enough. I wrote that on the maintenance sheet.”

She unfolded her authorized copy. Its old creases and tower registration number were verifiable. In the lower corner, fine lines marked her work replacing the control base's retaining spring. This wasn't a secret drawing that had appeared overnight. It was a repair detail previously considered unimportant.

“This is where I worked.” Tessa pointed with her pen cap to a line bypassing the main break. “It connects to the side discharge plate. Pull out the main-source connector and both its ends are still joined. I didn't open the inside. I don't know whether anything was stored there.”

Luna's left hand paused above the paper.

Adrian recognized the location. During the accident, he'd pulled the main lever, yet light kept returning from the control side. Only when the yellow handle shattered the ceramic neck had that complete path finally broken.

“Please include that she doesn't know the internal structure,” Luna said.

The recorder nodded. Luna requested comparison with the break diagram submitted by the independent examiners. Tessa shouldn't have to infer an interior she'd never inspected from a single accident.

Tessa's shoulders slowly lowered.

When the record was complete, she put down her pen and looked at Viola.

“I actually wanted to go last night.”

“Home?”

“To that door. I thought if I said things nicely enough, everyone could avoid trouble.”

Viola didn't tell her she shouldn't have been afraid. She retrieved the gloves Tessa had left beside her chair and handed them back with her right hand.

“You made yourself clear today.”

Outside, wheels crunched over frozen mud. Luna was still studying the independent circuit on the maintenance drawing. Then she looked up and asked the recorder for a blank sheet of thin paper.

“I'd like to try something.”

Elena immediately glanced at her bandage.

Luna raised her left hand a little higher.

“With paper. Really, just paper.”
