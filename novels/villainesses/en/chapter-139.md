# Chapter 139: An Empty Post Is More Than an Ink Line

Only when Viola turned the map upright did Adrian realize he had been reading it upside down.

“So the bridge isn't inside the palace.”

“If it were, yesterday's coal driver would have needed an audience first.”

On the morning of January 14, she pushed her notes across a low table at the Military Registry with her right hand. Her left arm rested securely on a cushion. The duty clerk had unrolled the map; no one handed the heavy roll to a recovering knight.

Yesterday's request had been approved, limited to the festival's guard handover, civilian passage, and the diagram's legend. Unrelated troop numbers remained covered. Her restored reputation did not entitle her to the capital's entire defenses.

Viola read the legend first.

The closing line did indicate a temporary road closure from the evening of the seventeenth. It meant neither demolition nor a permanent ban. The branch led to the maintenance entrance beside the Old Palace Watercourse. An alternative route appeared alongside it.

“The summary omitted that road,” Adrian said.

“And when someone will be responsible for opening it.”

The clerk brought an authorized extract from the actual handover order. Positions were clear. Viola paused over the departure time without immediately declaring a conspiracy.

The old guard would assemble farther inside after the fifth bell. The replacements would not leave the north gate until the sixth.

Adrian counted twice.

“An hour between them?”

“On paper, at least. We need to check whether they use the same clock, whether assembly means abandoning the post, and whether the replacements send anyone ahead.”

She asked to see the annotations.

This was not the four-year-old NR-42. That notice had forwarded a decommissioned wrapper and maintenance papers, explicitly excluding operational instructions. This was an actual order issued on January 11 of the present year, acknowledged by both guard units.

A genuine order could leave a genuine gap.

The duty officer took no offense, but he would not let two visitors judge his men from extracts alone. He fetched the acknowledgments and sent a clerk to question the dispatcher while he checked the time system.

Waiting, Viola adjusted her supported arm.

“Would you like a break?” Adrian asked.

“Waiting for a reply counts.”

“Perhaps not if you keep staring at the door.”

She looked at him. “Are you checking the orders or me?”

“We can answer those separately.”

After nearly arguing, she smiled and settled against the chair.

Pages turned across the table. There were no drums, swords, or enemies she could defeat immediately. She had learned to stand firm before visible danger. Now she had to wait patiently for other people to bring the remaining papers while danger was still an incomplete arrangement.

The dispatcher returned with wet snow on his boots.

All times followed the capital's public clock. There was no conversion. The departing guard would remove its barrier before assembling for an inside assignment. No replacements would go ahead; their expected arrival was a quarter past six.

“Who guides residents along the alternative route?” Viola asked.

The dispatcher pointed to the civilian summary. “The ward patrol.”

The officer requested the ward's reply.

It acknowledged receiving the coordination notice. It did not accept a new duty. The ward expected the military to leave handover staff. The military expected the ward to take over.

Adrian stared at two genuine receipt stamps.

The gap of more than an hour had not disappeared.

“The road isn't wholly cut off,” the officer said. “People can go around.”

“Then tell them they'll go around, rather than saying passage is unaffected.” Viola indicated a small slope. “A delivery cart might manage. What about a stretcher?”

The dispatcher studied the map again. His expression changed.

An ordinary clinic stood east of the branch road. The alternative included steps, impassable to wheeled transport. Holding vehicles until the new guard arrived could strand patients already traveling. Removing the old guard immediately would leave a hazardous maintenance entrance without anyone guiding people past.

They had not discovered an attack. They did not need to wait for a fall to recognize a missing piece.

The officer set the replies side by side and wrote a request for clarification himself.

“I can't alter the deployment on my superior's behalf. We'll ask the issuing office whether the old post remains until relief arrives. Civil authorities must confirm a route vehicles can use.”

“Please include a deadline for answering,” Viola said. “It's only the fourteenth. There's time.”

She did not order soldiers to stay or offer to fill the empty post herself.

Adrian saw her glance toward the training yard outside, then look away.

Before the dispatcher left, Adrian asked whether the steps had been converted to a ramp in autumn.

He remembered talk of roadworks, not their precise location. Viola did not reject his memory on instinct. She asked for the road inspection acknowledgment enclosed with the notice.

The steps remained. Winter ramp work had not begun. The clinic's pickup point lay on the old road.

“I asked the transport office yesterday,” the dispatcher said. “They said it was passable.”

“For what vehicle?” Viola asked.

“A light dispatch cart.”

She pointed to the diagram's small symbol. A narrow-wheeled cart could be lifted over two steps. A vehicle carrying a recumbent patient could not be treated the same way.

The officer summoned a messenger to confirm the clinic's scheduled journeys on the seventeenth and check a usable route directly with the road office.

Only after the messenger repeated the destination and left did Adrian relax his fingers against the map. Passable had nearly flattened a real problem all over again.

Viola moved her chair back to clear the staff's path.

“Don't stand here waiting for him,” she murmured. “They have their own pace.”

“You were watching the door earlier.”

“So now it's my turn to remind you.”

They left with acknowledgment that the problem was registered and the handover arrangements required completion. Two visitors had not voided the order. Its source and receipts remained preserved; no one silently changed five to six to erase the mistake.

A young clerk caught them outside to ask Viola's contact address.

“Send questions to the residence. I'm convalescing. I can't promise immediate attendance.”

He wrote it down and saluted.

“You didn't put yourself into the arrangement this time,” Adrian said after he left.

“I almost did.”

“I know.”

“You shouldn't always know.”

“Next time I'll wait for you to tell me.”

She seemed more pleased by that than praise. Pointing to a bench, she suggested a rest and went to sit first.

That afternoon, they took their limited findings to the palace's outer room. An attendant led them somewhere unexpected.

At the kitchen door, a maid held six little numbered cards.

Isabella stood at a worktable, sleeves neatly rolled, with a small piece of dough before her. Behind her, a clerk was recording each piece's weight.

“Your Highness,” Adrian said, “has it acquired an heir?”

“What?”

“The dough. It seems to have a full administrative staff.”

Viola lowered her head. Her shoulders shook slightly.

After making apple pastries two days earlier, Isabella had already arranged this afternoon's biscuit lesson. It was not an attempt to turn a military question into teatime. She simply hadn't expected so many people to need to remind her not to touch hot trays.

The cook corrected that assumption.

“Two people would have been enough. You asked for every piece to be weighed three times and numbered. It may dry out before the records are finished.”

“I want to know which thickness works best.”

“Then give the oven a chance. Nothing's reached it yet.”

She looked at the clerk's paper and finally told him to put it away. The six numbered cards disappeared. Dough went onto one wooden tray. The cook retained charge of the oven while Isabella copied his demonstration with the cutter.

Nothing burned. The difficulty was that she weighed everything so precisely that by the time she finished six, the cook had completed a tray.

“You needn't grant each biscuit an identical estate,” Adrian observed.

“You object to pastries too.”

“I have extensive experience of waiting.”

She set down her latest piece and laughed despite herself. Then she asked whether Viola could sit comfortably nearby. An armchair was brought, without anyone inviting the knight to carry heavy trays.

Once the biscuits entered the oven, Isabella removed her apron and returned to the outer room for the formal account. No flour touched the military copies. The attendants were told they had not discovered enemy movements.

Seeing the gap of more than an hour, she immediately asked whether someone had deliberately drawn the guards away.

“That's a question,” Viola said. “We don't have its answer.”

“Right beside the old watercourse.”

“Which is reason to examine the order's purpose. There is also a clinic and coal traffic there. We can't see only the part we've been investigating.”

Isabella read the ward's reply. Her attention shifted from the conspicuous times.

She asked Ellery to follow the supplementary coordination through and ensure accurate, usable directions were posted along the route tomorrow. Royal concern could not transform two acknowledgments into a shared promise. The royal guard could not substitute for the ward patrol's jurisdiction.

“What if the superior refuses?”

“Preserve the reason and refer it through the proper chain. Meanwhile, don't keep treating unaffected as work someone has agreed to do.”

The biscuits arrived.

Isabella's six, their edges imperfect, occupied a separate plate. The rest were the cook's. She did not claim the entire tray.

Adrian tried one. It was good.

“Quicker than I expected.”

“Weren't you complaining?”

“I'm correcting myself.”

Viola chose a smaller one with her right hand. Watching her finish it, Isabella finally stopped looking as though she awaited a judgment on royal competence.

“We could make fewer next time.”

“Invite fewer people,” the cook added from the doorway.

Isabella looked at him, then asked the maid to save him a share.

Back at the residence that evening, Viola put away the day's work before her planned rest. Adrian moved a low stool from her path and asked what she needed upstairs.

“The biscuits.”

“What about the military acknowledgment?”

“Already filed. Right now, I want biscuits.”

Smiling, he handed the bag to her right hand.

After two steps, she turned.

“If nothing new comes up tomorrow, would you sit with me again? Without studying a map?”

“Yes. After I return in the afternoon.”

“Then I'll prepare only one chair.”

He blinked. Her ears reddened.

“Two. I meant I won't prepare an escape route for the entire room.”

She did not retreat into calling it an assignment. He agreed and watched her climb slowly.

The evening's military reply confirmed that the issuing office had required a complete handover. Military and civilian offices were jointly checking the alternative route. Completion had not yet been reported. Adrian retained that unfinished point rather than mistaking willingness to correct a problem for a road already open.

Then he studied the five-column diagram.

Its marks separated five old records. Today, one ink line had nearly persuaded two groups that someone else already stood in the empty space.
