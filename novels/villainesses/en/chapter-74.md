# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Three: The Knight Says She's Responsible for Me
### Chapter 74: This Time, Bring the Permit First

On November 21, before the sky was fully light, the first thing placed in Adrian's luggage was a cushion.

It was followed by a flask of warm water, two portions of bread, and a few sentences written in Dr. Shaw's own hand. The doctor had approved a short outing by carriage, but no running, carrying heavy loads, or entering rooms thick with smoke or dust. If his chest felt tight or he began coughing persistently, he was to stop participating and be examined by the attendant accompanying the party.

“It doesn't say I can't look at accounts,” he said after reading it carefully.

“Because the doctor doesn't believe anyone would plead for permission to do that.” Elena folded the paper for him.

Her injured hand was still wrapped in gauze. When she tried to tighten the mouth of the bag, her movement faltered. He took over the knot, leaving her to settle the warm water securely with her good hand.

“I'll stay here and check the acknowledgments the trading houses have sent,” she said. “Investigator Bellamy will have someone bring back news.”

He nodded without describing her decision to stay as a safety arrangement he had issued himself. She had already asked how she could participate and understood that her recovering hand wasn't suited to a field inspection. The accounts people in town were reluctant to open would be no easier to get into than a door at the old post.

Before leaving, he held her hand for a moment.

“If I'm back early tonight, I'd like to finish the chapter about the seaside market.”

“If you're late, it can wait another evening.” She looked at him. “I like you keeping your promises. I also like you actually stopping when you need to.”

He didn't promise to be standing here before sunset, only to follow the instructions of the doctor and the person in charge at the site. She straightened his collar and, at the end, let her hand linger a little longer.

Downstairs, Viola was showing the clerk the temporary permission to leave town that had been approved the previous night.

After examining the fuel waybill, the property clerk's statement, and the lease register, Ward had issued an instruction authorizing assistance with the investigation at a specified location and within a set period. Ada had separately issued a permit for emergency inspection of the civilian storerooms and preservation of evidence. The scope of each authority was clearly defined. A civilian inspection paper couldn't be used to deploy troops, and military authorization to assist an inquiry didn't permit indiscriminate searches of other people's private letters.

Viola was allowed to accompany the party as a terrain adviser, report on returning to town, and follow Edith's directions in the field. Her command remained suspended. Her issued sword remained in the armory. The little leather pouch at her waist contained dressings and a whistle.

She wore her own light armor, its metal plates concealed beneath an old coat with a repaired shoulder fastening. It looked very different from her full uniform.

“Does this count as your return to work?” Adrian asked.

“It counts as having one thing I'm permitted to do.”

Then she noticed the cushion he was carrying and added, “I haven't put the training ground on the route again.”

“Thank you. I'm beginning to believe that your ordinary invitations and official duties really are separate.”

She lowered her eyes to inspect the carriage step but couldn't keep the corners of her mouth down.

Edith was waiting beside the carriage. The investigator was somewhere in her thirties, with tightly braided hair and a leather satchel worn pale at the edges hanging from her shoulder. A member of the outside review team, she had previously assessed disaster damage and knew when to put away a measuring stick and let a carpenter go in first.

The party also included six neutral guards, a craftsman familiar with old buildings, and an attendant. The guard captain was responsible for force and evacuation; Edith was responsible for the investigation. Resistance or structural danger meant halting the inspection. The captain under investigation would not take command of troops to deal with it.

Edith said all of this in front of everyone before telling the driver to set off.

“Why did you agree to let me come?” Adrian asked once he was in the carriage.

“You've checked Northroad's civilian forms. You can tell a waybill from a delivery acknowledgment or another merchant's private debt account,” Edith said. “Your job is to identify categories worth examining. Mine is to decide how to obtain them. You aren't the only person who can read.”

“That's extremely reassuring.”

“I hope you'll remember it if there's danger.”

The carriage stopped at the last hot-soup stall. While Edith studied the sketch the property clerk had supplemented yesterday, Viola brought three bowls of soup. She set the last one in front of herself, no longer postponing her own meal until everyone else had eaten.

Steam misted the carriage window. Adrian broke his bread in half and noticed that Viola's portion had two layers of meat inside.

“The groundskeeper gave it to me,” she said. “He said that if I brought a friend to the training ground, I couldn't let him eat nothing but hard chestnuts.”

“Has he revised his opinion of yesterday's activities?”

“He asked whether I'd be borrowing targets next time.”

Edith laughed without looking up. Viola glanced at her, then finally admitted with a smile that she might need to acquaint herself with a few more suitable places to invite people.

Once they reached the north road, the rattle of wheels gradually replaced their laughter.

Alder Watch wasn't far from town. In ordinary weather, the journey took half a day. Last night's freezing rain had left thin ice on several downhill stretches. The driver preferred the longer, gentler old supply road to having the carriage slide into a roadside ditch. After noon, they paused twice at a roadwork shelter. Adrian sat drinking water while Viola and the craftsman checked the old map.

“I learned to find my way around here.” She pointed to a spot beyond the edge of the map. “That slope is sheltered from the wind. Little white flowers bloom there in spring.”

“Why didn't you suggest that place before?”

“I thought that if I invited someone for a walk, I ought to choose somewhere I knew best.”

“That's already a considerable improvement on the stables.”

She started to argue, then suddenly laughed and rolled up the map. “Once I'm free to leave town, I'll ask whether you'd like to go.”

“Ask first when the time comes. Don't draw me onto the map.”

“I'll remember.”

She said it softly, without straightening her back as though receiving an order.

By the time they reached the alder wood, the afternoon light was already slanting.

The long embankment outside the post was exposed. Military wagons approaching the main entrance would be visible from the upper floor roughly two hundred and fifty yards away. The property clerk had seen people carrying empty baskets into the records room yesterday afternoon. A guard sent ahead to observe today had also spotted a small cart in the back yard with shredded paper covering its load.

Nobody concluded from that alone that all those papers were evidence, but the emergency permit issued last night now had an immediate reason for use.

Edith didn't hide the whole party behind some distant hill. The guards and craftsman stopped at a fork inside the alder wood, barely two hundred paces from the side gate, where they could see the agreed signal. Two guards went around to cover the exits a cart could use. The rest waited with their tools, ready to enter as soon as they received the signal. Keeping the main party from approaching openly along the embankment was intended to give those inside less time to empty the cabinets. Approaching quietly didn't mean dispensing with the presentation of the permit.

The three who went ahead to establish what was inside the gate were Edith, Viola, and Adrian.

The old post had no garrison; the town records confirmed it. Edith carried the permit herself, Viola knew the way, and Adrian would remain in an outer passage from which he could withdraw at any moment while checking civilian transport material. The plan was for the craftsman and guards to take over inspection and removal once they had located the receiving room.

Viola studied the place marked as the records office on the map and suggested approaching by the north stairs first. The room had once held dispatch and receipt ledgers. It might merit examination before the old shelving in the warehouse.

Edith asked when she had last been inside.

“Last spring, before the post was decommissioned and handed over.”

“So it's been more than a year since you saw the interior. We don't know how much it's been altered since it was leased.”

“If they empty the records office first—”

“I know what you're worried about.”

The investigator hadn't raised her voice. Viola nevertheless stood a little straighter, as though someone had noticed part of her reason that wasn't suitable for an application.

Adrian took his light bag down from the carriage and set it with the belongings the guards would watch.

“I want us to find something that tells us where the supplies went, too. But what we've brought today is an inspection permit. Needing answers doesn't turn it into an order to storm a building.”

Viola looked at him. “Aren't you impatient?”

“Very. I tried to bring two bottles of ink in case one wasn't enough for all the copying. Edith made me leave one behind.”

Her gaze fell to his bag. At last she let out a slow breath. She spread the map flat and marked the altered areas she couldn't confirm, no longer allowing old memories to guarantee today's safety.

“We'll establish the location from the side yard. Upstairs can wait until the craftsman's checked it,” she said.

Edith nodded, noted the change in the field plan, and asked the guard captain to explain the withdrawal signal.

“If you encounter armed resistance, fall back here,” he said. “If someone is trapped, call for help first. No sheet of paper is worth starting another battle.”

Viola glanced at the post.

Among those sheets of paper might be the one she most needed to clear her name.

Finally, she nodded. “Understood.”

They followed the old water-delivery path. The gravel was even. There was no need to scale walls or crawl through ditches. At a fork, Viola abruptly slowed. The shortcut marked on the map as leading directly into the warehouse yard had been cut by a newly dug drainage channel.

“This has changed,” she said. “We can't follow the old map.”

Edith crouched to inspect it, then chose the detour to the side gate. The loss of a few minutes made Viola press her lips together. Adrian noticed, but didn't urge her to prove that her memories were still useful.

The side gate stood half open. Traces of the post number painted over after decommissioning remained on the frame. The new warehouse tenancy sign had been hung facing the wall inside. Old twine for grain sacks lay piled beneath a window, and rainwater ran through shallow wheel ruts.

Standing outside the gate, Edith announced her identity, displayed the permit, and asked the manager on site to come forward. No one answered.

After her second call, a faint metallic clatter came from inside, followed by running footsteps.

She immediately sent the agreed signal toward the fork without letting Viola pursue.

“The guards covering the exits will handle it.”

The three entered the outer yard through the open gate. Adrian kept to the open air. First he saw a stack of cabinet doors against the wall; then he smelled stale stove ash. Half a cup of tea stood in the narrow gatehouse, its surface not yet filmed over.

This was no abandoned ruin.

Edith checked the ground for tripwires and sharp objects, asked him to stay in the outer passage, and looked into the warehouse from the doorway with Viola. On the second floor was a stretch of wooden gallery with iron-clasped paper cabinets at its end. Below it lay a repair room half sunk into the slope, several planks leaning at an angle outside its small window.

Viola looked up for a while. “The outer gallery used to be wider. They've added another layer of boards here.”

“Make a note. Wait for the craftsman,” Edith said.

On the receiving counter outside the gatehouse, Adrian spotted an empty paper sleeve. It bore no personal name, only a familiar freight-settlement form. He didn't touch it, but pointed out its location to Edith.

The investigator first sketched its position and recorded who had noticed it. Then she lifted the outer layer with tongs. There were no receipts inside, but the flap retained traces of a kind of sealing wax unlike that used by ordinary freight depots. She returned it to its place to be cataloged together with the shelving beside it.

At last, the guard captain's footsteps sounded outside the yard. He quietly reported that someone had escaped over the rear slope. The guards covering the exits hadn't risked pursuit into a gully they couldn't see into. One guard had been left to watch that direction; the others were coming in to secure the site.

Viola's gaze followed the line of the wall, then returned.

She didn't say that she ought to pursue because she knew the roads. She reached out to steady a loose board in the outer passage and first had Adrian take half a step back.

“We wait for the craftsman here,” she repeated Edith's words.

“I shall be extremely conscientious about waiting.”

Once steady, he noticed a piece of old bracer showing inside her cuff. An inelegant line of repair stitches ran across the leather. It was the same hand that had peeled chestnuts yesterday, though it hadn't been so cold then.

“When we get back, we can change the tea at the training ground,” he said suddenly.

She looked at him.

“We don't have to change the place. I really did like the patch of sunshine you chose.”

Her expression softened for an instant, and she answered quietly. Then the craftsman approached, and they moved aside together.

Once its supports had been checked, the warehouse door was slowly pushed open.

From outside, Adrian could see that someone had hastily painted the iron edges of the long military rack at the back of the room black. A patch near its base had flaked away, exposing a partly obscured outline.

Viola's smile vanished.

She didn't touch the object. She only asked Edith to turn the lamp toward the bottom.
