# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Three: The Knight Says She's Responsible for Me
### Chapter 78: Lady Elena Doesn't Get to Choose His Answers

Early on November 23, Elena Rosenberg pushed an unfinished expense form back toward the trading house's adviser.

“Lodgings, firewood, a carriage. You can put those down. A witness's answers aren't something we're buying.”

The adviser glanced at the final line, labeled “special assistance,” and crossed it out.

Adrian Reed sat in the infirmary's back room with a light blanket over his knees. After their return the previous night, Dr. Shaw had listened to his breathing and told him to limit his walking today. Elena had therefore brought breakfast, paperwork, and a conversation capable of making a commercial adviser sweat directly to his chair.

“I only wanted to leave some flexibility,” the adviser explained.

“Then write ‘reserve carriage fare.’ Don't make an injured man wonder whether we're waiting for him to name someone we like.”

The other document on the table had come from Edith Bellamy. Martin Bell, rescued from Alder Watch, remained at the infirmary, but the emergency guard arrangement covered only the initial handover after their return. Under the original plan, the next shift would come from the local garrison.

Unfortunately, the local handling of supplies and incoming documents was precisely what they were investigating.

“The family has an empty house outside town,” the adviser said. “Two guards would be enough.”

“He can't be jolted around in a carriage yet. And if we put him in a Rosenberg house, who decides who's allowed to see him?”

The adviser didn't answer immediately.

Adrian had thought of that quiet house too. Sound windows, a fire, no footsteps in the corridor. But if they controlled every door, Martin would have to explain the house before he could explain anything else when someone accused the estate of coaching his testimony.

“Let's ask what he needs first,” Adrian said.

“First, let's get the doctor's permission to ask.” Elena pushed his cup closer. “That includes you.”

Dr. Shaw allowed twelve minutes. Pain, exhaustion, or the patient's own request could end the conversation sooner. Propped against his pillows, Martin shrank back at the sight of the commercial adviser. Elena left the man outside and entered with an independent legal adviser instead.

Adrian sat by the door without taking out a pen.

“Do I have to move?” Martin asked.

“There are no medical instructions to move you at present,” Elena said. “We've come to ask whether you'd like to request continued protection from the review commission's neutral guards. Once you're well enough, you can consider suitable lodgings.”

“I can't name the person you want.”

“Then don't.”

He stared at her.

The legal adviser explained that protection would not exempt Martin from responsibility for anything he himself might have done. Nor did it guarantee that every statement would be believed. He could ask to have someone present and correct errors in the record. If allegations were made against him, he had to be told separately; a conversation about his care couldn't quietly be turned into a confession.

Martin clutched the blanket. “But I acknowledged deliveries.”

“Then explain which deliveries and what you saw. Acknowledging receipt doesn't mean you saw every item inside every crate.”

After a long silence, Martin made his first request.

“When the guards change, could someone tell me who's coming?”

Elena wrote it down without adding anything about courage, loyalty, or gratitude.

His second request was to send word to his sister. He wanted her to know he was alive and receiving treatment, but he didn't want the location of his room included. The adviser promised to verify the recipient and a safe means of delivery, then let Martin review the message before it was sent.

His third request came out as a question.

“If I get a date wrong, will I still get medicine?”

Outside, a medicine bowl touched a tray with a soft clink.

Elena closed the papers on her lap.

“Dr. Shaw decides your treatment. Her medicine cupboard doesn't judge your memory.”

Slowly, Martin released the blanket.

“What about his fee?” He indicated the legal adviser, though his eyes stayed on Elena. “If I say something bad about your family, will he leave?”

The adviser turned the engagement document so Martin could see it. His fees would come from registered legal-aid funding; his advice would be directed by his client. The sponsor couldn't demand an altered statement because she disliked an answer. Martin could also request another adviser, though arranging one would take time.

“You're my client,” he said. “She pays. She doesn't answer for you.”

Elena leaned back, giving him the place nearest the bed. At last, Martin asked the man a few private questions about his unpaid wages. She didn't lean in to listen. Adrian looked out into the corridor, where a nurse waited patiently for them to clear the doorway, the clean water on her tray trembling gently with each breath.

They didn't use all twelve minutes. Martin grew tired and said he wanted to stop. Everyone stood. Even the unfinished application could wait until the adviser returned that afternoon to confirm it with him.

At the end of the corridor, Elena stopped and pressed the corner of her eye with her uninjured hand.

“I arranged a very good carriage last night.”

“Perhaps it'll deliver blankets,” Adrian said.

“Was that meant to comfort me?”

“It was advice on avoiding wasted carriage hire. My actual profession finally had a chance to contribute.”

She laughed, then tucked the blanket more securely over his knees.

The morning meeting took place in the infirmary's outer room. Colonel Ward brought the current escort instructions; Ada Finch brought the town's patient accommodation register. They did not combine the documents into one enormous seal granting authority over everyone.

Ward first examined Bellamy's report. The outpost contained physical indications of destroyed evidence and unlawful confinement. Martin was a direct witness, and the local handling chain remained under investigation. Those circumstances justified continuing the commission's existing escort detail for this case. They did not give Ward authority to detain every resident of the town.

“The same neutral detail will remain. Handover reports go directly to the commission,” she said. “The infirmary records access to the room. Nonmedical interviews must be arranged in advance. No one may use a change of guard to remove the patient.”

Finch added that the town could arrange a room in an existing care annex nearby once Dr. Shaw permitted a transfer. That would be patient accommodation, not an excuse to establish a military jail. If Martin wished to leave, he should be told the actual risks and any lawful restrictions still applicable to him, rather than finding the door barred with “for your own good.”

The garrison's liaison sergeant frowned.

“This makes our whole unit look untrustworthy.”

“Which is why the order specifies avoiding the handover relationships currently under examination,” Ward said. “It doesn't designate your entire unit as suspects. And you needn't answer for registers you've never handled.”

The sergeant read the wording, appended his objection, and accepted the revised handover.

Elena offered to cover ordinary accommodation, meals, and necessary carriage fares. Payments would go directly to providers against receipts, independently of Martin's statements. The town would continue recording other relief expenses as usual. One conspicuous witness shouldn't cost the patient in the next bed his firewood.

Ward retained Elena's financial undertaking but pushed the blank visitor section back to her.

“Funding doesn't grant additional visiting rights.”

“I know. I'll register too.”

Adrian watched her write her name. Her surname didn't open the sickroom door. It occupied a space exactly as wide as anyone else's.

That afternoon, Martin signed the application after his adviser explained it. Dr. Shaw approved continued treatment where he was, Ward signed the escort extension, and Finch confirmed the accommodation arrangements. Bellamy read the guards' names to Martin and had him repeat how to summon assistance before the new shift accepted the keys.

The previous guards didn't hurry away. Together, they counted the keys for the room, the medicine cupboard, and the window locks. The medicine key went back to the nurse. Protecting a patient did not give a guard authority to decide when he received treatment.

Only after the handover was complete did Elena eat her long-cold pastry.

“This is more trouble than buying a house.”

“Cheaper?”

She looked at the expense sheet.

“For now.”

Back at the inn, Viola was wrestling with a buckle strap.

Her privately owned protective gear lay on the table, its exterior clean after the previous day's work. An old split showed behind the strap. With her left arm injured, she couldn't hold both the leather and the ruler in place using only her right hand.

“Does it have to be repaired immediately?” Adrian asked.

“No. But I'd like to know which section to ask the leatherworker to replace.”

“I can help with that.”

He sat opposite her and held the strap where she indicated. Viola tried measuring it twice. Her gaze moved from the broken stitching to his hand before she spoke hesitantly.

“You're holding the part I need to measure.”

Adrian shifted his hand.

“Now?”

“Now you're covering the end of the ruler.”

He studied the tabletop, beginning to suspect he needed training first.

Viola pressed her lips together and eventually lost the fight. Laughter displaced the tension in her face. This time, she pointed out a very specific place. He followed instructions, and the split finally stayed exposed.

“So cleaning the outside doesn't qualify me to fix the inside,” he observed.

“I can teach you.” She'd answered too quickly. She slowed down. “If you'd like to learn.”

“Start with how not to obstruct the ruler.”

Elena passed the doorway and put down a small sewing box. Viola recognized the needle inside and immediately stopped Adrian's hopeful hand.

“That won't go through leather. It'll break.”

“I thought we might finish the repair today.”

“Today, we can identify what needs repairing.”

She went quiet for a moment after saying it. Adrian didn't turn the remark into a grand metaphor for the investigation. He simply asked her to explain what the strap did.

One section needed to remain flexible. Sewing it rigid would transfer the strain to her shoulder whenever she turned. He drew the split and wrote down the measurements the leatherworker would need, then labeled left and right the wrong way around.

Viola tapped the error with the end of her pen.

“I'm beginning to worry about your clothes.”

“Martha does most of the rescuing.”

“She must work very hard.”

Elena laughed aloud. “That much I can confirm.”

Together, they finished the instructions. The gear still bore its old scratches. Viola's issued sword remained at the garrison. She set aside the repair costs for her private equipment instead of slipping them into military expenses.

Adrian returned the ruler.

“Next time, I can be responsible for holding the right place.”

“If you just want to sit with me next time, we don't have to wait for another strap to break.”

Only after the words were out did her ears begin to redden. She lowered her eyes to put away the sewing box, giving herself a moment. Then she let the invitation stand.

“What do you usually do when you're resting?”

He considered. Sometimes he read. Sometimes he simply sat and watched Martha chase anyone attempting to steal food out of the kitchen.

“You have a cat there too?” Viola asked seriously.

“Sometimes it's me.”

Her shoulders shook with laughter, and she quickly protected her injured arm. Adrian waited until she finished before moving the ruler away from the edge. They stayed a while longer without discovering another task that urgently needed doing.

That evening, he and Elena collected the teacups. She carried the empty cups in her good hand; he took the pot. They walked slowly down the corridor.

“Watching you two measure that strap today,” she said, “I still felt a little jealous.”

He paused. “I hear you.”

“You haven't heard the rest. I'm also glad you didn't have to ask whether I allowed it.”

Adrian set the pot on the kitchen table and turned to her.

“I'd like to sit by the window with you tonight. Not to make up for that.”

“I know.” She offered her hand. “I'd like that too.”

He gently took her uninjured hand. The travel book still lay open at the seaside market, its shell illustration as ugly as ever. They read about a captain dropping his compass into his soup and laughed for a long time, until Cecilia came to show them a new entry.

Bellamy's list of items authorized for examination included the arsenal transit sleeve recovered from the outpost. Cecilia hadn't touched the original. In the field sketch, at the edge of its lining, she had noticed a faint gray arc.

She turned the drawing toward the lamp. Her smile slowly faded.

“I've seen that stroke in the interface documentation we examined in the capital.”

Adrian released the book's spine. He did not reach out with his scarred left hand.

The lamplight on the table was steady. Tomorrow, they would have to ask how that line had found its way into an ordinary shipping sleeve.
