# Chapter 294: The Saint Moves Her Chair Outside

On May 30, Cecilia received two letters asking her to remain part of something.

The Temple administrative meeting confirmed her place presenting the revised oversight and relief charter. Elena's shorter letter said that she and Adrian had freely agreed to a betrothal. Wedding arrangements would follow. No answer was needed now.

Cecilia read that letter twice and put it in her drawer. Paper hadn't turned into a knife, but her chest hurt. The other letter didn't suddenly become more important. She wouldn't use it to prove someone still needed her.

She'd meant to go there already.

Her attendant unfolded the white ceremonial mantle. Would she wear the complete saintly ornaments? Cecilia chose her ordinary small badge. The long mantle would catch the intake office's low cupboards.

“Not solemn enough for today?” the woman asked.

“I'll be more solemn once I stop stepping on myself.”

The attendant smiled and fastened the pin. Cecilia thanked her, then collected her plain bag. It held used notices, actual duty arrangements, and the old instruction saying Anyone in difficulty should ask Cecilia.

The meeting occupied a side hall. It wasn't a ceremony restoring her place. Five existing meal-service and reception stations had authorized representatives to decide for them. Finance staff, ordinary believers, and duty workers had seats of their own. Other districts hadn't supplied authority. The chair clarified that they couldn't be announced as consenting today.

An elderly priest examined the old notice. “Are we supposed to conclude that every Temple institution must be demolished because you designed one box badly?” he asked.

The old notice lay between them. She had brought it as evidence of a mistake she could name, not a reason to stop listening to anyone who disliked her proposal.

“We still use the box,” she replied. “It's excellent since we moved the lock.”

People laughed. The priest didn't. Cecilia placed the revised drawing beside the old one rather than letting the laughter dismiss his fear.

He worried that outside examination would mean recording quiet prayers, suspecting every offering, making clergy explain every kindness to unbelievers. He wasn't demanding Oswin's token back. He didn't know how much of his familiar life would remain.

Cecilia showed him the records provision. Public relief spending, promises, and decisions required scrutiny. Private prayer and belief didn't enter the public register. Accepting food wouldn't require recording attendance at worship. Declining food wouldn't remove the right to speak.

“Do you still believe prayer helps?”

She remembered mornings when she'd had no idea how to help anyone.

“Yes. It hasn't given me the ability to answer for other people.”

He reread the page. The chair didn't record silence as support and preserved his opportunity to speak.

Doctrine wasn't what halted the first discussion.

A meal-service supervisor put a work ledger on the table. The intake trial expired tomorrow. If continuation meant the same two people collecting letters, attending private dictation, delivering urgent files, and setting out bowls, today's fine words amounted to another unpaid night shift.

Cecilia had a familiar answer ready: she could attend more often herself. The words nearly left her mouth. Then she saw the woman's hand still resting on the ledger. She wasn't waiting for a saint to sacrifice sleep. One extra pair of willing hands today wouldn't tell the staff who would be there next week, or whether their actual hours would be paid.

“Let's count the staff we actually have.”

The financial representative produced confirmed resources for next month, excluding unanswered donation promises. Only three stations could safely accommodate private dictation. The other two should continue accepting sealed submissions and making referrals. A neat new map wasn't a reason to open every service everywhere.

Representatives approved redirecting a proposed ceremonial-curtain purchase toward limited reception hours and cupboard-lock repairs. A gap remained. In-person reception would continue three afternoons weekly, with one additional clerk who'd accepted paid work. Existing ordinary counters would take submissions at other times.

“Not Tuesdays,” the clerk said. “That's my turn caring for my mother.”

Cecilia removed Tuesday from his name. The previously frowning supervisor added another person's confirmed short shift. At last, people on the page looked as though they'd really go home.

At noon, a woman in an old coat asked for the saint. She had no complaint receipt and hadn't come to testify. Since her husband's death, she'd continued sitting here sometimes. Recently, people had said that anyone still believing in the Temple must support sacrifice.

Cecilia offered a corridor seat and ordinary water.

“I don't understand the machinery,” the woman said. “I don't want anyone dying for me. I just miss him.”

Holding her cup, Cecilia almost produced an excellent explanation of history and faith. The woman hadn't requested one. She wanted to know whether she was still allowed to sit here.

“You can stay. Pray if you want. Say nothing if you prefer.”

“Could you sit with me?”

“For a quarter of an hour, before the meeting resumes.”

The woman nodded. A particular amount of time seemed more believable than a saint who would always listen. They sat without finishing their water. Somebody dragged a bench nearby. Not a reverent sound. A sound of people still living here.

Before the bell, the woman asked whether she could return. Cecilia showed her the ordinary resting corridor and duty attendant, rather than making another visit depend on seeing her. The woman stayed seated when Cecilia rose. She didn't have to stand and thank the saint.

Back in the hall, Cecilia deleted All believers will understand from the draft. Why, the chair asked? She'd just met someone who didn't want to understand a charter before being allowed a seat.

Final questions on the revised text continued on May 31.

One delegate proposed giving Cecilia ultimate access to special sealed submissions. She'd proved trustworthy. She declined. Complaints involving her would go through independent reception and judicial routes. She could learn what she needed to answer without first seeing every private detail the complainant had supplied.

The elderly priest supported excluding private prayers from public records and subjecting relief accounts to outside examination. He didn't endorse every judgment she'd made about the past. She thanked him for the particular contribution without requesting a declaration of enlightenment.

During the formal vote, each representative acted within their station's authorization. The approved charter applied to those stations. Other districts would receive a text to consider and join, not an announcement falsely enrolling them. The rules prohibited conditioning relief on worship, testimony, or a risk undertaking. Complaints, review routes, responsible posts, and actual funding were settled together.

Next month's reception shifts and spending were approved to succeed the trial on June 1. Enthusiasm wouldn't bridge an expired authorization. Long-term funding would return for scheduled consideration; unprovided expansions weren't promised. Cecilia accepted a defined term coordinating reform, accountable to the meeting and replaceable. She wouldn't own all the keys.

Checking her own arrangement, she found rest days reserved for recall. The clerk had copied a habit from ceremonial schedules. If the saint wasn't in the sanctuary, she could always be found somewhere else.

She showed the entry to the chair.

“I need a day that hasn't already been occupied too.”

“What if something urgent happens?” someone asked.

She asked them to check the duty supervisor already identified in the arrangements. Her name couldn't become the automatic extra shift whenever those arrangements failed. The clerk corrected the entry in front of her instead of merely promising that, in practice, nobody would trouble her very often.

Even after correction, she felt uncomfortable. Agreeing had once brought quick praise for compassion. Now someone might merely find her less convenient.

In the corridor, the meal-service supervisor mentioned an inexpensive weekend concert. Would Cecilia like a seat at the back, with no ceremony attached?

She checked the time and agreed to hear the first half. The thought of enjoying music unrelated to relief work lightened her steps.

Ceremonial staff then wheeled out the high-backed chair prepared for her. Where was her new office? Cecilia studied its gilded back. She didn't have a private office.

“What about the chair?”

“The resting corridor is one short.”

They tried moving it. It was heavier than it looked. She had to summon the actual movers and content herself with clearing a little table from their route. Giving up an elevated position, she admitted, didn't automatically make someone good at furniture removal.

On June 2, the first reception under the charter faced a test before opening.

A regular donor demanded the full text of a complaint mentioning his warehouse. Otherwise, he'd cancel his next, unpaid donation. The clerk neither produced the sealed submission nor ran to the saint for a secret exception.

The supervisor explained what he could learn and answer. Relevant material would be independently checked and provided within the proper scope. Unrelated addresses and private experiences weren't bargaining items. The merchant genuinely withdrew his intended donation, left a written objection, and departed.

The afternoon refreshment program was suspended. Basic meals had confirmed funding independent of that unpaid contribution. Extra tea and pastries did not. Cecilia wrote the notice herself without promising kindness would inevitably bring the merchant back.

Did she regret moving the expensive chair outside now, a helper asked?

She looked toward the corridor. The widow sat there, quietly sharing bread she'd brought with a stranger.

“No.”

She still needed to address the money. A touching scene couldn't pay wages. But somebody was using that chair, and a complaint hadn't left its proper cupboard in exchange for pastries. She would have to keep both facts in view while doing the less satisfying work that came next: preserving the service they could fund, and explaining honestly what they couldn't yet provide.

That evening, Adrian delivered an approved public maintenance explanation through the ordinary work arrangement. At the receiving desk, Cecilia acknowledged the copy. She'd read the personal letter, she added. She wasn't ready to discuss a wedding.

“All right.”

He didn't appear to be waiting for reassurance. She asked whether one term in the document could be made easier to understand. They discussed it and passed the suggestion to the person responsible. Nobody quietly added a private invitation on the back of the page.

Elena waited outside. Watching them leave, Cecilia still felt an ache. Today's choices weren't a consolation prize for losing a romance. Worship hadn't begun yet. First, she went to wash the ink from her hands.

There was no mirror at the basin. She needn't check whether her smile looked sufficiently generous.

At the evening bell, she entered the side chapel and took an ordinary bench. Someone in front turned and called her saint. She answered without correcting him or rising to explain herself to everyone. The title could be spoken without granting her another person's voice.

When prayer began, she bowed her head for those still missed, for unresolved difficulties, and for the small part she'd actually accomplished today.

Outside, the high-backed chair remained available to anyone who needed to rest awhile.
