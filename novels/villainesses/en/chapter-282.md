# Chapter 282: My Surname Cannot Give Anyone's Consent

On the morning of May 10, Elena laid two rings on the table.

One bore her family crest. The other was an old ornament she'd chosen from a box to weigh down paper. Adrian stopped in the doorway with an expression that pleased her immensely, then annoyed her just as much.

“They aren't for you.”

“I haven't asked.”

“You look as though you're waiting for a legal clerk.”

He approached, laughing, and saw yesterday's proposal beneath the rings. Designated bloodlines, training across generations, corresponding exemptions: a well-maintained ancestral house whose occupants apparently needed only to thank their forebears for the roof.

The family representative was already waiting. He brought a proposed statement for today's submission, not a decision the duke had signed. It supported ending the five women's immediate compulsory transfer, while leaving room to discuss designated families bearing future major crises.

“This separates the current danger from long-term arrangements,” he said.

Elena read the sentence twice. “Don't choose me now. You may still choose someone else later.”

“Families might nominate willing participants.”

“Then why preserve heredity?”

He looked at her. He understood. He simply hoped she wouldn't personally dismantle a bargain capable of securing support. Yesterday, family representatives had already said that rejecting such arrangements outright would make them reconsider prospective long-term funding.

Adrian set breakfast within her reach without promising to cover every shortfall. She finally bit into a slice of bread, as though establishing that people still needed breakfast after saying no.

“Take back my complete words,” she told the representative. “I can't dispose of the family's property for my father. I can decide my statement and whether I consent. Don't lend either out beforehand.”

He promised to preserve her wording, but warned that others might reject such distinctions. She knew. That risk wasn't new today. It couldn't mean speaking only words that pleased everyone forever afterward.

Before leaving, Adrian asked whether she wanted to wear the signet. After considering, she put it on herself.

“I don't have to pretend I lack this surname to be entitled to object.”

Nodding, he offered his hand. She briefly took it, then released him. If she trembled inside, he could see it. He shouldn't hurry to speak for her.

At the hearing hall, the noble representative had a fuller proposal. Yesterday's questions were included. Refusers wouldn't personally be compelled, but their families would lose some agreed priority protection and exemptions, and nominate another eligible adult. No fabricated charges this time, he emphasized. Bearers could receive public respect instead of being called villains.

The technical representative first observed that a pedigree wasn't evidence of inheritable marks. Nothing in the proposal established that a family's descendants could replace the existing five marks or qualified anyone for connection. Engineering could be examined later, the proposer said. Today, they should establish who owed the duty.

Elena watched him. Again, a person's place was written down before reasons arrived to fill it. Even if the apparatus later proved unable to recognize that name, consent demanded in advance wouldn't become harmless.

When he finished, she stood. “May the next nominee refuse too?”

“The family should settle that internally.”

“And if everyone refuses?”

A family protected by the kingdom for generations owed something in return, he replied. Some listeners agreed; others lowered their eyes. They were tired of noble inconveniences while people outside the city still lacked homes.

Elena didn't deny their anger. She requested two public records side by side: the noble roster naming people before supplying charges, and yesterday's proposal shifting refusal's cost onto their relatives.

“The first says I'm guilty, so I must go. The second says I'm noble, so I must go.”

She paused, resting her signet-bearing hand beside the table.

“To the chosen person, both end with the same question: why haven't you gone inside yet?”

The representative protested that honor wasn't invariably persecution. Freely accepting responsibility could be good. Why deny that possibility?

“I'm not denying anyone their own choice. I'm refusing the claim that their birth has already made it.”

Adrian heard her, shoulders still taut. This wasn't easy for her. She hadn't suddenly stopped enjoying everything she'd possessed. She liked beautiful clothes and warm rooms, and being heard when someone addressed her. Admitting that was harder than playing a sufferer who'd never benefited.

“My family received advantages. Its involvement must still be investigated. Money, land, specific actions: all deserve answers. But one person's liability cannot become another person's inborn debt of a life. Nor does my refusal mean the old expenditure needn't be examined.”

Given leave to question her, Oswin asked whether she would let the kingdom face potentially greater risk even if everyone opposed her.

“If everyone can consent for me, you needn't ask whether I'm willing.”

She didn't sit triumphantly. She answered the rest too.

“The danger must be addressed. Maintenance, evacuation, and testing the proposed alternative can't stop because I refuse. But I can't guarantee that sending me inside will settle every person's fear. You've acknowledged that the old apparatus still needs maintenance.”

A repair worker attending the inquiry suddenly asked whether she merely wanted ordinary people to share the expense while she spoke beautifully from a safe room.

Paling further, she submitted the limited borrowing guarantee she was currently responsible for. She didn't offer it as the complete account of noble obligations or count relief money as her own donation.

“This is nowhere near proof that I've done enough. Keep asking what I've paid and what I owe. But don't replace an unsettled financial account with someone's body. That makes things easiest for those who actually owe money.”

The worker studied her, then said nobles should still contribute more. Nobody expelled him. She didn't demand gratitude for the guarantees first.

“Then keep discussing what more, and who pays. Don't turn it halfway through into who dies.”

The representative turned to his last page and slid a prepared acknowledgment toward her. She need only agree to further discussion based on family tradition. Not go behind the five locks now. Support could be preserved. His tone softened: she shouldn't mistake every possibility for an execution order.

Elena studied the empty line. She knew very well how to make a concession look harmless: simply add “subject to further discussion” at the end.

“Before that line, write: this does not signify consent for myself or my descendants to be designated.”

He didn't write.

“That would defeat the entire purpose.”

“Then I don't agree to your purpose.”

She returned the pen without tearing the acknowledgment. The recorder preserved its presentation and her refusal. Later readers shouldn't receive only an image of her cold face and have to guess whether she'd acted out of spite.

No new law immediately passed. Several representatives maintained their proposal; others wanted further explanations of costs and alternatives. The chair confirmed Elena's statement as her explicit personal refusal, not the five women's collective agreement. Her family's representative couldn't supply her missing signature simply by being present. One woman's statement hadn't reformed the kingdom.

When she sat, her fingers were indeed trembling. Adrian moved water closer without seizing her hand for a public display of protection. She drank first, then touched his fingertips beneath the table.

At the break, the family representative found her outside. Two families considering long-term investment wouldn't sign new statements of support for now. Previously promised ordinary relief hadn't been withdrawn here, but future money was less certain.

She first asked whether anyone had described leaving the new discussions as reclaiming relief already paid. No. Both families still intended to honor existing commitments. Relieved, she asked him to record the distinction. She could endure losing support for her position. While people waited for bread, she couldn't inflate uncertain rumors into an actual cutoff or let genuine shortages disappear behind appearances.

Had he received explicit statements, or was this speculation? He repeated their words. She asked him to preserve the record and not falsely tell relief organizers that everything remained unchanged.

“You could still issue a further explanation.”

“The explanation is long enough. Keep asking about other possibilities. Don't find someone absent to fulfill the condition I refused.”

After he left, she stood by the window. Adrian asked whether she wanted company. Yes. He needn't swear eternal allegiance before being allowed near.

“I wanted to sign.”

He didn't protest in astonishment that she wasn't that sort of person.

“Because it said only discussion?”

“Because several people would immediately decide I'd finally become sensible. I know that feeling very well.”

Looking down, she rubbed the raised crest. “I don't want applause to tell me whether I'm allowed a chair today.”

He fetched a real chair from the window, first checking whether anyone needed it. She glared, then finally laughed and sat.

“You did that deliberately.”

“This one requires no signature.”

“Only endurance of your jokes.”

He sat beside her, listening. She still feared disappointing her father, and that people waiting for repairs would think she didn't care. She didn't ask him to prove everyone would understand. He could help with her letter home, he said, but the words were hers. He wouldn't rewrite a difficult refusal as a cheerful victory.

Moving closer, she asked to borrow his shoulder. After he agreed, she rested her forehead lightly against his coat. Today's clean one. At least she needn't worry about taking away an oil stain.

“You see,” he murmured, “laundry needs ongoing maintenance too.”

She pushed him, laughing into the fabric. When she straightened, her eyes remained a little red.

Viola passed with an ordinary book to return. Seeing the old list protrude from Adrian's pocket, she reminded him that “No guarding doors” didn't prohibit other people from emerging through it. Laughing, Elena told him to put it away. That paper was busier than an attendant now.

Viola laughed too. Their closeness didn't oblige her to perform magnanimity. After a brief pause, she admitted she wanted to return by herself to the resting area where her caregiver waited, rather than sit awhile. Adrian agreed. He didn't follow to smooth away her feelings with a different promise.

Before the afternoon ended, Cecilia submitted today's practical questions to the reception desk. If local organizers still traded housing, honor, or family penalties for signatures, who could discover it? Who could receive objections from people afraid to disclose their names? Who would inspect those responsible? Existing offices had separate remits, not a collective guarantee that everyone could safely refuse.

The chair required those concerned to explain workable measures at the next meeting. Cecilia kept her receipt without announcing completed oversight. Saying no inside this hall today didn't mean everyone outside its doors dared do the same.

Leaving, Elena still wore her signet. It hadn't gone into a fire. One refused signature hadn't made her unrelated to her family.

But her name wasn't on the paper arranging other people's futures.

Adrian waited below the steps with two ordinary dinner choices. She considered them carefully, selected what she wanted, and handed the other back.

This time, she answered only for herself.
