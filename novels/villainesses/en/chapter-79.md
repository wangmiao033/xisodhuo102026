# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Three: The Knight Says She's Responsible for Me
### Chapter 79: The Saint Finds No Answers in the Light

On November 24, Cecilia rolled up her sleeves before entering the town office's examination room.

The transfer ring from the capital was not on the table. There were only two sealed cases, a small inspection lamp, and a plain glass plate that the innkeeper had been using to weigh down menus the previous evening.

Once the glass was washed, a clerk checked its edges. Its intended use went on the borrowing receipt. The innkeeper still insisted that someone would pay if it broke.

“Good,” Adrian Reed said. “At least we know where today's mysterious phenomena start costing money.”

Cecilia smiled, then opened the interface notes she was authorized to retain.

She remembered the arc. Remembering something and confirming it were two separate tasks.

Bellamy checked the original seals first. The arsenal rack fitting went on the left. Its royal inspection stamp had not become a magical symbol. On the right lay the transit sleeve marked RW-6. The field sketch recorded a small gray-silver film inside its lining, originally adhered to a fold, with one edge lifting after exposure to moisture.

“I didn't scrape it off,” Bellamy said. “I only opened the fold for registration. The drawing was all I showed you last night.”

The town's licensed artificer put on thin gloves. He usually repaired sealing and registration equipment. Before agreeing to bring his ordinary low-power inspection lamp, he had asked for assurance that nobody expected him to dismantle an ancient core.

He positioned the lamp outside the sleeve and adjusted its shutter.

Two intersecting arcs emerged on the gray-silver film. The outer stroke was incomplete; the inner one was as fine as a paper fiber.

Cecilia stopped him from increasing the light. She moved the reference page aside and drew what she could see on a separate sheet. Then she asked the clerk to cover the reference diagram while the artificer made his own drawing.

The two new sketches had the same gap.

The diagram in the reference notes was complete.

“They may belong to the same family of linking marks,” she said. “But we can't use the complete diagram to fill in what's missing from the original.”

Viola sat by the door, her left arm supported on soft cloth. She was there to witness the handling of objects recovered during the inspection she had joined. She carried no sword and had no responsibility for guarding the room. At the word “linking,” she looked at the unremarkable sleeve.

“Linked to whom?”

The artificer looked up. “Let's not jump to people. Cargo registrations and old archival media can use linking marks too. First, I need to see whether it still responds to a basic examination.”

He proposed a very small procedure: one limited exposure to inspection light, with an artificial calibration piece made in a known interface pattern placed nearby. The piece would carry no name and connect to no personal mark. No wires would be attached to the original, and it would not touch anyone's skin.

Bellamy checked the recovery authorization and separately requested Ward's approval for the limited examination. While they waited, everyone stepped away from the table. Waiting did not amount to consent.

Cecilia sat by the window to drink some water. Adrian moved her chair back from where she'd placed it too close. Sunlight fell between them.

“Are you afraid it might be genuine?” she asked.

“I'm afraid it'll flash once and I'll try to explain everything.”

She looked into her cup. “So am I.”

He remembered the convenient scenes in Crown of Fate. A symbol glowed, a character named the traitor, and the story moved on without asking who had handed which paper to whom. But the real Martin had clutched his blanket even in his sleep the previous night.

Light couldn't remember those wagons for him.

“What if we learn nothing today?” Adrian asked.

“I'll go to the infirmary and finish the relief receipts I promised to organize this afternoon. They don't concern one person's winter coat any less because they don't glow.”

The door opened. Approval had been narrowly drawn. After reading it, the artificer struck out the suggested second exposure at increased intensity. If the first couldn't be understood within the approved limit, they would stop for the day.

The clerk recorded the lamp's starting level. A blank film went under the light first, producing no arcs. The artificial calibration piece displayed its own short line in the designated position, showing that the lamp worked. Only then did they examine the sleeve's lining.

Cecilia stood at the stipulated distance and maintained a low-strength isolation screen as directed. She had assisted with document examinations before and could perform that part. It did not qualify her to dismantle an unknown device.

The light narrowed onto the fold.

For the first breath, nothing happened.

On the second, an exceedingly fine line brightened inside the gray-silver film. The artificial marking beside it flashed immediately afterward.

The interval was brief. The artificer switched off the lamp without waiting for an exclamation.

Sweat showed at Cecilia's temples. Holding the screen had taken more effort than she'd expected, like supporting a tray that suddenly sagged. She released it and sat down before asking that the order of events be recorded.

“The original illuminated first. The calibration piece responded afterward.”

“I saw that too,” Bellamy said.

Using separate pens, the clerk recorded the two observers' statements without combining them into “verification successful.”

The artificer checked the energy gauge and covered the lamp completely. The film did not continue glowing, and there was no obvious change in the tabletop's temperature. A little energy remained, but instead of moving the calibration piece and trying again, he sealed the supply switch and recorded the remaining level.

One response could not rule out every source of error. Repositioning and repetition would require a fresh assessment. The fact that everyone was still seated there did not grant permission for a second exposure.

“That's enough for today,” he said.

Viola instinctively stood, as though to shield the suddenly pale Cecilia from something, then noticed there was no enemy beside the table. She pulled out a chair away from the draft and asked whether Cecilia wanted it.

Cecilia nodded and walked the few steps herself. She did not conceal her fatigue as though it had never occurred.

Adrian poured some water. “Dizzy?”

“A tight feeling across my forehead. I need a little rest.”

“Do you want to try again?”

She looked at him, a small smile forming.

“What do you want me to say?”

“The truth.”

“I want to know more. But I won't do any more today.”

He handed her the cup without claiming her decision as a victory of his own persuasion.

The conclusion was smaller than those two tiny flashes.

The film and a particular artificial interface mark had shown a limited response consistent with resonance between linked media. The material might once have been used for registration or transfer and later reused to line the transit sleeve. Other purposes remained possible. Under the available conditions, they could not establish what it had once recorded, much less read anyone's name, intentions, or responsibility.

“Does this establish that the royal authorities put it there?” Bellamy asked.

“No.” The artificer indicated the other case. “That inspection stamp shows that its object passed through some form of arsenal administration. This response is a separate phenomenon. Putting them on the same table doesn't give them a common owner.”

Cecilia asked the clerk to retain the uncompleted sketch and record the conditions, energy expenditure, and reason for stopping. She wrote a separate inquiry to Ivo, enclosing only the diagrams and observations cleared for sharing, without Martin's room or military deployments.

She did not expect his reply to solve the case before tomorrow.

Viola waited for her in the corridor.

“Your hand is still shaking.”

Cecilia looked down and noticed fine ripples in her water.

“I exerted myself a little more than expected.”

“If this really is connected to what you were investigating before...” Viola stopped, her gaze moving past Cecilia to Adrian.

She seemed prepared to take on something far too heavy. Even her hands were ready.

Cecilia spoke first. “You don't have to take responsibility for this as well.”

“I haven't said anything.”

“Your shoulders were quite clear.”

Viola blinked and slowly let her shoulders drop.

“They've been very busy lately,” Adrian murmured.

She turned toward him, ready to object, then couldn't help smiling. The movement tugged her injured arm, and she obediently adjusted the sling.

Back at the inn, Cecilia wanted to write her notes out neatly. Elena read the artificer's recommendation that she rest, then removed the unopened ink. Cecilia could dictate corrections to the clerk now and check the fair copy tomorrow.

“Is that a medical instruction?” Cecilia asked.

“It's a suggestion inspired by watching you put the date in the name field.”

“Then I accept.”

She laid down her pen and settled against the sofa.

A letter from the capital, received by the inn, had slipped beneath the blanket. Mira described the last few days at the advice office: one visitor's medicine receipt had been corrected; another still needed confirmation from the hospital. Even the nearly dead plant on the windowsill had produced a new leaf. Halfway through, Cecilia laughed softly.

“She says Vera borrowed my chair.”

“For an urgent case?” Adrian asked.

“To reach the tea on top of the cupboard.”

She folded the letter beneath that line. A faint disappointment showed in her eyes.

“They're doing very well without me.”

Adrian didn't immediately assure her that everyone was waiting for her return. She had spent so much time teaching people to check records. The office coming to a halt whenever she left was hardly the outcome she'd wanted.

“When you go back, you can sit and have tea,” he said. “You won't have to rescue the entire room first.”

She looked at him for a moment before her smile returned.

“Then I'll buy them pastries. With my own money, not relief funds.”

She reached for a pen, stopped halfway, and glanced at the ink Elena had confiscated. For tonight, she would simply remember what she wanted to say.

“Remind me not to ask only how much work they've finished. I also want to know whether Mira has finished knitting her scarf.”

“I will.”

Outside, someone called out freshly split firewood for sale. The voice grew fainter. Cecilia put the letter away and finally allowed herself to tend to what remained of the day's exhaustion.

Adrian offered her a light blanket. She accepted it, then looked up very seriously.

“Could you sit a little closer?”

“Yes. Just don't ask me to maintain an isolation screen.”

“I won't. At the moment, keeping two biscuits apart would be too much effort.”

He sat beside her. She didn't rest her head on him. She simply let her sleeve remain close to his and sat quietly for a while.

“This is enough,” she said.

Elena brought three cups of water. She glanced at the space between them, set one cup in front of Adrian, and sat on his other side.

“No one is using my biscuits as calibration pieces today.”

“They are remarkably uniform in size,” Cecilia observed.

“All the more reason.”

Their laughter was quiet. Viola brought over another chair, keeping the strain off her injured arm and accepting the innkeeper's help halfway. The four of them sat together without anyone first having to demonstrate greater usefulness.

Later, notice arrived of the military hearing. It would begin the following morning at Greymarch's assembly building, with Ward continuing to preside. Witnesses would attend in order; defense-sensitive records would be examined separately under restricted access.

Adrian read the date aloud. Bellamy brought acknowledgments from the regional relay route as well. The independent-record inquiries sent on the twenty-second were being accepted. The first local comparison documents would be available that evening. The more distant arsenal counterparts were still in transit.

Viola took her attendance notice and set it beside the cleaned shoulder plate.

She didn't ask whether the little light could clear her name.

“They'll ask about the books at the outpost tomorrow.”

“We'll tell them what happened,” Adrian said.

The books were gone. But there were still people who could separately describe who chose to rescue Martin, who confirmed the withdrawal, and where each had first seen the fire.

Cecilia opened her eyes and looked at the examination report on the table.

The brief light they had barely managed to observe had given them one more question to investigate slowly.

Tomorrow, they would still have to take paper, names, and incomplete memories into a room where living people made the decisions.
