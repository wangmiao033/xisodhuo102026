# Volume Four: The Witch Wants to Study My Betrothal

## Chapter 91: The Witch Says You Can Blame the Stairs

The carriage left Greymarch early on December 7.

From inside, Viola watched Owen step aside to let it pass. For the first time, he stood by the road without waiting for her next order. He raised a hand in farewell. She raised hers and kept watching until a line of bare trees hid the gate.

Across from her, Cecilia had already opened her sewing bag.

“I thought you couldn't knit,” Adrian said.

“That's why Mira only taught me the simplest stitch.”

She unfolded a half-finished strip. It was supposed to become a strap for a water flask. At present, one end was broad and the other narrow, as though it had decided to choose a different purpose for itself.

Elena studied it and recommended not naming it yet.

“If it ends up being a hat, you won't have to take anything back.”

Viola lowered her head, laughing, with the wooden bird's box in her lap. When the carriage jolted, she steadied it. Only then did she realize she had made a wooden bird more secure than her sword.

They stopped at the first posting inn in the afternoon. Adrian walked for a little while. His chest felt fine, but he kept their agreement and returned to sit by the fire. Elena draped the brown-and-dark-green scarf over her shoulders. Across steaming bowls of soup, they argued about exactly how many compasses the captain in their book could still have left to lose.

“At least one,” Adrian said. “Otherwise the next chapter can't start.”

“He could ask directions.”

“We'd miss out on so many wonderful mistakes.”

She laughed and passed him a piece of bread. Beside them, Viola studied a map sold by the inn. On discovering that its publisher had drawn two rivers across the same mountain, she questioned the owner thoroughly. In the process, she saved a driver visiting his relatives from taking an extremely long shortcut.

Their lodging for the evening was already booked. They did not continue simply because the road was dry for the moment. The reply left by the express rider arrived after dinner. The research tower had received their inquiry. Luna would see them on the afternoon of the eighth, and they could stay in the tower's guesthouse at the ordinary visitor rate.

A line in smaller writing ran along the bottom.

Please bring your original observation records. There is no need to bring a great conclusion already reached. The kitchen does not currently serve great conclusions for dinner.

“At least she has a kitchen,” Adrian said.

Cecilia read a little farther down. “She says breakfast costs extra.”

On the eighth, they continued up the slopes of East Ridge. The morning mist lifted before noon, and the tower emerged above the gray treetops.

It did not float. Nor was there a dragon wrapped around it. Five stories of stone stood against the hillside, with two rows of ordinary workshops adjoining the ground floor. Aprons and one very large sock hung from a washing line. The wind kept blowing the sock through a window, where an irritated hand pushed it out again.

The plaque beside the entrance read Forbidden Arts Research Tower. A wooden board beneath it added: Visitors, please ring. Third brick loose. Kicking the door is not an acceptable substitute for knocking.

After reading it, Viola made a point of standing well clear of that brick.

The bell had barely sounded when someone in the courtyard called, “Stop! Don't pull that!”

Adrian immediately let go of the bell rope.

“Not you,” a woman added. “The gentleman at the gate may come in. The gentleman in here needs to take his hand off the winch.”

A small cargo lift had caught halfway up its track. A young worker gripped a backup rope while the brass wheel below it turned the wrong way. The woman on the steps set down her drawing board, told him to get behind the stone wall, and pulled a red lever beside her.

A purple crystal inside a protective housing flashed once, then went dark. The platform descended slowly along the final stretch of track. Dust rose around the base. The wooden crate had, at least, landed on no one.

The woman went over, checked the worker's hand, then sighed at the crystal that had lost its color.

“Excellent. Today's heating budget died with dignity.”

Flushing, he explained that he had thought he could catch the crate before the pulley came loose.

“Next time, let go first. That lift isn't worth your fingers. And I'm not so hard up that I'd ask you to pay its repair bill with them.”

She instructed the caretaker to take the lift out of service until it had been dismantled and inspected. The crystal was a precharged braking component. It had absorbed the force of the last part of the drop. Recharging it would take at least half a day, which meant the temperature-controlled cabinet would have to surrender the same power supply that evening.

Adrian watched her cross an entire line out of her experiment schedule. The pretty purple light hadn't spared her the work of rearranging things.

She looked up at her guests.

Ink stained her dark apron. Her sleeves were neatly rolled, and a short pencil rested behind her ear. She was a woman in her twenties, openly curious, looking as though she wanted to ask each of the four visitors ten questions and was deciding who should go first.

“Luna Ashcroft,” she said. “The one responsible for research, signing bills, and occasionally keeping the others fed.”

Cecilia introduced everyone. When she heard Adrian's name, Luna's gaze lingered on his face. Unlike her counterpart in the game, she did not immediately produce a contract for him to sign.

“You're better-looking than the postman suggested.”

Elena raised an eyebrow. “Does he provide descriptions as well?”

“He said only that four visitors were bringing a great deal of paper. A great deal of paper doesn't usually make me happy.”

She accepted the introduction and suggested they settle into the guesthouse first. Elena explained that they meant to keep the rest arrangement agreed upon during the journey; they could talk later that afternoon.

Luna glanced at the schedule with its newly crossed-out line and looked faintly disappointed.

“Apparently even the visitors won't work for me this afternoon.”

“We haven't applied,” Adrian said.

She laughed. “Good answer. People who know to ask about pay on the first day generally survive long enough to collect it a second time.”

The guesthouse was clean. Its windows overlooked low, frost-covered shrubs. Adrian took a short nap. When he woke, the tower bell had already struck four in the afternoon. Someone downstairs was practicing a flute and getting the same passage wrong every time. It sounded more like a place where people lived than the game's forbidden-magic soundtrack ever had.

That afternoon, Viola checked the posting schedule above the correspondence cabinet. Letters from East Ridge to the regional military office went out every other day. Beside her copy of the leave approval, she noted the latest date on which she could send arrangements for her medical review. Distance from the garrison did not make fourteen days stretch forever.

Cecilia came back from downstairs with an ordinary visitor price list. The caretaker had asked whether they wanted to use the laundry. She carefully marked four portions of hot water, then discovered that even stubborn ink stains had a separate charge.

“I understand Luna's apron now,” she said.

“That might be more persuasive than forbidden magic,” Adrian said.

Elena moved her book chest beside the window and tried to open the stiff frame. The new skin on her hand pulled painfully. She stopped and asked the caretaker to help instead of hiding the inconvenience behind an insistence that she was completely healed.

The window opened a few inches. Cold air and the scent of the woods entered together. In the courtyard, workers had dismantled the damaged pulley onto the ground. Another stood below the lift to keep the vegetable cart from taking a shortcut beneath it.

Adrian watched for a while. He remembered sitting in front of a screen and complaining that this volume started too slowly. Back then, he had only wanted to reach the witch's character illustration. It would never have occurred to him to ask which route the vegetable delivery ought to take.

“What are you thinking about?” Elena asked.

“That the people here need dinner too.”

“Profound. You'd better not try to pay for our rooms with it.”

He laughed and adjusted the chair by the window for her. She sat down, opening the travelogue to where they had stopped the night before. This time, she didn't ask him to read. She asked whether he wanted to listen.

Adrian nodded. She read more slowly than he did. When the captain lost his compass for the third time, she stopped and looked up.

“You were right.”

“There's still time to praise me.”

“We'll discuss it when he finds a fourth.”

Outside, a faint rattle announced that the repair crew was testing the pulley. Elena went on reading. Adrian leaned back in his chair and finally stopped treating rest as a gap he would have to make up for as soon as possible.

Toward evening, they met Luna again in the visitors' room. She had taken off the apron, but the short pencil remained behind her ear. Their forwarded drawings lay on the table. Not far away, a small brass wheel stood beneath a glass cover.

Luna first asked which surface the gray-silver lining had originally adhered to, then had Cecilia mark the positions of the lamp and comparison specimen. When she heard that maintaining the isolation screen had been more demanding than expected, she didn't praise the saint's courage. She turned the drawing halfway around and asked whether the light shield had also covered the edge of the screen.

Cecilia paused and fetched the original notes.

“That part isn't clear enough.”

“Then leave it blank. Add it when you've asked whoever actually adjusted the lamp.”

On a separate sheet, Luna sketched two possible arrangements, showing how different conditions could produce the same observation. No new spell was cast. She did not ask Cecilia to repeat the experiment on the spot. Soon the table was so crowded with arrows that Adrian had to move his teacup to his lap.

“Is every table here dangerous?” he asked.

“Not that one.” Luna pointed to the brass wheel. “It only gives away one very ordinary secret.”

She lifted the glass cover to reveal a fine wire leading to a sensor. The sensor clipped onto a fingertip. Tiny changes in blood flow made a membrane vibrate; a small stored-energy cell amplified the movement and drove a pointer on a scale. The whole device was in plain sight, with a wire far too short to follow someone through a wall.

“A heartbeat,” she said.

Elena looked at the little clip. “What does that have to do with today's drawings?”

“Nothing at present. I thought I might show you something that doesn't require guessing while we wait for dinner.” Luna clipped it to her own finger. “So you won't assume every device in the tower decides the fate of the world.”

The pointer began to move. She timed it with a small sandglass, then removed the clip. The pointer stopped.

After inspecting the arrangement, Adrian asked whether it kept a record.

“This one has a pointer, no recording paper. You can count how fast someone's heart is beating. You can't see who they're thinking about. Want to try? Half a minute. Take it off if it feels uncomfortable.”

He agreed and let her attach the sensor to a finger of his right hand.

He flinched at the cool metal. Luna adjusted the padding and checked that it wasn't pinching before turning on the little switch. The pointer soon swung back and forth like a tiny pendulum eager to prove useful.

“Faster than mine,” she said. “Perhaps seeing me has—”

“You just promised it couldn't tell who he was thinking about,” Elena reminded her.

Luna laughed and raised her free hand. “Perhaps it was the stairs he just climbed. Don't worry. I'm merely nominating my charm as an entirely unsupported possibility.”

“Is that the purpose of the test?”

“That's the purpose of my running mouth. The only thing we can actually note is the number of beats in this half minute.”

Adrian watched the pointer.

“The stairs certainly contributed,” he said. “But you do have a lovely smile.”

Luna's hand paused around the sandglass.

She had plainly been ready with another elegant tease. At the straightforward compliment, she looked down at the glass instead, as though two grains of sand had suddenly gone missing.

“Mr. Reed, you're interfering with the observer.”

“We can put that in the margin of error too.”

Viola turned away, shoulders trembling. In an entirely serious voice, Cecilia asked whether they ought to attach a second sensor to the observer. Luna promptly switched the device off and removed Adrian's clip herself.

“Dinner. The kitchen's schedule deserves the greatest respect in this tower.”

Only after she turned away did Elena lean closer to Adrian.

“You've become very good at answering back.”

“I'm learning.”

“Remember to say something nice to me tomorrow.”

“I could start tonight.”

The corner of her mouth moved. She took away his cold tea.

Before they entered the dining room, Adrian removed his other glove to wash his hands. Lamplight fell across the old crescent scar on the back of his left hand. Passing the doorway, Luna glanced at it, then at the glove he still held.

She did not reach for him.

“Tomorrow morning,” she said, “let's find a wider table.”

He looked up. She was already holding the dining-room door for Cecilia and complaining that the soup smelled more promising than her research.
