# Chapter 138: The Saint Keeps the Lamps for the Living

On January 13, the first thing Cecilia took Adrian to see was not a sacred relic. It was a leaking lamp.

It stood on a bench in the chapel yard. The elderly woman organizing the Winter Lantern Festival was wiping oil from the wood. Seeing Cecilia, she held up a chipped jug.

“This one can't go out again. Someone told me yesterday it only had to last one night.”

Cecilia crouched to examine the crack, then asked the apprentice to use a sound jug. The broken one went into the tool room with a wooden tag marking it out of service. An important visitor was no reason to hide it.

“You wanted the old calendars.” She stood. “First, we wait until the custodian finishes giving out the new lamps.”

People queued with baskets and old shades wrapped in aprons. At the back, two children compared how little snow remained on their shoes. The five places Adrian had studied on paper yesterday suddenly seemed less remote.

He carried only approved copies of the transfer pages. The original register remained in custody. He had not turned the dates in those five columns into dates when disasters occurred.

Cecilia had said January 18 sounded familiar.

Today, she would not let familiarity stand in for an answer.

The woman, Mrs. Pike, kept the chapel's supplies. After issuing the last lamp, she cleaned her hands and unlocked a low cupboard containing old festival calendars and copies of relief accounts.

“Which year?”

“Transfer records from several years,” Adrian said. “All dated January 18.”

“Then we'll check each one. Heavy snow delayed the outer district's lamps by two days one year. This year's calendar can't speak for the past.”

Cecilia nodded at him with the faintest satisfaction.

“She said the same when I got dates wrong as a child.”

“You remembered festivals and days without lessons equally well,” Mrs. Pike said. “The second rather better.”

Adrian laughed. Cecilia did not argue. She pulled out the woman's chair.

The Winter Lantern Festival fell on January 18. Evening prayers accompanied distributions of lamp oil and old clothing to relief houses. The public calendars for the relevant years agreed. The dates on the transfer pages might still indicate ordinary annual accounting.

Cecilia opened an old commentary to a thumb-marked passage.

The previous register closed on the festival's eve; the next year's volume was delivered the following day. The text concerned recording and continuation. It said nothing about making anyone bear a calamity.

“So ordinary administration could explain the overlap,” Adrian said.

“Part of it. I don't want everyone lighting a lamp called an accomplice because someone may have borrowed my faith.”

He remembered the broken jug and nodded.

Mrs. Pike fetched separately stored collection receipts. Different relief houses had collected supplies on different days, according to weather, travel, and opening hours. The five transfers, however, all recorded completion on the eighteenth. Local delays had not altered them.

“These aren't the same kind of completion,” Cecilia murmured.

Covering the headings with two blank sheets, she asked Adrian to read them again.

The supply receipts said received. The old transfers said entered into the next cycle.

Registration in a new cycle, not confirmation that the oil had arrived. Not a death that day.

Adrian moved a question mark in his notes. He had feared the dates were appointed hours of disaster. That frightening guess lost half its shape, leaving a more precise question.

Who had placed five different matters within the same transfer cycle?

Cecilia did not answer what she did not know. Mrs. Pike approved extracts from the public commentary, with each edition identified. No private relief register left the cupboard. Only dates and institutional identifiers were copied from receipts showing that physical deliveries differed.

Before noon, raised voices interrupted them.

A man clutching an old lamp refused to leave its cracked shade with the apprentice. It had belonged to his mother. Replacing it would mean it was no longer her lamp.

The apprentice, red-faced, kept repeating that it mustn't be lit.

Cecilia went outside without raising her own voice.

“You may take the old shade home,” she told the man. “But use this one if you want a light tonight. We aren't taking your mother's things.”

He held it tighter.

“They say the old ones bring protection.”

“Then let's not let the fire destroy it.”

He stared. Eventually, he accepted the new shade too.

Under the eaves, Adrian watched her help the apprentice separate the lamp wicks. She had not called the man superstitious or promised, as a saint, that broken glass could never hurt anyone.

When she returned, he was still watching.

“What is it?”

“You didn't quote the liturgy.”

“He wanted to keep his mother, not a passage in a book.”

She wiped oil from her fingertips.

“I don't always manage it. I used to begin by explaining which sentence he'd misunderstood.”

Adrian offered a clean cloth. She accepted it without immediately returning to the papers.

“Do you have other plans today?”

“Delivering our findings this afternoon.”

“Then have lunch with me. There's a place nearby where you don't have to prove yourself worthy of the soup.”

“Soup can demand that?”

“Some formal dinners seem to.”

Her grave expression finally broke into a smile. He agreed. She folded the cloth carefully, as though his answer deserved more care than he had realized.

Before they left, Mrs. Pike called them back.

She had found a folded receipt they should not copy as they had the others. Water had blurred half the date. The surviving stroke might belong to seventeen or nineteen. Adrian had recorded seventeen.

“I remember rain,” she said. “Remembering the weather isn't remembering the date.”

Cecilia handed him his pen without vouching for an old friend.

He struck out the number and noted the damage. An apprentice recognized the recipient's mark and offered to ask.

“Do,” Cecilia said. “Don't tell him which day we're hoping for.”

The apprentice swallowed the words he had been about to say.

They excluded that receipt from the comparison. The remaining legible ones still showed that supplies had not invariably arrived on the eighteenth. Losing one example neither destroyed the finding nor gave them an excuse to fill the gap.

Mrs. Pike put it away, then patted Cecilia's sleeve.

“You hated leaving a question unanswered as a child.”

“I still do.”

“You managed today.”

Cecilia glanced at Adrian. He showed her his corrected notes. She smiled before walking out beside him.

The soup shop's tables were narrow. Sitting opposite him, she ordered stewed beans and two portions of bread. Their promised lunch did not become another hearing.

Instead, she described the paper lanterns she had received as a child.

She had thought a saint ought to choose the plainest. Only later did she learn that the woman handing them out saved a red one for her every year. Not as a test. She remembered how Cecilia had smiled at her first red lantern.

“I asked how she knew I liked them.”

“What did she say?”

“That I was always the last to leave, and I kept looking back.”

Adrian laughed. Cecilia watched him seriously.

“I try not to make people guess that long now.”

He set down his spoon, understanding that she meant more than lanterns.

“I'm glad we're having lunch together. You don't have to guess that.”

Her ears reddened. She did not press him to turn enjoying her company into another promise. Drawing her bread closer to the bowl, she said softly that this was good.

Reality attached a cost to that quiet when they returned to her office.

A deacon organizing the festival was waiting. He had heard about the calendars. He feared their findings would mingle the festival with the missing-person notices. People were already saying they could avoid misfortune by staying away from evening prayers.

“Please don't explain the dates publicly yet,” he said. “Let us finish this year's festival first.”

Cecilia asked him to sit. Rather than refusing outright, she proposed a joint statement of the verified facts: the public festival date, the difference between delivering supplies and entering a new registration cycle, and what matching dates could not establish.

He looked at Adrian and lowered his voice.

“If we mention questions, people will still be frightened.”

“If we only tell them not to be frightened, finding the old dates will make them think even the lamp oil was a lie.”

“You're the saint.”

“Which is why I can't let them believe I've examined every source behind those five pages.”

Adrian did not speak for her. This was not a proven villain across the desk. It was someone who also wanted to protect the festival, but would accept silence in exchange for peace.

Eventually, the deacon took a sourced explanation to his superior. He promised no joint signature. Cecilia retained her submitted version without describing hesitation as a Temple refusal to investigate.

When the door closed, she leaned back and exhaled.

“Was I too slow?”

“You gave him a choice that didn't require lying.”

“He might not take it.”

“Yes.”

After a moment, she smiled. “Your consolations really are difficult to misunderstand.”

The account delivered to the palace that afternoon was shorter than expected. The five records shared an annual transfer point; they did not prove simultaneous disasters. The questions concerned who compiled and received them, and whether the present year still used that cycle.

Isabella read it in the outer room without requesting the addition of periodic Temple sacrifices.

Her question was more practical.

“Where can we see this year's arrangements for the eighteenth?”

Cecilia produced the public calendar. Ordinary prayers and relief distributions were listed. Administrative transfers had only a separate index reference, beyond her current access.

Ellery then supplied a page from the year's road and guard coordination notice. It was an ordinary summary for people planning journeys to the palace. Laying it before a princess did not turn it into a secret military order.

Some guards would change positions on the evening of the seventeenth to support the festival handover.

The summary said civilian passage would be unaffected. Yet the diagram showed a solid bar across a public branch road leading toward the old watercourse.

Adrian studied it without declaring the road blocked.

“We need the legend and the actual arrangements.”

“And who received the orders,” Cecilia added.

Isabella asked Ellery to request the relevant full pages through the proper channels. Her private attendants were not to question soldiers about where they would stand that night.

Near dusk, Adrian accompanied Cecilia out through the side gate. She carried a red paper lantern Mrs. Pike had sent with an apprentice. It had no wick yet.

“You still like red.”

“Yes.”

She lifted it into the light beyond the palace gate. The thin paper needed no flame to have color.

“When the festival comes, I still want people to light their lamps happily,” she said. “Finding the truth shouldn't leave us with nothing but lessons in fear.”

Adrian sheltered her from the wind without taking the lantern.

Across the street, a coal cart stopped. Its driver studied a freshly posted notice for a long time, then asked someone whether the bridge would actually be open on the seventeenth.

Cecilia heard him too.

This time, the date on the paper had come out to meet them.
