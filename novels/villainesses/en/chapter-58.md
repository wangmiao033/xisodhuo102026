# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Two: The Saint Won't Let Me Get Married
### Chapter 58: The Saint Won't Die for Turning Out a Lamp

On the morning of November 6, Martha set the chestnut cake in the middle of the breakfast table and placed sliced apples beside it.

“The cake is yesterday's promise. The apples are today's breakfast. Please don't confuse their duties.”

Adrian's hand stopped halfway to the cake.

“I was checking whether someone had stolen a piece.”

“Your fork isn't suitable investigative equipment.”

Across from him, Elena nearly spilled her milk laughing. She had scraped her right palm shifting timber the previous day; it was cleaned and bandaged now. Adrian moved the apples within easy reach of her left hand.

She glanced at him, then silently confiscated the offending fork.

Cecilia wore a plain pale dress without her ceremonial crown. After their morning checks, she and Adrian had been cleared to attend a short review, with rest afterward and instructions to leave if unwell. The two hospitalized residents remained under observation. One could now talk with her sister. Grace had not mistaken that for a reason to end her care early.

“Nervous?” Adrian asked.

Cecilia nodded. “When I heard him demand death yesterday, I tried to look calm.”

“You don't have to look calm today.”

“May I have another piece of apple?”

He offered the plate. She glanced at Elena, who was already choosing a slice without any core attached.

“Take one,” Elena said. “We're not dividing up Adrian.”

“I know. He isn't this crisp.”

“Thank you. Difficult compliment to answer.”

After they laughed, Cecilia put Vera's papers in her bag. She hadn't reread the demand for her death until she knew every word.

Someone would read it today.

Someone would ask what justified it.

The urgent review took place in a small room at the independent review office. On the table lay a diagram, an equipment inventory, the referral, and the relevant statements. There was no public gallery, no bell waiting to announce an execution.

The duty adjudicator defined the question: did this heresy referral, including the demand to detain Cecilia, have a valid basis? The accident investigation was separate. Nobody would be convicted of everything in one morning.

Mercer attended with counsel. He maintained that Cecilia had interfered with a consecrated object without the officiant's permission.

Vera didn't begin with religion.

She laid Ward's permit on the table and asked whether Mercer recognized his own addendum.

“The side door was only to be closed briefly.”

“Did you receive an order rescinding that instruction?”

“Yes, but on the day—”

Ward produced the acknowledgment. After examining it, his counsel stopped disputing receipt and argued that an attendant had actually closed the door.

The attendant was brought in separately. He described Mercer's words and hand signal, admitting that after closing the door he had asked his colleague outside to bar it. The outer-passage duty record matched his account. Asked whether fear of blame had changed his story, he admitted that he too had disobeyed Ward. He did not withdraw his account of the order.

The adjudicator directed that both parts of the answer be recorded.

Next came the lamp.

The independent technician brought the cooled, numbered projection shutter. Using a work light, he showed how it cast a hand-shaped image. No oil burner was lit. Nobody had to endure another miracle.

The solemn hand appeared on plain paper. As the technician moved his fingers, it reached across Mercer's folder.

The room was quiet enough to hear paper slide.

“A symbol,” Mercer said.

“Yesterday you called it a divine answer to the dispute.” Sabine submitted the account of his words, checked with multiple witnesses. “In the referral, extinguishing it became destruction of an oracle.”

“Saintclair should have waited for the officiant.”

Ward looked up. “The exhaust hood was overheating. The operator was already burned. I had ordered the equipment stopped.”

The technician identified the valve, heat damage, and scorch pattern. Rafe's separately submitted statement, taken with medical permission, matched his written warning. The external cutoff was not an untouchable ritual core. It was an emergency control.

Mercer's counsel asked that intentional fraud not be treated as an already decided finding in the accident case. The adjudicator agreed: Mercer's criminal intent would not be determined that morning.

That did not make Cecilia's emergency intervention desecration.

For C-19, Sabine presented only the supplement directly connected with the reassurance speech.

Records clerk Nell Pike testified that she had taken the later completion summary to Mercer. He had told her to retain the completed-blessing wording and remove the note that clinic verification was still pending. She had believed medical confirmation had already reached her superior. On checking afterward, she found none attached.

Mercer denied dictating the wording.

Nell didn't raise her voice. She identified the amendment field he had filled in and described where she had received the signed approval. Independently retrieved dispatch records matched its page number, attachment count, and dispatch reference. The receiving office's retained copy contained the same amendment field. It wasn't something generated after yesterday's fire.

Mercer studied it for a long time.

“I approved administrative formatting.”

This time, he did not claim he had merely forwarded it.

Sabine placed the approval beside the records from both ends of the dispatch. Together, they established that he had signed off on the later supplement without the required medical confirmation. Who first switched the patient identifiers and who confined Mira remained separate investigative questions. His signature could not simply be stretched to cover every act.

Cecilia's earlier disciplinary case had not been reopened. The dismissal of those allegations remained in force.

The independent civil prosecutor then answered the referral.

“An old precedent does not give an oversight officer authority to put someone to death. Nor do these materials provide a factual basis for criminal prosecution. I reject the capital request and will seek neither arrest nor prosecution on this allegation.”

The Temple independent review committee's representative withdrew the heresy referral and directed every receiving office to cease acting on the restrictions requested with it. Mercer's equipment-review duties were suspended. The relevant evidence would go to the accident and archive investigators.

Nobody pronounced the entire Temple guilty.

But a particular man, with a particular signature and a particular machine, could no longer hide all three inside the word tradition.

Cecilia stayed seated.

“You can go,” Vera told her softly. “The formal confirmation will be sent directly to you.”

Only then did she seem to understand.

“Can I visit the clinic today?”

“Ask your doctor first.”

Adrian smiled in the back row. That answer was more familiar than any victory speech.

Outside, an older man from the hall waited by the door. He didn't block Cecilia's path. He removed his hat and asked for a minute.

“I thought it really was divine,” he said. “I was angry when you put the light out.”

She didn't answer immediately.

“I can't tell you what every light means,” she said at last. “But I could see that the operator was hurt. I didn't want to pretend otherwise.”

He nodded slowly. He didn't surrender the wooden token at his chest. He tucked it inside his clothes and went to join his family.

Watching him leave, Cecilia's shoulders eased.

“I wanted to say something perfectly right.”

“Did you?” Adrian asked.

“I don't know. I only said something true.”

Elena pointed out a strand of hair the wind had blown across her cheek.

“That's difficult enough.”

In the carriage, Cecilia turned to Adrian.

“May we find another time for that lunch?”

Elena paused halfway through straightening her gloves.

“Yes,” he said. “But today we should rest, and I want to finish yesterday's cake with Elena. We'll look at our schedules after that.”

Cecilia didn't look rebuffed. She nodded thoughtfully.

“I want to invite you because I want to. It isn't payment for rescuing me, or an extension of the old records.”

“I know.”

“And I won't make you sing.”

Adrian let out a long breath.

Elena looked up. “Why didn't you tell me that was on the program?”

“Because I want you to keep liking me.”

She watched him for a moment before she laughed.

“Best keep it secret, then.”

Back at the estate, Martha brought fresh tea. Cecilia went upstairs to rest, leaving the other two with the chestnut cake, finally cut.

No observer. No running order.

Elena tasted hers and frowned.

“Still too sweet?” Adrian asked.

“No. I was thinking. You refused a great many things today. You didn't refuse that lunch.”

He set down his fork rather than pretending he had missed her meaning.

“I didn't. I want to get to know her properly. And I don't want a meal to promise something I haven't worked out.”

“I don't like every part of that answer.”

“I know.”

She watched him. Outside, someone swept fallen leaves. The soft scraping left their silence oddly peaceful.

“I'd like it less if you gave me an answer you couldn't keep just to make me happy.”

Adrian offered his hand across the table.

“This hour, today, I want to spend with you.”

She took it.

“Then concentrate on the cake. You've wasted five minutes.”

Trying to cut it left-handed, she pushed the cream sideways. Adrian watched for a moment and asked whether she'd like help.

“One bite.”

He cut a neat piece and left the fork beside her plate instead of lifting it to her mouth.

Elena stared at it, then smiled.

“What?”

“If you'd been this attentive yesterday, I might have started asking for more.”

“Such as?”

“For you to sit with me a little longer.”

“You don't need to get hurt to ask that.”

She grew quiet, but the warmth in her eyes deepened.

Adrian shifted his chair closer. She leaned toward him until their shoulders met through their clothes. Neither had to explain that small distance.

“Do you feel that every time you see me lately, we have to discuss something terribly serious?” she asked.

“Yes. I'm trying to think of something that isn't.”

“Any luck?”

“Your cake is sweeter than your questions.”

She lifted her fork.

“Mr. Reed. Manners.”

“I mean I'm happy to take my time with both.”

She lowered it. Her ears had finally gone pink.

“You'd better mean that.”

He tasted the cake and nodded.

“This is good.”

“Just the cake?”

“I'm having a good day.”

“Better.”

That afternoon, the review office circulated its public finding: the hall's luminous sign had been produced by prepared imagery and projection equipment; Cecilia's emergency shutdown did not constitute the alleged heresy. The associated restrictions were withdrawn. Responsibility for the accident remained under separate investigation. Copies went to the hall and the places where the original notice had been posted. Withdrawal would not remain a fact known only inside one small room.

Sabine also arranged the next archive meeting. Independent custodians would bring the material available for examination. Cecilia would receive an invitation and choose her own participation. The later destination of the donations, and why the risk index kept citing an expired betrothal, still required investigation. A fake lamp could not answer those questions for them.

When Cecilia woke, she read her formal confirmation, then placed her volunteer notebook on the table for tomorrow.

The ceremonial crown remained in its box. She didn't hurry to put it on. She didn't throw it away.

Elena called from the hall to say tea was ready.

As Cecilia stood, she noticed a small slice of chestnut cake beside her papers. A note stood in it.

“The observer may eat. The principal subject of observation has already finished.”

She recognized Elena's handwriting and smiled for a long time.

Tomorrow she would return to the difficult questions.

Today she could eat her cake first.
