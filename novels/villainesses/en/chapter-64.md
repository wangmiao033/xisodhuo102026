# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Three: The Knight Says She's Responsible for Me
### Chapter 64: Why Must These Twelve Sacks Go Missing Twice?

On the morning of November 12, Adrian Reed stared at two sheets of paper and ate an entire slice of bread without jam.

Elena watched for a while, then placed the jar beside the papers.

“It's here.”

“What is?”

“You've put an empty knife in your mouth three times.”

Adrian looked down and decided to put the knife away. Last night's consignment number kept pacing through his mind like a guest holding two room keys and insisting he'd booked only one room.

WF-18.

Twelve sacks. Fifty pounds each.

One sheet was a commercial transport summary; the other, an extract of the quantities under investigation that civilian witnesses were permitted to see. The latter listed six hundred pounds as received at West Slope Depot. Turn a page, and another six hundred appeared as intercepted by Viola and possibly diverted out of the area.

He circled the entries without writing a conclusion.

Two people could share a name. Two loads could have the same number through a copying error. If that alone let him announce he'd solved the case, he wasn't much better than someone treating a rose stamp as a signed conspiracy.

“The weighhouse first?” Elena asked.

“The jam first.”

She returned his knife, looking thoroughly satisfied.

Viola was waiting below the inn steps. This time, instead of a full town map, she offered a small folded sheet.

Adrian opened it. Two kinds of arrows connected the inn and the weighhouse.

“Solid line for walking, broken line for the carriage. You were coughing yesterday.”

“The broken line is very short.”

“The carriage is right outside.”

He found Owen waiting at the curb with it and finally ran out of arguments.

Elena climbed onto the step and offered him her uninjured hand.

“Accept. You'll arrive in a reasonably three-dimensional shape.”

“I have no interest in the flat version.”

Viola steadied the door as though he'd stated a useful point of agreement.

At the weighhouse, someone was weighing dried beans. Ruth finished that transaction before taking them into the back room. Tobin sat behind a desk with a note pinned to his sling by a colleague: NO COPYING.

“To remind other people?” Adrian asked.

“To remind me. The doctor thinks my handwriting will outlast my hand if I give it a few days.”

The military clerk had arrived with only the specified extracts; the full army ledger remained at the barracks. Adrian explained yesterday's question and asked to begin with an ordinary sales document.

A runner brought the grain merchant's retained copy. It recorded the delivery agreement for November 4: WF-18, twelve sacks of winter wheat, fifty pounds net per sack, designated as Willowford's original reserve allocation.

The merchant couldn't attend; he was collecting payments farther north. Tobin didn't attempt to authenticate his signature on his behalf. He retrieved the weighhouse receipt for the same date from the municipal register and matched its reference to the transport copy.

As Ruth read out the net weight, Adrian drew the papers together.

“Same date, same wagon, same set of sack tags.”

The military clerk nodded.

“It's the same consignment.”

At last, someone could responsibly say it.

Adrian spread out the disputed summary. The first six hundred pounds appeared under “Total Received at Depot,” citing an annex to a transport plan. The second came from the suspension of that same planned transfer and had become “Separately Intercepted.” The final calculation deducted both amounts from Willowford's original stock.

Adrian picked up the empty cup on the table and put it down again.

“I deliver this cup to the depot once, then lose it from Viola's possession once. The table is down two cups. But I've only ever had one.”

“There could be an unrecorded second shipment,” the clerk said.

“Then we need its paperwork. It can't be born with these twelve sack tags.”

Ruth took the cup and filled it with hot water.

“Please don't send my crockery to the army.”

Viola hadn't spoken. She studied “Separately Intercepted,” fingers against the edge of the table, knuckles pale.

Rusk entered in time to hear the explanation.

He didn't accept the cup analogy. He pulled up a chair and had the clerk read the extract column by column. At “Total Received,” he held out a hand.

“Where's the acceptance copy?”

The clerk turned the page. Only the transport-plan reference was attached. There was no signature from a depot receiver.

For a moment, the only sound was dry beans pouring outside.

Rusk turned the sheet back and read it again.

“A transport plan isn't a receipt.”

Adrian didn't seize the opportunity to smile. He remembered the lieutenant watching every sack of sound grain depart yesterday. Someone who genuinely cared whether supplies reached the depot ought to hate this page more than anyone.

“These six hundred pounds may never have reached West Slope,” Adrian said quietly. “But the summary has counted the same consignment twice.”

“I'll check the receipt register.”

“Please preserve this extract as well,” Elena said. “The wording before correction matters to the earlier allegation.”

“I didn't say I was burning it.”

“Nor did I say you were.”

They regarded each other. Rusk flattened the sheet and told the clerk to record the duplicated source.

Tobin started to steady the page with his right hand. The note on his sling bumped the desk. He looked down and obediently switched to his left.

Apparently, everyone had to be restrained by their own paperwork at least once today.

The clerk stopped writing. Quietly, he explained that he hadn't compiled the summary. It had come through the previous handover. He had copied it cleanly after seeing the reviewer's signature.

“I used this to question Captain Blackwood yesterday.”

Viola looked at him.

“You did. I couldn't answer properly then. I hadn't seen the merchant's copy, either.”

His face grew redder, but he finally sat a little straighter and began listing the originals they needed.

Rusk removed his cap and rubbed a hand across his short hair.

“The pass posts were requesting grain. Willowford was reporting a shortage. I was given two deficits. I had to prepare for two.”

“The actual shortage still needs checking,” Adrian said. “At least now there's one less wagon chasing the second load that only exists on paper.”

Rusk didn't reply. He slid a query he'd been about to issue beneath his cap and rewrote it. Ink spread beside the final figure.

Outside, a porter pushed past with a cart stacked with bean sacks. Its wheel struck a gap in the paving with a heavy thump. Adrian caught himself drawing a sharp breath, fingers tightening. Yesterday's suddenly sinking crate corner hadn't entirely left his body.

Viola noticed. All she did was shift the chair a little farther from the door.

“That cart has just been repaired.”

He nodded. When he picked up his pen, his hand was steadier.

Ruth set the weight copy from the damaged wagon at the table's edge, reminding them to keep the two matters separate. The extra two hundred-plus pounds measured on November 11, the iron ballast, and the split water skin remained under their own examination. Today's six hundred pounds concerned a November 4 grain allocation counted twice in a summary.

“If anybody intends to boil iron for porridge,” she said, “warn his dentist for me.”

The clerk blushed. He had just stacked the two quantity sheets together.

Ada arrived before noon to confirm how the civilian originals would be preserved. Elena requested the relevant stations' return-crate registers. She also asked the supply office to obtain the issuing office's retained original of the November 4 supplementary order. The extract cited it, but nobody here could see the complete text as issued.

“I can send the application,” Ada said. “Major Voss has to authorize a military courier.”

Garrick Voss, commander of the Greymarch garrison. Adrian had seen his name on transit papers, signed with strokes so heavy that the pen seemed unwilling to give the paper a second chance.

Viola gathered her copies.

“I'll raise it formally at tomorrow's supply meeting.”

“You could let someone else speak first,” Adrian said.

She looked at him without mistaking his meaning.

“Someone else can point out the accounting error. Suspending the Willowford movement was my decision.”

She smoothed the edge of a page, as though unwilling to let one simple sentence make its cost sound small.

They ate lunch in the soup shop next to the weighhouse. The table was narrow; all three had to find space for their knees. Elena was still arranging her skirts when Viola surrendered the seat by the wall.

“Keep your injured hand away from passing elbows.”

Elena paused, then thanked her.

Adrian was about to compliment the consideration when the captain moved the soup bowls a hand's breadth from the table edge.

She wanted even the soup to reach its destination safely.

The proprietor brought warm bread. Viola took the hardest piece, casually leaving the two softer ones for her guests. Elena noticed. Without comment, she broke her own piece and placed half on Viola's plate.

“I can't finish all of this.”

Adrian knew that tone. She hadn't tasted her soup yet.

Viola glanced at Elena's bowl, but this time she didn't request verification of her appetite.

“Thank you.”

Halfway through the meal, a carpenter who'd moved from Willowford came in to collect the lunch he'd ordered. His wife followed. Each carried a bundle of bedding. Their voices dropped when they discussed his father, still in the village watching the livestock.

“He says as long as those twelve sacks are there, he can manage until we make room,” the woman said. “Go back tomorrow and bring him.”

Adrian's spoon stopped.

The quantity on the page had acquired a face he didn't yet know. He couldn't be sure the old man's twelve sacks were the allocation they'd examined, and he didn't turn overheard lunch conversation into testimony.

He simply found himself wanting to know which road they'd take tomorrow.

Viola stopped the carpenter to confirm where his family was and arranged for a municipal clerk to register those needing transport. She didn't promise to ride out immediately, or turn Owen into a messenger while he was eating beside his soup bowl.

By the time the carpenter left, her soup had cooled.

“The lower river road is passable,” she told Adrian. “Slow wagons. Daylight departure.”

“You're raising that tomorrow, too?”

“Yes.”

On the way back to the inn, Adrian stopped at a shop to buy a small, slender-handled brush. Elena couldn't work out its connection to military grain.

“Are you planning to brush away six hundred pounds?”

“Repairing yesterday's damage to my commercial reputation.”

He asked the shopkeeper for a dry cloth to place beneath her shawl, then gently brushed the mud from its hem himself. Her injured hand made wringing out a damp cloth difficult. She hadn't mentioned it. As the pale pattern reappeared, she studied it and slowly smiled.

“When did you notice?”

“You tried to clean it twice today. Both times you folded it differently instead.”

“Such close observation. Rusk may suspect you again.”

“I can explain my subject of study.”

She raised her eyes and deliberately stepped a little closer.

“Then explain.”

People were passing in the street. Suddenly, Adrian found this question much harder than the accounts.

“Someone I like.”

She didn't soften the words for him. She straightened her shawl and took his hand with her good one.

“Your research is progressing.”

Viola waited beside the carriage. As they approached, she naturally stepped aside. When Adrian climbed aboard, she steadied the poorly tied paper bag; the brush handle had nearly slipped out.

“A second would be useful.”

“Why?”

“Armor joints are difficult to clean, too.”

Elena looked at her, then at Adrian, amusement in her eyes.

“This research seems to offer opportunities for collaboration.”

This time, Viola recognized a second meaning. She failed to find a reply in time. She tucked the bag securely in place and turned to inspect a wheel, presenting them with an unusually serious view of her back.

Adrian looked from the brush to the fine dust caught along her shoulder plate. Before climbing in, he asked the shopkeeper to wrap another and put it in his coat pocket.

At dusk, the military clerk delivered a stamped acknowledgment of their correction request. The duplicated accounting would be addressed at the following morning's meeting. The existing allegation had not been withdrawn, and they still lacked the issuing office's retained November 4 order.

One page of agenda accompanied the receipt.

First item: conclude the local supply dispute and resume preparations for transport from Willowford.

Viola stood in the doorway. After reading that line, she said nothing for a moment.

Adrian placed the second brush he'd bought that afternoon beside her hand. She looked at it and finally lifted her gaze from the paper.

“Tomorrow may be unpleasant to listen to.”

“We came to hear the facts.”

She accepted the brush, thumb moving over its smooth wooden handle.

“Then please sit where you can hear them.”
