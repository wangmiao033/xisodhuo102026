# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume One: A Betrothal Written in Blood
### Chapter 19: A Research Trip for Two

On the morning of September twenty-ninth, Adrian noticed two apple turnovers listed on the Rosenberg household's purchasing order.

Above them were ink, paper, sealing wax, and binding string.

All reasonable supplies for investigating the old courthouse.

The turnovers were less obvious.

"My lady. Are the pastries evidence?"

"Lunch," Elena said, fastening her cloak.

"So we're going shopping?"

"And visiting the old courthouse."

"If we're going out together, shopping, and having lunch, doesn't that technically make this a date?"

Elena's hands paused.

"What else is involved in your technical definition?"

"Two adults going out together. Walking around town. Eating."

"Under the Rosenberg household's definition," she said, handing him an empty shopping bag, "you'll also carry the purchases."

"...Understood. Official business."

She looked as though she might say something else.

Then she smiled and thought better of it.

"Hale has obtained a permit for the courthouse's public accounts. Don't lose it. And don't go climbing through windows because you've decided the truth must be behind a locked door."

Adrian touched the folded authorization in his pocket.

Yesterday, he'd made a choice: investigate R-074 of his own free will, without allowing the Temple to take him away.

Now they had a legal way to begin.

The Old Capital Court no longer heard cases. Its eastern wing housed sealed judicial archives, while the western side had become a municipal accounts repository. Ordinary records—maintenance, purchases, and public expenditure—were available to people with the proper authorization.

*If you can't see a sealed case file,* Hale had said, *start by finding out who's paying to maintain the building.*

It sounded more useful than most walkthroughs Adrian remembered.

They traveled in an unmarked carriage and got out on South Street.

Elena wore a cream blouse and dark-blue skirt. Her golden hair was pinned with a simple silver clip. There were no ruby earrings, no elaborate fan, and nothing about her that demanded every passerby recognize a duke's daughter.

She looked like an academy graduate out shopping for herself.

Adrian glanced at her longer than he meant to.

"What?" she asked.

"Nothing. You look less like someone about to sentence a man to prison today."

"I was about to sentence you to pay for the stationery."

She handed him the order.

South Street was busy. A tailor had draped lengths of cloth outside his shop. A clockmaker peered through a magnifying glass. A flower seller stood on a stool to hang bouquets from a beam.

At the stationer's shop, the old proprietor recognized Adrian immediately.

"Mr. Reed. Not another court summons, I hope?"

"Just buying stationery today."

"Really?"

Elena placed the order on the counter.

"Please list quantities, prices, and delivery dates separately."

The old man visibly relaxed.

"Now *that's* the kind of request I like."

Adrian carried two bags out of the shop.

"One task completed."

"Three more to go."

"Why didn't you mention the workload?"

"I thought you might run away."

As they passed the bakery, a boy in an apron pointed.

"It's the gentleman from the ball!"

Another boy peered outside.

"And Lady Rosenberg! The pair from the newspapers!"

Adrian was already considering the fastest way to disappear.

Elena asked, quite calmly, "Do you have apple turnovers today?"

"Yes, my lady! Just out of the oven!"

"Save two for lunchtime."

The boy beamed.

Once they'd walked away, Adrian asked, "Why didn't you correct them?"

"Correct what?"

"They recognized us."

"We are, in fact, ourselves."

"They'll tell people we went on a date."

"We did go out together."

"That's not the same thing."

Elena stopped and faced him.

"If some newspaper calls two people walking down a street a date, are we obliged to walk on opposite sides of it?"

"No."

"Then come along. Don't let strangers decide how we move through the world."

She slowed a little so he could walk beside her.

Adrian realized that, for the first time, he wasn't keeping the respectful half-pace behind her expected of a servant.

It felt strangely pleasant.

Rain had worn the old courthouse's stone walls almost white. No ceremonial guards stood at its doors, only a cleaner sweeping leaves from the steps.

The receptionist checked their permit and introduced them to Mr. Miles, the accounts keeper.

He was an elderly man in a brown waistcoat, with his spectacles tied to his ears by a thin cord. An abacus lay beside his repair records.

"R-074 isn't my department," he announced. "The old case files are sealed."

"We're not requesting the trial records," Adrian said. "We'd like to know what maintenance and public expenditure continued after the court closed."

Miles checked their authorization.

"Public expenditure is fine. Anything involving private salaries has to be concealed."

"Of course," Elena said.

He led them into a north-facing reading room.

The shelves held volumes marked *Repairs*, *Stationery*, *Ceremonial Expenses*, and *Transfers*.

Adrian began with the books from seven years ago.

Roof tiles: three hundred.

Replacement iron locks: two.

Clock tower maintenance: once per quarter.

And one item that appeared every month, regardless of whether the building was open.

*Fate Maintenance Fee.*

Adrian stared at the words.

It looked like the title of a quest he'd be wise not to accept.

"My lady. Look at this."

Elena sat beside him.

"A fixed monthly amount. No change from year to year."

"Why would a courthouse that no longer holds trials need fate maintenance?"

"Possibly an old budget heading. They may have reused a category meant for clocks or protective equipment."

"Clock maintenance is listed separately."

They turned the pages.

Even during three months when the entire western wing had been closed for renovation, the fee had continued unchanged.

The recipient was listed as the **Old Records Preservation Office**.

Each transaction carried the same clearing reference.

**B-17.**

"Someone's paying a monthly fee to maintain fate at a courthouse that doesn't try cases," Adrian murmured.

Elena didn't immediately agree.

"Let's ask Miles."

The old man adjusted his spectacles and brought out an annual index.

"I've wondered about that entry myself. It used to be listed under religious ceremonial expenses. An auditor had it separated into its own category later."

"Who?"

"I couldn't tell you offhand. There should be a change notice somewhere. I only pay against approved vouchers."

Elena wrote down B-17 and the earliest recorded month.

"Could we have a certified extract?"

"Certainly. I'll cover the unrelated private accounts."

Adrian watched Miles carefully place strips over information they weren't authorized to see.

When he'd played *Crown of Fate*, uncovering a secret meant entering a dungeon, defeating guards, and opening a glowing chest.

Here, he needed a permit and an elderly bookkeeper with a strict sense of privacy.

There was an advantage to this slower approach, though.

If someone denied tomorrow that they'd seen this record, Miles's signature would still exist.

The bookkeeper showed them a note in the index.

"When the court stopped hearing cases, this line was renamed *Fate Maintenance Fee*. The amount didn't change. That doesn't automatically mean anything improper happened. Governments sometimes keep paying old obligations under outdated building accounts."

"So the name isn't proof of what was being maintained," Adrian said.

"Exactly."

Elena indicated the three months of renovation.

"Are there receipts for payments made while the wing was closed?"

"Of course."

Miles fetched a smaller book with thin slips attached neatly along the edges.

Each had been confirmed by the municipal payment office. All used B-17.

"This is a clearing reference, not a personal bank account," he warned. "Ask an ordinary banker about it and you'll get a blank look."

Adrian wrote that down.

"Can we find the original approval?"

"Possibly in the Treasury's public index. If it comes from a restricted fund, you'll need different authorization."

Elena thanked him.

She didn't announce they'd found the mastermind behind some great conspiracy just because a budget line sounded ominous.

Nor did she invoke her father's name to demand access to restricted accounts.

Adrian watched her compare the earliest and latest receipts. The stamp designs had changed over time; the reference number hadn't.

He almost wrote down the wrong word for *disbursement*.

Elena glanced at the page.

"You've misspelled it."

"My brain is busy thinking about fate."

"Then ask it to spare a little attention for spelling."

Even Miles laughed.

They finished arranging the certified extracts.

Adrian glanced at Elena again.

"Why do you keep looking at me?" she asked.

"I was wondering why you agreed to come."

"You invited me yesterday."

"You could have refused."

Her voice softened.

"You're trying to understand why a death record from seven years ago carries your name. I don't want you staring at that piece of paper on your own."

Adrian found no answer.

She returned to the receipts as if she'd merely reminded him to bring his pen tomorrow.

By lunchtime, the bakery's apple turnovers were ready.

Elena chose a table by the window. Adrian pulled out her chair.

The baker's wife looked delighted.

"Your first date? Shall I bring you some jam on the house?"

Adrian was preparing an explanation involving public-access permits, municipal accounts, and official purchasing.

Elena answered first.

"It's our first time having lunch together here."

"How lovely!" The woman returned with a little dish of jam.

Adrian leaned closer.

"My lady, she's misunderstood."

"I didn't tell her anything untrue."

"But she thinks—"

"She's given us free jam." Elena tasted it. "Would you prefer to hand it back?"

He considered that.

Then decided he didn't want to be an idiot.

The turnover was far hotter than he'd expected. His first bite sent scalding apple filling across his tongue.

Elena immediately passed him cold water.

"Slow down. Your evidence isn't going anywhere."

"I didn't want to delay the investigation."

"Lunch doesn't have a deadline."

She cut her own pastry into pieces and waited for the steam to settle.

Sunlight lay across the back of her hand.

Adrian realized he was beginning to enjoy these moments when there were no guards, no courtroom, and no need to pretend he wasn't frightened.

"My lady?"

"Yes?"

"If there weren't any investigation next time—if we just came out for a meal—would you still want to?"

Elena's spoon paused in midair.

"If you promise not to treat the turnover like evidence that needs rescuing."

"That was the pastry's fault."

"And don't call the next date official purchasing."

Adrian looked up.

"So you admit this is a da—"

"I haven't admitted anything."

She turned toward the window, the tips of her ears pink.

Adrian laughed and let the question rest.

On the return journey, they sat on the same side of the carriage so they could spread out the account extracts.

Adrian circled the first B-17 payment.

It had been made in the very month the old court stopped hearing cases.

"The trial was closed, but the maintenance wasn't."

"It's a financial irregularity," Elena reminded him. "We still don't know whether it has anything to do with R-074."

"Tomorrow we'll trace the source of the payments."

"All right."

The capital's clock tower sounded in the distance.

Adrian returned the extract to its sleeve.

Five plain words had appeared every month for seven years.

*Fate Maintenance Fee.*

More disturbing, somehow, than any prophecy he'd heard from the Temple.

If fate was something that needed regular maintenance, who was paying to keep it running?

Elena rested her head against the carriage window and closed her eyes.

Adrian didn't mention the date again.

He merely moved the bag of leftover pastries somewhere she could reach without asking.
