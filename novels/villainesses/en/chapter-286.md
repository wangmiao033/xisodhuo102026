# Chapter 286: The Princess Has No Vote

On May 16, Isabella received a temporary admission pass at the nobles' assembly.

She turned it over. Invited witness. An embarrassed protocol officer explained that this wasn't a royal audience. The legal committee had invited her to address the proposal, not granted her a vote. She studied him, then pinned the pass to her dress.

“I know. Which row?”

Beside her, Adrian heard the officer finally give a straightforward answer. He'd apparently prepared a speech against royal displeasure. Her declining to become angry halved his work.

Inside, her desk had no space for the pillow supporting her left wrist. She requested a narrow stool and waited for permission before sitting. Today's first successful reform was making her aching arm comfortable.

Members entitled to introduce legislation had jointly submitted the proposal. Isabella supplied evidence and public explanation. She wasn't borrowing lost powers back beneath a new sheet of paper. Three days of discussion had been announced. Objections and amendments remained admissible, regardless of her royal colors.

“What do you want us to agree to now?” the chair asked.

“Formal consideration of repealing authority for designated sacrifice. No family, institution, or office may supply an adult's personal consent.”

She explained the proposed change and its present limits. Existing preservation and suspension orders retained their actual scope. Committee approval today wouldn't make a law effective throughout the kingdom. Coercive provisions in marriage and historical contracts also required separate comparison; this document couldn't claim to have examined everything.

The first opponent didn't mention family honor. He brought the protective main bridge's maintenance requirements. If old authority disappeared before an alternative was ready, who would answer for the next surge?

Isabella requested the engineer's explanation first. Maintaining the main bridge, repairing shielding, and organizing evacuation didn't require authorization to sacrifice five designated people. Choosing bodies and immediately shutting protection weren't the only possibilities. Meanwhile, experimental nodes couldn't replace the kingdom's load. Inventing completed infrastructure wouldn't help pass an honest bill.

“And the expense?”

“Saying voluntary in this room doesn't make somebody pay.”

She submitted confirmed ordinary maintenance allocations, limited loss guarantees, and unfunded research separately. The two families declining new commitments stayed visible. A member pointed to the two missing prospective contributions. Surely this gap proved how impractical her refusal had been.

“The gap is real,” she answered. “It must change what we can build and when we can build it. It cannot become a different kind of consent, obtained from nobody, merely because we would find that convenient.”

She left the figures visible. Neither existing ordinary relief commitments nor a limited guarantee could be silently stretched to cover every future expense.

An elderly member proposed an exception. Designation would ordinarily be prohibited, but if unavoidable catastrophe threatened the kingdom, the High Priest could certify necessity and restore the old arrangement.

One small line. Isabella read it and returned it to the center of the table.

“Who establishes that it's unavoidable?”

“Those who understand the facility.”

“If the same person chooses available methods, certifies alternatives inadequate, and designates the bearer, repeal disappears whenever it matters.”

He didn't pound the table. Expert judgment was more reliable than a princess's wishes, he said. She didn't demand a respectful title. She requested yesterday's independent technical reservations, including those unfavorable to the new proposal.

“Experts can submit evidence and face other qualified reviewers. A difficult question doesn't justify putting explanations of danger and orders about who must die in the same hand.”

The chair allowed discussion to continue. Her objection wasn't a veto. The proposed exception remained on the table. Sitting down, she gripped her skirt, discovering that disagreement troubled her less than lacking the right to declare it finished.

At lunch, Adrian accompanied her to the ordinary waiting room. Someone arrived with a silver tray and offered a separate meal for Her Highness. Looking at the engineers and regional observers already seated, she chose the same meal counter.

Lunch included carrots she disliked. Watching her pick them out, Adrian questioned her example.

“I didn't propose abolishing picky eating.”

“Fortunately. That might not pass.”

She started moving a carrot to his plate, stopped halfway, and asked whether he wanted it. Only after his yes did she put it down. They laughed together, the argument about power briefly reduced to one excessively bright vegetable.

“I wanted to order him to remove the exception.”

“You didn't.”

“I wanted to more than once.”

“Then you'll have to bring that feeling back tomorrow.”

She studied her plate. Did he find her terribly slow now? Some things were slow, he admitted. She didn't need to prove her usefulness by finding another route people couldn't refuse.

She didn't immediately smile with relief. She liked the self who could accomplish things quickly. She liked him looking at that self. Now she had to discover whether, without an instantly fulfilled command, she dared ask him to stay through an ordinary invitation.

“When these three days end, I'd like to discuss something that isn't a proposal.”

“All right. Choose a time when you can actually rest.”

“It isn't a negotiation.”

He'd heard. She didn't secretly transform his agreement into any other answer.

On May 17, the committee returned to the exception. Representatives authorized by affected families and actual maintenance personnel now answered questions. A sponsor had wanted all five women present for a stronger impression. Isabella declined to accept for them. Luna arranged limited written answers. Viola had a scheduled care assessment. Elena chose to speak on family provisions; Cecilia described practical obstacles emerging through the reception arrangements.

Agnes asked whether lawmakers would overlook coercion if local officials stopped calling it designation and instead tied housing and family exemptions to a signature. The clerk referred this to the drafters. A prohibition needed to address actual compulsion, not merely rename it.

Maintenance staff feared language suspending all associated expenditure would freeze ordinary repairs. Isabella supported narrowing it, preserving examination of actual purposes and scrutiny of disputed spending. The clerk previously frightened by the royal payment-freeze draft finally loosened his grip on the wage ledger while she spoke.

By afternoon, a narrower text had emerged. It targeted designation of bodily burdens by status. It didn't invalidate every ordinary public obligation or invent an undiscussed tax system. After detailed argument, the one-person necessity exception was omitted from the committee's proposed recommendation.

Its supporters weren't silenced. They requested recorded dissent and the opportunity to raise it again in formal debate. The chair agreed. Isabella resisted interpreting preserved opposition as the destruction of everything achieved.

Afterward, a young copyist asked her to sign the recommendation. Her name had been squeezed below the committee members'.

“I can't sign here.”

“It's only an indication of support.”

“Then give me a place to support it. Don't give me a vote I don't have.”

He retrieved the page, scarlet-faced. She requested a recorded correction and a clean copy rather than destroying the original. If a well-meant overreach became acceptable because she benefited, today's hardest words meant nothing.

On May 18, the committee voted whether to recommend formal consideration. Isabella remained in the witness seat, pillow beneath her wrist. Some supported the proposal; some opposed it; others requested additional financial-impact information. She didn't count silence as support.

The result was sufficient to advance it, with the opposing opinions still recorded beside the recommendation. The clerk read the text, dissenting opinions, and next procedural requirements. Full debate and the steps needed for enactment still lay ahead. Nobody could use today's recommendation in the street to declare every old provision void.

But repeal was no longer merely a group of affected people outside the door asking for mercy. It was on the formal agenda, requiring an answer.

A regional observer asked whether he could tell those waiting at home that they were safe now. The chair refused that wording. Existing protection depended on actual orders; legislative progress wasn't an inspection report for local facilities. Isabella requested that distinction in the short public explanation and checked the copy herself. Encouraging news mustn't become a reason to remove a distant warning barrier.

The people still defending the exception would be there at the next stage. So would the questions about resources. A recommendation had not made either disappear. For once, Isabella could recognize a step forward without requiring the room to pretend it was the final step.

At reception, she obtained the registration receipt. Before looking at the tally, she checked that the submitted text still said families couldn't substitute for personal consent. It did. Her shoulders slowly relaxed.

Outside, someone invited her beneath a royal banner to describe the princess's victory for the kingdom. She indicated the sponsoring members and ordinary witnesses leaving with her. They could decide whether to speak. She wouldn't stand in front and swallow every other name.

She described the stage completed and those remaining. The elderly member passed, remarking coldly that she'd discover how expensive ideals were. She knew, she replied, and let him pass. A polished retort couldn't pretend the account was paid.

Back at the house, Elena brought ordinary pastries. Seeing no crest on the box, Isabella wondered whether this prevented diplomatic controversy.

“The baker wouldn't print special wrapping for one small box.”

They laughed. Resting against her chair, Luna listened to the recommendation being read and asked whether the committee had approved funding for her new chair while it was there. Everyone looked at her. She explained that the compliments chair urgently needed an occupant.

Adrian praised her for finishing work on time. She considered that insufficiently academic, but adjusted her cushion and settled more comfortably.

“What happened to your red line?” Isabella asked.

“I'm keeping it. Perhaps I'll show it to new researchers first.”

Cheaper than hanging her portrait, Elena observed. Luna raised an eyebrow, prepared to insist that beauty shouldn't be priced by production cost, then laughed instead.

Unanswered problems remained in the room. Nobody needed to perform cheerfulness to escape them. The laughter nevertheless had a place of its own.

Near dinner, judicial staff delivered two separate attendance notices. The first concerned Evelyn's specific registration, concealment, and execution-related conduct. The second concerned the existing investigation into Leon's participation in false evidence. Sources, representation, and the scope of each proceeding were listed separately. Prospective institutional reform didn't erase past acts.

Isabella fell quiet at her brother's name. She couldn't shield him for the royal family's benefit. Nor could she prejudge him to demonstrate her reforming resolve.

Adrian read Evelyn's notice first. He remembered the maintenance passage she'd provided, the earlier pages she'd withheld, and the roads they'd found only through her help.

Tomorrow, someone who'd helped them would have to answer for what she'd done.

Isabella placed the recommendation in her own thin folder. She didn't lay it over the two notices. She'd gained no vote today. At last, she was willing to let outcomes leave her hands.
