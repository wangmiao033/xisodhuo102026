# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Two: The Saint Won't Let Me Get Married
### Chapter 36: The Temple Says Nobody May Talk About It

On October sixteenth, Cecilia Saintclair brought a letter to breakfast.

Adrian read the opening paragraph.

Then he put down his bread.

*"No unauthorized party may publish, discuss, or interpret the irregular dual-date registration associated with Case R-074."*

He looked up.

"Am I an unauthorized party?"

"That," Cecilia said, "is part of what we need to establish."

She wasn't wearing her white-and-gold ceremonial robes. A plain gray coat lay over her dress, though she still wore the badge of the independent review committee.

Adrian noticed a faint mark beneath it, as though she'd removed and fastened the badge several times during the night.

Elena read the letter through.

"Restricting public release of confidential documents may be reasonable. But the footnote seems to prohibit the people named in those documents from receiving an explanation of their own records."

Hale set down his teacup.

"The Temple can protect confidential religious archives from unauthorized disclosure. A person's right to inquire into records about their own identity is a different matter. This notice treats them as the same thing."

"So Cecilia and I can still talk about our alleged engagement?"

"You're allowed to discuss your own lives. You aren't permitted to publish protected originals in the newspapers without authorization."

"That's a relief. I was beginning to think I'd need to file an application before opening my mouth."

"You've mentioned applications five times since breakfast began," Elena said.

"I've developed an involuntary reaction whenever I hear the word."

Cecilia didn't correct his joke.

That worried him more than another three pages of regulations would have.

She was looking down at the badge on her coat.

"Yesterday, I sent the committee both date comparison reports. I included only observations we could verify. I never wrote that we'd found a document from the future. Internal Oversight issued this notice that same evening."

"Do you think they're targeting you?" Elena asked.

"I don't know. Confidentiality exists for good reasons. It protects patients' private records as well as historical contracts. What concerns me is that the office hasn't identified which material is protected—or why the people named in the file would be barred from understanding it."

Hale moved the notice to the middle of the table.

"We can request clarification and preservation of the originals while the dispute is reviewed. That's not publishing confidential material. It's ensuring nobody uses an argument over access as a chance to make documents disappear."

Cecilia nodded and began drafting the request.

At the third line, she stopped.

"There's another matter I need to handle."

She produced a worn little notebook. Its pages contained sketches of medicine bottles, hospital beds, and names written in a careful hand.

"I volunteer at Mercy Clinic in the lower district. One of my junior attendants brought me a revised patient prognosis register yesterday. The room numbers didn't match the medication records I helped prepare."

"A prognosis register?" Elena asked.

"The Temple sometimes offers prayers for patients who are seriously ill. A prognosis is supposed to tell physicians whom to monitor more closely. Recently, I've seen administrative copies turn 'requires close observation' into 'fated not to survive.'"

Adrian felt a familiar chill.

There'd been a small quest in *Crown of Fate* in which a Temple priest refused to help a sick man because his fate was supposedly settled.

He'd once dismissed it as poor writing.

He didn't find it funny now.

"Which patient?" he asked.

"Jonah Payne. Twenty-eight. He works loading cargo on the river docks. He was admitted with a high fever last week, and the clinic has been treating him."

She turned a page.

"I remember his bed in the east ward. The revised register lists him in the west observation room."

"Beds get reassigned," Hale pointed out.

"Exactly. I want the clinic to confirm whether this was an ordinary transfer or a registration error. Not publish the prognosis. Not interrupt his treatment."

Elena considered her for a moment.

"You're worried this secrecy order could be used to obstruct the hospital's own corrections."

"Yes." Cecilia looked up. "If I stop going to the clinic because someone in Internal Oversight is unhappy, the people who lose out first will be the patients."

"I can contact the charitable supply office that works with our household," Elena said. "They can confirm whether medicine has been delivered, without asking to see anyone's private medical history."

Cecilia blinked.

"You'd help?"

"Not because of the betrothal. You told me you'd promised to assist with medicine distribution. I remembered."

Adrian watched the two women exchange a quiet look.

For a moment, he felt remarkably unnecessary.

So he tried offering a practical suggestion.

"I can deliver the request."

"No," Elena and Cecilia said together.

He froze.

"Why?"

"You promised to help me collect the books we reserved yesterday," Elena said.

"And you're unfamiliar with the guesthouse's delivery rules," Cecilia added. "Someone could mistake you for a related party answering the summons."

"Those are completely different reasons."

They looked at one another.

Then laughed.

Cecilia had already summoned her junior attendant, Mira Finch.

Mira was twenty-two, with short hair, freckles, and a gray uniform whose cuffs had been carefully mended. She'd grown up in the lower district and had volunteered at Mercy Clinic long before joining Cecilia's service.

"I know Mr. Payne," Mira told them. "He once hurt his ankle unloading a barge. He was still joking with the nurses, asking if they could replace his medicine with apple pie."

"That sounds like me," Adrian said.

Mira smiled.

"Except he usually remembers to take his medicine."

Elena slowly lifted her head.

"So all of you have decided my advice about eating and medicine is a joke now?"

"Not at all," Adrian and Mira said together.

Cecilia invited Mira to sit.

She explained exactly what she was asking her to do.

Mira was to deliver a stamped medicine acknowledgment request, confirm the hospital's public receipt records, and ask the clinic supervisor what procedure applied if a bed number needed correction.

She wasn't to obtain restricted medical notes or demand that patients show her injuries or magical marks.

"If anyone changes your route, come back and tell me first," Cecilia said.

Mira folded the written instructions into her inner pocket.

"And if someone tells me the oracle is more important than the clinic's medicine?"

Cecilia was quiet for a moment.

"Tell them that a sick person doesn't stop needing care because somebody claims fate has spoken."

Mira nodded solemnly.

Adrian watched her leave.

She was a person with friends, a life outside the Temple, and a very practical sense of humor.

Not the kind of minor character who could vanish from the story without anyone noticing.

Hale had Mira's intended route and return time entered in the guesthouse register.

"That way, if another official tries to redirect her, there's a record of what she originally agreed to do."

Later, the three of them visited the capital's public records office to request clarification of the oversight order.

The Temple's Internal Oversight Department sent a clerk who repeatedly insisted that headquarters was still considering the matter.

Hale asked him whether Adrian and Cecilia retained the right to inquire into their own identity records.

The clerk spent a long time reading the relevant paragraph.

"This order prohibits publication of speculative findings," he admitted. "It doesn't itself authorize the refusal of a lawful personal-access request."

"Could you put that in an official response?" Adrian asked.

The man's face tightened.

"I'm not empowered to issue binding legal interpretations on behalf of Internal Oversight."

"You've explained your understanding," Adrian replied. "We won't present that as a decision from your department. Please simply record that we raised the question and haven't yet received an answer."

Hale submitted the written objection and obtained a filing receipt.

Cecilia signed her own name.

Her hand was steady this time.

Outside the office, a few ordinary citizens were struggling to understand where to submit applications for charitable medicine.

An elderly man held a card covered in tiny print, asking the entrance guard whether he could still collect a refill.

Cecilia asked if he wanted help, then wrote directions to the correct window in large letters.

Adrian watched her speak with a volunteer who offered to accompany him.

"Doesn't helping one person just make everyone else ask you for help too?" he asked when she returned.

"Possibly. But I'm standing here. I can't pretend not to see someone who needs directions merely because I'm not wearing my ceremonial robes."

Elena nodded.

"Our household charity office could help the medical dispensary put up clearer signs."

"Thank you," Cecilia said.

This mystery wasn't only about Adrian and two young women whose names had appeared in an ancient document.

It touched people who depended on those ordinary forms to receive medicine.

All the more reason, Adrian thought, not to let the word *confidential* become a convenient excuse to move their names around.

By early afternoon, Cecilia still hadn't received Mira's receipt from the clinic.

She arranged for the guesthouse to be told where she would be and when she expected to return.

"Isn't that a little cautious?" Adrian asked.

"I'd rather my colleagues know where to look for me than have them make up their own theories when they can't find me."

He remembered the solitary escape routes he'd once drawn around the old courthouse.

His ears grew warm.

Elena noticed.

"Thinking about a past mistake?"

"A regrettable professional decision."

"At least there's hope for you."

As they walked toward their carriage, Elena asked why Adrian hadn't made a single joke about the historical betrothal at the public records office.

He glanced at Cecilia, who was speaking with a charity coordinator ahead of them.

"She's worried about a real patient. If I only think about my romantic problems, I'm not being much of a friend."

"I thought perhaps you'd prepared another escape plan."

"My escape route has become the road to a bookshop."

Elena laughed.

Adrian took the opportunity.

"After we collect the books, would you like to have dinner?"

"Just dinner?"

"A proper date. No lawyer. No account books."

She took his arm.

"Then I accept."

Cecilia turned at the sound.

Her gaze lingered for a moment, then she smiled sincerely.

"Have a lovely evening. I'll be at the guesthouse reviewing the medical supply records."

Adrian suddenly wondered whether she was taking it rather too well.

"You're not upset?"

"Why would I be?" Cecilia considered. "I've only known you for a few days. I'd much rather find out whether Jonah received his medicine than compete for a date I haven't even asked for."

Adrian couldn't argue with that.

They parted at the carriage.

That evening, Adrian and Elena had barely returned from dinner when the head steward met them at the Rosenberg door.

"Miss Saintclair has sent word. Her attendant Mira left the Temple guesthouse this afternoon and hasn't returned."

Adrian stopped walking.

Elena released his hand.

"Who saw her last?"

"The guesthouse says she left with a medicine delivery request. Later someone claimed she'd been redirected to another Temple receiving office, but the messenger didn't give a name."

"Is there an exit record?" Adrian asked.

"Yes. The afternoon entry appears to have been amended."

Elena was already moving toward the study.

"First we contact Hale, the clinic, and the guesthouse. Let's not call someone kidnapped simply because she missed a return time."

Adrian followed.

The lamps threw their shadows along the hallway.

Their first proper dinner had ended before they'd finished dessert.

But Adrian was more worried about something else.

Mira had been sent to check a potentially incorrect patient record.

And she'd disappeared on the same day the Temple ordered everyone to stop talking about irregular records.
