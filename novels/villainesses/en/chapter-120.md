# Chapter 120: The Princess's Invitation Took Four Days

On December 28, Luna reached breakfast before Adrian but failed to fulfill her promise to wake him.

She was engaged in a losing struggle with a traveling glove that was far too thick.

It couldn't squeeze the dressing around her right palm. Halfway on, it resembled a gray bird that had landed on a branch and refused to move. Watching from the doorway, Adrian asked whether she'd try a larger size.

“I was considering a more elegant solution.”

“Cutting it open?”

“How did you know?”

“The scissors are beside your left hand.”

They found a loose old glove among the spare clothes and folded its cuff back. Luna shook it, checked that it didn't touch the injury, and announced that the bird had agreed to migrate.

Hensley arrived for her follow-up examination in time to hear that.

“Migration is permitted. Flight isn't.”

He checked the wound and sensation in her fingertips, then replaced the dressing. Ordinary carriage travel with company and daily rest was acceptable. Casting and exertion with her right hand remained prohibited. Viola's left arm hadn't miraculously healed because they were leaving either. She could ride in a carriage, not spend the day guarding them on horseback. Adrian had recovered better, but was warned against sitting in drafts to prove it.

They listened in turn, looking like recipients of a particularly undistinguished award.

Elena collected the instructions. “At least nobody claims to be perfectly healthy this time.”

“We're waiting for an opportunity to appeal,” Luna said.

The porter entered with the delivery register.

A messenger traveling along the palace postal route had arrived at the tower.

The breakfast jokes paused. The first thing Adrian thought of wasn't a golden crown. It was the note delivered to their East Ridge guesthouse, demanding that Tessa go somewhere private at night to check her testimony. Before rising, he asked where the visitor was.

“In the entrance hall. He has relay receipts. The administrator is checking them.”

The courier demanded no private audience. Leaving his wet cloak outside, he drank hot water on the indicated bench, mountain mud on his boots. A sealed heavy-paper envelope lay on the table, addressed to Adrian Reed at the research tower.

Its wax bore a smaller private royal device, not the king's great seal.

“An invitation from Princess Isabella. Not a summons, sir. You may refuse it or read it before replying.”

Elena's expression changed slightly at the name.

“You know her?” Adrian murmured.

“At court. She rarely describes what she wants as something others must give.”

“That sounds admirable.”

“Sometimes she first makes every other choice more expensive.”

The administrator checked the courier's identity, previous relay number, and envelope number against the tower's register. Elena recognized the princess's private mark but didn't substitute recognition for verification. The accompanying receipt matched the envelope and the local station's record. Only then did Adrian open it.

The paper smelled faintly of dried flowers. Dated December 24, it had been written while they investigated the East Warehouse apparatus. Naturally, it contained nothing about the amended directions of the twenty-sixth.

The princess mentioned preliminary review reports already received in the capital and disputes concerning old contractual equipment's funding and archives. She invited Adrian and trusted companions to a private meeting in the palace's west wing to discuss a possible joint inquiry. December 31 or the first two days of the new year were available. Neither night travel nor bringing tower originals was required.

The final line was written more heavily.

I wish to hear your judgment, rather than only the identities others have written for you.

Luna guessed something from his face.

“Does she make you want to go?”

“Yes.”

“Then count that among her abilities.”

Adrian looked at Isabella's name again. In his memories of Crown of Fate, court scenes always placed her high above others. Jewels, long stairways, and neatly bowed heads encouraged players to decide her character before she spoke.

He'd done that too. Since then, he'd encountered much that a character description could conceal: a travel-sick witch, a knight learning not to stand night watch, a saint whose palms sweated with nerves.

The letter told him what Isabella would discuss, not why. He wouldn't let an old impression answer for her before they met.

The courier's receipt asked only whether he would attend, his expected arrival, and the number accompanying him. It requested no unseen commitment. Adrian asked him to wait instead of letting a beautiful sentence hurry his pen.

They returned to the sitting room and closed the entrance-hall door. Their half-packed luggage suddenly looked as though it had foreseen today's destination.

“Private doesn't necessarily mean we must keep everything secret forever,” Cecilia said after reading.

“We should clarify that,” Adrian agreed.

Viola held the map with her right hand and identified three ordinary stopping places.

“If we leave this afternoon, decent roads should get us there by the thirty-first. Heavy snow means another day. An invitation isn't a military order.”

She turned an old nighttime shortcut notation facedown. Elena noticed without teasing.

“What if she only wants signatures on a conclusion already written?” Luna asked.

“We don't sign.”

“What if she dislikes that?”

“We still don't sign.”

Nobody mistook the simple answer for a spell against every court difficulty. The Rosenbergs had somewhere to stay in the capital. They wouldn't depend immediately on the princess's rooms and carriages. Each companion could leave independently. Original evidence wasn't traveling with them. Practical arrangements mattered more than imagining themselves fearless.

Elena studied the invitation until Adrian asked about her remaining concerns.

“Why is yours the only name on the envelope?”

Luna visibly wanted to speak.

Elena glanced at her. “I'm aware of the official reasons. I'm asking about another possibility.”

Adrian didn't pretend to misunderstand.

“I'll ask when we meet. A private invitation doesn't make this a romantic engagement.”

“What if she means one?”

“You know whom I'm courting.”

Elena blushed while carefully straightening the paper.

“Of course. I'd like the palace to know too.”

Cecilia drank her water. After struggling briefly, Luna murmured, “This journey won't be cold.”

Adrian wrote a short reply. He would meet, expecting to arrive on the thirty-first, with delays if weather or injuries required. His companions could attend. Discussion didn't constitute advance agreement to any contract; later personal decisions remained each individual's own.

The courier accepted it. He would travel ahead along the main road to deliver their reply. They could continue resting at the stations they had already chosen. He didn't accept conditions on the princess's behalf without authorization, nor claim that a bird could carry their questions across the entire mountain range in less than a quarter-hour.

Before they left the sitting room, Luna stopped Adrian.

“Research funding would tempt me. Every independent inspection means wages somebody must pay.”

“You're allowed to want it.”

“You like saying that.”

“Wanting something doesn't mean accepting every attached condition.”

She checked her funds again without putting away the academy allowance. She kept her original source of support available. Only by preserving a route she could already take could she avoid seeing a golden threshold and forgetting how to find her way home from outside it.

From the doorway, Elena suggested settling their luggage and eating before the palace visit. Luna agreed seriously. Discussing funding hungry was as dangerous as shopping hungry. Cecilia added that discussing a marriage contract hungry might be worse.

She sounded so grave that all three looked at her.

“I was thinking about the word private,” she explained.

“Then we'd better pack more bread,” Adrian said.

Before noon, departure became the noise of moving trunks, finding hats, and checking wheels.

They hired two ordinary carriages, leaving ample space for passengers and luggage. Caught trying to squeeze another dictionary into the drawing tube, Luna settled for her handwritten index. Cecilia had a different problem: too many identical cloth bundles.

“This one's books. Is that one books too?” Luna asked.

“Refreshments.”

“You're misleading your companions.”

“So you won't eat everything before we leave.”

Viola waited seated beside a carriage, holding a small checklist in her right hand. She gave the drivers no orders. Instead, she asked who had the medicine and who knew where they'd change carriages. Elena raised a little cloth bag.

“Needle and thread. We've already investigated one missing button. I'd rather not trace another.”

Luna pulled her coat closed and declined comment.

At the tower door, Tessa gave the administrator a repaired traveling lamp, then came to say goodbye. She and Luna didn't attempt a dramatic embrace. Their uninjured hands touched lightly.

“No reading in the carriage,” Tessa said.

“What about an important letter?”

“Stop to read it.”

“You sound increasingly like everyone here.”

“Possibly because everyone here has a point.”

Luna laughed and conceded. Once she had a new address, she'd send it by ordinary post. Nobody would have to find her through rumors.

The administrator confirmed that C-17 remained at the tower, with accident debris and original records under existing custody arrangements. Luna left her contact arrangements and looked up at the windows. The mint was somewhere it would be tended. Someone in the ordinary drawing room was smoothing out a sheet.

She left the old desk lamp behind.

Adrian held the carriage door, waiting until she was settled before climbing in. Elena sat beside him. Cecilia and Viola took the second carriage, the medical case within easy reach.

Wheels rolled slowly and clearly over the tower's stone approach.

Luna waved her left hand through the window, then snatched it back from the cold. Looking at the oversized glove, she smiled.

“I used to think leaving meant bringing things back.”

“And now?” Adrian asked.

“First I'll see what I can take out into the world.”

At the first bend, she handed Elena the map and leaned against the cushions to watch the distant mountains. Adrian hadn't needed to remind her. She remembered the last rough journey.

They changed horses in East Ridge and continued slowly along the main road. As the sun lowered, trees cast shadows like black ropes across the snow. That evening they stopped as planned at the first relay inn, still several days from the capital.

The inn smelled of stewed onions and damp wood rather than palace flowers. The drivers tended their horses. Viola confirmed they needn't leave before dawn, then poured herself hot soup. Luna placed her document bag at the table's corner. She hadn't read a single line during the drive, and rather proudly invited everyone to inspect it.

In the courtyard, Elena looked at the sky. She didn't ask again why the princess had written that sentence.

Adrian joined her and offered his hand. Skirting the puddles, they walked toward the lighted doorway, leaving parallel footprints in the thin frost.

The invitation rested in a document bag. It didn't glow or possess the power to change their direction.

Their carriages headed toward the capital because they had chosen together to go.
