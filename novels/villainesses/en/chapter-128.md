# Chapter 128: A Princess Doesn't Need a Procession

On the morning of January 4, Elena stopped Adrian in the entrance hall.

“Your collar.”

He looked down. “It isn't tucked under.”

“I know.” She came closer and freed the scarf caught beneath it. “I needed a slightly less obvious excuse to look at you.”

That was considerably harder to answer than a reprimand. His prepared response vanished, leaving him with a rather foolish smile.

Elena stepped back, satisfied, then looked less happily at the waiting carriage.

Today's walk had been arranged two days ago. The sapphire cuff links had gone back, but the princess had not canceled their ordinary meeting. Elena knew the arrangements and had plans of her own: a consultation with the lawyer about the rescheduled preliminary succession council, then an afternoon meeting with Adrian.

“You needn't repeat every word afterward,” she said. “But if she sends you home as a member of the royal family, I will expect an explanation.”

“I'll try to return under my original name.”

“And don't spend the walk rehearsing your testimony. You promised a walk, not a traveling interrogation room.”

He agreed, then asked what she wanted for dinner.

She hadn't decided. They could ask the kitchen together when he returned.

That made him remember the time better than any warning not to be late.

Six liveried attendants stood in two rows outside the garden beside the western greenhouse.

Adrian stopped.

One carried a hand warmer, another cushions, and the last a silver pot. Beyond them, two musicians sheltered beneath an awning, their instruments not yet tuned.

Isabella waited on the steps in a dark blue cloak, composed as though the entire procession had grown there naturally.

“What's wrong?”

“Are we going for a walk, Your Highness, or taking possession of another palace?”

She followed his gaze. “I've already reduced the arrangements by half.”

“Did the original plan include siege equipment?”

A young attendant bowed his head. His shoulders moved suspiciously.

Isabella closed her route card. “Your definition of ordinary is broader than I expected.”

She sent the musicians indoors, left the tea in the heated pavilion, and told two maids to wait there. The guards remained at the garden exits and side arcade. A guest's joke was no reason to abandon their posts. Ice had been cleared from the inner gravel path; the roped-off pond remained out of bounds.

Only Isabella and Adrian descended the steps. A guard followed at a discreet distance, keeping them in sight.

Without her procession, the princess seemed to have lost a familiar pair of hands. After a dozen steps she glanced back, then realized no one was waiting for her to decide the temperature of the tea.

“What do you usually discuss on walks?”

“The weather. Sometimes nothing.”

“That doesn't make the conversation more useful.”

“Perhaps the point is not to be useful all the time.”

She regarded this as an expensive recreation with no adequate budget attached.

The roses were bare pruned stems, the beds covered in straw, the fountain dry. Around a corner, an elderly gardener struggled to retie a screen around a shrub. The wind kept lifting one corner while the cord dangled out of reach.

Adrian offered help. With permission, he held the screen from a dry patch of path while the gardener tied it securely.

Isabella picked up a pair of old gloves from the path.

The gardener thanked her so profusely that he nearly stepped into his tool basket.

“Watch your feet first,” she said.

After he left, Adrian noticed her studying her fingers. There was damp dirt on her gloves. She didn't look displeased, merely surprised to have accomplished something without instructing anyone else.

“Do you come here often?”

“In spring. Depending on who's visiting.”

“And when there's no visitor?”

She was quiet.

They passed two bare lindens and a frost-whitened bench. Isabella pointed toward the pond. As a child, she had watched white geese there. They chased her silk skirts and pecked at the bright trim.

“So you dislike geese.”

“I like them. They're equally rude to everyone.”

Adrian laughed.

She glanced at him, uncertain for a moment whether her remark deserved it. Then her mouth curved too.

“The master of ceremonies decided they were unsuitable. They were moved.”

“Did you object?”

“I was told it would give me a more peaceful garden. Objecting to a gift given for your own good is harder than objecting to a demand.” She looked at him. “You're rather accomplished at the latter.”

“The cuff links were beautiful.”

“Do you know what they cost?”

“No. I thought knowing might make returning them hurt longer.”

Her laugh wasn't the encouraging sound she used to keep negotiations moving. She slowed, crushing a brittle leaf underfoot, and looked at the fleck of snow on her shoe.

Adrian asked where she would go today if she had no visitor to entertain and no impression to create.

She suggested the greenhouse, then remembered the plants were being moved. She couldn't interrupt the workers. The overlook was icy. One possibility after another failed her.

“I don't even know where I can go in my own garden.”

“There's a sunny corner ahead.”

“There's nothing to see there.”

“There's sunshine.”

They stood against the sheltered wall rather than sit on the frosted bench. Small pieces of snow occasionally fell from distant roofs.

After a while Isabella said she liked bridges. Real ones, with carts crossing and water underneath. The kind that made an entire road complain when they broke.

“Why?”

“Because their usefulness doesn't depend entirely on what the person standing beside them says.”

For once, she didn't immediately turn her answer into a political position. Adrian didn't praise her fitness to rule. He told her about an icy bridge on their journey. Until the workers spread grit, everybody's travel papers had been equally useless.

She asked what the stall beside it sold.

“Hot soup. Very good, though perhaps we were simply cold.”

“An honest review.”

Their twenty minutes were nearly up when the wind drove them toward the pavilion. A maid visibly relaxed at their return and handed Isabella a warmer. The princess did not ask whether the walk had qualified as successfully ordinary.

There were two pots of tea. The maid placed the milder one before Isabella, whose gaze lingered on the other.

“Would you rather have that?” Adrian asked.

“Strong tea makes one seem impatient.”

“Does the tea know?”

The maid failed to hide a smile, then explained that she had followed an old menu, not a new instruction.

“Then I'll change it today,” Isabella said. “Don't throw the mild tea away. Someone will want it.”

She drank slowly and did not become irritable. Adrian tried the same tea, discovered it was far more bitter than expected, and reached hastily for a biscuit.

“You're allowed not to like it.”

“I'm trying not to let my face speak first.”

She laughed outright. The maid restored his mild tea as naturally as though the palace had always permitted small mistakes.

Chamberlain Simon Ellery arrived outside the pavilion. He waited while a maid explained: the council secretariat had sent the agenda accompanying yesterday's notice and wanted acknowledgment before noon.

Isabella asked whether Adrian minded ending their informal conversation. He checked the time and agreed.

This was no proclamation granting a throne. The king still reigned. January 6 would bring a preliminary council on succession affairs, beginning with oversight of old royal projects and related funding. The first session, originally set for January 12, had been brought forward. No new monarch would be chosen in two days, but the candidates' ability to explain their use of power would shape what followed.

Isabella had been allotted fifteen minutes.

Immediately afterward came Lord Alistair Crowe's questions: why had outsiders been brought into the old equipment project, what reliable evidence discredited existing Temple supervision, and what justified further funds?

Isabella tapped the page.

“Three matters requiring different people, all packed into fifteen minutes.”

“You can request separate answers.”

“I could also tell them I won't attend unless they intend to listen.”

Ellery did not rush to write that down. A gust lifted the curtain. The dirt on Isabella's glove had dried pale.

She looked at it and withdrew her hand.

“No. Then they would hear only the answers they brought themselves.”

She asked Ellery to check attendance and filing deadlines, then asked Adrian whether he could explain the connection between the East Ridge accident and the royal dossier.

“I can describe what we saw. I can't say we've proved they followed the same order.”

“I need them to believe this deserves investigation.”

“Then give them the reasons it deserves investigation. Don't ask me to say I've traveled a road I haven't.”

She did not congratulate him for refusing. She studied the agenda, calculating what remained after the loss of an attractive conclusion.

“And if Crowe says I've purchased this entire review?”

“Telling him I returned the cuff links would sound like a quarrel. Separating expenses from access arrangements might help more.”

Her eyes rose. “You finally admit they have a use.”

Ellery coughed. Isabella set aside the remark unsuitable for the agenda.

She asked for concrete things, not a declaration of support: an archivist's account of the surviving versions, a conservator's brief opinion without operational details, and separate speaking time for direct witnesses. Each person would have to agree. Her invitation could not become their answer in advance.

Adrian agreed to check his own observations. He reminded her that Elena had independent questions and would do more than polish their wording.

“I've noticed,” Isabella said. “Ask her to put the least pleasant ones to me. Preferably before the day after tomorrow.”

That afternoon, Elena looked positively delighted to hear it.

“She has finally found a position suited to my talents.”

They entered the house together, and Adrian asked the kitchen what was available. He did not describe the walk as a date or turn Isabella's brief quiet into dinner gossip. He told Elena that their understanding was clear, and that the princess still intended to fight for the position she wanted.

“I didn't expect a garden to change her profession.”

“There really are roses. I identified them correctly this time. And lindens.”

“Good.” She smiled. “Someone has finally answered my original question.”

The secretariat's reply came that evening. After Isabella's statement, Adrian would have five minutes for firsthand observations, Elena three for documentary questions. Technical and custodial opinions could be submitted in writing, without any presumption of acceptance.

Adrian stared at those meager five minutes, picked up his pen, and put it down again.

Elena turned his paper over and wrote the dinner menu on the blank side.

“Rehearse after we eat,” she said. “The palace has put you on a timer. I haven't.”
