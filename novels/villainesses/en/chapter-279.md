# Chapter 279: The Line That Wouldn't Disappear

When Viola handed him the old slip, Adrian thought he'd missed another appointment.

It said only ten minutes, followed by: Sit in the corridor. No walking. No guarding doors.

“A quarter past two.” He noticed the later addition. “Today?”

“January twenty-ninth. I put it back in my notebook afterward. It's been there ever since.”

May 7 sunlight touched a dried pear-juice stain at its corner. She pushed it closer with her right hand, her left arm still firmly supported. Her knightly dating checklist was shorter than a grocery list, yet each item had once made her earnestly blush.

“No guarding doors today either?”

“During today's hearing, please watch the witnesses.”

He laughed. She closed her old notebook. Routes drawn for ordinary invitations still protruded: three evacuation points, somewhere sheltered, don't let your companion mistake the copper whistle for something else. At first he'd thought those red lines led to dates. Later he'd learned that even milk carts enjoyed two evacuation points.

“Why give this to me?”

“A keepsake. Not a voucher for another invitation.” She paused. “I don't want remembering that time to mean remembering only how foolish I was.”

“I remember the pear being rather large.”

She stared, then laughed so hard she turned away. When the clerk called them inside, he put the slip among his personal belongings, not in the evidence catalog. Today someone would ask how much future remained. He wouldn't begin by turning ten past minutes into an argument.

Before Oswin stood no divine image, only three lines on coarse paper.

The first tracked central peripheral preparation, reaching its parked position on April 29. The second showed protective-main-bridge load, still fluctuating high on the page. The third came from a local maintenance station, labeled with measurement and arrival times. It didn't pretend to be a live nationwide view.

“Don't look at the names first,” he said. “Look at which line is falling and which isn't.”

An independent academy engineer checked the original recording strips against the copied charts. The second line had briefly fallen, then risen again. That couldn't establish the next highest peak's date. A local gauge had been replaced; joining its readings directly would create a false jump unless the calibration difference was noted.

He also found half a day missing from the third line. Rather than connecting the gap, he called the local courier forward. Recording paper had run out during a gauge-case replacement, the man explained. Duty staff had noted several manual readings on the back; the chart copyist hadn't transferred them.

There were indeed entries, but a required countersignature was missing. The chair prohibited signing retrospectively in the hall. That section would remain blank for now. Someone muttered that they were falsifying things again.

Flushing, the courier gripped his hat. He had only delivered the papers. Road mud still clung to his boots. Nobody knew how that accusation might follow him home.

“Something is missing,” Adrian said first. “That doesn't yet tell us who omitted it or why.”

Oswin glanced at him. He too demanded that the gap remain, instructing his clerk not to turn incomplete readings into a solid line.

It didn't make them friends. It removed one false certainty either might conveniently have used. The courier released his hat and went to the side table to record when he'd actually received the papers. The main-bridge line didn't fall, but anger had to return to what could be proved.

Oswin retained the correction without withdrawing his argument.

“Imprecision doesn't mean it has disappeared.”

Adrian watched the thick line. That night, how desperately he'd wanted every needle to reach zero. Someone publicly proving one still wouldn't obey made him instinctively feel a piece of his success being taken away.

A man asked why people in the city were celebrating if everything hadn't stopped.

“Because that round of compulsory preparation really did stop,” Adrian answered. “Celebrating what we've achieved doesn't oblige anyone to claim the unfinished work is finished.”

“But my warehouse roof still leaks.”

The man had brought half a muddy roof tile, not a technical challenge. Duty staff asked him to place it on a side table so it wouldn't fall and cut anyone. He hadn't thrown it. Wiping its edge with his sleeve, he explained that the temporary covering needed pulling back into place after every rain.

Oswin asked his district. The clerk recorded only the public detail relevant to installation reports, not his family's full address. Main shielding there still worked but couldn't guarantee every building escaped damage. The roof crack needed inspection; one tile couldn't establish that suspension had caused it.

“I didn't say you broke all of it!” he snapped. “I'm asking who'll pull that cover back next month.”

The room fell quiet. Isabella didn't promise a carriage. Elena didn't reach for her purse. Once disaster became somebody else's life next month, a heroic victory was no longer a sufficient answer.

Oswin tapped the paper.

“Precisely. Disaster will return. One area's respite may coincide with another's trouble. Existing protection continues wearing down. Your stable trials established only limited scale.”

“Can you prove a lethal peak must arrive next month?” the chair asked.

“No. I can establish that lacking a date does not mean lacking another surge.”

He admitted it plainly, without manufacturing tonight's apocalypse from uncertainty. The engineer explained that recurrent historical surges and sustained present loads justified continued shielding, monitoring, and evacuation preparation, not precise predictions of every incident.

Luna occupied a side seat for her approved brief attendance, right palm cushioned. She'd brought no glowing equipment. Answering required her assistant to turn to the correct page from the May 5 demonstration.

“The April twenty-sixth data are genuine,” Oswin said. “Are they sufficient for this load?”

“No.”

Her quick answer made a merchant supporting the alternative frown. She could at least begin with its successes, he murmured. Luna turned toward him.

“We covered those at the previous public demonstration. If anyone proposed replacing the main bridge with those two dummy loads today, I'd block the doorway first.”

“What exactly have you given us, then?”

“A method worth further testing. And the preparation we've actually stopped. Not a new kingdom hidden up my sleeve.”

She coughed. Her assistant offered water. Adrian saw that she didn't reach with her injured hand or offer exhaustion as proof of correctness. Questions beyond what she'd verified went to the engineer.

Oswin asked whether designating five people must remain forever beyond discussion if participation proved insufficient, materials failed to arrive, and districts refused mutual aid.

Viola's right hand stilled on her knee. Many listeners would remember comrades who hadn't returned. Calling that a question only a villain would ask would push those people behind him too.

“We can discuss why you propose it. We cannot begin by putting the designated people behind the locks.”

Fear was real, Cecilia added, as were people who wanted to pray while afraid. Opposing consent signed in the name of divine will didn't mean requiring the warehouse owner to abandon faith before seeking help.

Stroking the tile's edge, he asked Oswin whether five people's compliance would mean never repairing his roof again.

The high priest paused.

“No. The old installation also needs maintenance.”

“Then you can't guarantee it either.”

“Not every place. My argument is that known costs are more controllable than an unproven replacement.”

It wasn't easily dismissed. He hadn't claimed spring rains would cease after five deaths. Yet he still presented five people as a price others could choose. Adrian stopped waiting for a sufficiently absurd statement to make standing up easy.

He rose and requested time to respond tomorrow.

“I'll distinguish existing maintenance from possible future alternatives, explain who does the work and what happens when it can't be done. I can't use an institution that doesn't exist yet to sign for everyone's tomorrow.”

“You acknowledge disaster will return?”

“I acknowledge continuing danger and further surges. We can't eliminate it all today.”

Someone shook their head in disappointment. Another asked whether this meant another postponement. Adrian earned no magnificent applause, only a seat he would have to return to tomorrow.

At the break, Luna left as agreed. Beside him, she said today's best result was not having to pretend she could answer everything. What was the worst?

“My assistant has more questions to record than after the demonstration.”

“Perhaps listen to fewer tomorrow?”

“That sounds suspiciously like recommending a quieter hobby.”

He laughed that he wouldn't dare. Smiling too, she actually went to rest, without staying another moment to prove he mattered.

Viola waited outside for her transport arrangements. Adrian took out the old slip and asked again whether she'd like to sit for ten minutes today. She checked her attendant's schedule. Now would work, provided the chairs were out of the draft.

He moved two chairs. She didn't inspect which was closer to the exit. Before them, a sparsely flowering pot had soil still wet on one side. Apparently its keeper also struggled to finish everything.

“I didn't like your expression in there,” she said.

“Like I'd answered incorrectly?”

“Like you intended to carry every question home and not put them down even to sleep.”

He admitted wanting to fill every blank before tomorrow. Looking at the slip, she said she'd once filled date plans with tasks because one unprepared detail might mean she wasn't worth visiting.

“Then I discovered the hardest thing to prepare for was you suddenly wanting something different to eat.”

“My strategic advantage.”

She nodded solemnly before smiling. Had every route included three evacuation points? No. Eventually one drawing had shown only two chairs.

“I liked that time very much.”

Holding her cup, she flushed. “So did I. You needn't call today something else because I said that.”

He understood. He treasured her company, but present tenderness couldn't promise a future he hadn't chosen. Nor did not receiving that future deprive her of the right to say she'd enjoyed today.

Wind lifted the slip. He anchored its blank corner beneath his cup. Reaching toward him, she stopped short and asked whether she could straighten his turned-up collar.

“Yes. No disciplinary action for improper uniform, though.”

“I no longer hold that appointment.”

Her right hand smoothed the fold more gently than she'd once adjusted shoulder armor. After withdrawing it, she smiled and sentenced him to passing her the prettiest little pastry. No disaster vanished during those minutes. Nobody became less deserving of love.

Parting, she told him to keep the slip safe, not use it to prove punctuality to engineers. He would. A January appointment had frightened him enough today; no need for more victims.

Returning, Adrian passed the maintenance office. Someone sorted worn connectors into two piles. Another person waited with the shift book to verify facts for tomorrow's presentation.

Adrian first asked when his shift ended.

Surprised, the man gave the time, then added that his replacement was still on the road.

Adrian pocketed his polished opening, fetched a chair, and asked him to explain how tonight's shift could actually be handed over.

Tomorrow's answer had to start here.
