# Chapter 178: The Furnace Wouldn't Wait

Before leaving on February 8, Adrian enclosed Luna's borrowed pot sketch and asked a household messenger to return it as promised, with a brief thank-you. Returning it needn't require her to receive him again.

Cecilia put a sewn cloth bag in front of him.

“For lunch. I finished it before yesterday.”

There was still distance in her voice. Today's inspection was previously arranged business, she told him, not proof that yesterday no longer hurt. He understood. A gift needn't certify that she felt nothing.

A crooked flower decorated one corner. Had a child at the relief house made it?

“I did.”

Adrian immediately examined it more carefully. She waited patiently until he asked what kind of flower it was.

“It began as a lily. The stitches disagreed.”

He laughed. “Now it looks like a goose waving.”

“It can guard lunch, then.”

She handed it over without requiring him to return for another meeting after eating. He promised to wash it before returning it, then realized she hadn't said he was borrowing it.

“It's a gift,” Cecilia said. “You needn't return a goose to the Temple.”

They carried lunch to yesterday's confirmed inspection. The Temple's northern annex stood behind its laundry, sheets hanging like gray flags along the approach. It lay outside the registration archive's preservation boundary. Existing orders didn't automatically extend to every building bearing a Temple sign. Today's permission covered specified old impression plates and their maintenance catalog.

The porter checked their names and led them inside. Bitter-smelling steam escaped a low window. Adrian thought someone was boiling medicine.

Cecilia stopped.

“Adhesive wash.”

She couldn't identify every chemical by smell. As a child organizing lecture boards, however, she'd watched workers soften old adhesive, then clean copper surfaces with hot water. The boards survived. Their writing wouldn't wait for later questions.

A young custodian waited under the gallery, one corner of his list damp.

“You're early.”

“This is our appointment.”

He glanced at the treatment room. Cecilia asked him to check yesterday's notice first. Its time matched the porter's register. In fifteen minutes the designated materials were due for non-contact inspection.

Another sheet scheduled duplicate impression plates for bulk cleaning this morning.

Three references appeared on both lists.

The custodian dried the windowsill before laying the papers flat. Adrian waited until the corners were secured and read each reference, rather than trusting similar final digits. Cecilia indicated an alteration on the second line. The young man fetched the receipt book for comparison in his presence.

Adrian's stomach tightened. He hadn't snatched either list. Beside the third overlapping reference, yesterday's note read: dual-layer record, content awaiting interpretation. Today it merely said duplicate.

“Who established they're the same?” Cecilia asked.

The custodian hadn't read the lower layer. He'd followed a classification sheet. Yesterday afternoon he'd delivered the inspection notice to Deacon Rusk, responsible for maintenance disposal. Rusk had told him to keep the original plan.

The deacon emerged from the treatment room with the bottom of his apron wet. The porter’s duty register lay open nearby; Adrian could see the assignment rather than relying on an accusation shouted across a courtyard. He wasn't Martin Veyr and didn't manage the old project's money. Today's duty register specifically assigned him maintenance disposal. That fact mattered more than Adrian's immediate thought that the Temple was destroying everything again.

“Some boards are no longer useful,” Rusk said. “We need room for new ones.”

Cecilia indicated the three references.

“You knew yesterday someone was coming to inspect these.”

“An inspection appointment isn't a stop-work order.”

“Nor have you established that the lower impression matches the upper.”

“The old classification says duplicate.”

He met her stare. Damp storage was making films adhere to one another, he explained. Delay would cost more material. Contractors charged by the day. The furnace was already lit. Maintenance couldn't stop whenever somebody thought something might matter.

Not all of that was nonsense. Adrian could see why a yard that stored physical material needed maintenance, and why a worker already hired for the day could not simply be wished free of cost. That still did not answer which particular objects needed which treatment. Several curled backing boards against the wall visibly bore mold. But the shallow tray approaching the bath had a recent tag: awaiting technical reading.

“Is that tray mold-damaged too?” Adrian asked.

Rusk looked at it before answering. “They're being processed together.”

“Please record the reasons separately.”

“You don't run this place.”

“No. I can't order you. I can ask you not to call an unanswered question answered.”

Someone inside asked whether the second tray should go in. Rusk hesitated, then called, “Keep to the plan.”

Cecilia stepped forward. The custodian instinctively blocked her. She stopped rather than squeeze between hot liquid and narrow racks. Adrian was gripping the bag; one corner of the bread flattened.

“You know these materials concern historical records under examination,” she said.

“I know somebody says so.”

“Some concern an old relationship cited against me personally. I'm formally requesting their preservation.”

He would register it for a supervisor's response tomorrow.

“After cleaning?”

Rusk didn't look at the list.

Cecilia asked the young custodian to record her request, the answer, and the time. Not a verdict. Had he personally seen that one tray hadn't entered the bath?

He quietly said yes.

“Then please write that it's still here.”

Her composure embarrassed people more than shouting. Adrian followed her into the outer gallery, expecting a search for someone whose rank outweighed Rusk's. Instead, she asked the porter to send an urgent message through the review contact named on their notice, attaching the conflicting arrangements and disposal time.

That contact already existed for inspection disputes. It didn't require Isabella to regain a silver badge.

After the messenger left, the porter took yesterday's duty sheet down, intending to burn it with wastepaper. Cecilia asked whether the sheet pinned behind it was their inspection arrangement.

It was. A knot concealed half a name.

“Rusk said to replace it.”

“You can replace the display. Please retain this one. It records delivery.”

The porter looked reluctant, afraid even one unburned sheet might cause trouble. She didn't ask him to surrender it to her. Could it go into the original receiving folder, marked as requested for review? After hesitating, he complied. He could dispose of the ordinary wastepaper as usual.

Adrian saw the notation on the back: yesterday, fourth bell, received by Maintenance. He asked permission to add that observation to his copy, not treat a reported instruction as proven. The porter agreed, then slowly removed an old pin from the corner and kept it in his drawer.

“I only mind the door.”

“Then record only what you know from doing that.”

Cecilia stepped aside so clean linen could still be delivered. The elderly woman carrying it recognized her from the relief house. Was the Temple opening another registration office?

“No. We've come to inspect something already arranged.”

She didn't use a familiar person's trust to gather a crowd or promise today's resolution. The woman tucked a loose strip into her basket and continued to the laundry. A prayer bell rang. Several workers paused; the treatment furnace did not.

A metal tray clattered inside.

Adrian almost ran toward it. This time delay really could erase something. Procedures weren't invariably timely. But overturning a hot bath wouldn't make him capable of reading the old words.

“I'll ask whether the next tray can wait.”

Cecilia nodded. “I'll wait here for the answer. I won't agree to your going inside for you.”

He remained beyond the dry line the custodian permitted. The contractor was a gray-browed woman holding long tongs. She first told him to gather his coat hem.

“Don't touch that rack.”

He obeyed. Did she know preservation had been requested for this tray?

“No. He said old samples.”

Rusk returned and told her to continue. She compared the inspection copy Adrian had given the custodian with the tag awaiting interpretation.

“I agreed to clean scrap,” she said. “Who examined this tray?”

Maintenance accepted responsibility, Rusk replied. She laid the tongs across their support, not into the bath.

“Then show me a disposal sheet identifying it. Not one with the other tray's number.”

She wasn't testifying for the world. She simply didn't want later guesses about what she'd washed away. Rusk threatened to deduct the waiting time from her pay. She asked him to put that on the work order, then moved the second tray back to the dry bench.

Adrian didn't promise compensation. He couldn't spend the old project's balance, and didn't know whether the Rosenbergs would pay. Would she explain the work-scope dispute to the reviewer?

Yes. And he should stop blocking ventilation.

The pause wasn't victory. The first tray remained where the work had already begun. Nobody could reach into that heat, point at the softened surface, and honestly claim to know which of the disputed impressions had survived. The first tray had already soaked; its upper film curled into gray skin. They couldn't yet identify its references beside the hot bath. Saving the next tray wouldn't recover words already washed away.

Cecilia returned with mud on her hem and a response. The duty reviewer had issued an interim preservation direction covering the three overlapping references and their catalog. The officer serving it entered behind her. It didn't close the entire maintenance yard or order everyone's arrest.

Rusk asked whether the issuer understood the cost of stopping.

“You may itemize that,” the officer said. “These three items are to remain unprocessed.”

The deacon's fingers paused on the paper. Finally he ordered the heat extinguished beneath the cleaning bath. The contractor warned that the liquid remained hot; it couldn't be moved immediately. Guards stood beyond the dry bench rather than grabbing the tray.

Cecilia studied the references that had so recently been waiting for a reply. She didn't announce that faith had won. They had a direction to stop certain work, not a repaired past. She kept looking at the actual references on the paper, where the names of what might yet be saved were small enough to fit beneath her finger.

“Keep the original disposal sheet too. And the record of what happened before I arrived.”

The room had to cool before the inventory. They sat at the end of the gallery and opened the goose bag. The bread resembled a badly written letter.

“I've crushed your lunch.”

“It's less likely to roll away now.”

He couldn't laugh. She stopped trying, tore off half, and ate.

Eventually she said she knew this prayer bell. She'd delivered clean clothes here before. She wanted the people inside merely to have confused two lists. That would make things easier for her.

“And now?”

“Now I can't choose that answer for them.”

He offered her the remaining bread. She pointed at his hand instead.

“You eat too. Hunger won't make the afternoon quicker.”

When the gate opened again, the inventory officers and a conservator arrived. Rusk stood beside a gallery pillar, no longer between the bath and every question. The first tray was marked damaged, pending examination; the second remained sealed. Neither had been handed to Adrian.

He folded the empty bag. The crooked goose vanished into a crease, then appeared when he turned it. Only then did he notice he'd been gripping it with his left hand. His palm had offered no miraculous answer.

This afternoon he'd have to use his eyes to discover what remained.
