# Chapter 200: The Stop He Pushed Past

Adrian hadn't scheduled the trial before breakfast.

On February 27 he sat opposite Elena and finished the porridge he'd complained about. She didn't ask whether sleep had resolved anything or hide the new judicial notice beneath a napkin. They discussed road repairs until he put down his spoon and admitted that today might still show him nothing.

“You won't need to invent something for me tonight.”

He meant to smile, but nodded instead. She wouldn't wait outside the trial room today. He'd chosen to decide about seeing her after returning home. She had preparations of her own; concern didn't require canceling them all.

During reassessment he reported waking once in the night. No continuing headache, no remaining nausea from the first trial. Fear of the next action remained. The physician permitted one limited exposure with stopping conditions, not an appointment he was obliged to complete.

Luna sat with her right palm supported, her left hand free of a recording pen. Beatrice read back yesterday's exposure: the yard entrance, low arch, distant view of the mounting, and the examiner's limited explanations. Those could affect later interpretation and must remain documented. He hadn't seen the internal controls or read the maintenance instructions authorized for retrieval today.

Another examiner held those instructions sealed until his account was finished.

The apparatus had changed little. A shifting joint had been removed from the new positioning frame. Ordinary sample tests showed repeatable alignment at the specified angle. A newly made film remained behind its transparent barrier; power would not exceed the first trial's setting. The old energy case and temple originals stayed in their separate repositories. Nobody would show him internal controls to imitate or read what should happen after the handle moved.

“Why does this still feel like an examination?” he asked.

“Because you want someone to announce that it's over and you can go home.”

“No announcement?”

“You can go home. I can't promise it's over.”

Luna let the joke stop there. In the stopping rehearsal, the falling card brought immediate disconnection. The physician checked the indicator had returned to zero. Adrian trusted that small sound more than reassurance.

He chose to begin.

Cold came first, followed by damp ash. No fire burned in the room, but his throat reacted to the choking smell. Then familiar knuckles, the brass handle, the dark abrasion along his palm.

“The west passage isn't clear yet.”

The unseen speaker remained unidentifiable.

“Wait another moment.”

His own voice.

The handle sank slightly. This time the fragment didn't end there. A cross-stop lay below it. His left thumb rested against a projecting latch. The handle had been blocked. He was waiting for a fine needle to fall within a marked line. It wavered outside. Beside it, a short label read NOT ENGAGED.

An urgent voice came from another direction, muffled by something heavy. He caught only don't disconnect. His view shifted slightly. A white line passed through thick glass. Beyond it several faint points seemed ready to go dark.

He didn't wait for the needle.

His earlier thumb pushed the latch outward. He felt an edge digging into skin, remembered using more force than usual. No accidental stumble. Nobody holding his hand. Once the latch withdrew, he forced the handle past the stop and down into the lower notch.

“Disconnect the main bridge first,” he heard himself say. “At least they won't be able to send any more.”

What followed that sentence remained absent. Who intended to send whom, and where? No answer hung outside his view. Between his fingers, lettering emerged on the handle mounting: DO NOT ISOLATE BEFORE TRANSFER ENGAGES.

His earlier self had already read it.

That knowledge arrived with the urgency in his hand, not as an explanation supplied beside today's table. He'd believed he must stop what was happening first and deal with the other danger afterward.

The handle settled. The white line darkened in the middle.

Two points beside the wall went out, one after the other. Gray haze, previously held beyond the threshold, surged inward around his shoes. Someone shouted in the distance, the sound suddenly unobstructed. The entire world didn't visibly break. This room and the section of doorway he could see had lost something beneath his hand.

He tried lifting the handle.

It wouldn't move.

His view fell while he remained seated in today's chair. The positions collided. Nausea rushed into his throat. He didn't keep hold of the card to identify the shouting person.

“Stop.”

The card dropped. Power disconnected. Nearby, Beatrice said it had stopped.

He raised his empty left hand. No brass. His fingers still curled around an absent grip. Slowly he opened them. Leaving water on the table, the physician first asked where he was.

“The workroom.”

“Sitting or standing?”

“Sitting.”

Only after saying it did he feel the chair back. Tears soaked his collar. I shut it down broke once between his teeth. The physician asked him to breathe; he needn't immediately surrender everything he'd experienced.

Luna remained behind and to one side. She didn't hold his hand down. Her observation sheet was sealed first so that its sequence couldn't become a prompt in his initial account.

When he could speak fully, Beatrice asked whether to record.

“Yes. Don't show me the instructions yet.”

He described the handle, cross-stop, latch, unengaged needle, his words, the extinguished line, and entering haze. He said he'd known engagement should come first and still released the latch. That knowledge accompanied the fragment; it wasn't a physical exhibit others could watch.

“I don't know how many people died afterward. I didn't see the entire world. I saw myself do that.”

During readback he corrected one sequence. Haze entered after the white line darkened, not before the handle moved. Distant impacts, however, had begun before shutdown. Both remained recorded rather than one being deleted to simplify the story.

The physician prohibited further exposure. Adrian didn't request it. He needed follow-up for heaviness in his head and cold hands. Answering questions wasn't permission for repetition. The recorded interval of visible gray traces was longer than in the first trial, but the film couldn't display his mind to observers. Account and technical changes were sealed separately.

At the short afternoon comparison, the maintenance instructions were opened. Not the final account's conclusion: an operating appendix retrieved from the works file through the room subnumber established yesterday. Two stopping positions appeared in its diagram. Manual latch release could bypass the intermediate stop. Normal isolation required confirmation that the bypass had taken the load.

Adrian saw only the authorized detail. Notch shape, stop orientation, and warning text matched his previously sealed account. None had been visible from the yard or described in the public notice.

The examiner retained the list of information Adrian had already encountered. Agreement didn't end investigation. Independent structural evidence now supported serious examination of his manually isolating the main bridge before transfer was complete. The appendix didn't pronounce on the destruction of the world.

“What did the equipment maintain?” Adrian asked.

The works examiner indicated a readable section. Besides transmission, the main bridge maintained part of the western passage's protective barrier. Disconnecting without transfer would remove that protection. Earlier load conditions and remote faults belonged to another inspection file being sought through the related request.

Luna didn't tell him wanting to save people meant he shouldn't grieve.

“You wanted to prevent something. You also saw that something else wasn't ready.”

He nodded. The earlier hand was no longer a vague shape moving half an inch. He had pushed past an actual stop, trusting himself to decide the order of other people's dangers.

“That was a wrong shutdown.”

The room didn't collapse around the sentence. Beatrice asked him to separate the action he acknowledged, the understanding returned with it, and today's technical comparison. He did. Admitting an error didn't entitle him to have every word certified at once.

Before they finished, Luna had the assistant read her unfinished assessment. Its opening had been Nonhistorical imagery cannot be excluded. A revision mark crossed the position, not the limitation: she had moved it back among evidentiary conditions.

“I wanted to lead with that.”

“It's true.”

“And saying it first might hurt you less. I need to know why I chose it.”

She looked at the cloth under her palm without seeking an appreciative smile. Adrian had thought the people most concerned for him should be first to find innocent explanations. Now she set an unfavorable comparison squarely before him. He trusted her continued presence more than hurried comfort.

“I still hope it won't make you unwilling to see me.”

Her eyes reddened. “I want to see you today. I can't promise always to like what you've done.”

He nodded and moved his chair back to leave space for her caregiver. Luna had sat long enough. Keeping him company couldn't justify another session's worth of strain. Next time they would first ask whether the other wanted to talk, not turn today's words into permanent obligations.

The rest of his observation took place in the courtyard rest room. Through the open door somebody wheeled past with empty flowerpots. Thinking of Miriam, he reached for paper, intending to ask whether she would accept his apology.

The pen stopped.

She hadn't witnessed the operation or agreed to receive remorse requiring a response today. He couldn't place every unknown casualty into a letter and make her answer for them. Turning the sheet over, he noted practical questions for the examiner: would she want to hear the new findings at an appropriate time? Did she need to revise anything in her route description?

When sunlight reached his chair legs, Cecilia brought the lunch he'd agreed to receive. She didn't open the sealed papers.

“Do you know the result?”

“Only that the trial is over and you need rest. Tell me, or eat first.”

He ate two mouthfuls before saying he'd seen the shutdown. She set down her spoon and listened to the scope he chose to share. No divine plan was supplied to give the error a comfortable place.

“Now there's a definite wrong, I'm afraid people will pile everything else onto it.”

“We can still stop them. Without hiding this wrong.”

He looked into his bowl. The soup wasn't better than usual. He could finally taste its salt.

At home he chose not to see Elena yet. His own note said the trial had ended, there was new material, and he needed rest tonight. Her reply acknowledged it without asking whether he regretted sitting opposite her at breakfast.

His left hand rested on the blanket, the crescent scar unchanged. He'd pressed no switch today. The earlier action could no longer be avoided by leaving it unnamed.

Outside someone called for box carriers to stop a moment. Their footsteps stopped, then resumed. Adrian didn't force himself through the haze again. He would rest and take this particular error into tomorrow's inquiry. Neither reduce it to half an inch nor let it occupy the whole catastrophe.
