# Chapter 198: The Woman Who Came Out of the Fire

The woman in the interview room didn't look at Adrian's left hand.

She looked at the door. Only after establishing that the bolt remained open did she move the cloth bag from her lap to the table. Outside the independent receiving office, someone was scrubbing the steps. Water splashed against stone in the February 25 morning. Sitting down, Adrian understood that avoiding reporters wasn't her only reason for choosing this room.

“My name is Miriam Sayer. I bind ledgers for bookshops now. I have work this afternoon.”

She looked about forty, with a rough callus along her forefinger. No solemn air of someone preparing an ancient revelation. At her request Cecilia had left her ceremonial white robe behind. The examiner read the agreed conditions and waited for everyone to confirm them. Cecilia recognized the woman who had delivered repaired ledgers and asked about confidential intake a few days earlier. Only today did she learn her name.

Yesterday's application had promised verifiable information, not someone who could save Adrian. This woman might bring worse answers.

“Mr. Hale didn't introduce me. I don't know him. I haven't seen your private experiment records.”

Adrian nodded. He didn't mention the brass handle or warning.

“Start with what you want to tell us.”

A custodian brought a small paper box. Miriam checked yesterday's intake seal across its lid without opening it. The examiner brought a separate envelope. Three years earlier Miriam had deposited a private statement with her bookbinders' guild custodian. Not a report of prophecy. Increasingly unsure which experiences were dreams, she'd wanted a version she couldn't keep rewriting.

The guild certified when it had received the statement, not its truth. The deposit register ran continuously between neighboring loan receipts. Two former custodians had initialed the seal. Miriam had authorized retrieval yesterday; each handover was documented.

“This predates the notice,” the examiner said. “It wasn't written this month in response to publication.”

“I wasn't waiting three years for you either,” Miriam told Adrian.

The slight edge loosened his hands on his knees. He too readily treated every old object as a letter addressed to him.

The examiner put the sealed statement aside and asked Miriam to describe her experience afresh. Nobody should supply words. She'd been copying names at a casualty-transfer station, matching stretcher tags to receiving entries. When the fire spread, injured people and empty stretchers crowded westward. Steam smothered the smell of burning cloth. Her left foot caught in a slanted iron grating.

“Who helped you?” Cecilia asked.

“Someone carrying a lamp. Put it below the step and lifted the corner of the grate. I didn't see a face.”

She didn't invent a name for her rescuer. She crawled into a low passage beside the wall and heard two heavy impacts overhead. Outside, someone bandaged her foot and asked her name. She answered. Then they asked her occupation.

“I said I wasn't writing any more names that day.”

She rubbed the knot on her bag. Questions of dates and identity acquired a human weight.

“And afterward?” Adrian asked.

“A great blank. I remember being helped up. I remember waking in another bed. I don't know whether a day passed between. That fire isn't in the present chronology. I can't explain it.”

The examiner recorded the gap without asking her to fill it with an account of resetting the world.

She hadn't carried a complete second life in her head. Three years ago, repairing a ledger, she'd used an old wrist tag to weigh down a curling edge beside her lamp. Light entering its stone inset at an angle had brought the smell of scorched wet cloth. Twice afterward she'd tried the same position. Sometimes the same broken sounds returned; usually nothing happened. Once a headache lasted until evening. She stopped deliberately repeating the experience and stored the tag separately from her statement.

“I don't want to do that today.”

“Nothing of that kind is arranged,” Cecilia said.

Her shoulders finally lowered. Even courteous questions could make someone believe that bringing evidence meant agreeing to hurt again.

The box was opened. An old copper strip lay inside, its clasp replaced, gray-white stone visible within a narrow inset. Yesterday Miriam had permitted a preliminary exterior and material comparison by Beatrice. This morning’s report accompanied it; formal examination remained incomplete. Layered traces were comparable to known historical contract media; that didn't authenticate every recollection. The tag hadn't been activated or connected to anyone's body.

A transfer subnumber was engraved inside. The examiner produced an authorized old receiving slip located through that number. It came from a separate casualty directory, not the temple's final account or Miriam's own envelope. Its entry named copying clerk Miriam Sayer: left ankle trapped; received through western refuge opening; able to answer on arrival.

Adrian's throat tightened at the last words.

Able to answer.

An old record finally did something besides sorting people into accusations and consequences. Somebody had once crouched down to establish that this woman could still say her name.

Miriam read slowly, nodding at the occupation. She stopped at an unclear character without deciding what she wanted it to mean. Longstanding guild and residence records established her present identity. Connecting her with the historical entry required more than a shared name.

Only then was the older envelope opened.

Her writing three years ago was more hurried than today's signature. Short sentences crowded narrow pages. Three crossbars in the slanted grate. A blue direction sign installed upside down. An opening so low she had to narrow her shoulders. Her refusal to write more names after escaping. A copying desk whose right leg stood on a strip of leather folded twice.

On the receiving slip's reverse, a property-transfer column preserved a temporary repair note for that desk: missing wedge beneath right leg; leather substituted. Miriam hadn't been given access to the original before this inquiry. The public notice certainly hadn't described it.

Cecilia didn't immediately declare the matter settled. Who had been able to consult that directory? Recent verified borrowing records included neither Miriam nor her employer, the examiner said. A single register couldn't exclude every earlier circulation. But independently formed records, the receiving entry, and her sealed statement now supported firsthand experience far more strongly than a story invented this month.

“This isn't an account inherited from a grandfather. We can proceed with her as a survivor witness to the old catastrophe, with substantial supporting evidence.”

Miriam didn't smile. She pushed the paper slightly away.

“I wanted somebody to hear that they didn't all die because of one man's name. I didn't, at least.”

The chair seemed to harden beneath Adrian. Among people he'd imagined as distant shadows, here was one still calculating time for afternoon binding work. She possessed neither omniscience nor a complete map brought back from the world's end. She had survived the catastrophe she experienced. Old material retained traces across the break in her experience. Now she sat opposite him.

“Do you recognize me?” he finally asked.

She studied him. “A little like someone I saw. I won't turn a resemblance into a claim about where you stood.”

Disappointment arrived faster than expected. He'd still hoped to hear yes, followed by you saved me.

“I'm not here to certify you as a good man.”

“I know.”

“You didn't look as though you knew.”

He admitted what he'd hoped. She didn't comfort him. Instead she took her first drink of water. The cup left a damp ring when she set it down. She wiped it with her sleeve, sparing whoever would clean the room that afternoon.

Cecilia asked why she had waited until now to come forward.

Miriam turned the cup halfway. “I approached people before. One said the fire had frightened me. Another asked to keep the tag for research. I asked how long. Until the research was finished, he said.”

“What happened?”

“I took it away. Lost a binding job that afternoon too.”

Lost wages sounded easier to measure than being misunderstood. Adrian looked at the box. Its intact custody wasn't proof of unbroken faith that somebody would eventually understand. When nobody gave her a limit, she had taken her own property home.

Yesterday's public statement by the princess hadn't made her trust every noble. She'd heard that assistance needn't require signing support for someone, and decided to try an independent counter. Her inquiry had specifically asked whether actual travel expenses would still be covered if her account failed to support their preferred conclusion.

The examiner showed Adrian the written answer: yes, against tickets, regardless of favorable content. No fee purchased ownership of her memories.

“You're disappointed. I'll still leave in the part about not recognizing you.”

“It should stay.”

“I've heard should. Today I'll see whether you let it.”

He didn't pile promises across the table. During readback, when the clerk reached her inability to identify him by appearance, Adrian confirmed that those were her words. Miriam looked at him. This time she didn't reclaim the bag to her lap.

They discussed how to continue. She could draw the route she'd traveled, but didn't want a ready-made map to choose from. She'd let an examiner investigate it. She wouldn't bring a procession of nobles to her home. Nor did she presently consent to publication of her name.

Adrian almost asked her to inspect an old direction plan. He stopped. She hadn't survived that fire to satisfy his urgency. He requested a separate drawing appointment, requiring no contact with the tag. Any visit could be discussed afterward.

Before leaving she drew a crookedly sewn finger guard from her bag and slipped it on, already returning to the afternoon's ledgers. Cecilia offered transport. Miriam had bought a public-coach ticket.

“If you really want to help, don't escort me outside as though I'm an important witness.”

They remained in the room and let her leave first. Nobody had locked the door.

That afternoon Luna received the comparison summary Miriam had authorized for sharing. Her right palm remained cushioned during the short session. Hearing about the angled light, she asked the assistant to note that the tag and Adrian's scar couldn't be assumed to share a structure. They certainly couldn't borrow it to try on him.

“I didn't suggest it.”

“Keep not suggesting it.”

She was serious. No joke about who most wanted to touch his hand softened the warning. Adrian put a bag of soft cake on the corner. Had it passed the tooth examination? she asked. At the personal cost of half a piece, he assured her. Then she smiled.

“She draws tomorrow. I want to watch. I'm afraid my map will get into her drawing first.”

Luna suggested arriving later, after the independent drawing was sealed. He agreed, leaving his old maps in his bag rather than opening them for convenience.

Downstairs Elena asked about the meeting. He said independent evidence now supported an old-catastrophe survivor's experience; her identity remained private. Elena accepted that limit and asked what troubled him most.

“She didn't stay alive in order to forgive me.”

The book in Elena's hands lowered. “Then she can keep living for her own reasons too.”

He nodded. Tomorrow they would begin with a path somebody had actually traveled, not ask her to stand on an answer drawn in advance. A public coach passed the corner, wheels splashing through shallow water. He didn't know whether Miriam was aboard. He didn't go to check.
