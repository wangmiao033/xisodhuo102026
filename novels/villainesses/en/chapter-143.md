# Chapter 143: She Wasn't Asking for Her Father's Chair

The first thing Elena brought back was her father's refusal.

Adrian was changing the water in the flowers by the window. He nearly poured it into his shoe. Outside, on the morning of January 17, last night's ice still glazed the sill. Elena removed her gloves and rescued the vase before setting down her folder.

“Refused what?”

“To lend the Rosenberg seat to the princess as proof that her succession eligibility should be restored.”

“We didn't ask for that.”

“So I asked him to read the second page.”

Her mouth twitched at his wet cuff. Duke Edmund Rosenberg had risen before dawn to meet the family lawyer. Elena had brought the two separate applications they had prepared, not a private appeal for Isabella. After the second page, her father had not withdrawn his objection. He had narrowed its object.

“He'll hear the factual case. He won't guarantee her the outcome.”

“You won him over?”

“I won an appointment before noon. Not his sponsorship.”

She produced the lawyer's procedural extract. The family's existing assembly seat could raise questions concerning its former charitable property. It could not squeeze an entire succession dispute into that scope. Being the duke's daughter did not give Elena another vote. Her father could designate her to present this case; formal sponsorship and voting remained his responsibility.

Adrian steadied the vase. “When you said you'd win a seat, I thought—”

“That I intended to sit on Father's head?”

“Beside him, at least.”

“Winning a chair where I can finish speaking will keep me busy enough.”

She didn't ask him to write her statement. She asked him to hear it and identify anything he couldn't support. At the claim that royal projects had deliberately exploited the family donation, she stopped and crossed out deliberately herself.

“I want to say it.”

“I know.”

“Aren't you going to tell me not to be angry?”

“The anger can stay. That word needs something else behind it.”

She laid down her pen and caught his dry sleeve.

“Come with me. But don't hear Father disagree and immediately start proving you're worth his support.”

After a moment, he nodded.

Before they left, Viola delivered two carriage confirmations. Passenger names were larger than the routes. She handed them over with her right hand; her left arm remained supported.

“Two. Elena and Adrian. The other carriage carries the princess and the attendant she confirms herself.”

“You needn't write my full name three times.”

“I omitted someone last time. Not this time.”

Elena checked the papers seriously and asked whether the two-person carriage still had the lowest step. Viola said yes, then noticed that Elena had already taken Adrian's arm. Her ears reddened.

“This wasn't my arrangement.”

“It was mine,” Elena said.

Adrian found it difficult to pretend he spent the entire journey studying documents.

The duke met them not in his grand study but in a small room at the family business office. Winter repair estimates and two tenants' letters lay on his desk. Neither was less important than his daughter's papers.

“If our seat sponsors this, who bears the consequences when our old accounts are examined too?”

Elena did not say the innocent had nothing to fear.

“We bear what belongs to us. I can't promise no one will find a mistake.”

The lawyer indicated the restricted purpose in the original donation deed. It gave them grounds to ask questions. It also meant the family could be asked why, after receiving vague accounts of later use, it had not pursued them.

Adrian saw Elena close her hand in her lap.

Her mother's name on the page did not make every subsequent decision right.

“I'll answer for my part. Father, you can oppose restoring the princess. But embarrassment cannot mean that once money leaves this room, no one is responsible for it.”

He studied her without immediately approving.

“You're asking more than my company in a meeting room.”

“Yes. That's why I asked instead of putting your name down first.”

Adrian kept quiet. His prepared arguments suddenly seemed unnecessary, like rushing to push a door someone was opening herself.

The duke turned to him. Did he trust the princess?

“I trust specific choices she's made recently. I don't know what she'll choose every time.”

“Cautious.”

“I haven't always been.”

Under the table, Elena lightly touched his shoe. It wasn't a request to change his answer.

The lawyer proposed a narrower route. The matter would concern public examination of old-project receipts, expenses, and personnel transfers. Isabella's appeal would remain separate. The Rosenberg seat would sponsor it; a qualified member unconnected to that property would second it. Neither would guarantee witnesses' conclusions. Families could attend voluntarily or submit written accounts.

The duke agreed to submit that scope and designated Elena to explain the family sources. He did not give her authority without limits.

Accepting the signed proposal, she surprised herself by asking whether lunch was still at the usual time.

“Did you think agreeing meant I would stop eating?” her father asked.

She looked down and laughed.

They stayed for a brief lunch. He asked about traction on the carriage wheels, the weather, and whether Elena was still reading her travel book. Not every domestic question became a test for Adrian. Before they left, however, he called his daughter back alone.

Adrian waited outside. Through the partly open door, he heard the duke ask whether she understood that obtaining a place to speak also allowed others to contradict her publicly.

She said she did.

Their voices lowered. Adrian moved farther down the corridor, unwilling to turn concern into an excuse to listen.

At the assembly registry that afternoon, progress slowed again.

Baroness Merriwell, approached as a possible seconder, had not seen the full application. Sitting by a window, she read every page. Her first objection was to their requested hearing date. Staff already had festival duties; demonstrating concern for a princess did not justify rearranging everyone's night work.

“We can request the public questioning period the day after tomorrow. I won't guarantee every part can be open.”

Elena agreed, then insisted on reasons for closed portions. Royal affairs could not serve as a blanket over every page.

They argued for nearly a quarter-hour. Adrian had imagined supporters would make things easier. People willing to sign often asked more exact questions than those who politely showed visitors out.

At last, the baroness removed her spectacles.

“Do you want Her Highness to win, or a result someone can examine again?”

“I want the necessary questions asked.”

“Including questions for your father?”

“Including those.”

She did not sign yet. Instead, she pushed back the paragraph on family participation and asked who would explain it to people unable to read a long document.

“The receiving office can explain,” Elena said.

“How far away is it? How much work will a visit cost them? If they don't understand what they're deciding, free choice becomes an attractive phrase.”

Elena flushed. Yesterday she had thought the arrangements thorough. Permission to submit a written account could not give a pen to someone unable to compose one.

Adrian suggested using the inquiry office's existing contact arrangements to ask whether families needed someone to take down an oral statement. The recorder would read it back for their approval. The princess's attendants must not select their words. Refusing to attend must not suspend a search.

The baroness asked who would pay. Adrian instinctively glanced at Elena. She did not immediately offer family funds.

“Let's first ask whether the registry's existing oral-submission service covers this. Ordinary applications shouldn't all become favors requiring a patron.”

They went next door to check. A clerk produced a thin register. There were already two regular periods each week for people who needed to dictate an application. This case qualified, but the approaching hearing left no guarantee everyone could come in time.

“What happens to the late material?” Adrian asked.

“It can still be added afterward. Specify a deadline. Absence on the day mustn't be treated as withdrawal.”

Elena added the arrangement. On their return, the baroness had crossed out another overbroad phrase: representing all concerned.

“You represent your own application. Don't agree for absent people.”

“I'll change it.”

Elena did not hide the omission behind the lawyer's name. She rewrote a page, leaving a faint red pressure mark on her finger. Adrian moved water beside her writing hand. She looked up first to ask whether he had drunk any himself.

He fetched his own cup too.

The baroness's voice softened a little. “At least you know people at tables get thirsty.”

Elena nearly laughed, restrained herself, and finished the line.

Once revisions and original were retained together, the baroness signed. The pen made no dramatic sound.

The clerk verified the two sponsors' scope and signatures, then entered Elena as a proposed presenter. Their application could finally proceed to agenda review. Before dusk, a reply approved a preliminary document examination the following afternoon and limited public questioning on the morning of the nineteenth. Private identities and restricted safety material would be handled separately. The chair could stop irrelevant accusations. Nothing guaranteed Isabella's separate appeal.

Elena read it, then laid it before the princess.

Isabella looked first at the seating order, then the participation terms.

“They haven't given me the head of the table.”

“Nor me.”

She looked at Elena and finished the page.

Outside that evening, people were already carrying lanterns. Two girls argued over which to light first. Their father shielded them from the wind while his own hair blew wildly about.

Elena stopped to watch.

Adrian asked if she was tired. Yes, she said. Too tired to hear anyone tell her everything was only beginning.

“Then what has ended tonight?”

“I finished speaking. Father listened to the end. And you didn't speak for me.”

He smiled and offered his hand. She took it without immediately moving.

“You didn't listen outside the door?”

“No.”

“Father said I used to inspect the knots before arguing over my turn on the swing.”

“That sounds like you.”

“He also said not to tie myself to it this time.”

She put her gloves in her coat pocket, leaving her bare hand in his. He did not hurry her. Only after the family disappeared around the corner did she ask whether he still wanted to read tonight. Not to comfort her, nor to reward a signed application. He said yes; he wanted to know whether the captain who kept losing keys ever reached his own room. She laughed, without restraining it this time.

She watched the two lanterns finally glow.

“I'm learning.”

A new letter awaited them in the sitting room. Another assembly member was willing to support restoration of some coordination powers, provided the families' public accounts were postponed and the search placed under the royal household's internal relief procedure. This would prevent the hearing from spreading alarm.

Isabella had not ordered an immediate reply.

Outside, the wind tipped a little lantern sideways. Its bearer steadied it. Looking at the letter, the princess slowly set down the pen she had picked up.
