# Chapter 108: The Second the Witch Had Not Prepared For

On December 18, Luna brought a comb to breakfast.

Adrian looked from her to the comb.

“If that's a new instrument, I'd keep it away from the jam.”

“Its function is ancient.” She pinned it beneath her palm. “Making someone look as though she slept last night.”

“Is it working?”

“You may choose a kind answer.”

Elena glanced over her teacup. “Take the pen out of the left side first.”

Luna reached behind her ear and extracted it. Cecilia offered a small mirror. Viola moved the bread basket beside the mirror, ensuring that the witch would remember to eat afterward.

Adrian stopped teasing. He had slept better than he feared. The unfamiliar corridor had not returned, and no footsteps had followed him into his dreams. He had woken to a broom sweeping thin snow off stone.

Dr. Hensley had questioned him again that morning. No new dizziness, warmth in his hand, or difficulty breathing; an ordinary examination revealed nothing requiring immediate treatment. That did not explain yesterday's two breaths. She advised him to avoid the receiving plate's light path. He agreed.

Today he would not sit at an observation window.

“You really don't want another look?” Luna asked.

“I want one. I won't have one today.”

She put away the comb and nodded.

Elena pushed the honey toward him. He noticed the tiredness beneath her eyes. Instead of telling her not to worry, he tried something else.

“After we're finished, shall we buy the next volume of the captain's travels?”

“We haven't finished this one.”

“I want to check that he makes it home alive.”

“Yesterday you were against reading the ending first.”

“Only the back cover, then.”

She smiled. “Those usually lie more convincingly than the captain.”

“You make discussing a book sound like planning an elopement,” Luna said.

“An elopement would require a map first,” Viola observed.

After a moment's silence, everyone laughed. The knight's ears reddened, but she did not retract her contribution.

She had her follow-up appointment today. The original fourteen-day rest period had reached the point for reassessment; a doctor must set further restrictions. Turning a calendar page would not restore her ability to carry a shield. She packed biscuits separately and asked Adrian to confirm her afternoon carriage with the administrator.

“Don't wait for me. But don't put me down as present.”

Luna agreed and crossed out the empty space on the attendance sheet.

Before they went to the workshop, she fetched her coat upstairs. Adrian picked up the empty basket by the door. A blue ribbon was caught in its handle. What had presumably been intended as a graceful bow had become a determined knot.

“No commentary,” Luna called from the stairs.

“I'm admiring its resolve.”

“It refuses to come undone because I haven't decided who gets the basket.”

“An empty basket?”

She pointed beyond the courtyard wall. The pastry shop beside the bookshop sold candied-orange biscuits only in winter. She had ordered some days ago. Originally she meant to collect them alone, but carrying an entire basket home would make her look like someone who needed reminding to eat.

“You already do,” Adrian said.

“So I'm disguising them as a gift.”

“I can help eat them. I can't promise to conceal the motive.”

Her fingers loosened on the ribbon. A window shut upstairs, sending a gust through the porch. She held the basket against her skirt without moving inside.

“I used to order things far away,” she said. “An excuse to leave my desk. Then I'd forget to collect them. Shopkeepers reminded me more often than my supervisor did.”

“You remembered this time.”

“Someone will be going that way today.”

It was quieter than her usual invitation. Adrian asked whether she wanted to share the carriage down, collect her biscuits, and then let him and Elena visit the bookshop.

“I only get the first half?”

“And a basket of orange biscuits.”

She frowned theatrically before laughing. “Not terrible. I want the window on the way back.”

“You'll have to ask whoever's in the carriage.”

“I will. I'm even prepared to admit that witches get travel-sick.”

He left the basket safely inside the guesthouse. She did not take it to the workshop or use a promised afternoon to hurry the research. The stubborn knot waited in ordinary daylight.

The approved work was narrower than yesterday's. C-17 remained sealed beneath its glass cover in its original support. No further illumination. The new receiving plate would go into a separate enclosure to examine how its recorded trace decayed under a low-energy probe. Yesterday's authorization did not cover this. After reviewing the anomaly and removing the human observation position, the deputy inspector remaining at the tower, acting under the lead inspector’s commission, had approved this equipment check alone.

Adrian's scar was outside the optical path. The sealed December 10 plates had not been taken out.

Tessa showed the inspection clerk the white ceramic ring on the new rig. A restricted power cell supplied probe light through a fixed filter. Outside the enclosure, Luna would use a control ring to make one predetermined phase adjustment. Her magic drove an isolated control pattern; it was not intended to supply the receiving plate. The isolation had worked on comparable teaching plates. A mechanical master disconnect and backup discharge path remained available.

“If she lets go, the main path withdraws,” Tessa explained. “If she can't, this handle isolates the ring too.”

Adrian rehearsed without power. The handle had a definite resistance. At the end of its travel, a gray ceramic pin dropped into a padded channel.

Luna watched from behind the shield.

“You look prepared to drag me away from a very good date.”

“If the date starts smoking, yes.”

“Couldn't you first ask whether I'm creating atmosphere?”

“No.”

She pressed her lips together, failing to hide her smile.

Elena tapped the unpowered handle. “He answered. Don't turn a safety rehearsal into evidence of unsuccessful courtship.”

“Unsuccessful? He promised to take me away.”

“Outside,” Adrian clarified.

Cecilia moved a chair near the window. “There's water outside. And a doctor.”

“Your arrangements for romance are wonderfully thorough,” Luna sighed.

Even the clerk laughed, then finished recording the rehearsal. Luna did not skip her acknowledgment. Stop call, verbal confirmation, hands off, main supply disconnected, independent indicator checked. The sequence was unhurried. Adrian thought learning did not always have to require another injury.

He hoped today would prove it.

The first teaching plate behaved ordinarily: pale lines appeared and faded; the backup path did not respond. The blank control left no extra decay trace. Tessa waited for cooling before exchanging them. Meanwhile, Luna showed Adrian a drawing on scrap paper.

Not a circuit. A captain riding a gull, his hat larger than himself.

“Would this persuade Elena to read my book instead?”

“What would you write?”

“A handsome assistant finally realizes a witch deserves more company than a ship's log.”

“Does he get an opinion?”

“I'm researching it now.”

Adrian studied her deliberately casual expression and chose not to turn everything into another joke.

“You can simply ask me to spend time with you. You're interesting without a diagram in your hand.”

Her fingertip stopped on the gull's wing.

A trolley squeaked past the doorway. She used the distraction to fold the drawing and put her pen in her pocket rather than her hair.

“After lunch. Unless the captain abducts you first.”

“We could have tea before the bookshop.”

He did not promise her the whole afternoon. She did not complain that one cup was too little.

Elena heard them and continued checking pages. When he returned to his position, she left a wrapped mint beside his free hand.

“For me?”

“You said last night you wanted something less sweet.”

“You remembered.”

“I study you occasionally too. No lamp required.”

She turned away, leaving him a smiling profile. Looking at the mint, Adrian decided she deserved a proper answer. He could not keep letting the captain provide all his compliments.

The new receiving plate, D-17, went onto the rig last.

It was not C-17's lining, nor something removed from a person's body. Yesterday's optical tracing was difficult to see in ordinary light. Opaque guards now covered both sides of its enclosure, with narrow viewing openings for Luna and the clerk. Adrian faced away from the observation path, watching the independent status strip and Luna's face.

She put away the gull drawing and placed her right hand in the outer grip of the control ring. Its white ceramic isolation layer was intact. Main and backup paths had passed separate unloaded checks. When the administrator asked if everyone was ready, she looked at Adrian first, waited for his nod, and answered.

The first breath: pale blue on the status strip.

The second: a decay line narrower than the teaching plate's appeared along D-17's edge. Luna reported only color and position. She did not increase the probe light. The clerk recorded his observation independently.

The third: the scheduled probe ended.

The shutter closed; the main source withdrew. But the line did not contract in the order shown by the teaching plates. It divided. One branch ran toward the backup discharge channel. The other brightened along the reverse edge of the control pattern.

“Return light on the control side,” Luna said. “Stop.”

She released her grip immediately.

Adrian pressed the external stop handle.

The gray pin fell. The main-power indicator reached zero. The administrator disconnected the supply; the clerk stepped back. Nobody waited for an argument.

Luna's right hand did not quite leave the ring.

Her fingers were open, but the control pattern stretched between fingertip and rim like a taut transparent thread. She was not refusing to let go. Adrian saw tendons tighten on the back of her wrist. Beneath the ceramic isolation layer, a hairline seam began to shine.

Tessa swore softly and pointed at the independent residual-energy indicator. Main power was gone, but something remained accumulated on the control side. The teaching plates had never returned the small amount of adjusting magic at this phase. An unloaded check had not reproduced this condition.

“Don't pull her,” Tessa said sharply. “It's still connected.”

Adrian stopped outside the shield instead of reaching for Luna's hand. The backup discharge lever was descending. The seal over the sand channel changed color, yet the control-side indicator kept climbing.

Sweat appeared at Luna's temple.

“Discard the whole ring,” she said through tightened breath. “Sacrificial disconnect. Yellow handle.”

The last step they had rehearsed and hoped never to need. Breaking the ceramic neck would drop the receiving and control assembly into the closed dissipation box. The unfixed layer image would be destroyed with it. There would be no complete set of readings to examine afterward.

Adrian moved his right hand to the yellow lever.

Luna looked up. The eyes that had teased him about dragging a date outside had no time to arrange a charming expression.

The bright seam reached the side of her palm.

Adrian closed his hand around the lever.
