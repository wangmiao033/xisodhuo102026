# Chapter 292: They Didn't Leave Through the Same Door

Three people were missing from breakfast on May 27.

When Martha moved the empty chairs, Adrian suggested leaving them. For whom, she asked? He stopped. Luna had already arranged breakfast in her room. Cecilia had an early handover. Isabella's meeting started earlier than usual.

Martha took out the kitchen's little meal note and asked whether to keep lunch for him. He glanced at the chairs, then answered only for himself. The others' meals would go where each had already requested. The kitchen needn't wait for him to gather everyone before deciding whom to cook for. She recorded his arrangement without reducing the food to be delivered simply because breakfast attendance was smaller. Empty chairs didn't mean the people who normally occupied them no longer needed feeding.

Their absence wasn't a mistake he needed to repair.

Elena passed the jam. Was he reporting the chairs missing too?

“I'm learning not to promise things to furniture.”

She smiled and buttered her bread. Viola appeared, left arm still supported, carrying an ordinary sheet. She requested breakfast first. After half a slice, she asked Adrian for time to talk without rushing anywhere.

“Now, if you like. Where?”

“The courtyard. Not the route where you have to help me down every step.”

Elena didn't answer for him or hastily abandon her plate. She finished breakfast and went to her scheduled expenditure discussion with the family representative. Adrian returned his cup, then followed Viola through the level side entrance.

Her paper contained the civilian group's new arrangements. The district coordinator wanted two limited training sessions during her recovery. Actual transport and coordination remained with duty staff. She'd accepted those sessions, not the overnight watch somebody had automatically attached to her name.

“I want to do this properly. Not merely wait until my arm heals so I can return. But I didn't bring you out to approve a schedule.”

She folded the paper beside her.

“I love you. I've hoped the next time we sat together wouldn't be ten minutes before going our separate ways. That we'd already be sharing a life.”

Her right hand smoothed a crease in her skirt. This wasn't an oath. Rejection wouldn't become a requirement to train harder.

“I treasure time with you,” he said. “You've shown me protection can mean letting someone walk for themselves. But I love Elena and want to be her partner. I can't return the romance you want. I won't leave you waiting for a betrothal I don't intend to offer.”

Her hand stopped. Someone shook a rug near the gate. Dust drifted through sunlight.

“The old list you gave me...” She corrected herself. “That I gave you. You can keep it. I wanted you to have it. It wasn't a claim awaiting payment.”

“All right.”

“But no arranging our next tea for a while. Don't schedule it to prove nothing has changed.”

“I won't.”

Her eyes reddened. She didn't force a smile.

“I know you aren't judging who's more deserving. It still feels like losing.”

He didn't immediately deny that anyone had lost. She hadn't received the answer she wanted. His choice couldn't require her to surrender disappointment so it looked kinder.

“You can be unhappy. You can not want to see me. We can handle work contacts however suits you.”

“Ordinary work can be direct. I don't want three adjutants between us when I ask where a form is.”

She finally smiled a little. He smiled too, without erasing anything said.

Viola asked him to return first. She wanted to sit alone. He didn't stand guard beyond the door or circle to a window to check for tears. Her caregiver had an agreed meeting time. His concern needn't secretly become another protective order.

Before noon, she submitted her limited arrangement at the district office. Could they also count her for another understaffed session? She pointed to the care reassessment after the first two and declined the third. It wasn't impressive, but it preserved an actual day of her own.

Meanwhile, Luna had her assistant reopen a funding proposal she'd prepared to return.

It offered a new laboratory and two sets of equipment. All review questions would first pass through her, then the sponsor would decide whether results could be released. Questions weren't forbidden. Each simply faced a door difficult to bypass.

She'd once liked rooms only she could unlock. She still wanted the equipment: gauges she wouldn't have to compete to borrow, a chair that didn't hurt her back, wages paid on time.

“Could we accept first and change it gradually?” her assistant asked.

Beside her supported right palm lay the red-marked drawing. She didn't pretend indifference. Then she revised the reply, declining the existing terms and proposing independent review with publication of nonhazardous findings. The sponsor could refuse funding. Her plans would shrink. She couldn't swallow incompatible conditions first and explain necessity to later arrivals.

Her assistant marked the unsent acceptance void and substituted the approved reply. It went to the ordinary correspondence desk, not out as an announcement that a research institute now existed. Empty equipment entries stayed empty.

Adrian arrived with the historical-copy index approved for her comparison. Could he leave it? Yes. She didn't invite him to sit. The space where the travel book had stood remained empty. She hadn't hurried to fill it with another.

“Don't look so careful. Paper can't suffer heartbreak.”

He set the index away from her drawing. “Then I'll only ask whether you want to check it now.”

“My assistant will return it tomorrow morning. Today I still have to argue over a chair's price.”

He acknowledged that and left normally. Only after a while did she pick up her pen. Being hurt didn't turn the funding decision into a performance for him. Whoever she loved, this was how she wanted to work.

Cecilia's afternoon began at the meal-service site. The reception custodians asked her to help an ordinary visitor test their new explanation. She didn't reclaim a key. She listened as the visitor paused at the recusal instructions.

“If I'm complaining about you, I use this?”

“Yes.”

“Could you stop watching me practice, then?”

Surprised, she stood and waited next door, no longer watching the test. The visitor wasn't moved for her convenience. Afterward, reception staff supplied feedback without identifying details. Only then did she note confusing words and help the actual supervisor clarify them, preserving the revision record.

A Temple elder urged her to resume the old ceremonial seat. If she first declared the oversight problems resolved, further reform could be discussed internally. She chose to remain in the existing reception and charitable work, submitting concrete questions for the next reform discussion through its proper meeting.

She didn't proclaim herself a new leader or dismiss her previous faith. She still wished to pray. A beautiful service couldn't close the cases of people awaiting answers.

That evening, Adrian met her at the gate and asked whether work required more materials from him. No; the duty staff had acknowledged today's revisions.

“You needn't ask whether I'm all right,” she added. “I'll decide when to tell you.”

“All right.”

She shifted the empty basket. Yesterday's words still tightened her chest. Continuing to trust him didn't require pretending a day was enough. She said good night and went to her own room. No shared reading would demonstrate her generosity tonight.

On May 28, all five women finally met in one room. They weren't meeting about Adrian.

The borrowed rooms needed reallocation. Each brought her actual schedule for next month. They couldn't occupy every space merely because outsiders liked imagining them always together. Isabella first surrendered two entire days reserved for her, requesting only the shorter meetings she needed.

She'd submitted the maintenance-cost supplement to the sponsoring legislators. Formal consideration of the coercive provisions continued. Someone at court had proposed restoring a special route under her sole seal to accelerate funding. She'd refused in writing, keeping the unresolved issues within ordinary proposal procedures. Losing that shortcut meant listening this afternoon to a legislator who disliked her.

“Save me a little sympathy,” she said. “Not a permanently empty reception room.”

With her left hand, Luna submitted her short drawing-review period, requesting that it not start immediately after care. Viola chose a room near the level access route. Cecilia specified that visitors needn't pass through her private quarters. Their different needs weren't combined into one elegant arrangement for ladies.

Elena hadn't brought a promise that House Rosenberg would pay for everything. She and the representative had verified what she could personally authorize. A proposed private entertainment expense became a limited contribution toward ordinary equipment, with receipt and purpose separately recorded. The family's old accounts still followed the existing process. Giving up a dinner couldn't answer the questions her father owed.

She'd also rejected a suggestion that the representative retract her May 10 statement for her. Her letter to her father had gone out, its wording preserved. Tomorrow she'd answer the family questions she could answer, without asking Adrian to sit beside her and absorb uncomfortable silences.

“But I'd like you to be free when I return,” she told him.

She didn't lower her voice. Other people's disappointment wasn't a reason she must disappear. Adrian agreed to keep time available and confirmed when she expected to be back.

The scheduler copied one entry wrongly, marking Luna's rest as available. She corrected him immediately. An empty space wasn't a promise to wait indefinitely. He revised the entry and handed copies to the actual users, not only to Adrian for distribution to all the ladies.

They didn't leave together. Viola waited for her caregiver. Luna went to rest. Cecilia headed to reception; Isabella followed a clerk in another direction. Each goodbye was different. Nobody had to turn back and pose as five young ladies finally made happy.

Elena packed her thin folder last. Adrian was still beside the table.

“Don't look like that. As though choosing me means we must forever be happier than everyone else to justify it.”

“I don't regret choosing you. I hope I didn't make the words hurt more.”

“You took care with them. Keep respecting what they tell you. We don't have to hide guilt in every dinner.”

He nodded and offered his hand, waiting for her to take it.

“I love you. Not because you're the last person left in the room.”

Her eyes were a little red. Smiling, she pointed out that if he'd left first, she'd have gone to find him. Staying this time wasn't fate making their choice for them.

Outside, someone tried repairing the jammed sunshade. It opened with a small sound. Listening, Elena asked whether, after tomorrow's visit, they could discuss something belonging neither to her family nor the court.

“Our future?”

“Start with ordinary days. Maintenance budgets can't be all your love talk.”

He laughed and agreed. No rings today, no vows written in advance for anyone. She held his hand and chose her own direction through the door. Tomorrow's conversation no longer had to wait for everyone to stop hurting before it could begin.
