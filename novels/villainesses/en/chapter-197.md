# Chapter 197: One Less Chair Beside the Princess

Isabella returned the gold sash to its box.

On the morning of February 24, her attendant had already lifted it toward her. It was decoration, carrying no investigative authority. After considering it, she chose a smaller brooch.

“Someone is waiting to accuse me of using the royal family as a shield.”

Adrian held the single page she intended to take. They would recognize her without the sash, he observed.

“Yes. At least they can't complain the shield is blocking the words.”

The attendant smiled and fastened the brooch. Isabella's left wrist remained supported. With her right hand she turned over Baron Fenwick's letter. Its terms were clearer than before: if the public audience presented Adrian as a particular object of protection, Fenwick would withdraw his sponsorship of the next proposal on public answers.

No insult appeared. The paper overflowed with consideration for timing and public confidence.

“You can remove my name,” Adrian said.

“Do you want it removed, or do you want to spare me regret?”

He thought. “The second.”

“That's mine to deal with.”

She returned the letter to its envelope, not the wastebasket. Before leaving, she asked whether the baron would attend. Losing his support evidently wasn't an achievement she intended to celebrate.

The audience occupied a small hall in the city-affairs building. Rows of wooden chairs divided representatives from questioners, with a passage left clear at the back. Isabella had one registered speaking period. The chair reminded her that a bell would mark its end; others needed time too.

Adrian sat among ordinary observers. His name was checked at the entrance, and he was admitted. Several heads turned. One person shifted a chair. He gathered his coat clear of the aisle.

Fenwick sat at the front. He bowed to Isabella and nodded to Adrian with impeccable courtesy. Another chair stood beside the table for a joint proposer. The baron kept his papers on his knees instead of moving toward it.

The first speakers discussed drainage and stalls near the bridge. A seller of secondhand cloth complained that rain ran through new paving into her boxes. The works representative asked for the location without promising an immediate repair. Adrian's impatience for Isabella to begin hadn't made somebody else's wet cloth unimportant.

At her turn, she unfolded the repeatedly corrected speech with her right hand.

“I support continued examination of Adrian Reed's conduct and its consequences in the historical record. I support preserving testimony unfavorable to him.”

Whispering briefly ceased.

“I also oppose denying anyone ordinary access to travel, submission of requests, and answers solely on the basis of that circular. In my own capacity, I support his access to appropriate legal assistance. Concrete danger must be addressed through facts and measures put forward by those responsible, subject to examination. My name cannot exempt him from investigation or order others to stop it.”

Fenwick closed his folder.

A representative immediately asked whether she guaranteed that no catastrophe would occur in future.

She couldn't.

“Then why protect him?”

“You cannot guarantee that everyone you receive will never do wrong. Protection against unsupported restrictions shouldn't require that guarantee first.”

Did she consider the old final account entirely fraudulent?

She couldn't establish that either. Yesterday's independent technical examination had identified gaps in the equipment record inspected. It couldn't supply continuous historical imagery. It hadn't declared an entire old world nonexistent or issued anyone a certificate of innocence.

Harder to hear than I believe him. A listener lowered his pen in disappointment, denied the magnificent declaration he'd apparently come to copy.

A middle-aged man stood. He worked near the coach station; his children passed it daily. He didn't understand fragments and records. Who would consider them first if danger was real?

Isabella didn't demand faith in royalty.

“Submit specific concerns to the responsible office. An object, an act, or a threat today can be inspected by the appropriate people. Historical attribution must also be examined. I object to pinning a named warning to a person and letting everyone think that avoiding him has dealt with every danger.”

Still dissatisfied, he asked what happened if the office didn't answer.

She indicated her draft. This was why it sought identification of responsible departments and reasons for unanswered requests. The report on the limited trial didn't mean every district already had a common arrangement. Further work needed support.

Fenwick rose.

“Your Highness has tied a general proposal to a highly contentious individual case. I cannot put my name behind that political judgment.”

“You can oppose my position on the case and still support ordinary people receiving answers.”

“The public won't distinguish.”

Her right hand stopped on the paper. She knew that familiar sentence. She had used it. Now he returned it gently, almost consolingly.

“Then make the distinction in your own explanation.”

He offered no answer. Instead, he submitted a brief withdrawal of sponsorship and requested that it enter the audience record. The chair acknowledged it. Without the promised joint support, the draft would need to satisfy submission requirements anew. It could not enter the next agenda as arranged.

An attendant removed the name card reserved for the co-sponsor. The empty chair remained, its clean wood guilty of nothing.

Behind Adrian, someone asked why she would do this for a servant. Another remarked that at least the princess had made herself clear. Neither voice reversed the withdrawal.

The chair asked whether Adrian would answer a question concerning himself. He first requested its scope. Someone wanted to know whether he was undergoing a potentially anomalous experiment today.

He stood and said no. The latest authorized technical review hadn't involved connecting a person to equipment either. Private medical details weren't part of today's discussion. Then he sat, leaving the remaining time intact rather than delivering a speech of confession or gratitude.

When the bell rang, Isabella stopped. Unfinished questioners protested. She asked them to leave their questions and promised answers within what she could properly address. Her attendants were not to remove objections unrecorded. Descending from the platform, she gripped a deeply creased corner of her speech.

Fenwick waited beside the door. It wasn't too late, he murmured. Change protection to concern, and they could discuss the proposal again.

“How many people are already concerned?” she asked.

“Wording can leave room.”

“It can also leave the person outside without anywhere to go.”

He sighed, bowed, and left. She didn't call him back.

Before she departed, a steward from that evening's small aristocratic dinner arrived. Seating arrangements had changed; regrettably, they could no longer receive her. He spoke as though the dining table had unexpectedly lost a leg.

Isabella confirmed that this particular invitation was being canceled. Then she returned it, refusing to rewrite the event as her own illness.

In the outer gallery she stopped, looking at the rain-polished steps.

“I wanted to tear up that invitation.”

“You've returned it.”

“So I'm denied even the pleasure of shredding paper.”

Her laugh was brief. She fumbled with her wrist support, allowing an attendant to adjust it. Then she requested a copy of the withdrawal. Her draft would remain; other support must be sought through ordinary channels. She couldn't order fresh names to appear today.

An older attendant reminded her that Fenwick had also promised introductions to two representatives willing to consider the draft. They probably wouldn't come now. He sounded almost guilty for mentioning it.

“Have they refused individually?” she asked.

“No. They were waiting for the baron's arrangements.”

“Then ask them separately whether they'll meet me. Don't refuse on their behalf. If they decline, bring their own words back.”

He relaxed a little. She added that he needn't report today's event as an unqualified success. After years in her service, he knew what a pleasing report sounded like. He also knew how much she'd once preferred it.

Adrian watched him collect the papers. She'd lost more than a dinner. Every door the baron would have opened now needed knocking on again. There might be nobody willing to answer.

She raised her chin at his expression.

“I still remember how to knock.”

“I'm worried about the door.”

“No need. My left hand is forbidden political activity today.”

Adrian waited beside her until the last audience member descended.

“Thank you for saying my name today.”

“Don't say you'll repay me.”

“I wasn't going to.”

“I know. I'm reminding myself not to wait for it.”

That hurt more than a performance of generosity. She'd once folded every favor into future leverage. She could still feel the absence of things removed from her hand.

“You can tell me how angry you are.”

She looked up and genuinely did, in several sentences unsuitable for the official minutes. He laughed at the last one. She laughed too, then forbade him to produce a publicly respectable version.

Lunch was later than expected. They bought hot pies at a street stall before the attendant found a sufficiently dignified dining room. Isabella held hers in her right hand. Its crust chose the worst possible place to split.

Adrian offered the untouched half of his own. She compared the neat portion with her collapsed one and accepted the exchange.

“At least this half has no disputed inheritance.”

“It has onion.”

“Easier to manage than certain relatives.”

They ate beneath an awning out of the wind. She didn't request a replacement dinner or call in their unfinished chess game as payment. Before leaving, she said she wanted some time alone. Adrian agreed, letting her own attendants accompany her home.

That evening the independent receiving office delivered a letter for Adrian alone to open. An applicant had requested a confidential meeting, offering to submit to verification concerning medical evacuation through the old west passage. Permission to retrieve independently sealed material from years earlier accompanied the request.

The notification didn't certify the account. Name and residence remained on a sealed sheet. The applicant consented to a first meeting the next morning with Adrian, Cecilia, and an independent examiner. An old medical wrist tag had also been voluntarily placed in custody. Its layered interior bore a subsidiary registration number that could be compared with transfer records. Authentication remained unfinished.

Adrian reread the date: February 25.

Elena arrived with her own new acknowledgment slip and saw the notification open before him. Did he want her to come? He explained the applicant's chosen attendance. She nodded and placed her receipt where it belonged, without making tonight hold every explanation the unknown might demand.

Cecilia's confirmation followed. She would attend. She added only that she remembered someone asking for the address two days earlier, but couldn't yet say it was the same person.

Adrian wrote his acceptance without supplying any answers in advance. He didn't ask Luna to reopen an experiment or send the letter to people waiting for news.

Night settled beyond the window. The street circular was still there.

Tomorrow, someone was willing to sit across a table and speak beyond what the paper said.
