# Chapter 123: The Princess Doesn't Have the Key

On New Year's Day, the royal archive was colder than the Rosenberg breakfast room.

Adrian Reed breathed out a small white cloud and developed a very practical appreciation for the funding he had refused the night before. If part of it could have been converted into a stove, the offer would have been tempting indeed.

“Sudden heat damages the originals,” said the man inside. “People may warm themselves next door.”

Julian Hartwell took a key from his belt. The keeper of the west wing's historical records had gray hair and an impeccably fastened collar. Princess Isabella Aurelian stood beside him holding an access authorization.

She did not have the key.

That distinction helped Adrian relax.

“Disappointed?” she asked, catching his glance. “Were you expecting a secret door?”

“I was hoping there might be a fireplace behind it.”

“Occasionally the Crown disappoints its subjects.”

“Today the fireplace can take responsibility,” Elena Rosenberg said.

Hartwell did not smile. He set the authorization on the outer desk and asked them to check the names and date. They could consult specified maintenance, transfer, and preservation records for historical equipment. Approved passages could be copied; originals could not leave. Private identity records concerning other people remained restricted. Any conservation examination required a separate professional arrangement.

Adrian left his working copies from East Ridge on the designated table, apart from the royal material.

He had told the others about the meeting when they returned last night. On learning that the political betrothal had been refused, Luna had first asked how much ordinary archive access remained, then how large the apple pastry had been. This morning she was resting at the house, Cecilia had gone to her assistance office, and Viola was having her left arm examined.

They were not a group incapable of taking a step unless every member came along. Nor did an invitation from a princess oblige everyone to attend.

Hartwell opened the cabinet and removed an archive box in an undyed cloth cover.

Its label read: West Wing Transfer Dossier W-24.

“This was already in the royal collection,” he said. “It is not a story Her Highness's staff assembled for you last night.”

Isabella's eyebrow moved. “You could omit the second part, Mr. Hartwell.”

“Then I would have to explain it three more times.”

Adrian found himself liking the keeper.

The old leather spine rasped against its padded support. The cover was dark with wear, with a later cloth repair along one corner. Isabella placed an approved catalog extract beside it without touching the original.

“I found the summary while reviewing expenditure on old projects. Four years ago, the Crown accepted maintenance papers and associated material from decommissioned equipment. One item is summarized as ‘Overhaul completed; cleared for renewed use.’”

“You think it relates to the warning we found at East Ridge?”

“I think it is worth checking. One receipt here is close in date to the transfer of that maintenance paper. The summary also mentions an insulating lining.”

She did not claim it was the same device. Elena did not lower her pencil in appreciation of that restraint, either.

Hartwell supported the cover with a thin board. A comparatively pale catalog slip was pasted inside the cover, outside the numbered text leaves. Beneath a royal archive classification stamp were the words Isabella had quoted.

The first actual text leaf was yellower, its creases pressed flat.

Received for conservation.

Not quite the same promise.

“Receiving something for repairs doesn't mean it's ready to use,” Adrian said.

“There may be an acceptance certificate farther in,” Isabella replied.

“Then let's keep looking.”

He did not mistake his first uneasiness for a victory. The princess did not urge the keeper to skip ahead to a favored page.

A following sheet listed ordinary expenses: cloth covers, drying paper, shipping crates. No person's name appeared among the materials. There was no ready-made answer about Adrian's former life.

He found those unremarkable purchases unexpectedly reassuring. At least this money had bought things someone could touch.

“Two crates, but only one wrapper entered into storage?” Elena asked.

“One crate held documents.” Hartwell indicated a note on the reverse. “The other contained a fitted support. That does not mean it also held an entire device. The units matter.”

“Please include the reverse in our copying permission.”

Isabella glanced at her. “You are very accustomed to finding what I have overlooked.”

“I look for what Adrian overlooks, too.”

“Yesterday she found some bread I hadn't finished.”

Elena gave him a look. “There was none left today.”

The princess watched them for a moment. Nothing in last night's proposal had accounted for a conversation that fitted together without either party arranging it beforehand.

She looked back at the dossier.

On the next expenditure sheet Adrian recognized the term clearing payment. The reference was not one they had previously investigated, however, and the paying office differed. He drew a line beside his note rather than joining two payments because they shared a word.

“You aren't marking that as a connection?” Isabella asked.

“As something to verify. If every clearing payment belongs to the same conspiracy, we'll have to arrest the capital's bakers.”

Hartwell finally chuckled.

Elena slid her notes closer. She had copied the unit, date, and page location, leaving space at the right. Adrian crowded Payment source unverified into it. She shifted the paper toward him again.

“Slow down,” she murmured. “You don't have to prove your usefulness before she finishes asking.”

His pencil stopped.

Isabella was studying the approved catalog and had not heard. Adrian realized that he had been hurrying to answer, as though refusing last night obliged him to demonstrate today that they still deserved the invitation.

He took a breath and rewrote the last word legibly.

“You don't always have to find my problems for me.”

“I enjoy it. Particularly when you're preparing to pretend there aren't any.”

He looked up. She held his gaze. After a moment, they returned the papers to their proper places.

Halfway through the dossier, an object entry appeared: Royal Archive Empty Contract Wrapper K-9. Stored separately with the papers. Inner lining must not be removed.

Adrian's breath caught.

Hartwell pointed to a fitted box in a side cabinet. Its label matched the entry; storage locations and previous moves had their own register.

It was not C-17. That object remained at the tower. And another box in the palace could not bring back the lost readable image from D-17.

“Today you may look through the cover,” the keeper said. “Anything more will depend on a conservator's assessment.”

Under the glass lay a dull, thin wrapper with a gray-white repair strip at one edge. No writing rose from it. No light answered the scar beneath Adrian's glove.

Isabella waited across the case.

“What does it remind you of?”

“That we need someone who understands old linings.”

She had evidently expected another answer. “I can request access for Miss Ashcroft.”

“Let her see the scope first. Her hand hasn't healed.”

“I remember.”

She asked Hartwell for an application for ordinary optical observation. He corrected her: it would be a conservation examination, not an opportunity for visitors to conduct experiments.

Her mouth tightened, but she did not use her title to alter the form.

After an hour, Hartwell closed the inner cover and announced a break.

“Two more pages,” Isabella said.

“You may read two more pages after the break.”

Adrian looked down to put away his pencil. Laughing too visibly might cost him access to the warm room as well.

There was hot water next door, and a basket of bread. Isabella considered ordering lunch. Hartwell reminded her that a second consultation was scheduled that afternoon, and a formal dining table would require moving half the boxes.

The princess sat at the small ordinary table and regarded the unevenly cut bread.

“This is a break during official business,” she said first.

Adrian nearly choked on his drink.

“Thank you for clarifying.”

“Before you ask the chef to certify its purpose.”

Elena gave Adrian the chair closest to the fire. He moved it sideways so both of them could warm their hands.

Isabella looked at the chair legs.

“Is there always something wrong with the seating we provide?”

“This time we made the improvement ourselves.”

Only after speaking did he notice Elena's smile behind her cup. She did not move his chair away.

The silver knife was considerably finer than the bread. When Isabella cut her first piece, a bit of hard crust shot across the table. She paused as though it had committed an offense requiring inquiry.

Without comment, Adrian turned his own bread over and cut from the softer side.

She copied him. The second piece came away neatly.

“You saw that?” she asked.

“I saw you successfully acquire lunch.”

“Is this how the two of you spare each other's dignity?”

“Sometimes he makes no effort at all,” Elena said.

“Only with you.”

He recognized the ambiguity too late. Her ears slowly reddened.

The princess did not interrupt. She took a quiet bite of bread.

For a moment she did not have to declare a winner, and the table did not empty because she had stopped directing the conversation. That kind of quiet appeared unfamiliar to her.

The spread was plain apple jam. Isabella seemed to find it acceptable. She began explaining the resistance to reopening the old projects: some officials considered them closed and feared an exercise in assigning blame. Others disliked allowing a candidate for the throne to build a reputation with historical records.

“You do want to build your reputation,” Elena said.

“I want support that can accomplish something. At the preliminary succession council on January twelfth, we will begin by discussing which royal affairs the candidates should oversee. Demonstrating that I can handle an old project would give me something to negotiate with beyond my ancestry.”

Adrian set down his cup. The box carried another kind of weight for her.

“What if it proves the project was never properly cleared?”

“Then I must decide how to explain that.”

“Starting with the fact that it wasn't cleared would help.”

Her gaze cooled. “You leave people very few pleasant choices.”

“I'd rather not watch you take a pleasant sentence into a room where someone else has the originals.”

She did not answer immediately. Outside, a servant carried in more wood. The fire was finally beginning to thrive.

That afternoon Hartwell recorded the new opening time. Instead of starting with the text, Elena asked him to place the earliest receipt beside the current contents list.

The receipt from four years earlier recorded twelve leaves of text, with one empty wrapper as a separate attachment.

The present list said fourteen original leaves; attachments complete.

“Two pages added later?” Adrian asked.

“Possibly,” said Hartwell. “Or an omission in the old receipt. We need the rebinding record.”

Elena did not call the file a fake. She studied the edges, then pointed on her permitted copy rather than touching the original.

“The thirteenth and fourteenth leaves are narrower. Their old sewing holes don't quite match, either.”

Isabella leaned closer, the gold chain at her shoulder giving a soft chime.

“Different paper doesn't make the contents false.”

“No,” Elena said. “Nor does it prove they were always together.”

For the first time, the keeper's expression lost its routine calm. He checked the register and found a rebinding date two years after the original receipt.

Adrian looked at the polished conclusion inside the cover. Suddenly the chill did not seem to come from the door.

Every sheet the princess had shown them might be genuine.

But someone had already decided the order in which those genuine sheets would speak.
