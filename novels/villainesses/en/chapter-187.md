# Chapter 187: Wait Another Moment

There was no clock in the room that afternoon.

Adrian noticed immediately. Not to make him lose time, Luna explained, but to avoid suggesting the bells they'd just investigated. An independent timer enforced the limit, outside his view and visible to the physician.

The table held a positioning frame, a developing sheet behind a transparent partition, and a shallow tray. No old originals or sealed cartridge. The room was too ordinary for the brave expression he'd rehearsed.

Luna sat diagonally behind him, her right palm padded. Noticing his glance at the tray, she said there'd be no pear cards today. They shouldn't turn another experiment into a snack investigation.

His laugh was brief but genuine. He had expected the room to announce the importance of what they were about to try. Instead, there was an uncomfortable chair, a familiar voice, and equipment whose ordinary arrangement gave him nowhere to hide behind a dramatic occasion.

The physician asked about food, headaches, and medication affecting judgment. Adrian answered honestly, adding nervousness. He nearly minimized it, then admitted he was more frightened than this morning.

Nobody tore up his consent or said fear meant getting it over with faster.

Beatrice repeated the choices: blank positioning only, interruption after starting, no obligation to answer unwanted questions. Technical records didn't authorize distributing his account, much less publishing it.

He listened through the choices without interrupting to assure everyone he understood. Wanting to get to the beginning quickly was itself something he needed to notice. When the reading ended, the alternatives still felt like things he could actually choose, not words recited over a decision already made for him.

He chose the single limited attempt. He genuinely wanted to know, not simply prove endurance.

They rehearsed stopping. The technician disconnected the supply for inspection; Adrian dropped the card. Its small sound stopped everyone in the room.

“You almost apologized,” Luna said.

“I didn't.”

“Save the words for complaining about my chair afterward.”

Her chair was uncomfortable too. Rather than prove fairness through pain, she requested another cushion. Those two minutes weren't charged against him as delay.

Before beginning, he looked at the door.

Elena waited outside without peering in or sending a final message that would make stopping harder. He removed his hand from his pocket and positioned it. The crescent scar didn't shine.

At the lowest setting, the supply indicator slowly changed color. Nobody recited the old record, sounded bells, or asked whether he could see the western passage.

Initially he felt cold, like a hand near winter glass. An incomplete gray line appeared on the sheet. The technician recorded at agreed intervals without announcing success.

Adrian worried nothing would happen. He nearly pictured the maps to help. As the impulse arose, he admitted it.

“Don't do the device's work,” Luna said. “If you think of today's maps, report that too.”

Then the room's sounds seemed to step away.

First came not an image but wet ash.

No fire appeared here. Yet the smell pressed against his tongue, cool and choking, like rain on something newly extinguished. Before he could explain, his palm felt something other than the table.

A ridged brass handle.

His knuckles strained. The skin looked dirtier; a dark scrape crossed the edge of his palm. His view was narrow: part of the handle, a dim edge beneath. No whole apparatus, no location, no date written for him. There was too little to understand and too much to dismiss as an empty moment. The ridges against his palm seemed definite in a way the surrounding world was not. He could not turn that vividness into knowledge of what lay beyond the edge.

Somebody outside his view said, “The west passage isn't clear yet.”

Not a voice in this room. He couldn't identify the speaker or distance. Other noises pressed around it, less distinct.

He heard himself answer.

“Wait another moment.”

He had wanted, before this, to find an explanation that would make his own voice easy to recognize as right. The words offered no such comfort. They did not explain what he was trying to achieve, only that someone had warned him and he had answered.

Not a heroic shout. Frighteningly ordinary, as though he'd believed it a reasonable instruction.

The handle sank perhaps half an inch beneath his palm.

He wanted to know why. To widen the view, see the speaker, his other hand, what followed. Nausea rose. The chair back seemed far away.

“Stop.”

He released the card too.

A tap.

The gray line vanished as the sheet darkened. The supply was already disconnected. The physician came alongside without holding his left hand in position. Beatrice said it was over.

Shoes. Workroom floor. Blank card in its tray. He knew he was seated; several breaths passed before he believed nobody waited for him to keep something pressed down.

Could he hear his name? Yes. Water? He nodded. The cup was placed within reach, not forced into his trembling cold hand.

The cup stayed where he could reach it when ready. That small absence of pressure mattered while he was still trying to locate himself in the chair. He watched the water until its ordinary surface became easier to believe than the movement of the handle.

Luna didn't ask whom he'd seen. Pale herself, she didn't turn his fright into her need. He could recover before choosing what to disclose.

Eventually he said, “Brass handle.”

Did he want that recorded?

Yes. Beatrice requested only what he'd experienced, not a complete story.

Wet ash. Narrow view. Handle. An unidentified voice. The west passage isn't clear yet. His own Wait another moment. A small downward movement.

At the last phrase, tears came. Not understanding everything: recognizing the ordinary tone of someone who thought he still controlled the situation.

He reached to wipe his eyes. The physician offered clean cloth instead of rubbing. Nobody recorded tears as confession.

“I made them wait.”

“In the fragment you experienced, you heard yourself say that,” Beatrice replied. “We don't know the whole sequence.”

“I can't pretend I didn't hear it.”

“You needn't.”

Luna explained that the developing sheet had registered a brief change. It couldn't establish the words' historical source or complete his missing view. She'd record sequence, not call a gray line the shape of a world's destruction.

He nearly requested another attempt, then saw the card. Today's attempt was finished. Five minutes wouldn't convert nausea and trembling into permission.

Observation continued until he could drink steadily. No collapse, vanished scar, or awakening into full understanding. Just a cold room and gradually feeling the floor beneath his feet again.

Beatrice read the account back. Quotation marks enclosed what he'd said; source, date, and consequences remained unknown. Adrian corrected Pressed down the apparatus: he'd only seen a handle move, not the whole thing stop. His own testimony needed that uncomfortable narrowing too.

The assistant packed the equipment. The old cartridge remained untouched; the new sheet was separately sealed. Luna didn't ask him to stay and reassure her that the trial had been good enough.

“When you said stop, they stopped.”

He looked at the tray and nodded. Not compensation for an old instruction to wait, but something real today.

The physician accompanied him to the door. Elena rose, asking whether she could approach. Yes, but he didn't want an embrace yet. She stayed an arm's length away.

“Something appeared.”

Her hands tightened, then relaxed. “Do you want to tell me now?”

“A little.”

He shared the warning and his answer, not the entire record or a demand for acquittal. She didn't insist it must be false.

“I'll walk out with you. If you still want.”

Yes.

At the corridor's end someone washed a window. Water ran down the glass. Adrian waited for the physician to confirm he could continue instead of forcing himself onward because people could see him. Elena stood beside him without counting the remaining steps.

In the carriage he smelled ordinary mint from her pocket, not ash. He asked for some. She passed the packet. Coolness didn't erase the fragment, only separated now from then a little.

At home he wanted solitude. Should Elena save dinner? Yes, but she needn't keep waiting. She went to her own work without leaving someone outside his door.

He washed his hands. No dark scrape marked his palm. He almost scrubbed harder, as though invisible dirt could be removed from his chest. Then stopped and replaced the towel. This wasn't grime on today's skin.

Yesterday's map lay on the desk. He didn't seek the narrow doorway. Every line could too easily enter the fragment now and seem like additional recollection. He covered it with an empty notebook and poured warm water.

Martha asked outside whether he wanted soup.

“Wait a moment.”

He froze at his own words.

She heard nothing terrible, agreeing to set the tray aside. Her retreating footsteps reminded him this request wasn't the one beside the handle. Punishing every ordinary phrase would stop him asking anyone to wait without explaining why people had waited then.

Opening the door, he said he was ready; he'd needed to clear space. She glanced at the empty desk without exposing his clumsiness, asking where he wanted the tray. He moved the cup and made real space.

Halfway through, Elena knocked. Would he like ten minutes' company? Yes, without further trial discussion. She sat by the window and described mislabeled saplings; the gardener insisted on waiting for leaves before naming them.

He didn't make that another parable. They debated whether the kitchen would notice misplanted herbs first. At ten minutes she offered to leave. He wanted longer, but she needn't stay if busy. She wasn't, and stayed.

No kiss. Silence wasn't a decline in their relationship. He finished lukewarm soup without smelling nonexistent wet ash in every spoonful.

That evening he put his retained personal account in a drawer without labeling it Truth. The handle's purpose, speaker's identity, and world's subsequent fate remained unknown.

The drawer closed on an account that was neither nothing nor everything. He had corrected its wording himself, even though each correction seemed to take away the certainty he had gone there to find. The remaining words still hurt. Their limits did not make them painless.

He could no longer merely hope every unfavorable word would prove somebody else's copying error.

He left room for the agreed medical follow-up. Walking home didn't mean unaffected. His head felt heavy; tonight he wouldn't investigate further. Stopping meant actually stopping afterward too. Nobody needed to win him that right again.

There would be time to decide what he owed that fragment, without inventing missing consequences tonight or asking someone else to make the choice harmless for him.

Tomorrow's response to Wait another moment couldn't be decided by today's fright.
