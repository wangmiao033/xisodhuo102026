# Chapter 115: A Marriage Contract Cannot Hold Every Debt

On December 23, Luna Ashcroft lined up three empty cups at breakfast.

Adrian Reed reached for the nearest. She held its rim with a left fingertip.

“Borrowing it.”

“What for?”

“You'll know when I work out how to explain.”

He looked from her bandaged right hand to the water jug. Following his gaze, she immediately pushed the jug away.

“No magic. No plans to pour water over you.”

“You were also the person who said we'd only look last time.”

Instead of snapping back, she paused.

“That's why I'm asking first. May I borrow three cups until breakfast ends?”

“They're the proprietor's,” Adrian said, smiling. “Ask him.”

The proprietor agreed readily enough. He had only one condition: she was not to include his sugar bowl among the objects taking part in her experiment. Luna assured him that breakfast law still protected it. Viola Blackwood murmured to Cecilia Saintclair that Luna seemed to be naming crockery again.

Luna heard but didn't object. Recovering her teasing was easier than recovering her hand. At least the former required no bandages removed as proof.

At the tribunal, the old load tables awaited them. They came from technical appendices retained with disposal inventories at East Ridge's church equipment repository. No secret original had flown overnight from the capital. Yesterday's application had secured local access. Page numbers, transfer records, and borrowing receipts were verifiable. The originals stayed at the registry; the group used copies approved there.

The tables described bench tests of old, double-layered blank media. Dimensions, thickness, and coating batches differed. External energy plates and mechanical loads had been used. There were no names or records of experiments involving living oath-bearers.

Luna read the fracture notes, then returned to the first column.

“We can't apply these directly to C-17.”

“The external specifications are similar,” the academy representative observed.

“Similar isn't identical. We don't know about internal repairs or deterioration.”

She requested the headings before reading the attractive maximum figures. Adrian noticed that one appendix required division by effective material area, whereas another had already converted its figures. Copying only the totals suggested a difference under ten percent. Recalculation using the marginal notes revealed a much wider gap.

“I nearly missed that,” he admitted.

“The units are on the back.” Luna sighed. “I've done that myself. Assumed everyone remembered what I was thinking on the previous page.”

She requested that the comparison include the units. The recorder held the original to the window, verified that the reverse carried no recent addition, and asked both sides to check the pagination. Nobody announced a spectacular conspiracy. An omission that could have misdirected them was restored where it belonged.

Luna kept the tables and set the December 18 fracture drawings alongside them. The independent restorer distinguished heat damage to the control base from mechanical fracture of the ceramic neck. Both couldn't be blamed on the final pull of the lever. The old tables likewise recorded control-side damage before the main-source reading reached the rated value.

“This is what I wanted.” She circled an entry left-handed. “A small individual input doesn't mean a small total remaining inside.”

Adrian read a test series. Initially, equal inputs caused no fractures. Later, a smaller input caused failure. Discharge intervals had been shortened. An inconspicuous mark beside the entry indicated withdrawal from service.

“The previous load hadn't fully discharged?”

“That's the recorder's explanation. The closed side circuit found during independent examination makes comparison worthwhile. It doesn't establish identical accidents.”

She drew three circles for her cup analogy. The second went crooked. Adrian resisted correcting it until she asked him to add a horizontal line.

“So that's why you confiscated the cups.”

“A very polite confiscation.”

“Which produced no tea.”

Luna extended the line beyond the circles.

“Each medium has a carrying capacity. Dividing a load can change what each receives, but can't make the original load disappear. Once the receiving side is full, pushing more into it may cause damage faster.”

Cecilia studied the circles. “So even a voluntary marriage contract can't solve everything by handing all the danger to one person.”

“These records certainly offer no support for that. And cups don't feel pain. We cannot call the fracture threshold of a blank material a person's safe limit.”

Adrian looked at the interconnected circles. He'd once imagined a marriage contract as a game shortcut: choose the correct option and move the bad ending off the map. The paper offered a plain truth. Anything moved away still had to arrive somewhere.

“What if I'm willing to bear it?”

Elena Rosenberg's hand stopped. Luna looked up.

“I mean,” he continued, “does willingness alone tell me what I'm actually taking on?”

Luna slowly shook her head.

“No.”

“Then don't write me into the solution.”

For a moment, everyone around the table fell quiet. Cecilia lifted the hand she had been resting on an open page. She let Adrian say the sentence in full to the recorder himself.

The church advocate suggested that the tables might establish only inadequate historical materials, rather than limits inherent in vows. Luna acknowledged that material limits weren't a universal limit on every mechanism. But actual damage had occurred. No claim of unlimited capacity could rest on an unsupported miracle of materials.

“Are you admitting your research may have no use?”

“I'm admitting it can't provide you with a guaranteed unbreakable husband.”

Adrian nearly choked on his water. The recorder raised his pen and waited for an official version.

Luna's ears reddened.

“The current results cannot support any specific proposal for transferring risk to a human body.”

Elena moved Adrian's water somewhere he wouldn't knock it over. Her face was solemn. Her eyes weren't.

The afternoon discussion ended slowly. The old tables had not supplied the answer any of them had hoped to find. They had, however, drawn boundaries around several guesses that could otherwise have become dangerous. Continued prohibition of self-powered operation and unassessed bodily connections entered the tribunal's recommendations. Final decisions awaited the remaining technical and custody evidence.

Luna offered no objection to those recommendations. As she signed her name with her left hand, the point of her pen stopped against the paper twice.

Afterward, she asked to sit in the covered courtyard at the street corner. Her dressing change was later; the short walk fell within her medical allowance. Elena asked if she wanted company. When Luna said she wanted quiet first, Elena took the others to buy hot drinks.

Adrian stayed to carry her paper bag. Luna watched the opposite window until children chasing a wooden hoop passed the corner and their laughter faded.

“Did you hear that person outside?”

“Which one?”

“Brown coat. Collecting a certificate. Said the tower kept dangerous witches, and now they'd finally burned themselves.”

Adrian hadn't heard. He said so. He didn't pretend one person's kindness could instantly erase a stranger's malice.

Luna gripped her skirt, released it, and gripped again.

“I thought I didn't care about that word anymore. Wear a pointed hat. Deliberately smile like someone wicked. Call everyone cowards before they can say they're afraid. At least it looks like my choice.”

She looked at her bandage.

“But people nearly got hurt because of me that day. You were the one who pulled the handle. Tessa Quinn was standing right beside us. Now saying not to fear me sounds like pleading, even to me.”

Wind rustled the bag. Adrian held it steady without touching her injured hand.

“The design needs investigating. Your old conclusion needs correcting. Someone saying witch outside a door doesn't settle either question.”

“Aren't you afraid?”

“Yes. When the control-side light stayed on, I was terrified.”

Luna lowered her eyes. He paused before continuing.

“Now you're willing to stop, and to write down what you don't know. That gives me a reason to keep investigating with you. Not because you'll never make mistakes.”

Her lips moved.

“And not because you like me?”

It was quieter than any of her teasing had ever been.

Adrian looked at her without pretending to misunderstand.

“I care about you. I trust the changes you're making. But I can't turn that support into a promise of romance.”

The smile in her eyes stopped, then slowly returned.

“You choose such unromantic moments to be honest.”

“I'm afraid making it romantic would hurt you more later.”

She rubbed her nose left-handed, her voice lowering.

“Then let's leave it there today. You don't have to make everything sound nice.”

He nodded. They sat quietly until Elena arrived with the drinks. She didn't ask what they'd discussed. She placed a cup on Luna's left and reminded her to test its temperature.

“He can be rather foolish,” Elena said.

Luna looked up.

“But not always.”

The witch studied her for a moment. At last, she laughed aloud. “Does that count as a recommendation?”

“My own assessment. You can make yours.”

Cecilia returned with a paper parcel. “My assessment would be longer,” she added earnestly. “But the hot chestnuts come first.”

Viola handed Luna a peeled chestnut without proceeding to peel everyone's. Her own still steamed.

Back at the guesthouse, Luna had her dressing changed and rested as instructed. Only toward evening did she open the authorized copies. Adrian sat opposite, flattening an appendix.

Elena brought a lamp and removed a loose thread from his sleeve. Turning toward her, he quietly asked whether she needed rest too.

“Yes. But I'd like to finish my tea.”

“Shall I join you?”

She glanced from the papers to Luna. The witch immediately drew the appendix closer.

“I won't forget how to read because you leave for fifteen minutes. Unless you're taking the lamp on your date.”

Elena sat, placing her cup between them. Adrian didn't hurry to explain the courtyard conversation or offer Luna's vulnerability as reassurance. He only said that hearing the word bear today had shown him how often he'd rushed to decide without first asking what it meant.

“Then ask first next time,” Elena said.

“You too.”

She nodded. Her hand paused beside the cup, then gently touched the back of his.

Behind the load tables, another line recorded the destination of old equipment. After those tests ended, their accompanying load-limiting device had gone to the eastern warehouse of East Ridge's church equipment repository. Its transfer number linked to the local disposal inventory. It wasn't C-17, nor was there evidence it had ever connected to that casing. It might, however, preserve the structure once used to handle return flow.

Adrian followed the number to a note.

“No destruction record.”

Luna leaned in. Her eyes brightened, but she didn't propose collecting it that night.

“We'll apply for an inspection tomorrow. No activation. Only the casing we're legally permitted to open, the identifying number, and maintenance attachments.”

Adrian copied the reference for her to check. After confirming it, Luna suddenly remembered the breakfast cups.

“I haven't returned them.”

“The proprietor collected them.”

“Did he say anything?”

“That your experiment produced a tableful of cold tea.”

Luna covered her face with her left hand. Her shoulders shook.

This time, she wasn't laughing to keep people away.
