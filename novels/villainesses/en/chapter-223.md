# Chapter 223: Don't Draw the Arrow into Her Hand

On March 22, Luna had her assistant remove the prettiest diagram from the table.

Five symmetrical curves met in a flower. Adrian had studied it that morning and thought it looked like something they could bring to an argument with the High Priest. The sheet Luna kept was an untidy collection of boxes, with a crooked teacup in the margin.

“What does the cup represent?”

“I wanted tea when I reached that part.”

“Should we remove it?”

“Bring me tea first. Then we'll negotiate.”

Smiling, the assistant put a cup within easy reach on Luna's left. Her right palm remained cushioned. She dictated; the assistant wrote. Having an idea for a model hadn't changed her medical restrictions.

Adrian turned over the flower diagram. On its back he found a sentence: When one participant withdraws, the remainder automatically makes up the difference.

His smile vanished.

“You wanted to keep this?”

“I thought of it first. Then decided I couldn't quietly keep it.”

She asked the assistant to copy the sentence onto the page of rejected approaches. More people sharing sounded kinder than five. But if every departure simply added its burden to everyone left, greater numbers only made a wider net.

The academy had provided a small classroom. There were no ancient objects or energy cases on its tables, only an erasable board, wooden counters, and yesterday's authorized extracts from ordinary work arrangements. Luna was proposing a contract structure for engineering and legal scrutiny, not a system ready to connect to the Crown of Destiny.

She asked Adrian to name a box.

“Elena,” he began, then stopped. “Node A.”

“Not because you should pretend you don't care about her,” Luna said. “Because wanting her alive doesn't establish how this box ought to function.”

He erased the name. The assistant labeled the other counters B, C, and Buffer. Their limits were hypothetical, not measurements of anyone's bodily capacity.

Luna used the maintenance rota to illustrate handovers without treating a waterwheel as the Crown. Shift work suggested questions, not proof that magical risk could be separated. The available historical maintenance directory identified some connection names. The damaged volume remained under conservation, and crucial conditions were missing. Dotted lines marked connections they didn't yet know how to achieve.

“And solid lines?” Adrian asked.

“Relationships explicitly defined on this sheet. Not pipes we've discovered underground.”

He had nearly mistaken a diagram's legend for evidence again.

When Viola arrived, an assistant had placed her chair near the door. With her right hand, she removed Node A's counter. If that represented someone unable to continue, who ordered the next step?

Adrian started moving B over. Luna asked him to read its reverse first.

Remaining capacity unknown.

“I can't move it because it's nearby,” he admitted.

“Or because it's wearing a uniform,” Viola said.

She described a difficult kind of praise in military life. Whoever had been bravest during the last rescue attracted everyone's gaze before the next. Before that person answered, others were already talking about honor. They might still want to go. But if fatigue, family, and refusal couldn't be mentioned, praise had cleared the way for an order.

“People looked at you that way?” Adrian asked.

“I've looked at other people that way.”

She didn't exile that admission to some distant army. Organizing witnesses or assistance today could still tempt her to keep choosing whoever seemed most willing to remain.

Luna dictated another restriction: A contribution already made does not authorize an automatic increase.

“That leaves a gap,” Viola said.

“Yes.”

“Then people must see the gap.”

Adrian erased his hurried arrow between A and B and replaced it with a dotted line awaiting confirmation. The empty space made the diagram awkward. He understood why the flower had felt comforting. Other people's futures already filled every opening.

An academy engineering lecturer arrived before lunch for an initial examination. The word distributed didn't impress him. He turned the sheet around and asked whether they were distributing energy or danger.

Luna said the model separated those categories. That didn't prove the actual mechanism could. If approved, an initial stage could only examine transfer order using independent, limited energy supplies and nonliving substitutes. Any relationship to actual risk required further evidence.

The lecturer circled a box. “You have a buffer taking the missing load. Where does it come from?”

Silence settled over the table.

Adrian remembered yesterday's three little wheels. Luna asked the assistant to turn the Buffer counter over. Its reverse was blank.

“We don't have one,” she said.

“Then it can't remain in your conclusion on the strength of its name.”

She moved it into the section requiring proof. The model shrank and looked less like an answer.

Adrian expected disappointment. Instead, Luna asked whether, without a buffer, they could first define a paper model excluding simultaneous departures at two nodes and examine the necessary switching conditions. The lecturer accepted that as a hypothesis, not as an obligation for future participants never to fall ill.

“A condition under which the model fails,” Luna said. “Not one under which the people are at fault.”

Adrian relaxed a little. He suggested leaving the maximum waiting interval blank rather than borrowing an ordinary maintenance rota's timing for an entirely different structure.

The lecturer looked at him. “At last. Someone willing to let an empty space do its job.”

The lecturer didn't put the papers away. He replaced Node A and asked Adrian to act only as a messenger. Viola would watch what the diagram actually did after someone asked to stop.

“Node A requests an end,” he said.

Adrian passed a request slip to the assistant. After it was read aloud, he laid it before Luna. She acknowledged receipt and asked whether B had spare capacity. As Adrian reached for another sheet, Viola put her right hand over a red counter at the table's corner.

“Where is the stop?”

They looked at her. No energy was running, no actual danger present. Yet a simple request had already become a circuit of questions. If a future experiment required immediate stopping, this journey couldn't substitute for the stop itself.

Luna had a line emphasized in the model: a participating node must first have an independent means of ending its own experimental input. The remaining components would be considered afterward. Effects on neighboring components required separate measurement; whoever acknowledged the request couldn't guarantee safety merely by saying it was received.

The lecturer warned that this narrowed the initial research further. They might need to examine independent interruption before handovers. A complete loop couldn't be justified simply because it made the first demonstration more satisfying.

Adrian wanted, for a moment, to turn the flower diagram back over. He had imagined Oswin's face when confronted with a complete alternative. Now their opponent didn't even need to object. They were dismantling the drawing themselves.

“I imagined winning with it,” he said.

“So did I.” Luna asked that today's exercise be recorded as a paper workflow check, not measured results from a small-scale simulation. Nobody at this table had interrupted a real energy supply or proved a connection safe to break.

Viola returned the red counter to the assistant. “Next time, whoever calls a stop shouldn't need me beside them to point it out.”

That sentence remained beneath the submitted diagram.

Elena arrived with lunch during an argument about whether cardboard deserved to be called equipment. Setting her basket on another table, she announced that she was responsible for food, not finding the model a name suitable for fundraising.

“How many portions?” Luna asked.

“One for each person actually staying to eat.”

“A working allocation model,” Adrian said.

Elena took out the last box. “This is mine. Nobody divides it into thirds to make the diagram symmetrical.”

They laughed. Luna ended the morning discussion as agreed, and her assistant covered the outstanding questions. Lunch didn't quietly become a second consultation.

She asked how far Adrian had read in the travel book. He told her the author had lost his hat at a mountain pass and spent two pages blaming the wind.

“Reasonable. He can't hold the wind responsible, so he's making the reader suffer.”

Adrian read a short passage in the author's exaggerated manner. Laughing, Luna nearly braced her left hand on the table. She stopped herself and settled against the chair back instead. Laughter hadn't healed her injury. It didn't have to.

After eating, Elena closed her box and asked which diagram they intended to submit. Adrian indicated the dotted lines and empty boxes. She didn't advise removing the blanks to make support easier to obtain.

“At least nobody reading this will think you've already assigned them a place.”

After resting, Luna reviewed only one dictated section. She named the proposal Distributed Contract Structure Model, Pending Review. It was no new oath, no recruitment of risk-bearers, and no conversion of five marked locks into entrances for additional people.

Adrian took responsibility for explaining it to the receiving clerk. In the next room he began, “We've found a way for more people to share—”

At way, the clerk reached for the stamp used on trial applications.

“Wait. I said that too quickly.”

Adrian started again. Today they had proposed a structure with stated limits, withdrawal conditions, and unknown connections. It hadn't been tested or shown applicable to the Crown. The clerk put down the trial stamp and used the ordinary research-proposal receipt instead. Adrian signed beside the correction.

His signature took a moment. The heat in his face lasted much longer. He didn't excuse the difference as something that left the general meaning unchanged.

On his return, Viola was waiting in the corridor while an assistant fetched her coat. When he explained, she asked only whether the correction would stay with the diagram.

“Yes.”

She nodded. Next time she spoke about courageous people, she should remember to include the parts they hadn't answered yet.

“You don't seem inclined to award me a medal today.”

“Do you need one?”

“No. Dinner, possibly.”

“That seems more promising.”

Late in the afternoon, Cecilia read the model's consent section and identified an unanswered question. When someone said yes, were they permitting an explanation, preparations, or an actual operation? If all three meanings wore the same oath, they would eventually return to those five combined forms.

She offered to examine ordinary existing oath texts tomorrow, beginning with service work she knew. No believer would be asked to try a new risk-bearing contract.

Adrian agreed to submit the diagram's language to her criticism too. He kept the flower drawing instead of tearing it up. The rejected version received a date and a clear label. Being prettier mustn't allow it to reappear later as their original promise.

On the way home, Elena noticed white board dust on his sleeve and asked whether she could brush it away. He agreed. After two strokes she paused.

“You're staring.”

“You weren't inside any of the boxes today.”

She looked down and smiled. Then she reminded him that she still needed to read her family's correspondence tonight. Finding a satisfactory sentence didn't authorize him to clear her evening.

“Afterward, would you like a short walk together?”

“Yes. If it's too cold by then, tea indoors.”

This time he didn't add an arrow from her answer to somewhere further away.
