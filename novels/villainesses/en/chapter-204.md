# Chapter 204: This Time, Tell Him First

Elena knocked on March 3 while Adrian was arguing with his neckcloth.

“Come in. Unless you intend to testify for it.”

She inspected the knot from the doorway. “Its predicament deserves sympathy.”

He stopped pulling. Before offering help, she put an opened letter on his desk. A case notification within their shared-arrival agreement, just delivered. She hadn't replied. The accompanying slip gave the actual arrival time, without an explanation intended to reassure him first.

The office reviewing the old-history notice had added the earlier supplement to the file. A preliminary examiner had an opening before noon to hear House Rosenberg's account. The family invitation named Elena, not Adrian. An informal discussion wouldn't replace his own response; he could submit observations afterward.

Nothing explicitly said he couldn't speak. It simply arranged who spoke first, who summarized, and who heard later into a particularly convenient route.

The messenger had suggested that private discussion might help Elena discover which corrections were most likely to succeed. She hadn't agreed.

“What was your first reaction?” Adrian asked.

“To go. Make them stop using that heading.”

“And now?”

“I still want to. That's why I'm showing you before I make the reasons sound better.”

He reread it. She didn't ask him to reward the first correct step by approving the next. The knot in his hand felt like a stone.

“I don't want you answering for me.”

“I could explain only my family's position.”

“What if they hear what I did from you, then give me something already summarized?”

She suggested asking rather than assuming. An hour remained before the response deadline. They would prepare separate questions and visit the ordinary reception desk. No carriage to take him somewhere else to rest; no vaguely affirmative answer sent ahead.

Before they left, she indicated his neckcloth.

“May I try?”

He offered the knot. She loosened its innermost turn and returned the cloth, without using proximity to declare their unfinished discussion resolved.

Reception was crowded. A man delivering binding supplies offered Elena his bench when he saw her clothes. She took an existing space instead. Adrian sat one person away with his own folder.

The assistant bowed to her, then noticed him. He called no guard. Today's opening had been intended for a family statement, not a joint examination.

Could Adrian attend the portions concerning his actions, Elena asked, or receive the proposed questions first?

“Other sources are involved. We can't discuss each one here.”

“Then we can confine it to what I can explain independently.”

Separating their scopes would make a complete preliminary account difficult today. Better to hear her first, prepare a summary, and let Adrian inspect it afterward.

At afterward, his fingers pressed into the folder's edge.

Separate interviews weren't necessarily unfair. Confidential sources couldn't be spread across a waiting room. What frightened him was having to seek permission for one final no after someone else had finished his story.

“Can I inspect statements about me before the summary goes forward?”

“We can't guarantee that today. You may supplement it later.”

Elena didn't answer for him. The assistant reminded her that after noon she would need an ordinary appointment. Her previous material remained received, but no alteration to the public notice could be promised today—or sooner afterward.

“I can accept losing this opening,” she told Adrian. “Not to put you in my debt.”

Anger rose in him, not at her. He hated every choice becoming a test of their relationship. Keeping his place to speak shouldn't create a favor he would hear about forever.

“Don't say I made you give it up.”

“I don't want to finish your account before you can inspect it. I can give that reason myself.”

She offered her family's independent position through the ordinary procedure. Statements about Adrian would be confirmed by him or attributed separately. She sought neither access to other people's confidential testimony nor precedence for her surname.

The assistant recorded her answer. Today's opening went to the next person. Her tone didn't attempt to reclaim it.

Adrian submitted his own questions. He watched the clerk add their page beneath his application. No old notice came down. No promise transformed every counter. The outcome wouldn't immediately end people's fear of him, but it hadn't purchased a better opening sentence with his silence.

Outside, Elena asked whether he wanted to go home.

“Bookshop first.”

It wasn't an investigation disguised as an outing. His travel volume had loose pages. Earlier he'd asked about bringing it today for stronger binding. Elena wanted an old guide to navigation charts. Each could conduct their own errand.

The bookseller inspected the loose gathering and offered three threads. The prettiest was too fine for repeatedly opened folding maps. Compliments, he observed, wouldn't make thread stronger.

Adrian looked at Elena.

“You may not turn that into a remark about the neckcloth.”

“I haven't spoken.”

“Your face has finished the sentence.”

Wisely declining to judge, the bookseller threaded his needle. Elena browsed while Adrian chose the binding himself.

The repair would take a little time. She found a thin illustrated guide and offered to show him a ferry diagram.

“I don't want to look at another bridge right now.”

She closed that page without asking why an ordinary drawing mattered. They moved to secondhand novels by the window. One cover showed a knight carrying three swords.

“Where does the third go?” Adrian asked.

“We could ask Viola.”

“She'll demand that the artist add a luggage cart.”

Elena opened the book. On the first page the knight drew all three swords. After checking the surrounding paragraphs, she established that he had only two hands.

“Perhaps he holds one in his teeth,” Adrian suggested.

“How does he deliver this oath?”

“Puts the sword down?”

“It says he never does.”

From the counter, the bookseller observed that two veterans had already returned it. Not for implausibility: the artist had omitted the horse, which could at least have carried a sword. Elena closed the book, indignant on behalf of the uncredited horse.

Adrian was about to answer when he recognized an old notice folded between other books. The bookseller removed it. A previous seller's bookmark, not something the shop required customers to sign. Did Adrian need it for comparison?

He already had that edition. The sheet went into the wastepaper box without requiring another explanation from him.

As Elena turned back, her smile faded. Her fingers brushed a spine, giving her something to do.

“You worked hard not to decide for me today,” he said.

She stilled. “I'm afraid you'll hear that as something I did so you ought to feel better.”

“Sometimes I do hear it that way.”

A passerby carrying a flowerpot scraped its rim against the window. Adrian waited until the man had gone.

“But I don't want everything you do to count only as proof that you were wrong before.”

This time she couldn't immediately hide the hope in her eyes. Hope wasn't a demand unless she made it his assignment.

“I don't completely trust that you'll do the same when the next bad letter comes.”

“Then we wait for the next letter.”

“Not so you spend your life being tested.”

“And not so you have to set a deadline today.”

He nodded. The past remained, but a method of living together was beginning to seem believable. Not that she would never be frightened. That she could be frightened and still put the letter on his desk first.

While they waited, the bookseller offered an ownership label for two copper coins. It wouldn't prevent theft, he said, but would help distinguish books brought in for repair. He produced a stack of decorative blanks. Two doves held a ribbon across the topmost one.

Adrian had just picked it up when the bookseller explained that it belonged to a wedding-gift set.

He put it down immediately. Elena selected a plain border and laid it beside the stack without writing his name for him.

“Don't you like doves?”

“I don't like two copper coins coming with an entire wedding.”

“Then check the reverse for a concealed guest list.”

He turned it over for her inspection. Empty. He wrote Adrian Reed inside the border, taking care not to catch the wet ink with his cuff. Elena chose a label for her illustrated guide and wrote her own name. They left the slips beside each other to dry. Neither went into the other's book.

The bookseller called him to inspect the repair. New thread passed through old holes. The pages retained their creases; this wasn't an undamaged new object. Adrian opened a middle gathering, thanked him, and paid.

The wind outside was cool. Elena asked whether she could take his arm. Not to shield him from strangers' looks. She wanted to be nearer.

After considering, he said yes.

She didn't grip him tightly. At the next corner he wanted to inspect his book and withdrew his arm; she let go naturally. Neither attached a guarantee to the ending. Those few steps hadn't thereby become false.

The next message arrived that afternoon.

The messenger carried separate notifications. In the hall, Elena inspected her own envelope, then directed him to Adrian's address. She didn't take his because he'd just gone upstairs. It wasn't private correspondence she had permission to open. Their shared arrival slip recorded only delivery of a related notification.

When Adrian came down, she was sorting her illustrations, not waiting at the stairs to study his face.

“You received it?”

“Mine. An invitation to confirm the scope of attendance at tomorrow's receipt of old material. You'll need to read yours.”

Through an independent representative, Evelyn Vale had offered the associated inquiry a historical working ledger she had retained. She would answer questions about its present custody. Reset appeared in its title; that didn't authenticate every entry. Tomorrow's first session would cover inventory, preservation, and provenance. Invitees could accept or decline separately.

Adrian read the final line twice. Evelyn hadn't sent him a personal demand for belief or offered the book in exchange for his abandoning questions.

He still needed time.

“I want to check the scope with my own representative first.”

“All right.”

“We may read a little later tonight. Don't cancel it for me.”

“I haven't. The traveler is still complaining about the bed.”

“Perhaps he'll find another inn before tomorrow evening.”

She closed her guide and let him prepare his questions. Taking her arm hadn't obliged him to provide a complete answer about them.

When they sat together that night, the repaired book lay open steadily. He kept the new notice within his own reach and asked her to continue. She mispronounced a place name. He corrected it. She disputed his version, consulted the appendix, and finally admitted he was right.

He laughed. Trust wasn't a declaration that could never be examined again. Like the new thread through the spine's old holes, it was holding today's page, one stitch at a time.
