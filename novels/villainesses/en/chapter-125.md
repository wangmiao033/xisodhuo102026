# Chapter 125: The Saint Knows It Isn't a Betrothal Seal

On the morning of January second, Adrian received a pair of cuff links worth more than his entire coat.

The royal attendant set the box on the entrance-hall table.

“Her Highness says they are a New Year's gift. No official conditions are attached.”

The sapphires rested in silver settings, their blue quiet and deep. The craftsmanship was so fine that Adrian's first thought was not refusal. It was that he should wash his hands.

Cecilia Saintclair was waiting to accompany him to the archive.

“You like them?” she asked.

“They're beautiful.”

“Your cuffs don't have the right holes.”

He looked down and returned abruptly to reality. The ordinary button, dulled by washing, suddenly appeared exceptionally reliable.

The attendant immediately offered to arrange a tailor.

“Please don't,” Adrian said. “I haven't started wearing them and I already need a second gift.”

Cecilia lowered her head over her book bag, unsuccessfully hiding a smile.

Adrian closed the box without making the messenger uncomfortable. He asked him to wait and wrote a short reply thanking the princess for the thought, declining the valuable personal gift, and asking that the unworn cuff links be returned intact.

He paused over the final line.

“What if she simply wants a chance to discuss something other than records?” Cecilia asked.

“I can discuss ordinary things without first owing someone a new coat.”

He added an invitation: if Her Highness wished, they could walk for twenty minutes in one of the palace's normally open gardens on the morning of January fourth. No gifts, and no betrothal discussion.

When Elena came downstairs, he showed her before sending it. Her gaze lingered on twenty minutes.

“Have you allowed a minute per tree?”

“I'm unfamiliar with the court's unit of measurement for walks.”

“She knows the unit for negotiations.”

“Then I'll remember this isn't another way to accept that proposal.”

Elena returned the note. She did not add a requirement that she accompany them, or pretend to feel no discomfort.

“Tell me afterward what actually grows there.”

“That should be easier to establish than the records.”

“Don't promise too soon. Last time you called holly a rose.”

Cecilia looked up and solemnly observed that both could prick you, but the distinction was considerable.

The attendant took the box back unchanged. His receipt recorded its return, not a misleading acceptance. Only after the carriage left did Adrian straighten his ordinary cuff.

“Do I look shabby now?”

“You look able to turn pages,” Cecilia said. “Useful today.”

On their way to the palace, she discussed the old seal specimens and the usage notes she had checked the previous night. She had not casually removed an original from a restricted Temple vault. The specimen volume already belonged to the royal archive. In her bag were the approved explanatory notes and extracts from public procedures held by her office.

Hartwell would bring out the specimen book, and they would check which rules had applied at the relevant time. Recognizing a rose did not authorize Cecilia to substitute a feeling for authentication.

Elena had family business that morning. Luna was keeping her dressing appointment. Only Adrian and Cecilia entered the archive, where Isabella was already waiting.

The princess wore blue stones at her own cuffs.

Adrian tried not to look. She caught him immediately.

“These are mine.”

“I know.”

“You look concerned they might migrate.”

Cecilia drew a very quiet breath beside him, suppressing an undignified laugh.

Isabella did not discuss the returned gift before the keeper. She invited them to sit. The old forwarding slip rested on its support. A conservator had assessed the folded flap beforehand; much of the impression could be seen from the already loosened angle without flattening it.

“Tell me what you recognize,” the princess said.

Cecilia did not lean too close.

“An old Temple document-routing seal. Not a betrothal witness seal, not a priest's personal seal, and not an authorization to return equipment to use.”

Adrian studied the central rose. That elegant pattern had once made him think first of oracles, trials, and expired betrothals. Cecilia read something far more ordinary in it now: an item passing from one office to another.

“Why?” Isabella asked.

Cecilia pointed to the comparison book, not the wax. “The forwarding office's abbreviation belongs around the outside. A betrothal witness seal puts the witness reference there. The central ring is open, with its end indicating the receiving direction. It isn't an oath enclosure.”

As a child, she too had regarded every rose-marked seal as the same sacred assurance. Then she had first delivered accounts for a relief office, carrying receipts from the east entrance to the west entrance before being directed to a small window on the south side.

“I went around three times. The last clerk drew a map and wrote down what each required stamp meant.”

“He didn't recognize a saint?” Isabella asked.

“He did. He asked me to sit while I waited, because if I stood at the window the people behind me wouldn't come forward.”

Adrian laughed at the picture. Cecilia did not mind. The clerk had ultimately accepted her completed receipts, not her title.

“I wasn't pleased at the time. Later I realized he had helped me.”

She opened the notes to the distinct designs for forwarding, receipt, and approval. Covering the explanations with blank paper, she asked Adrian to identify them by shape. He promptly confused two similar borders.

“That's why asking whether it looks familiar isn't enough.”

“I've provided a very useful counterexample.”

“Yes,” she agreed. “You needn't sign it for preservation.”

Isabella inspected the designs as well. She declined the little test, but changed Approval seal in her notes to Routing seal, pending verification.

Hartwell shifted the paper support under the daylight. Two characters on the blurred outer ring became visible, consistent with Cecilia's explanation.

She still did not declare it genuine.

They compared the retained period specimens. One rose petal had a small break; a fine line at the lower left of the rim came from a repair to the die. Both appeared in the old impression. Those details might still have been copied, so Hartwell also produced the independently kept royal receipt register.

On November ninth, four years earlier, the Temple document-forwarding office had delivered twelve leaves of maintenance papers and one separate empty wrapper. The parcel reference matched the original slip. The receiver's entry sat among routine receipts before and after it, without an entire page inserted later.

Cecilia checked the wording and laid down her pencil.

“The specimen, period usage, parcel reference, and independent receipt support one another. We can establish that this document passed through the Temple's forwarding office. It isn't simply a rose someone drew for us.”

Isabella leaned forward.

“So the Temple knew the material was to be used again.”

“We cannot say that.”

Cecilia answered calmly. The princess plainly disliked the qualification.

“They stamped it.”

“For forwarding. The clerk might have known the listed contents, or only checked the sealed parcel and its reference. We have neither that person's account nor an approval for renewed use.”

Adrian remembered the attendant who had brought the cuff links. Delivering the box did not enable him to explain every unspoken expectation inside it.

“Delivery isn't endorsement of the contents,” he said.

Isabella's fingers paused beside the sapphires on her cuff, as though she had heard a second meaning he had not deliberately supplied.

Cecilia wrote Temple with particular care.

She would acknowledge that the institution had handled the material. Her faith did not require her to hide its seal. Nor did the malice she had already encountered entitle her to assign an unproved crime to every clerk on duty.

Outside, a coal cart caught a wheel in a gap between stones. Hartwell's assistant went to help and returned a little later. The seal did not begin speaking for itself while they were away. It remained old paper that someone needed to read properly.

A narrow source field appeared on the reverse of the forwarding slip.

Northern Route Depot Liaison Office. Forwarding copy NR-42.

It was dated November sixth, four years earlier, three days before the royal receipt.

“A military order?” Isabella asked.

“A military source. I can't tell you on behalf of the military whether it is an operational order.”

Hartwell found the thirteenth leaf identified yesterday. It was the appended military forwarding copy, with the same NR-42 reference at the corner. Its phrasing differed from an ordinary palace delivery slip.

Adrian remembered Viola's almost instinctive displeasure at seeing an order described incorrectly.

“Miss Blackwood should look at it.”

This time the princess did not ask him to guess first.

During the break, Cecilia went to the outer table to write a short note confirming her afternoon return to the office. Two registration applications still needed her attention. She could not leave all her work to the people waiting for her.

Adrian stood by the window. Isabella joined him.

“The stones weren't to your taste?”

“They were.”

She turned, surprised by the straightforward answer.

“So I chose correctly and gave them incorrectly?”

“We've only just begun working together. I don't want to wear something that encourages everyone else to explain our relationship for us. Or accept it because it's beautiful and let you think I've accepted more.”

Isabella watched the path below.

“I chose blue because you looked at the blue glass by the window when we dined. You seemed to like it.”

He paused.

She had not simply ordered someone to bring the most expensive thing in storage. That gave his refusal an uncomfortable weight. It did not make the gift right to accept.

“You noticed. Thank you. The glass was beautiful.”

“Your thanks is remarkably sparing.”

“I'd like it to be genuine, at least.”

After a silence she offered him an ordinary note. January fourth, morning, the garden outside the west conservatory. Twenty minutes. The usual guards would remain, other visitors would not be cleared out, and no change of formal dress was necessary.

“I haven't forgotten the word ordinary,” she said. “Although it is still my garden.”

“I'll remember you can decide you don't want a walk, too.”

“I do want one,” she replied a little quickly, then added, “To discover how much trouble a conversation without a gift box saves.”

She did not profess affection. One returned present had not taught her everything. Adrian checked that the time did not conflict with his existing plans with Elena before agreeing. He wrote down the date, unwilling to let a casual invitation turn into a careless promise that left someone waiting.

Back at the table, Cecilia had finished her note. She broke her bread in two and offered him half.

“This needn't be returned.”

“Why not?”

“No jewels. It won't arrange a tailor for you.”

He laughed and accepted. A thin slice of cheese filled the bread. She had packed a simple lunch for herself before leaving the office.

“I thought you were busy this afternoon.”

“That's why I'm eating now.” She paused. “And I wanted to eat with you. Both can be true.”

She said it so naturally that he could not find a joke in time.

Seeing his expression, Cecilia colored slightly but did not retract the words. Outside, the coal cart had passed. The noon bells began to ring.

She listened for a moment, then reminded him that he would drop crumbs if he only watched her.

Adrian looked down and caught them on the paper.

At the other end of the table, Hartwell entered NR-42 on the next consultation sheet. The document that truly required Viola's judgment had not been hurried into an explanation by the noon bell.
