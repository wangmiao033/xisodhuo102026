# Chapter 297: Even a Crown Can Wait

On June 21, Isabella spent fifteen minutes outside the council chamber before discovering that the man ahead of her was not a hostile marquess. He repaired boilers, and he wanted last month's wages.

The porter directed him across the hall. The workman held up a referral slip.

“They sent me here. The paper's made a complete circuit. I haven't had lunch.”

Isabella had an introduction that might have persuaded the porter to move the rope. Instead, she folded it with her right hand and asked to see the referral headings, without examining his private earnings. With his permission, she read them.

One office said payment must await a review of the old facilities. The other said ordinary maintenance did not belong in the special settlement. The repeal effective since June 15 required neither office to withhold his wages. Between them, they had built a door that opened nowhere.

“I'm not asking you for charity,” he said.

“I couldn't afford to pay everyone. But I can take this question inside, if you wish. And someone should give you an answer that doesn't involve another circuit.”

He agreed to supply redacted copies and went to the actual wage-claims desk. She promised neither immediate payment nor priority. Her own wait continued.

That afternoon, his creased slips lay on the hearing table.

A maintenance representative warned that freezing every disputed royal and Temple allocation might first extinguish night lamps and send qualified technicians home. An older councilor seized on the difficulty. Surely, in a crisis, the Crown must retain the right to appoint one final authority.

Isabella waited until the chair recognized her.

“We need someone responsible for payment, and someone able to review a refusal. We do not need to solve a payroll problem by giving another person power over other people's bodies.”

“And when the crisis arrives? Must we wait for everyone to finish lunch?”

“Some people have already missed lunch because of our crises.”

A few people laughed. The repairman was absent; the laughter quickly faded.

She described bounded emergency maintenance powers with expiration dates and review. Compulsory marriage and designated sacrifice could not slip through as emergency exceptions. Consent protections already in force must not disappear beneath the next royal seal.

This was no proclamation of her own. Qualified members introduced provisions. Delegates checked the limits of their mandates. Under the existing amendment procedure, the authorized bodies considered new boundaries between royal power, public expenditure, and independent adjudication. Isabella answered questions. She had no vote, and progress did not restore her former eligibility.

The public questions required an extra session on June 24. Devon, the river-port councilor, refused to count two missing regional replies as support. He also challenged a hearing schedule that gave working maintenance staff no practical way to attend.

“Work all day, testify all evening, report again at dawn? Is that participation, or an unpaid second shift?”

The budget drafters cut a ceremonial reception and entered limited attendance and replacement-shift costs. They could not bring every distant applicant to the capital. Written submissions and later hearings remained available; the uncovered places were not called covered. Opponents had their objections recorded without being added to a list of dangerous people.

Isabella returned late. Her left-wrist support pressed against her sleeve. She handed the papers to her clerk instead of copying one last page herself.

Adrian was returning a borrowed measuring rule in the entrance hall. He asked whether she needed the kitchen told to keep supper, rather than inviting her to a private meal.

“Already arranged. You may tell them that if they carve my carrots into crowns, I shall question the expense.”

He laughed. She smiled and went to her waiting attendant. A joke at work did not quietly reopen their paused private invitations.

In another room, Elena was waiting with curtain samples. She disliked his pale gray. It suggested they intended to spend married life in an archive cabinet. He thought her dark red would make everyone at breakfast look newly sentenced.

“Blue, then,” she said.

“A compromise?”

“You've finally finished offering wrong answers.”

He held the fabric against the window. She joined him in the evening light. They were not yet married, and a registration date still required an ordinary appointment. But a window they hoped to open every morning was worth a serious argument.

“Are you still worried your father won't like the life we're planning?”

“Yes. Fortunately, that and the curtains are two things I can worry about separately.”

He put down the cloth and asked to hold her. She leaned against him, laughing into his shoulder.

“If we finish every disagreement like this, somebody will still have to pay for the purchases.”

“I proposed an immediate solution. I don't have budget authority.”

On June 26, the budget annex came back for correction. No new conspiracy had emerged. Two apparently separate maintenance allowances referred to the same reserve components. The treasury's inventory would otherwise contain one more set than the warehouse.

Isabella had repeated the number in her own statement. She retained the draft and explained the error to the hearing. The older councilor asked why anyone should trust a proposal that still contained arithmetic mistakes.

“Which is why I shouldn't be its only reviewer. You found it today. Someone must be able to find the next error without wondering whether the question will cost them their livelihood.”

The corrected amount forced a proposed regional inspection to be delayed. The maintenance representative refused to describe it as completed coverage. The local delegates refused to let their area vanish from the schedule. Initial documentary checks would proceed, with a later site review retained. Those affected would receive notice and could object.

Afterward, Isabella sat in the corridor, pressing her right hand against her forehead. Elena set a cup of water beside her.

“You looked ready to overturn the table.”

“For a moment. I'd even chosen which end would spare my wrist.”

“You still aren't quite suited to tyranny.”

Isabella laughed, then asked whether the changes might slow the Rosenberg review. Elena admitted they might. Supporting reform would not entitle her family to an early resolution. They drank their water and discussed the unfairness of deciding cases by how likable their participants were. Their conversation did not need to pass through the same man to continue.

On June 27, the constitutional amendments passed their formal votes after detailed consideration, satisfying the existing amendment requirements. The clerks checked and announced the result. Opposing votes and reservations entered the record alongside the reasons for approval. The chamber had not acquired a single voice.

When the text went for royal approval, Isabella was asked to explain its limits on emergency powers. A senior attendant wondered whether she truly intended to surrender conveniences that might someday be hers.

Seated in an ordinary low chair, her left wrist supported, she watched workers replace a gutter outside.

“It wouldn't be a limit if it applied only to other people's power.”

“You could do better yourself when the time came.”

“I hope people won't have to believe I'm good before they're allowed to object.”

She did not sign for the kingdom. The king received legal and financial advice and formally approved the amendments. The responsible officers countersigned. Publication followed on June 29, with July 1 as the effective date. Regions received authenticated texts and implementation arrangements, not a private appeal from the princess.

During the final check, a clerk found an old unilateral-restoration route still printed on the dispatch envelopes. The legislation was correct. The error lay in the convenient box someone would later fill without thinking.

The printer stopped that batch. Staff replaced every prepared envelope. The correction cost half a day and actual money.

Isabella meant to stay until the work was finished. Her attendant reminded her of the agreed departure time. She looked at the remaining pile, checked who would take over, and left. A system that worked only while a princess sat through pain had not traveled far from its old habits.

On the morning of July 1, Adrian and Elena attended the first public implementation session. Neither scar nor mark was required to bear witness to the new law.

Staff were already at the independent review desk. Budget authorization and facility operations belonged to different responsible officials. Emergency action required a stated scope, an expiry, and subsequent review. Those personally or institutionally involved in a dispute had to recuse themselves. Neither Crown nor Temple could remove unfavorable records and appoint itself their sole interpreter.

In practice, these changes were not always dramatic.

An employee brought an old-format temporary instruction proposing to restore a designation list under emergency maintenance powers. The officer checked that qualified staff could still perform the maintenance, refused the attached bodily designation, preserved the instruction, and referred the disputed provision for independent decision. The pump room continued operating. The people on the list were not summoned to stand ready.

The boiler repairman returned too. He carried no royal purse. The wage office had confirmed the payment actually credited to him. Settlement staff had recognized one ordinary maintenance debt, not cleared every old special allocation.

He asked to thank the princess. Isabella asked him to wait until she finished her current answer. He need not display his wage notice to the room.

When his turn came, he said only that he had eaten breakfast.

The laughter lasted longer this time.

After the session, the older councilor stayed to challenge the emergency-review deadlines. His application was accepted. Opposition was not sabotage. Isabella listened, and he pointed out an incorrect page reference.

“You needn't look so pleased,” he said.

“I'm not pleased you found an error. I'm pleased we can correct it without first requiring you to like me.”

No attendants cleared the steps for her. An ordinary guide working with Viola's assistance group asked people to leave the level route open. An older visitor took his time crossing it. Everyone else waited.

Adrian watched Isabella accept her coat with her right hand and settle her next appointment with her clerk. She was going to examine the new budget-supervision records. She would not join them to buy curtains, and she owed them no smiling performance of blessing.

“Even a crown has to queue today,” he murmured.

“So do you tomorrow,” Elena said. “The registry gave us a morning consultation. Saving the kingdom isn't permission to cut the line.”

He agreed solemnly, then asked whether she wanted to look at the blue fabric now.

She linked her arm through his. “Yes. Provided you admit how terrible the gray was.”

He considered her. “I'm willing to hear the argument again.”

July sunlight crossed the steps. Some people went to meetings, some to their shifts, and one man could buy lunch with his own wages. Adrian and Elena joined the street. Nobody declared the kingdom free of future calamity. Nobody had to surrender a lifetime to one person's goodwill.
