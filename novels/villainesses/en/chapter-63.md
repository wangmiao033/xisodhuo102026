# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Three: The Knight Says She's Responsible for Me
### Chapter 63: Why Does the Spy's Notebook Mention Apple Pastry?

On the afternoon of November 11, Adrian Reed discovered that once someone removed the visitor badge from his chest, he no longer knew what to do with his hands.

Lieutenant Rusk placed both wooden badges in a drawer and gave them a receipt.

“Your access to the military inspection area is suspended until we verify your identities. You may wait at the town hall.”

Someone outside murmured, “The duke's spies.”

It wasn't loud. It was enough to make a young soldier step back into the space he'd been clearing for Elena.

She stopped in front of him.

“Excuse me.”

He blinked and hurried aside. She didn't crush the accusation beneath her family title. She adjusted her shawl and entered a waiting room that smelled of damp wood.

Following her, Adrian realized he was still gripping his pen.

“Bad news. My profession may have changed.”

“The good news is that spies should earn more.”

“Who do we ask about that?”

“Don't celebrate yet. You spent the entire morning behind a white line without touching a single wagon. I doubt you'd pass the assessment.”

She pulled a chair toward the window. Her smile didn't last. Outside, the crate bearing the rose had been moved into a roofed storage area. The number carved into its frame had been copied onto a fresh evidence tag.

R-417.

A few old red strokes seemed to weigh more than the entire crate.

Viola arrived without any sweeping assurance of their innocence. She carried their original access authorization. A municipal clerk followed with a folder.

“Military access has been suspended. Your invitation still permits examination of civilian freight records. The army's identity check doesn't close the town's registers as well.”

Rusk entered behind her, stripping off his wet gloves.

“I need to know what they've recorded. People have been asking about our supply routes.”

“You can compare today's transport notes,” Adrian said, putting his notebook on the table. “Some personal things are tucked inside. Let me remove them first.”

Rusk's gaze settled on a pale corner protruding between the pages.

Adrian pulled it free. Halfway out, the slip unfolded.

One neat line read: Save the unbroken apple pastry for me tonight.

It was signed with a single E.

Silence.

Elena snatched up the note so quickly she didn't even need her injured hand.

“A trade secret.”

Viola turned earnestly to the lieutenant.

“It has no bearing on the supply route.”

Adrian might have managed until then. That finished him. He bent over, coughing with laughter, and felt his chest tighten. He caught the table's edge and stopped to breathe slowly.

The amusement vanished. Elena slid water within reach while Viola moved an obstructing chair and helped him sit securely.

“Does it still hurt?” Elena asked softly.

“Just for a moment. It's easing.”

She watched until he could speak comfortably before taking her hand off the chair back.

Rusk waited for him to recover, then accepted copies of only the inspection notes. The municipal clerk listed the pages and obtained signatures for the handover. Nobody demanded their travel book or searched their rooms.

At least one apple pastry had survived the encounter.

An argument broke out beyond the door. An elderly egg seller had caught hold of the clerk and refused to release him until he told her whether carts could still enter the afternoon market. Her son served at West Slope. She wouldn't believe soldiers had deliberately tried to overturn a grain wagon, but she had no intention of leaving her eggs at the bottom of that slope, either.

“My rent's due tomorrow. You can wait. The hens can't.”

The clerk explained that the safe lane had reopened; only the damaged wagon remained impounded. She released his sleeve, noticed Elena, and hesitantly shifted her basket behind her back.

Elena's gaze followed it. After a moment, she asked the clerk for paper and had him sketch the open entrance for the woman.

Nobody accepted any eggs. Nor did a little map suddenly persuade the woman to trust House Rosenberg. She thanked them and left, clutching her basket.

Adrian watched Elena lower her hand.

“Does it hurt?” he asked quietly.

“A little.” She didn't pretend otherwise. “I understand what she's afraid of. I still don't enjoy people avoiding me.”

She looked out of the window. Fatigue showed through the sharp composure she'd maintained.

He didn't tell her to rise above it. He moved his chair a little closer. The legs scraped horribly across the floor.

She smiled anyway.

Magistrate Ada Finch arrived a short time later. A woman in her forties with mud on her coat hem, she first asked whether freight was still blocking the market, then read the two documents refusing to yield to each other.

“I have authority to preserve civilian originals.” She set down her folder. “The army decides who opens its warehouse doors. Where my registers go requires my signature.”

She sent for Tobin Quill.

He was the injured transport clerk mentioned in their invitation. His right hand remained in a sling; his left held an ink-stained sleeve guard. His first question on seeing the papers was who had sharpened his pen so viciously.

“It's too fine. It'll snap as soon as I press.”

“Today you're here to read,” Ada said.

“That costs more than writing.”

She gave him a look. He sat down obediently.

Adrian liked the ordinary voices returning to the room. A man who complained about his pen felt more trustworthy than a witness who had descended from heaven specifically to establish somebody's innocence.

An evidence custodian took a rubbing of the crate's carved number and sketched the stamp's position beside it. Elena didn't touch the original crate. She explained how her family's stations had used red transfer marks.

“The number is carved into the base frame. Changing the contents doesn't change it. We don't usually plane off the rose, either. Thin the boards, and they can split under a load of crockery.”

“So it could carry your crest and somebody else's goods?” Rusk asked.

“Yes. The records should tell us whether this one did.”

Fine rain began outside as the clerk brought in the returns register. The room darkened. Tobin asked for the lamp on his left and read out dates while the clerk turned the pages.

October 28: R-417 had passed through a Rosenberg civilian transfer station as packaging for a commercial shipment of soap-making supplies.

November 2: inspected empty, returned to the shared pool of shipping crates, and released for reuse.

A weighhouse receipt number followed the entry.

Ruth was called in, rain dripping from her hat. After checking the receipt, she remembered that batch. Sawdust had gone everywhere. A porter had even left his lunch inside one of the crates.

“That establishes a batch,” Rusk said.

Tobin didn't immediately argue. He had the clerk turn back a page, then forward again, making certain the receipt number hadn't slipped onto the wrong line. Ruth set her wet hat well away and produced a narrow retained receipt. The original binding thread still passed through two holes at its edge.

While they waited, Adrian noticed that Viola remained beside the door. She moved aside when people entered and held its weight for the clerk carrying the register. It was neither a commanding position nor a useful place to read the table.

Needing this result didn't make her walk over and point Tobin toward the entry she wanted.

“That's why we have the carved number.” Tobin indicated the line with his left hand. “This crate had a chip in the right base frame. We made a separate note during inspection. The lid was opened. I counted the crates while Ruth checked the weight. It was empty when released.”

The custodian's sketch also showed an old chip at the lower right corner.

Adrian didn't write that House Rosenberg had nothing to do with the matter. His pen paused after the date.

“Who took it after it left the pool?”

Tobin turned a page and frowned.

“This book ends at release. Subsequent collection and loading go into each station's return-crate register.”

They had reached the end of this stretch, not the end of the road. But the room had changed.

The rose on the crate no longer had to answer every question by itself.

With her uninjured fingers, Elena pushed forward the statement she'd written about the station.

“Please request the corresponding return register. I'll tell the manager to preserve the relevant records.”

Rusk didn't take it immediately.

“You understand that this may lead further into your family's business.”

“I am talking about my family's business.”

Her voice was even. Beneath her shawl, however, the lightly bandaged hand tightened. Adrian noticed the gauze pull taut and quietly shifted her tea to a position where she could lift it easily. She touched the handle and relaxed her other hand.

Ada had both sides check the preservation inventory. The municipal originals would remain in the town hall's evidence cabinet, with the outer seal signed independently by her and the duty military clerk. Both had to be present when it was opened. The army would retain the crate, ballast, and original transit seal; the town kept corresponding custody receipts.

Tobin signed the witness column with an infuriatingly crooked signature.

“It doesn't normally look like that.”

“We can all see your hand,” Ruth said.

“I don't want posterity thinking I have bad handwriting.”

Adrian sympathetically refrained from laughing. His own official notes contained a blot resembling a beetle under enemy siege.

At the end of the questioning, Rusk produced their visitor badges.

Each now carried a slip specifying the restored scope: civilian records and designated observation areas. Military warehouses, patrol rosters, and troop maps remained closed to them.

“Someone asked at the checkpoint when you arrived,” he said. “We're looking into it. Restored access doesn't mean you can wander wherever you like.”

He didn't apologize for his earlier question. He didn't call them spies again, either. Adrian accepted the badge. That seemed more useful than a polished courtesy.

At the door, the young soldier avoided Elena's eyes and held it as wide as he could.

She nodded to him and went out.

The rain had stopped. Wet gray stones gleamed beneath their feet. Someone rolled up the bakery awning and emptied a pocket of water over half of Owen's boot. The corporal jumped, finally breaking the party's orderly pace.

Viola looked back, counted them, and handed Adrian his map.

A fresh detour joined the old red line.

“Someone stopped a municipal clerk at that tavern to ask where you're staying. It may be unrelated. Until we know, use the other street. I've placed you both on the list of visitors requiring particular attention.”

Adrian studied the noticeably thicker line.

“I've been promoted?”

“Temporarily.”

“Does it include dinner?”

Viola looked toward Owen, apparently considering the visitor regulations. Elena put a hand on the map first.

“Dinner is already arranged. Please don't issue orders to the kitchen.”

Viola started to answer, then finally noticed their expressions.

“I have never issued an order to a kitchen.”

“Last winter's hot soup against the frost,” Owen murmured.

“That was a suggestion.”

“They framed your signature.”

The captain turned away. Her ears were conspicuously pink in the cold air.

Back at the inn, Elena requested warm water and sat to adjust the dressing on her hand. Adrian cut a loose thread as she directed, keeping the scissors clear of tender skin.

“You didn't rush to vouch for me earlier,” she said.

“You wanted to establish where the crate had been.”

“Yes.”

She watched the little scissors. After a while, she added, “I was still glad you were beside me.”

Adrian looked up. The feeling in his chest had nothing to do with the pain from earlier.

“So was I.”

She offered her good hand. He took it, his thumb resting lightly across her fingers. Neither immediately reached for the newly delivered papers.

Then the landlady brought in dessert.

Elena opened the box. The last apple pastry lay perfectly intact inside.

She set the inspected note beside it.

“Time to honor our commercial agreement.”

“May I apply for a dividend?”

“That depends on performance.”

She cut it in half and gave him a piece.

At the corner of the table, the fresh civilian summary showed a consignment number appearing twice: WF-18. Its twelve sacks seemed to have made two separate journeys in another summary.

Still eating pastry, Adrian placed the pages together.

Tomorrow, he needed to ask why the grain could duplicate itself.
