# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Three: The Knight Says She's Responsible for Me
### Chapter 61: Her Date Route Has Three Evacuation Points

By the afternoon of November 10, Adrian Reed had learned that two inches on a map could shake a man half to death.

They had left the capital on the afternoon of the eighth, spent their first night at a roadside inn, and crossed miles of rolling hills on the ninth before changing horses again. By now, even Elena's third shawl had justified its place in the luggage.

Two days on the road. Not one mile had shortened because he was anxious to arrive.

They had meant to read a few pages of their travel book the previous evening. Adrian had managed to read the same sentence twice before Elena closed it and told him to get some sleep. When he asked whether that counted as breaking their plans, she carefully tucked the bookmark into place.

“I want to spend time with you. I'm not assessing your ability to stay awake.”

So they had sat together over a cup of lukewarm tea for a little while, then said good night. Being allowed to stop trying to make it a perfect evening had made it the easiest part of the journey.


“Any further observations?” Elena asked.

“I owe Martha an apology. She should have packed another cushion.”

Elena pushed hers toward the middle of the seat. “You get half.”

“My faith in the aristocracy is restored.”

When the coach stopped, he almost regretted it.

Greymarch lay between two ridges. Pale repairs marked its low gray walls. A bakery chimney smoked; a smith brought his water bucket indoors. A little girl led a goat directly across a patrol horse's path with absolute confidence in her right of way.

This wasn't scenery waiting for a disaster to begin.

At the gate, a chestnut seller insisted that Corporal Owen Hart settle the two copper coins he owed. Owen searched every pocket before finding them in his boot. The knight on duty waited without intervening. Somehow, his embarrassment made the border feel more real than any music Adrian remembered from the game.

Some people here only wanted to buy salt before dark.

Owen led them to the inspection shelter. A young woman in dark-colored light armor waited beneath its roof. She looked about twenty-six, perhaps twenty-seven, with a dark brown braid tucked behind her shoulder and a faint healed scar near one eyebrow.

When she looked up, Adrian noticed her eyes first.

Gray-green. Alert. The sort of eyes that could turn “I'm sure I packed everything” from a comforting remark into a false statement.

“Viola Blackwood.” She saluted. “Thank you both for coming.”

Elena returned the greeting and presented their entry authorization.

Recognizing the Rosenberg crest did not make Viola wave them through. She checked their names, paperwork, and luggage, then studied the box of apple pastries with equal seriousness.

“Fragile contents?”

“They are now,” Adrian said. “They were more intact when we left.”

Viola noticed the crumbs escaping one corner.

“The road surface,” she said after a pause. “I'll include it in the maintenance report.”

Elena turned away, her shoulders moving slightly.

Adrian couldn't let pastry casualties become another burden on the border administration.

“I ate some of them, too.”

Viola nodded. “That portion isn't the road's responsibility.”

She meant it. That made keeping a straight face considerably harder.

She handed them temporary visitor badges and spread out a town map.

A thin red line led from their inn to the trading office, followed a riverside path past the bakery, and ended at a little roofed pavilion.

Adrian studied it, then glanced at Elena.

A river, fresh bread, somewhere sheltered from the wind. The border was unexpectedly thoughtful about welcoming guests.

“A pleasant walk after dinner,” he ventured.

“Walking beside the river after dark is prohibited.”

His smile stalled. “Then why does the route go there?”

“Good visibility during daylight. The patrol can see you. There are three evacuation points. The bakery's back door leads toward the infirmary, and the pavilion has an alarm bell.”

Elena laughed.

“What did you think she'd arranged?”

“A tour.”

“That wasn't a sightseeing expression you gave the pavilion.”

Viola hesitated. “If you need a route suitable for courtship, I can consult the innkeeper. I only know which streets are easy to evacuate.”

“Please don't add to your workload,” Adrian said quickly.

She had already written: Visitors may require evening walks.

Elena rested a hand on the map. “I'll take responsibility for that requirement.”

“Understood.”

Adrian had the peculiar feeling that some official transfer had just occurred.

On their way to the town hall, Viola took them past the pavilion. Its alarm bell was three times the size of an ordinary dinner bell. A long fire hook hung beneath it.

“Lovely view,” Elena murmured.

“I'm trying to appreciate it.”

“Then stop staring at the hook.”

“Difficult. It's given me entirely new concerns about dating.”

Viola stopped to ask whether they needed a rest. She showed no impatience, merely shortened her stride.

Only then did Adrian notice how carefully she kept her sword scabbard from brushing passersby. She yielded to children and stepped aside to let anyone carrying water use the smoothest part of the road.

None of that had appeared in the game's severe little character description.

The room at the town hall was cold. A clerk from the quartermaster review office brought their registration and explained which records outside witnesses could inspect.

Viola retained provisional command of the south checkpoint and the patrols protecting nearby civilian evacuation routes. Significant actions required the duty officer's presence. Her authority to issue new grain requisitions or order distant deployments had been suspended. The treason allegation remained under review; she had not been convicted.

“If they suspect you,” Adrian asked, “why leave you guarding the gate?”

“Suspicion isn't a verdict. And the gate still needs guarding.”

She laid her gloves neatly on the table.

“Leaving a position vacant proves nothing except that it's vacant. My remaining authority is set out here. If you see me exceed it, record that, too.”

Elena read the order. She didn't rush to declare her faith in its subject.

“We'll establish what happened.”

“That's what I need.”

Viola showed them Willowford's evacuation map, pointing out the damaged stone bridge and the grain originally reserved for the village.

The supplementary order had demanded that all remaining grain go to the western-slope military depot. It also redirected the villagers accompanying the wagons beyond the bridge. The inspection report describing cracks in a bridge pier had arrived just before the order.

“I suspended the movement and requested confirmation of the requisition authority and an alternative route. The next day, someone reported grain missing from the village and suggested I'd sent it across the border.”

“Where are the villagers now?”

“Some are staying with relatives east of town. The infirmary arranged assistance for those unable to travel. Others remained to watch their homes and animals. We didn't evacuate everyone.”

She didn't make one difficult decision sound like the salvation of the entire frontier.

The witnesses' inventory of retained grain had blank spaces. Adrian pointed to one.

“You haven't put an estimate here?”

“I didn't see the last wagon.”

“A blank looks bad.”

“Better than an incorrect figure.” Her answer came at once. Then, perhaps fearing she sounded abrupt, she added, “I think so, anyway.”

Adrian withdrew his hand. “So do I.”

Viola smiled for the first time. Briefly, like sunlight through winter cloud, before she bent over the next document.

Her face was striking when it relaxed.

Adrian had just become aware of noticing that when Elena set hot tea in front of him.

“Drink. You said you were freezing.”

“Thank you.”

“I remember very clearly.”

He wasn't certain which part she remembered. He held the cup in both hands.

At dusk, Viola escorted them to the inn. The landlady had prepared neighboring rooms with fireplaces sharing a chimney and a night attendant stationed near the stairs.

Viola checked the window latches, rear exit, and corridor lamps. Finally, she gave Adrian a small brass whistle.

“Three blasts if you're in danger.”

“What if I only need hot water?”

“Ring the bell on your table.”

“I might mix them up.”

She stopped and tied a conspicuous blue cord to the whistle.

“There. Now you can distinguish them.”

Elena couldn't suppress a laugh.

“Have I missed something?” Viola asked.

“No. It's just rare to see his jokes dealt with so thoroughly.”

Understanding dawned on the captain. Color touched her ears, but her posture grew even straighter.

“I'm not good at identifying that sort of situation.”

“That's all right. I'm not good at handling actual danger. We each have our strengths.”

She considered this and accepted it.

The landlady handed over their keys. “Shall I push two little tables together for dinner?”

“Yes,” Adrian said.

“Keep the passage clear,” Viola said at the same time.

Elena raised an eyebrow. “Captain, how would you arrange a ball?”

“Count the exits first.”

“Then?”

“Check the carpet edges. Make sure the candles can't catch anyone's skirts.”

“And the choice of dancing partner?”

Viola thought about it. “That should be up to the individual.”

Elena's teasing expression softened into genuine approval.

“On that, we agree.”

Adrian realized he'd worried too much. Viola's seriousness didn't mean she wanted to fit people into boxes she'd drawn. She planned routes so guests could safely reach places they chose for themselves.

She simply hadn't realized that most people didn't consider evacuation points a welcoming gift.

Owen carried the luggage upstairs.

“She makes maps for the milk cart, too,” he offered. “Last winter she found a route around an icy slope.”

Adrian looked down at his carefully marked map.

“Then we're receiving excellent treatment.”

“Yes, sir. The milk cart only gets two evacuation points.”

Even Viola turned toward the window, apparently pretending not to hear.

Dinner was stewed beans and roast fish. Adrian invited her to stay. She checked with Owen that the patrol handover was complete before sitting and removing an inconvenient shoulder plate.

A silver fastening fell beneath the table.

Adrian retrieved it. Instead of simply taking it, she shifted the plate toward him.

“Would you hold this? The strap's loose. I can't manage it one-handed.”

“Of course.”

He steadied the leather. There was a clean scent of soap, cold air, and well-kept armor. She wasn't particularly close, yet her concentration made an ordinary repair feel oddly absorbing.

Elena rescued his cup before his elbow knocked it over.

“So your first knightly lesson is how to be a coat stand.”

“I'm advancing quickly. This morning I was a wagon full of pastry.”

Viola looked up. Her laugh came half a beat after theirs, but it was a real laugh.

Once the fastening was secure, she thanked him and ate. Nobody suggested that accepting assistance incurred lifelong obligations. Adrian could stop worrying that a loose buckle might create another marriage proceeding.

Halfway through dinner, Owen delivered the following day's transit notice.

A military-sealed supply wagon was due through the south gate on the morning of the eleventh. Its accompanying commercial paperwork listed a transfer point leased by House Rosenberg, bringing it within the civilian records they were permitted to examine.

Viola read the notice and frowned slightly.

“I'll be at the checkpoint tomorrow. If you're rested, you may observe from the designated area. You aren't my subordinates. If you can't confirm something, say so.”

Elena and Adrian exchanged a glance and agreed.

After Viola left, they finally settled beside the inn's window. The town grew quiet beyond the glass. Across the river, a lamp burned in the alarm pavilion.

Elena opened their travel book.

“No evacuation required at present.”

Adrian set the whistle farther away and moved closer of his own accord.

“Then I'd like to start where we left off.”

She shifted the book between them. Her shoulder rested lightly against his.

Outside, not a single alarm sounded.

For this evening, at least, their route only needed to reach the same page.
