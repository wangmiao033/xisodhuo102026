# Chapter 118: The Doors They Could Open Today

On the morning of December 26, Luna placed her left-handed application on the table and asked Adrian whether he could read the last word.

“Resume reading?” he ventured.

“Good. The clerk thought it said resume fishing.”

“Does the tower have a pond?”

“No. That's why he was more concerned than you.”

She smiled and put it away, her right palm still resting on a cushion. Today the tribunal would decide which doors could open. She wasn't wearing her ink-stained work coat, but had folded it at the top of her luggage.

The supplementary review was shorter than the previous sessions. Tessa's maintenance copies were registered. Sitting beside the witness stand, she no longer clutched her bag before strangers. The false note and employment threat had a separate case file; witness contact required official notice. Nobody had declared the messenger the person behind it all.

After reading the independent inspection report, the church advocate stopped insisting that the returning light represented a valid marriage contract. Instead, he requested that, since the old apparatus came from the church, related materials and research be returned to its exclusive custody pending internal review.

“Including C-17?” the academy representative asked.

“All similar carriers. Fragmented interpretation risks danger.”

Luna's shoulders stiffened. She didn’t try to speak over him. Instead, she pressed the application on her knees with her left fingers. The edge of the paper gave a small rustle.

Adrian remembered the short horizontal track along which the blank contractual casing had first slid back into its cabinet. Sometimes protecting an object meant repeatedly explaining why nobody had grounds to take it.

“Similarity doesn't establish common provenance,” the representative said. “We've submitted the tower's custody documents. The East Warehouse apparatus can remain where it is under investigation. That doesn't justify taking unrelated holdings.”

Asked for further grounds, the advocate cited church expertise, but produced no document sufficient to alter ownership or custody.

Then Luna was permitted to speak.

“I support keeping the dangerous circuits shut down. I was injured. Material was destroyed. The independent inspection doesn't merely endorse my defense. But an unpowered reading room, ordinary drawing tables, and a preparation room don't become equally dangerous because their doors bear the same paper.”

She submitted her application with the administrator's revised floor plan. Red outlines enclosed the equipment and accident rooms. Thin lines marked outer rooms temporarily sealed while related records were inventoried. The kitchen and residences had never closed; she hadn't added them to exaggerate her losses.

“Ordinary work can continue. Experiments requiring fresh approval must still obtain it.”

“Can you guarantee no further accidents?” the advocate asked.

“No,” Luna answered immediately. “If someone offers that guarantee, ask whom they intend to use to prove it.”

Silence followed. More quietly, she explained what their paper comparisons supported. The two-layer medium retaining a reference position, the independent return pathway, and the old load warning showed their original estimate had been optimistic. They supplied no safe value applicable to a person.

A marriage contract might transmit certain burdens. It wasn't a voucher authorizing unlimited costs for somebody else.

Cecilia didn't applaud. She found the correct page in the public commentary the tribunal had requested and passed it to the recorder. Beside Adrian, Elena slowly relaxed her gloved hand.

Before noon, the officer read the amended directions.

Ordinary reading, drawing, and preparation areas would reopen under academy management. Accident equipment, damaged components, and relevant original records remained preserved; similar dangerous experiments stayed suspended. C-17 would remain lawfully at the tower under the confirmed conditions. The accident didn't transfer it to the church. Research safety, correction of the earlier report, and the custody officer's misconduct remained under separate investigation. Those responsibilities had not disappeared simply because some rooms were being opened again.

Hearing her name, Luna looked up to confirm her next submission. She wrote down the deadline rather than asking Adrian to take it away.

Outside, Tessa exhaled.

“So tomorrow I can still repair ordinary spring strips?”

“Unless they've secretly become engaged,” Luna said.

Tessa laughed, then told the administrator she wished to continue her maintenance work but wouldn't attend unregistered nighttime questioning. Before everyone, he agreed. Her employment would continue under its existing terms; testifying wouldn't cost her wages.

Only then did Viola leave the doorpost. Wearing no duty badge, she gave no orders to the patrol officers. She noted the designated contact and made sure Tessa had the paper bearing that person's name.

Before leaving East Ridge, Luna bought the locking diary she had wanted yesterday from a stationery shop. She chose a dark blue cover herself and pocketed the key. Adrian carried the wrapped parcel without asking whom she intended to write about first.

After lunch, they took the carriage back. Elena asked the driver to stop briefly on a sunny stretch so Luna could drink and shift position. Viola's left arm remained protected beneath her cloak. Adrian wasn't coughing, but accepted warm water anyway.

“You're becoming easier to look after,” Elena remarked.

“You don't sound pleased.”

“Fewer chances to scold you.”

“I could deliberately forget my gloves next time.”

She looked straight at him. “Absolutely not.”

He laughed. Realizing she'd been teased, she moved the almond biscuits to her side. After a while, she offered him one.

At the tower, the porter was repairing a crooked sign. He waved, asked about the mountain road, and only then asked about court. Answering, Luna realized the place hadn't been holding its breath awaiting her return from judgment.

Steam rose from the kitchen. Someone passed carrying clean sheets. The old clock by the stairs still ran a quarter-hour slow.

Her smile ached a little.

A court clerk verified the delivered orders. With the administrator present, the corresponding seals came down. Paper lifted a sliver of old paint from a doorframe with a barely audible sound. The accident room's seal stayed intact, its corridor clearly roped off.

Luna paused outside her drawing room.

“I thought opening the door would be louder.”

“Shall I kick it?” Adrian asked.

“Can you afford a replacement?”

“Better preserve the solemnity.”

Laughing, she let the administrator inspect first. The window catch was intact, the paper dry. Her chipped cup still stood on its shelf. What coated the bottom wasn't dust, but tea she'd forgotten to empty.

Someone from the kitchen brought her mint plant back. Before the room was sealed, the administrator had moved it to the kitchen windowsill so it wouldn't go untended. It had two fewer leaves, but damp soil. Tessa explained that someone had watered it daily. The leaves had made tea for an apprentice with a sore throat.

“I didn't authorize lending it out.”

“Last month you said whoever remembered to water it could pick the leaves.”

Luna remembered. She moved the plant toward the light without demanding replacement leaves.

A young researcher appeared with a borrowed ruler. He held the ruler tightly and seemed ready to hand it to the administrator and leave at once. Luna called him back to ask whether he'd finished his support-frame drawing.

“Yes. I wasn't sure I could still ask you to look.”

“An ordinary frame?”

“Ordinary. Two weights on it. No people.”

“Good. Don't add any yet.”

He laughed and spread the drawing on the empty table. Without touching powered equipment, Luna sat and identified a reversed support. Watching from the door, Adrian heard her familiar briskness return.

She finally put on her old coat. Tugging it with her left hand only tangled the turned-in collar. Adrian offered help, waited for her nod, straightened it, and stepped back.

“You're very practiced. Whose clothes do you usually straighten?”

“I used to be a footman.”

“What a pity. I'd prepared a misunderstanding.”

“You could ask instead.”

Luna looked at him. The next teasing line, which she had already prepared, suddenly failed to arrive when she needed it. Across the room, Elena glanced up from returning a borrowed map but answered for nobody.

A drawer caused the first trouble during tidying. Luna insisted it held only old paper. It also contained two rock-hard biscuits, a ribbon, and a button belonging to no instrument.

“Undeclared materials?” Adrian asked.

“The biscuits have exceeded their load limit,” Tessa said.

Even Cecilia laughed. Viola lifted no boxes, sitting to check ordinary tools against their names. Recognizing the button from Luna's present coat, she earnestly recommended repairing her clothes before attempting to repair the world.

Luna raised it left-handed. “This time, I agree to start small.”

When they finished, Tessa put her lent file away. Luna asked whether she'd like to help investigate old apparatus in future. After considering, Tessa said she'd rather clear the tower's maintenance backlog.

“If you need me to explain what I've seen, I'll come. But I don't want testimony to become an audition for a different job.”

Luna paused and solemnly agreed. Then she asked whether a thick wooden sleeve could make her pen easier to hold left-handed.

“That I can do. I'll make it tomorrow.”

They agreed on measurements and pay. The pen finally had a solution unrelated to dangerous experiments or personal debts.

Toward evening, the administrator accompanied them to inspect the restricted cabinet room. They stopped at the permitted position. The outer seals were intact. C-17 remained on its original support, its gray-silver layer unremarkable behind the closed inner cover.

The dissipation box containing D-17's remains wasn't opened that day. Luna didn't promise they could always recover what was lost. That unfixed layer image was gone. Future inquiries must rely on surviving tracings, records, and other independent sources.

“I still want to know what it was,” she said softly.

“So do I.”

“We aren't opening it today.”

“Not today.”

Turning away, she didn't look back again.

After dinner, ordinary oil lamps lit the outer room. While the others arranged their rooms, Luna sat at the drawing table with the button on a blank sheet. Adrian brought tea and found a chair cleared opposite her.

“Someone in the entrance hall asked whether they'd let the witch out,” she said.

“Who?”

“The firewood delivery man. He'd heard about a trial and feared they'd seal the stoves too.” She smiled. “I explained instead of arguing. He even carried the wood to the door.”

Adrian put down the tea and listened.

“But my first thought was still that they'd finally realized how dangerous I am.”

“You've done dangerous research. You've also corrected your judgments,” he said. “I won't erase the first part. But it isn't the only thing I'll know you by.”

She lowered her eyes. After setting down the cups, he remained seated, offering no hurried, beautiful guarantee.

“If I didn't do experiments here, what would you think I was good at?”

“Noticing what others overlook. Asking difficult questions when you admit you don't understand. Brewing tea that tastes like medicine.”

“You could leave out the last one.”

“You asked what you were good at.”

Her shoulders shook with laughter before she protected her right palm and settled herself. Eventually she drew the blank sheet toward her and wrote a crooked beginning left-handed.

Adrian didn't peek. When he rose, he asked whether she needed anything else.

“Save me a little time after breakfast tomorrow.”

“Of course. For research?”

Luna raised her face. This time, she didn't hide behind an experiment.

“For where I want to go next.”
