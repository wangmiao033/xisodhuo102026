# Chapter 172: She Guarded the Road Sitting Down

Viola had brought no sword.

At the carriage yard on the morning of February 3, Adrian found her asking the driver to move a hard case away from her left side. Her support needed room. Yesterday she'd checked with her physician: a short carriage journey, with rest, was permitted. Riding, combat, and lifting were not.

“This isn't new duty clearance,” she said.

“I haven't asked.”

“More than one person needs to hear it.”

The neutral duty sergeant responsible for today's journey nodded. Command and physical protection belonged to him. Viola was a voluntary companion offering route advice. The two visitors could still decide whether to make statements. Arranging a carriage didn't entitle anyone to deliver them by force.

Beth Rowan stood beside the other carriage with her folder. The registration agent stood beside a third carriage, carrying a thin case of copies for comparison, not originals removed from official custody. Neither person was bait accompanying valuable evidence. Neither had promised convenient answers.

Adrian intended to arrive ahead, but now wanted to remain. Viola noticed his repeated glances into the carriage.

“Worried?”

“A little.”

“Someone should confirm the receiving location hasn't changed.”

She wasn't ordering him away. He considered it and kept the original plan. Any change would be communicated through the assigned contact, not by Adrian inventing a shorter route in the street.

Isabella brought breakfast parcels and asked who wanted one. No ballot followed. Beth wasn't hungry. The princess left that parcel without explaining how much work the kitchen had done.

Adrian received a lighter one.

“Not a reward for supporting me.”

“Safe to eat, then.”

“Nor an invitation for tonight.” She frowned. “I'm starting to sound like a sales notice.”

He laughed and suggested calling it breakfast. She smiled, securing it with her right hand. Her burned left wrist didn't need extra work to demonstrate recovery.

The reading location occupied a side room of an ordinary public notarial hall. Adrian checked its responsible staff and designated room. Two separate tables shared a normal exit. Nobody asked him to sign the witnesses' statements in advance.

The documents here were specified certified copies. Some overlapping historical impressions remained unclear. They would compare directly readable text, not repeatedly expose anything to force out missing words.

Adrian thought of Luna and the earlier unspoken I can fix it. The room hadn't prepared an experiment without asking her.

Presently the assigned contact brought news: the carriages had stopped at Clockmakers' Street. Someone with a transfer notice demanded the agent accompany him to another verification location.

Breakfast lost its taste.

Adrian told the receiving official rather than racing off to rescue anyone. The arrangement hadn't changed. Staff would verify the notice through established contacts and ask duty personnel to keep the route clear. Adrian could wait indoors or accompany the liaison by a safe route, without overruling those on site.

He chose to go. He wanted to see what happened, without turning wanting to know into needing to be useful.

The wheels still stood on dry stone when he reached the corner. Viola sat inside the first carriage, door partly open, route sheet in her right hand and left arm supported. The sergeant was moving spectators away.

The man holding the notice wore an ordinary short coat and carried an administrative delivery badge. He insisted he was following instructions, not robbing anyone. The agent looked pale inside his carriage. This was the third time he'd heard refusal would count as abandonment.

“Ask him to read where it says the person must go,” Viola told the sergeant.

The notice concerned supplementary copies, listing an old reference and a custody location. It didn't require the applicant to change destinations. Its issue date preceded yesterday's independent reading arrangement.

“My superior said that means going for verification.”

The sergeant neither tore the notice up nor immediately arrested him. The badge established delivery authority, not that oral demands matched the written scope. The notice could be received normally and clarified with its issuer. It didn't empower its bearer to stop the convoy.

The man resisted stepping aside. Returning empty-handed would get him punished. Viola didn't guess his superior's name.

“You can request a receipt saying delivered, requirement disputed. You cannot take another person back as proof you've done your job.”

Beth recognized Adrian and asked whether the hall had stopped receiving them after all.

“It hasn't. The location is unchanged.”

She held her folder tightly, shoulders easing. No heroic guarantee would have helped more than the specific fact she needed.

A wagon delivering long boards slowed beside the crowd. Pedestrians squeezed toward the curb. Avoiding them, its driver let a front wheel slip beside a loose drain cover. The boards swung across half the road, blocking the second carriage.

A horse startled, tightening the reins. Viola gripped the frame and leaned forward instinctively. She stopped, briefly pale.

The sergeant had a guard steady the horse while another moved spectators toward the corner. The driver inspected the wheel. Viola didn't offer herself, or make an injured arm the rope everyone could borrow when frightened.

“Don't reverse the second carriage yet,” she advised. “Beth needs room to get out behind it. Ask whether she'd prefer the porch.”

After checking the narrow gap, the sergeant agreed. Someone steadied the step and asked Beth. She chose to move, carrying her folder slowly to a bakery porch whose owner offered a seat. The agent preferred to remain. His carriage door stayed usable; distress hadn't canceled his judgment.

Adrian wanted to lift a board. The sergeant asked him to stay beside the porch rather than add uncoordinated hands. Two workers fetched short wedges. The person managing the wheel called each movement before the load shifted. Nobody dragged timber across the horse's head.

A spectator shouted that the blockage must be deliberate. The baker swore: that drain cover had been loose last week, and she'd reported it. Adrian noted her claim without declaring the accident wholly unrelated to the interception. Things happening together didn't automatically form one plan.

As the crowd dispersed, the deliveryman moved aside. A guard stopped him. Nobody convicted him in the street. His badge, notice, and oral demand were recorded. He agreed to explain at the regular counter; an unnamed superior wouldn't end the inquiry.

Viola leaned against the padding, sweating. Asked what she needed, she answered rest. Please tell the sergeant she couldn't extend the journey. She didn't wait until everyone arrived before admitting discomfort.

They paused by a nearby safe courtyard entrance. Actual protection continued while she rested. Taking water in her right hand, she asked whether Adrian thought she'd only talked.

“I nearly tried lifting something I shouldn't either.”

“That isn't an answer.”

“I think you had them ask Beth first.”

Viola looked at the woman on the porch. Beth was brushing crumbs from her hat. Her folder remained on her lap, not surrendered to someone offering to take complete responsibility for her.

Viola finally released a long-held breath.

Once the road cleared, the sergeant reconfirmed destination and willingness. Both visitors would continue. Viola's carriage would return by the short route with her caregiver. She wouldn't remain as a symbol of safety. She'd offered advice; other people owned the remaining work.

Before leaving, she called Adrian back and produced a paper slip with her right hand: within the physician's permitted range, ten minutes, courtyard bench.

“Tomorrow afternoon. If you'd like.”

He smiled. “Not an inspection?”

“No broken railings to examine. I've asked.”

“I'd like that.”

She gave him the slip without adding something he must bring to prove sincerity. As the carriage moved, she settled her left shoulder properly and looked after herself.

Adrian walked the last stretch beside the rear carriage. Beth called through the window. If her account differed from everyone else's, would she be wasting the guards' effort?

“No. They protect your coming here, not a particular answer.”

Still uneasy, she repeated that she'd really forgotten the page. It wasn't entirely the counter's fault. Adrian knew; that admission remained in her statement. Only then did she lower the curtain.

From the other window, the agent said he wasn't changing his opinion either. Adrian laughed. That much agreement, he suggested, they could retain. The passengers weren't suddenly friends or allies. For this short journey they needn't negotiate how to please the people helping them.

They reached the hall late. Staff offered water before confirming willingness separately. Beth didn't apologize for the delay. Protection didn't make the agent retract his belief that preserving the archive was necessary.

Before the comparison began, Beth asked for somewhere to put her coat where she could still see it. A clerk showed her the peg beside her chair. The agent wanted his case kept closed until it was his turn. Neither request was treated as suspicious.

Adrian had expected the arrival to feel like the end of a dangerous story. Instead, people needed water, somewhere for their belongings, and a moment to remember what they had intended to say. He waited outside the separate questioning area rather than following Beth in to supply the answer he thought she wanted.

When she emerged briefly to ask where the washroom was, she thanked him for confirming the destination at the corner. Not for saving her entire life, not for resolving her husband's old agreement. He was glad she could name the actual help. He showed her the passage the staff had indicated and returned to his chair.

They compared readable copies. A completion status still didn't match the receiving inventory's scope and remained under inquiry. Overlapping historical impressions left one passage uncertain. Nobody supplied it from memory. Missing pages and the old project's mysteries weren't all recovered today.

As Adrian collected his permitted acknowledgment, a custodian asked whether an equipment-condition statement could be passed to Luna. Would she consider reviewing a non-contact inspection proposal for the old reader? This wasn't an order to repair it or removal of existing restrictions.

He could forward the question. He couldn't accept for her.

At home, Luna requested the complete condition report and existing safety requirements before deciding. Her right palm stayed cushioned. Another interesting problem hadn't healed it.

Isabella looked furious hearing about the street interception. She wanted the messenger's superior, then asked who was already investigating. With a formal record in place, she would supplement what she knew about the arrangements rather than issue a private command.

Before supper, she asked whether Adrian might finish their carriage-office chess game a week from now.

“No vote selected this.”

“I don't know that day's plans yet. May I answer later?”

Disappointed, she agreed. Viola's invitation hadn't won an election. Isabella wasn't a defeated candidate.

Adrian put tomorrow's slip on his desk. Some people had safely arrived; someone else had gone home to rest before reaching the destination. Neither had been called failure. The last delivery cart rattled outside. Only after its wheels faded did he slowly unwrap the breakfast he hadn't finished.
