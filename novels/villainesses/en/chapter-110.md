# Chapter 110: She Finally Stopped Calling Fear a Joke

On December 19, Luna announced that she could write left-handed.

She turned the page around.

Adrian studied it. “Why is that fish wearing your name?”

“It's a signature.”

“It appears to be trying to swim away.”

She propped her face on her left hand and sighed. Three letters leaned in different directions; the final stroke crossed the date. She had slept fitfully. Her palm still hurt that morning. The numbness in her fingertips had eased considerably, but Hensley had not given permission to test magic merely because she was improving.

There would be no experiment today.

Not a punishment carefully imposed after failure. The room was out of service, the plate destroyed, and the researcher needed recovery. Courage could replace none of those requirements. The administrator had dispatched the incident report with the official post. Records and damaged components awaited independent examination.

Luna turned over the dreadful signature.

“Fine. Only fish today.”

“They'll be delighted,” Adrian said.

Morning light was softer in the guesthouse. Elena worked on merchant correspondence at another table. Viola wrote to her posting with the new medical instructions beside her. Cecilia went downstairs to ask about an extra lunch manageable with one hand. Everyone had a life to continue. Nobody formed a circle to watch Luna display remorse.

That was surprisingly difficult to get used to.

She shifted once in her chair, then again. Adrian looked up from his book and asked if she was uncomfortable.

“I'm deciding what a researcher forbidden to research should do.”

“You chose fish.”

“I've drawn two. The kingdom needs no more for now.”

“Help me choose a passage.” He put the travelogue between them. “Something Elena would enjoy tonight.”

Elena didn't look up, though her ears reddened.

“I can hear you.”

“Then you can make suggestions.”

“Not the captain's love letter. He sent the same one to three people.”

Luna laughed and tapped the page with her left hand.

“My rival must first learn to address envelopes.”

“He isn't your rival,” Adrian said.

“What am I, then?”

She paused after asking.

Elena's pen stopped. Viola turned a sheet unusually slowly, as though making clear that she did not intend to overhear an answer she shouldn't.

Adrian looked at Luna. Her bandaged hand did not entitle her to a sweeter answer than he meant.

“A friend I care about. Elena and I are seeing each other. That hasn't changed.”

Luna nodded, a little smile remaining.

“That sounds like you.”

“I don't want yesterday to answer other questions for us.”

“Neither do I.” She said it quickly, then slowed. “Sometimes, watching you care for someone, I wonder where I can stand.”

Elena put down her pen. She neither answered for him nor treated Luna's question as an attack requiring immediate repulsion.

“Today, as my friend,” Adrian said. “If you have something else to say later, we can discuss it seriously. I won't let a rescue leave things conveniently unclear.”

Luna touched the page.

“Then today your friend wants orange biscuits.”

“That, we can arrange.”

The delivery cart returned at noon. The pastry shopkeeper had received the postponement and sent a note: the biscuits could wait until tomorrow evening, after which she'd have to bake a fresh batch. There was no charge for imaginary restorative broth. Luna laughed so hard her shoulders shook, apparently knowing exactly who had added the last sentence.

Elena suggested that the administrator collect the order while in town. She first checked the quantity and payment with Luna. No ducal declaration that she would buy the whole shop; no competition with an injured woman.

“Why would you help?” Luna asked.

“I want some too. And letting you eat quietly is usually preferable to letting you talk because you're bored.”

“You refuse to let me be moved.”

“Be moved. Don't eat them all.”

They exchanged a look and laughed together.

The weather brightened that afternoon. Hensley allowed Luna a short spell on the sheltered porch. Adrian placed her chair where sunlight reached her shoes and sat on the neighboring step.

A sweeper pushed snow into a narrow ridge. It looked white until the buried coal dust showed. Beyond the courtyard came the sound of a horse being brushed.

Luna listened, then said that when she arrived at the tower, she hated how slowly everything happened.

“Letters. Parts. Even the kettle seemed to await academy approval.”

“And now?”

“The kettle is still slow.”

After a pause, she touched the button she had sewn onto her cuff.

“I thought if anything slowed down, someone else would take it. Publish the conclusion first. Get the equipment first. Decide that whatever I'd brought out of that old instruments office was worthless. If I stopped, I'd end up behind those forms again.”

Adrian watched her fingers. She wasn't touching a wound. She was checking that she had learned one small thing.

“I saw you reach for the yellow handle yesterday,” she continued. “I knew D-17 would be destroyed. But losing the record wasn't my first thought.”

She drew a breath. Cold air thinned her voice.

“I was afraid you'd come and pull me away.”

Adrian looked up.

“I could see the return light on my fingers. It wasn't withdrawing as we'd expected. I thought you'd see me stumble and touch the ring. Then you'd fall, and the others would come closer. Perhaps we'd be left with a perfect record proving I'd placed everyone willing to trust me beside the same danger.”

Her smile was gone.

“I was afraid of losing all of you.”

It came quietly. No lingering invitation, no pause to see whether he blushed. Nothing like her polished teasing.

Adrian did not promise that it could never happen.

“That's why we practiced going around the shield. I didn't touch the ring. Tessa reminded me too.”

“I saw.”

“You called stop. You told me which handle. Those things count.”

Her eyes reddened as she watched the ridge of snow.

“It still went wrong.”

“Yes. We have to find out why. You can be afraid and still tell us what you know.”

A tear fell onto her cuff. She seemed offended by the dampness and hurried to wipe it with her left hand, tangling her hair. Adrian offered a handkerchief. He did not wipe her face for her or suggest she had finally become an ordinary girl.

She had always been someone capable of fear. Usually, she spoke first so nobody could see it.

“This handkerchief,” she said thickly. “Will I owe you another one for ages?”

“You can wash it and give it back.”

“You said that last time.”

“I haven't changed my mind.”

She laughed into the cloth, still sounding tearful.

“Could you sit higher? Looking up like that makes you seem to be awaiting a solemn verdict.”

He fetched another chair, checked that it didn't block the doorway, and sat on her left. She did not take his hand. Her shoulder brushed his sleeve, then withdrew slightly.

“This is enough.”

They sat quietly. Baked apples scented the air from the kitchen window. Luna suddenly wondered whether the doctor would let her eat them. Adrian suggested asking at the afternoon check. There was no need to turn the menu into an application immediately.

She laughed again. Afterward, she didn't rush to become the woman nothing could trouble.

Indoors, she asked the administrator for the research plan she was authorized to amend. With her left hand she drew a crooked circle while the clerk took her dictated revision. Not a permanent end to every investigation. Until the control-side failure was understood, no operator would connect personal magic to a comparable circuit. Continuing required an entirely external adjustment method and a new assessment.

“You'd abandon the fastest approach we have?” Tessa asked.

“Right now its fastest achievement is depriving us of another usable hand.”

The technician watched her, then discarded the long argument she had prepared.

“I can sketch mechanical transmission. I can't promise the same response.”

“Draw something different first.”

No vow that the future would work. Luna had turned her fear of losing companions into a concrete change rather than leaving it in a tear on the porch.

Before dinner, Elena received the merchant's corrected handover list. The previously missing recipient was named, but one amount still failed to match. Adrian expected her to return the letter immediately. Instead, she turned the half-calculated account toward him.

“Would you check this column?”

“Because I'm good at sums?”

“Because you'll ask whether I've made a mistake instead of saying the duke's daughter must be right.”

They calculated separately and found a delivery charge entered in two subtotals. Elena stared at the number with an unusual expression.

“I copied it incorrectly.”

“You haven't sent the letter.”

“I nearly challenged the manager over it last night.”

She corrected the page without turning an ordinary error into a subordinate's failure to warn her. Then she closed the book and thanked Adrian seriously.

“Does this count as the start of our evening?” he asked.

“If you're willing to call a delivery charge scenery.”

“I prefer watching you discover a mistake without blaming the light.”

She tried to restrain a smile, failed, and squeezed the fingers of his free hand.

“Your compliments grow stranger.”

“They're true.”

She pulled her chair closer so they could both hear the returning footsteps downstairs.

The orange biscuits arrived that evening.

The administrator tucked the receipt under the basket. The ribbon remained knotted. Luna declared that her packaging had survived the mountain road. Viola brought small scissors and, with permission, trimmed the trailing end while leaving the knot as a handle marker.

“Some things needn't be taken apart completely.”

“Are you offering advice?” Luna asked.

“I'm rescuing biscuits.”

Adrian set the basket in the middle and took the last portion. Luna displayed her two left-handed fish as the only research achievements approved for exhibition. Cecilia studied them and asked whether she could keep one.

“Why?”

“Mira's aid station is collecting little pictures for the dining room. That one looks as though it would encourage people to eat.”

Luna blinked, then chose the drawing more closely resembling a fish.

“Don't say a witch drew it.”

“I'll ask how you'd like it signed.”

Luna handed it over, smiling. Perhaps a day without results was not quite the right description after all.

Later, Adrian and Elena read the captain's ridiculous love letter. They agreed that he should begin by saying different things to different people.

“And you, today?” Elena asked.

“I said what I could genuinely promise.”

She closed the book and leaned gently against him.

“I heard.”

The tower's lights went out earlier than usual. The carriage bearing the incident report had gone down the mountain. On another road, an envelope stamped by a court was traveling toward them.

Luna did not yet know what it would require her to face.

Tonight she laid the handkerchief beside her bed and did not get up to find a pen.
