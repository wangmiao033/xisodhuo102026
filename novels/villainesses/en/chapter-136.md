# Chapter 136: A Search List, Not a List of Supporters

The first name Isabella saw at the printshop was her own.

It stood above every name being sought, in larger type, with ample room beside it for the royal arms.

On the morning of January 11, the printer eagerly explained his proof: people could see at a distance who was leading the effort.

Isabella studied the line and almost nodded.

Adrian stood behind her without answering for her.

After a moment, she returned the proof to the bench.

“Move my name to the end of my explanation. Put Seeking Information at the top.”

The printer hesitated. “It will look like an ordinary notice.”

“It's for ordinary people to read.”

Cecilia looked up, then continued checking the last-seen dates on two proofs without congratulating the princess. One year was missing a digit. More attractive lettering must not add a year to someone's disappearance.

Between yesterday and this morning, the office had met all six households through their established contacts. The original staff had checked the corresponding petition numbers. Current records contained neither confirmation that the people had been found nor replies delivered to the families explaining their whereabouts. That established only that the searches remained unresolved, not that all eight had met the same fate. It certainly did not place them on a death roll.

Five households agreed to publish seven missing adults' names and limited search information. The sixth permitted inquiries, not publication of the name. That case remained in the total. The public notice explained that another person's identity was withheld at the family's request. The responsible office must still handle it.

Refusing a place on a wall did not forfeit anyone's right to a search.

Clara arrived with soap powder still dusting her apron. She read her brother's name first, then asked why the contact address was not her home.

“As you requested yesterday, information comes to the office first,” Cecilia said.

Clara read it again. “I thought you'd find that troublesome.”

“It means another handoff.”

“Then—”

“That's the handoff we promised to make.”

Clara fell silent and ran a fingertip along the paper's edge, avoiding the fresh ink.

Adrian read her entry aloud, word by word. Milo Senn, hired four years earlier to transport equipment with an old project. Last verifiable location based on departure and transfer slips. Actual observations requested; rumors of a matching name must not be treated as proof he had been found. It said nothing about childhood mischief or a badly repaired bucket.

Clara nodded. “That's right.”

Another relative wanted a bold heading: Missing Royal Personnel.

“They had royal slips. Why can't you write it?” the gray-haired man asked.

Isabella did not immediately contradict him. Cecilia turned his copied slip to the employment field. It recorded temporary project labor, not an appointment to the palace staff. The notice could identify an unresolved disappearance connected to a royal project. It could not change someone's identity to make people care.

“They already deserve to be looked for as who they were,” Adrian said.

The man studied him, then slowly sat. He did not seem intent on deceiving anyone. He had waited so long for an answer that, without large enough letters, he feared his son would become small again.

The printer reset the proof. Isabella's statement appeared at the end, explicitly acknowledging receipt of the eight-person petition summary and her failure to ask whether anyone had continued handling it after referral. She had now requested a review. It did not accept guilt for every person involved or claim she had brought anyone home today.

“People will use that passage against you,” Ellery said.

He had come to check delivery of Isabella's statement, not to choose the families' answers about publicity.

She looked at the still-wet type.

“Then at least let them have something I actually did.”

She approved the printing. No attendant quietly replaced the family-approved wording with a more flattering version. Original petitions and identity documents remained with their respective custodians. The printshop received only the final public text, not bundles of addresses and private correspondence.

Before noon, the first notices went up on the approved search board.

There were no musicians and no princess addressing the street from the steps. A young bread delivery worker paused over several lines and pointed to one name he thought he had encountered. Cecilia asked him aside to record an approximate place and time, first establishing whether it was only hearsay. No crowd gathered shouting that someone had been found.

He meant last winter. The verified last journey dated several years earlier. A shared name remained possible. The lead could be recorded; the conclusion must wait for checking.

Clara stayed beside the board until another person crowded close. Then she stepped aside.

She did not weep at seeing her brother's name on the wall. Instead, Adrian saw her counting the time left to deliver her laundry.

He asked whether someone should help carry it to the corner.

“Do you know which basket goes to which house?”

“No.”

“Then someone's bedsheets will arrive as tablecloths.” She finally smiled. “I'll do it. You keep the answers safe.”

She lifted her basket and left. Isabella watched without sending an attendant after her with an unsolicited bag of compensation.

A woman in an old gray cloak waited for the crowd to thin before approaching. She belonged to the household that had refused publication of a name. Having left contact information at the office the night before, she had taken a detour to see the board.

“Does not wanting the name printed make things difficult for you?”

Isabella was about to say Of course not when the woman indicated the seven entries.

“They all agreed. If something goes wrong later, will people say it's because I wouldn't help?”

Cecilia took her aside, clear of those reading, and lowered her voice.

“You've provided what you can. Publication is separate. It doesn't make anyone more entitled to have their missing person sought.”

The woman looked at Isabella, apparently still waiting for the person she believed truly decided matters to agree.

Isabella seemed to realize that a word of permission was insufficient. She asked the search-registration clerk to bring the case's receipt counterpart. The woman could see that its number remained, its follow-up contact period had not been canceled, and it had not been moved to the end.

“If we need anything more, we'll contact you privately,” Isabella said. “You won't have to stand beside my name first.”

The woman put away the counterpart without offering an extravagant bow. She drew her cloak close and quietly thanked her.

Adrian watched Isabella watching her leave. She had not assembled eight lives into a tidy display of achievement. For once, she had not reached out to pull back someone who declined to stand in it.

The first objection arrived quickly.

Lord Crowe submitted a written question to the secretariat. Public activity involving unresolved old-project petitions might exceed Isabella's approved scope of access and explanation, he argued. He requested her sources, authority to publish, and use of funds. The final paragraph sought review of her fitness to handle succession affairs.

He did not demand that families' search notices be torn down in the street. He had circled Isabella's own statement.

Her finger stopped at fitness.

“A couple of days ago, someone agreed for me. Today someone thinks I shouldn't speak for myself.”

“They're different,” Adrian said. “Your statement can be questioned. A family's consent to publication doesn't grant you the right to release entire archives.”

She looked up coldly.

“You think I shouldn't have done it either?”

“I think we should provide the basis for each part, not treat an objection as a defeat.”

Cecilia separated printing records from arrangements for the families' review. The published list used information voluntarily supplied by families and limited details the responsible office permitted for the search. Isabella was responsible for her statement. Costs reflected actual printing. W-24, original military documents, and restricted fiscal records had not been published with the notice.

Ellery organized these as attachments to a reply. The secretariat's notice required supplementary explanations within two days. It did not instantly strip Isabella of succession eligibility.

But the challenge would enter later deliberations. The absence of a ruling did not make it a paper she could simply crumple.

Isabella put it away and prepared to return to the palace.

The printer cautiously asked whether her statement should be removed to avoid further trouble.

She looked back at the search board.

“Correct an error, yes. Pretend I never said something correct merely because someone questions it, no.”

She did not impose that choice on everyone. Families could still change the scope of their public information through the office. Her willingness to face a challenge did not require them to stand in the wind beside her.

Adrian and Cecilia ate a late lunch at a corner stall that afternoon.

Isabella had returned to prepare her reply. Elena was handling family correspondence. Luna and Viola were resting according to their medical instructions. No one had to crowd onto one bench to prove closeness.

Cecilia broke a hot filled pastry in half. The filling was hotter than it looked. After one bite, she lowered her head with the solemn expression of someone awaiting a delayed judgment.

Adrian hurriedly passed the water.

“You're allowed to say it's hot.”

She sipped. “If I admit it now, will I look foolish for that first bite?”

“No. It will just mean the pastry was hot.”

She laughed and left the other half on its paper to cool, abandoning the attempt to oppose steam with solemnity.

They watched people pass without immediately reciting the day's difficulties. Cecilia gathered hair loosened by the wind with her free hand. Adrian held down the light paper wrapper.

“I'm glad you agreed to walk with me today.”

“Did you know we'd be busy this long?”

“No. I'd still have asked.”

She looked at him without testing his response.

“But I don't want every meal together to become a discussion of your responsibilities. Do you like their filling?”

He tasted it carefully. Too salty, he said, but certainly good.

“We could share one next time.” She added, “I'll ask next time.”

Adrian nodded without attaching a promise she had not requested to an ordinary invitation. The pastry cooled, and they finished it at leisure.

Back at the office, the new leads had brought home no missing person.

A retired old-project registrar had left his name, however. The occupations on the notice reminded him of a hazard and personnel transfer register he had once compiled. Archived indexes for five old receiving points might help trace final journeys. He did not claim to remember the eight people. He would identify only the register references he could.

Cecilia first verified his former employment and the custodian he named, then submitted the inquiry through an authorized desk. The original was already at the capital's old-project archive. No carriage needed to race into the night after it.

Adrian felt a small stirring of recognition at the five old district names on the acknowledgment. He did not record that feeling as an established connection.

The day's last visitor was still waiting outside. Cecilia invited him in and filed the two separate leads involving matching names.

Wind lifted a corner of the notice. Adrian borrowed paste, fastened it again, and waited until it stopped curling before returning the brush. The bread delivery worker would still be able to read those names tomorrow.

The new list had brought no one home at once.

But from today, anyone calling something closed would have to answer which matter had ended, and for whom.
