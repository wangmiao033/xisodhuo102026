# Chapter 251: What Didn't Belong in the Box

On the evening of April 14, the warehouse agreement reached Elena inside a box embroidered with two birds.

The steward said his employer had borrowed an old family box to protect important papers from damp. Adrian was willing to believe him until the second page revealed a marriage guarantee.

Outside, workers were unloading tools. Repairing a line hadn't restored everything. Relief workers needed rest; supplies needed somewhere they could be stored and returned without obstructing ordinary traffic. Parts of the noble rescue network were genuinely functioning. That hadn't created a warehouse from nothing.

The offer supplied exactly the space they lacked: two dry rooms near the cart road, at a rent so low Adrian checked the decimal again. The term covered the immediate difficult period.

The price followed. Two adult heirs must exchange declarations of intended engagement before delivery, witnessed by the Rosenberg household. Their future family connection would guarantee neither side's sudden withdrawal.

“Nobody requires a wedding now,” the steward explained. “Only stability for the cooperation.”

Elena placed the second page beside the first. “Then tell me separately who owns the rooms and who decides the marriage.”

A young woman sat near the door. An adult co-owner in one household, she hadn't been summoned merely to nod. The other prospective partner was away on estate business. Everybody knew the families were discussing marriage, the steward said. Their parents approved. Surely they needn't make a generous offer sound ugly.

The woman spoke softly enough to stop Adrian's pen.

“I knew they were discussing it. I didn't agree to guarantee these rooms with it.”

She hadn't objected yesterday, the steward replied.

Yesterday, she said, she'd been asked about lending empty rooms. Nobody asked whether she could refuse marriage later if relief work still needed space.

Adrian almost closed the box. Elena first asked whether the woman wished to continue here or consult her own adviser. She would continue, but didn't want to become a persecuted witness whose story traveled across the capital. She still had to live with her family.

“Then today we deal with this offer,” Elena said.

She asked the recorder to separate warehouse conditions from marriage, retaining no unnecessary personal information. She neither refused on behalf of the absent adult nor accepted on behalf of the woman present.

“Warehouses aren't free,” the steward said. “My employer needs security.”

“Then discuss rent, a deposit, and actual damage. Not somebody's lifetime added after the deposit.”

“People might say such principles come easily when you already have someone willing to stay.”

Adrian looked up. Elena didn't seek rescue from him.

“Wanting someone beside me doesn't make him owe me an engagement. Even if I had nobody, I couldn't sign for this lady.”

Only the tool cart rattled outside. The woman's grip on her sleeve gradually loosened.

Negotiation didn't become easy. The steward consulted his waiting employer and returned with another quotation: ordinary short-term rent without the marriage condition, requiring more advance payment. The owner neither surrendered the rooms free nor revealed himself as a snarling villain. His goods needed somewhere else to go. Empty rooms still had costs.

Elena requested the breakdown rather than accepting a total whose parts nobody could discuss. After seeing it, she was silent for a long time. The alternative was real, and its higher price was real too. Refusing the marriage clause had not obliged the owner to pretend that moving his own goods would cost nothing.

Adrian knew why. I'll find a way couldn't conceal their existing shortfall and commitments again. Nor did he want the young woman to think her refusal had forced everyone back into the rain.

“Don't count both rooms yet,” he said. “Equipment needing shelter tonight isn't the same as goods that might arrive later.”

“You want to reduce what we accept?” Elena asked.

“First establish the need. We can't hide the rent until tomorrow to prove refusing the engagement costs nothing.”

She considered him, then requested the actual storage list. They went into the yard. This shouldn't become a discussion consisting only of words.

A long crate wouldn't fit the smaller room. The mover had tested the doorway and refused to tilt and force it through. Inactive equipment could still be damaged by impact. Adrian had estimated that one room would suffice. Beside the door he admitted his mistake and measured the crate properly.

The woman joined them. The larger room's side entrance would admit it, but neighbors shared the passage. She could promise warehouse space, not cart noise outside their windows all night. The steward began to say emergencies were naturally different.

She interrupted. Naturally couldn't answer for other people again.

They didn't obtain both rooms. The larger one would be available during specified hours, with delivery ending by the agreed time and residents' exits kept clear. Actual funds covered a limited term; extensions required another agreement. Ordinary supplies would remain at their previously agreed locations, not quietly move here to create the appearance of complete consolidation.

Before handover, the tailor who lived beside the entrance returned. Hearing Elena had agreed, he'd assumed complaints would be useless. He stood outside with a basket of unfinished clothes. The young woman asked which access times must be preserved.

His wife needed to go for a dressing change at night. If carts blocked the step, leaving a gap somebody could squeeze through wasn't enough.

Elena moved the proposed unloading line outward herself. The steward pointed out that one fewer cart could wait there.

“Then one fewer cart waits. We can't permit people to leave on paper while making them climb an axle.”

Adrian rearranged the queue with the drivers. The first vehicle would unload and depart before the next entered. Several people's urgency needn't become a wall.

The tailor wasn't immediately convinced. He waited until the cart actually moved, then walked the route with his wife before setting his basket indoors. He hadn't supported any particular contract scheme. He'd established that he could still leave home tonight.

Returning, the woman told Elena she'd discovered that owning the warehouse didn't mean deciding everything that happened around it with one sentence.

“I nearly assumed that too,” Elena admitted.

She recorded her correction beside the original diagram rather than keeping only a handsome revised version. Adrian had once thought helping meant ensuring she never made mistakes. Now he could let her acknowledge one without rushing to cover it.

Pricing, signatures, and unloading consumed the remaining daylight. Their arrangement cost more and held less than the original offer. It contained no prospective married couple entered in the relief accounts.

Taking her copy, the woman asked whether Elena regretted it.

“I regret not asking sooner why the box was so pretty.”

She stared, then laughed. Her document went into an ordinary damp-proof bag. The embroidered box returned with the steward. It remained its owner's property, not their victory souvenir.

Before leaving, she said perhaps she really would marry the other person. They didn't dislike each other. She simply didn't want tonight to become the reason she could never say no.

Elena hoped that when they discussed it, they would discuss themselves, not two rooms.

Adrian entered the actual additional rent. Preventing coercion couldn't be converted into money for a driver. The driver still waited for acknowledgment, and carts couldn't be unloaded with praise.

Viola rested next door after removing the outer protective gear she no longer needed to wear. Her left arm remained supported. Medical staff had just left after setting the next examination time. Seeing Adrian pass, she asked him to watch her door briefly.

“Only keep out people wanting directions. Wake me for genuine emergencies. Don't announce that I've lost the ability to judge anything.”

He agreed and sat outside. Through the partly open door, she asked whether this demoted an administrator to a porter.

“I've already mismeasured one warehouse door tonight. Here's one that doesn't require an estimate.”

She laughed quietly. What if someone brought an extremely reasonable request?

“I'll ask whether it can wait. Not who can speak loudest.”

A messenger soon wanted her immediate signature on tomorrow's proposed duty roster. Adrian checked the covering note. Its deadline was later that night; nothing urgent had changed. The messenger admitted he feared forgetting and wanted it finished now. They left it with the coordinator still on duty for its allotted time. Viola's rest wouldn't pay for somebody else's anxiety.

Silence lasted long enough that Adrian thought she'd fallen asleep. As he began moving his chair away, she spoke.

“I do want someone who chooses to stay.”

He stopped.

“Not out of gratitude. Not because my rules are long. But that doesn't require you to give me a pleasant answer tonight.”

He didn't promise to guard this door forever. The half hour he'd agreed to hadn't ended, and he would stay until handover. If she wanted another conversation later, they could have it when both were awake.

“Very unromantic.”

“I know.”

“Better than saying everyone matters exactly the same.”

She drew the door a little closer, leaving a gap through which real urgency could reach her. Adrian resumed his seat. Remaining outside could be a way of approaching someone without invading her room.

When the agreed interval ended, relief arrived. He explained the papers still requiring Viola's own review before finding Elena. Two partly filled cups waited beside her at an empty dining table.

“You said I had someone who wanted me beside her,” he said, sitting opposite.

“Was I wrong?”

“No. I'd just like to hear it outside a negotiation.”

She considered him. The tips of her ears reddened.

“I want your company. Now. No warehouse and no witnesses.”

“Two cups.”

“They won't testify.”

Laughing, he poured the warm water. She offered her hand. He held it beside the table without converting the moment into a deed claiming their future in advance.

Dinner arrived with an attendant from the relief hall. He carried no summons, only a leaflet inviting witnesses to the South Street chapel tomorrow morning. It claimed a miracle had identified unwilling bearers and called for residents to leave names supporting designation outside the relief hall.

Cecilia had requested preservation of the original. She hadn't established its issuer or endorsed its miracle. Before a crowd assembled, she wanted to examine the venue permission and available witness accounts.

Adrian placed the leaflet on a clean space rather than beneath a bowl. Elena read it and asked whether anyone had already been denied food because of it.

Nothing confirmed, the attendant said. People feared tomorrow would be different.

“Don't record fear as something that has happened,” she replied. “Don't wait until it happens before listening either.”

Outside, the final cart departed. The warehouse held equipment and goods, not two people's engagement. Tomorrow they would have to find out who wanted to place another kind of promise into frightened hands.
