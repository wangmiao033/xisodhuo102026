# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume One: A Betrothal Written in Blood
### Chapter 25: I Want to Stay by Your Side

At nine forty on the morning of October fifth, the appointment column in Mr. Hale's diary was still empty.

Matthew Holt was supposed to arrive at ten.

No one had seen him since he'd left the Archives Preservation Society dinner the night before.

Adrian stood by the office window, looking up every time a gray carriage passed.

"Should we go straight to his house?" he asked.

"No," Hale said. "The threat mentioned his family. Turning up at his door could make his sister even more vulnerable."

Elena had already placed the club's visitor register, the carriage records, and the threatening note in separate evidence sleeves.

"I've asked our guards to file a report and requested that the municipal patrol investigate his disappearance," she said. "We shouldn't call it an abduction before we know what happened."

"He left his coat behind."

"That gives us cause for concern. It doesn't identify whoever took him."

Adrian nodded.

A week ago, he would have insisted on rushing through the capital after any suspicious carriage.

Now he could see how dangerous that might be for the person inside it.

Hale's young assistant burst into the office.

"We've found the carriage."

Everyone looked up.

"A gray coach stopped at the old harbor weigh station last night. The driver used a temporary pass marked 'Judicial Document Transfer.' There's no record of it leaving this morning."

"The weigh station?" Adrian asked.

"It's rented out as private warehouse space now. The tenant has no authority to detain anyone."

Hale took his coat.

"I'll ask the magistrate for an order to inspect the premises. Wait for my signal."

Adrian glanced at Elena.

She was looking at him.

"Through the proper entrance," they said together.

They both laughed, just a little.

The old weigh station sat behind a line of low warehouses by the river. Faded markings for coal wagons remained on its street-facing doors.

When Adrian and Elena arrived, municipal officers were already checking the vehicles in the yard. They remained outside while the officers carried out their work, rather than forcing their way in with Rosenberg guards.

A warehouse tenant approached the magistrate before anyone could question him.

"I rented the upstairs room for temporary records storage," the man complained. "Last night someone claiming to be from the Judicial Office told me to clear it. They didn't even pay the rent!"

"Do you have their authorization?" the magistrate asked.

The tenant produced a sheet bearing a circular stamp.

**Old Records Preservation Office.**

Adrian recognized the name immediately.

That was the office receiving the monthly Fate Maintenance Fee.

But the authorization permitted the use of a room.

It said nothing about holding a person against his will.

The magistrate asked the tenant to witness the inspection, then went upstairs with two patrol officers.

The second-floor door had been bolted from the outside.

A faint noise came from within.

"Is someone there?" the magistrate called.

No reply.

An officer drew back the bolt.

Footsteps hurried across the room.

Matthew Holt stood by the window, pale and disheveled. Ink stained his cuffs. A fresh scrape marked the back of one hand.

When he saw the patrol officers, he recoiled.

"Did they send you too?"

The magistrate displayed his badge and inspection order.

"Municipal patrol. Someone reported that you may have been threatened. Are you free to leave this room?"

Matthew stared at the open doorway.

For a moment he looked incapable of believing it was real.

"No. Last night they told me my sister had been hurt. They brought me here to check some documents, then locked the door and told me to sign a replacement transfer record."

"Who did?"

"A masked man and an assistant wearing the old Judicial Office badge. I didn't see their names."

Adrian stepped forward from behind the officers.

"Mr. Holt. The independent patrol has contacted your sister's clinic. There's no report of her being injured."

Matthew's shoulders sagged.

"They said if I didn't sign, she wouldn't get home safely."

"Did you sign anything?" Hale asked.

"No. I told them I'd have to see the original ledger first."

The magistrate examined the table in the presence of two witnesses.

An undated statement had been prepared, with spaces left for Matthew's signature.

Its heading read:

**Correction of Historical File Transfer Records.**

According to the text, all materials for R-074 had been properly transferred seven years earlier. Any discrepancies in the surviving seals were merely errors made while producing training copies.

"They wanted you to fill in the missing pieces of history with a convenient answer," Adrian said.

Matthew stared at the paper.

"Yes."

Elena kept away from the evidence table.

"Please preserve that as an unsigned document," she told the magistrate. "It isn't Mr. Holt's testimony."

The magistrate had Matthew confirm that he hadn't signed and entered his answer into the inspection record.

A disturbance sounded in the yard below.

Someone had attempted to drive away in the gray carriage. The patrol required the driver to verify ownership and sign the exit register first.

Adrian tensed.

"Should we pursue him?"

"Let the patrol handle it," Elena said.

Adrian took a slow breath.

He stayed where he was.

Matthew sat and drank a little water. His hands were still shaking.

"I'm not a brave man," he said. "Seven years ago, I knew something was wrong with the records. I kept quiet because my superior told me to."

"You refused to sign a false declaration today," Adrian said. "That's important."

"You don't blame me?"

"I don't yet know what you knew back then. We should establish that before deciding what responsibility you bear."

Matthew looked up.

For the first time, something steadier returned to his eyes.

That afternoon, Matthew agreed to let an independent notary examine his private work notebook.

It was kept in a locker in the old archives employees' room. He held the key, and the notebook was retrieved under the joint supervision of a magistrate and Hale.

A thin blue volume emerged, its corners rubbed almost white.

It contained dates from the R-074 transfers.

Tucked into the back were several payment summaries.

Adrian recognized the reference at once.

"B-17."

Matthew nodded.

"Every month, the sealed-records accounts received a transfer from a royal special fund. On paper, it passed through the Old Records Preservation Office and then on to the Temple's Old Contract Records desk. After each settlement, my office was told to update the preservation status."

"What did the money pay for?" Elena asked.

"I don't know. The records said 'maintaining archival stability,' but there was never an itemized list. I acknowledged receipt. I didn't authorize the expenditure."

Hale noted the notebook pages, receipt numbers, and the time of examination.

"These documents support the existence of a longstanding financial and archival relationship between the Judicial Office and the Temple," he cautioned. "They don't, by themselves, establish that either institution conspired to forge the treason letter."

"We need the underlying approvals," Adrian said, "and whoever ordered the R-074 entry changed."

Matthew looked up.

"I'm willing to explain what happened. But I need my sister protected through the proper authorities."

"You and your lawyer can decide the form of your testimony," Elena said. "No one will expect you to claim knowledge you don't have."

Matthew nodded.

Hale didn't immediately take the blue notebook.

"Are you certain you want us to rely on these entries? If they show an error you made, you may be required to explain that too."

Matthew stared at the book.

"Yes. But mark the mistakes first."

He turned to a line in the middle, where an old case number had been crossed out.

"When I first received R-074, the transfer sheet said 'temporary custody, pending review.' Several months later, I was told to amend it to 'receipt complete' using a new form. I refused."

"Who ordered you?" Adrian asked.

"Someone conveyed an instruction from above. There's no name in the surviving record. I know you want one, but I won't invent it."

"Not knowing is a better answer than accusing someone at random."

Matthew turned to the receipts sewn into the back cover.

They weren't the Treasury's entire accounts. They were small acknowledgment slips routinely returned to clerks when transfers were reconciled with the Temple.

Each carried a Judicial Office receipt stamp and a five-petaled seal from the Temple.

Three also bore the same contact name.

**Evelyn Vale.**

"Does that establish that Ms. Vale personally received the money?" Elena asked.

"No. She was an authorized contact on the counter's register. Someone might have signed on her behalf. We'd need the Temple's original receipts to distinguish that."

Hale compared the dates carefully.

On several transfers, the Judicial Office had recorded completion one day before the Temple acknowledged receipt.

"That's not necessarily forgery," he warned. "Transfers between institutions can take a day. But if the offices claim simultaneous dual custody, they'll need to account for the discrepancy."

Matthew rubbed his forehead.

"That's why I wouldn't sign the correction. They never let me see the complete third copy afterward."

Adrian looked at those thin slips of paper and remembered the registry page lost in the warehouse fire.

If a record could be made to disappear beneath the phrase *all procedures completed*, any clerk who insisted on writing down the actual date became a problem.

"We don't have the whole answer today," he said. "But at least we know which original books to request next."

Under the notary's supervision, Hale distinguished the notebook from the individual slips in the evidence record.

The originals still belonged to Matthew, who chose to provide certified copies and keep the sealed originals until a court issued a proper production order.

Elena didn't pressure him.

She helped him check that the witnesses' signatures were complete.

"If anyone threatens you again," she said, "contact the magistrate. Don't feel obliged to take risks alone just because you've agreed to help us."

Matthew nodded slowly.

Before they parted, Adrian noticed he'd finally straightened his crooked tie.

That one ordinary gesture made Adrian feel more relieved than the discovery of another ledger.

By the time they left the Judicial Office, evening had settled over the city.

Adrian walked beside Elena toward their carriage.

If he hadn't met her on September seventeenth, he wondered, would he still be thinking like a player who wanted nothing more than to skip the inconvenient parts of the story?

"My lady?"

"Hmm?"

"When this case is over, if there's no reason for me to stay at the estate anymore... will you let me leave?"

Elena stopped walking.

"Of course. You're not my property."

"I know."

"Then why ask?"

Adrian touched the appointment letter in his pocket.

"Because I still don't know where I came from. I don't know whether something might force me to leave someday. But right now, I want to stay by your side."

She looked at him without speaking.

"Because I saved you?"

"Not just that."

He thought of the greenhouse, the academy archives, those half-burned apple biscuits.

And the way she'd never tried to make his choices for him in court.

"Because when I'm with you, I don't have to pretend I understand everything. I'm allowed to be afraid. And I still get to decide what I do next."

Elena lowered her eyes.

Her smile was small.

"Then stay."

"Just like that?"

"Would you prefer I make you sign a lifetime contract?"

"Absolutely not!"

She laughed.

Before they boarded the carriage, the final page of Matthew's notarized interview reached Hale.

It contained one point he'd been too frightened to mention earlier.

Prince Leon had once personally asked whether R-074 was real. He'd reportedly been assured that it was only an old disaster-prevention file whose administration was complete—and that no living person could be harmed by it.

Adrian read the statement twice.

If Matthew remembered correctly, then from the beginning, the prince might not have understood everything that others had persuaded him to approve.
