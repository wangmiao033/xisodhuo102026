# Chapter 192: The Half-Step They Left Empty

The baker recognized him and put down the tongs.

Adrian had selected two rye rolls, one for himself and one for Martha. On February 19 the morning oven was hot, the first tray replenished. He hadn't come to test public fear. He simply didn't want buying breakfast permanently delegated to someone else.

The baker looked at his left hand, then the door.

“Cash only today.”

“I was going to pay cash.”

“Yes. Of course.”

The tongs rose and fell again. A moment, the baker said. A woman peered from the back room. This wasn't about change. A customer by the doorway moved aside, leaving considerably more room than two rolls required.

Adrian could invoke the duke's household. He could almost hear the sentence: pressing a stronger name over his own until his name stopped frightening people.

“If you don't want to sell to me, please say so.”

The baker flushed. “I didn't say that.”

“Then I'll wait.”

He moved clear of the doorway. He could still see the rolls on the rack, and the tongs beside them. Nothing about the bread had changed while the baker decided how to behave toward its customer. Nobody ordered him out. Nobody discussed the weather either. Two young men entered, stopped talking, then changed subjects without recovering their former ease.

When the bag arrived its top wasn't folded as usual. The baker was busy; a nearby child was crying with hunger. Adrian paid, folded it himself, and didn't offer extra money to purchase a normal good morning.

Outside he stood still. The bread remained warm, which almost made things harder. No easily described atrocity. Many people had each retreated half a step, collectively making a familiar street wider and colder.

At the public coach stop a handwritten warning had been added: Unusual devices must be declared in advance and may not be brought aboard. The existing hazardous-goods rules remained beneath it. The new sentence didn't explain who judged unusual. The ticket seller noticed his bag and asked what it contained.

“Bread.”

“The other hand.”

His right held a repaired book clasp he was returning to the bookshop, metal wrapped in cloth. He opened it without exposing his left-hand scar to prove his body wasn't a device.

The seller wanted the driver's opinion.

Adrian could wait. Passengers behind him complained that one person shouldn't delay departure. A man said anyone aware he was dangerous ought to avoid crowds. Someone else asked what danger: the notice concerned old events; who had seen anything today?

Their voices rose together. The argument circled him, but nobody asked where he'd intended to go.

“The bookshop two streets away,” he said.

Was he taking something there? the first man demanded. Adrian gripped the cloth, chest tightening. One answer couldn't dismantle every suspicion. Silence shouldn't be mistaken for admission either.

The driver inspected the clasp and the new warning. Was there a specific dangerous item? The ticket seller shook his head and mentioned the notice. Returning the cloth, the driver said the clasp wasn't prohibited. Adrian could buy a ticket.

An ordinary workplace judgment, not acquittal of an entire history. Adrian was grateful for it without asking the driver to do more than his job. The clasp had been inspected against the rules that actually applied to luggage. Nobody had examined a vanished world in those few moments at the stop, and he didn't need them to. He needed to know whether he could take this particular journey with this ordinary object.

Inside the coach a mother moved her child toward the window. She said nothing. He didn't rebuke her. He bought his ticket and sat near the door with his back painfully straight.

As they moved he heard his name ahead, followed by apparently. He didn't turn to explain himself to each passenger or disembark early to surrender his route to fear. Two streets were short. Until now he'd never thought they required courage.

The bookseller accepted the clasp. The travel book's next volume had arrived; did Adrian still want it? The ordinary question took a moment to understand.

“Yes.”

The man checked its binding and named the price. No increase or consoling discount. After paying, Adrian asked whether he'd seen the notice.

“Yes.”

“Then why…”

“I don't know about those old events. Today you returned a clasp and bought a book. I sell books.”

No heroic declaration of belief. He kept his knowledge at its actual size. Adrian realized he had almost asked the man to justify behaving normally, as though fear were now the expected response and every untroubled transaction needed an explanation. The bookseller hadn't volunteered to become his defender. He had answered the question in front of him, and Adrian could accept that without making him promise the same answer to every future question. Adrian thanked him and was reminded not to leave his change behind.

He walked home because he wanted to, not because the coach barred him. At the bridge he ate one roll and kept Martha's. The bag crackled in the wind. He remembered how he'd viewed game characters labeled dangerous. A sufficiently definite description had once let him assume their next action before they'd spoken.

That couldn't make the street kinder. It stopped him dismissing everyone as stupid.

Below the bridge two workers cleared old rope from a pier. One pulled from level ground. The other called stop: someone hadn't moved clear below. Adrian froze with bread difficult to swallow.

No brass handle appeared. No new memory unfolded. His body had linked a voice with earlier fear. He leaned on the railing, swallowed, and waited for his breathing. The person below moved; only then did pulling resume. An ordinary warning, heard and followed.

He watched, envying their lack of need for pages of testimony to manage that simple sequence.

An elderly thread seller asked if he felt unwell. A little; he wanted to sit. She indicated her stool, provided he didn't block customers choosing thread. He bought a small spool to repair his fraying bookmark, not to purchase her kindness.

She replaced his overly thick choice.

“That won't pass through your needle.”

“You haven't seen my needle.”

“People mending bookmarks seldom bring sailmakers' needles.”

He laughed. She didn't ask why he sounded newly taught. Perhaps she didn't know him. He didn't announce his name to test whether she'd reclaim the stool. Brief kindness didn't need the hardest possible examination before it counted.

He returned the stool clear of the path. Remembering places to sit wasn't a vow to take deserted routes forever. Tomorrow he might need a coach; someone might retreat. Today's choice didn't sign restrictions over every future day.

At home the porter offered an inquiry record. Two suppliers had moved ordinary deliveries to the side entrance, unwilling to enter the hall after reading the notice. Neither refused supply entirely. Nobody had come with a search order.

Elena arrived and read it. They shouldn't be making such demands, she said. Adrian saw her preparing to suggest replacement suppliers.

“Find out what they're afraid of first.”

“Aren't you angry?”

“Yes. Not every kind of anger should become punishment.”

She didn't immediately agree. He didn't require gentleness as proof she'd learned respect. Eventually she asked the steward to establish practical delivery needs, forbidding insults or rumor used to humiliate household members while allowing ordinary exchanges at the side gate. Neither surrender nor immediate loss of livelihood for being afraid.

That afternoon the five women met gradually in the small sitting room. Nobody declared a rally. Luna stayed briefly with her palm cushioned. Viola sat where her caregiver could collect her, supported arm not miraculously healed by urgency. Isabella turned a reply with her right hand while the left wrist rested.

Adrian described what he'd actually seen and heard. The baker sold, the driver admitted him, the bookshop traded normally, suppliers changed a location. Distress didn't merge those differences into the whole city expelling him.

“Did that handwritten warning bear a supervisor's signature?” Viola asked.

He hadn't seen one. It could be checked later. She nodded without launching a patrol beyond medical restrictions.

Isabella's replies listed no current restriction order. That didn't mean every ordinary employee understood how to respond. She still needed to ask whether the public notice carried implementation guidance. Inquiry didn't restore her former coordinating authority.

Cecilia said some questioners feared for children; others wanted a frightening story. They weren't identical, and the first group's concern shouldn't open a path for the second's malice. She couldn't promise explanation would change everyone.

Luna suddenly asked whether he'd bought the next volume.

“Yes.”

“If that captain loses his hat again, I'm complaining to the author.”

Adrian laughed aloud. She raised an eyebrow. Her satisfaction wasn't at making him forget the street. She genuinely wanted to know where the unlucky captain went next.

“Shall I read today?”

“My listening time is nearly finished. Ask tomorrow.”

An easy refusal. His distress didn't require her to overspend herself. He agreed, book on his knee, grateful nobody had to sacrifice rest to prove willingness to remain nearby.

After she left, worries remained unfinished. Five people needn't devise identical responses today. Adrian didn't know how steadily he'd meet more strangers tomorrow either. They agreed to record actual requests and incidents first, not treat the worst possible future as already present. Adrian kept the morning's differences in mind as they spoke. The discomfort had been real. So had admission to the coach, the offered stool, and the ordinary sale of a book. Recording one didn't cancel the others. He wanted tomorrow's decisions to begin with that mixed day, not a simpler version made entirely of persecution or reassurance.

Toward evening Elena found him at the garden doorway. Would she walk the short dry path? Yes. At the side gate two delivery workers were counting boxes. Seeing them, they paused.

Her hand moved at her side. Did he want to hold it?

He didn't agree to demonstrate publicly that someone loved him. Looking at her first, he established that he actually wanted to, then reached.

They passed the workers. She neither tightened her grip in challenge nor hid it under her cloak. Counting resumed, missed one box, and started again.

“Still a difficult day?”

“Yes.”

Her hold steadied. No promise to make the whole city unafraid tomorrow. He told her about the thread seller and showed the spool. She thought the color mismatched his bookmark. The seller had guaranteed only that it fitted the needle, he replied.

She laughed and seriously suggested testing a short length before cutting the whole piece. He put it away without hearing every suggestion as another attempt at control.

At the end they turned back. The path retained its ordinary length, his hand another person's real warmth. The half-step left empty in the street hadn't been filled. Tonight, however, he didn't have to stand outside every distance.
