# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Two: The Saint Won't Let Me Get Married
### Chapter 59: The Saint Decides to Buy a Table First

On the morning of November seventh, Adrian Reed was ordered to move a table.

The order came neither from Elena nor from a Temple inspector. Ada, the nurse at Mercy Clinic, was in charge.

"Long legs, take this end. Expensive clothes, out of the way."

Elena glanced at the bandage on her right hand and briskly rolled up her left sleeve.

"Better?"

"Expensive clothes with a mind of their own can hold the door."

After examining them that morning, Dr. Winter had cleared them for light activity, with the heavy work left to others. The furniture shop assistant bore most of the table's weight; Adrian steadied it and guided the way.

Cecilia laughed. She reached for a corner of the table, but Ada put a cloth in her hand instead.

"The saint can wipe it down. A position better suited to your talents."

"I agree," Cecilia said, entirely serious.

The table came from the secondhand furniture shop across the street. One leg was short, but it cost four silver coins less than the repaired one. Adrian slid folded scrap paper beneath the leg. When he let go, the tabletop wobbled again.

Elena watched him.

"What will you use to steady it? Judicial certification?"

"Preferably from a closed case. An open case might not have a leg to stand on."

Cecilia shook with laughter, and Adrian paused.

For days, she'd been listening for footsteps outside, afraid the next notice would send her back to face another charge. Now the door stood open to the autumn sunshine, and she was crouched beside a crooked table, laughing as though this morning actually belonged to her.

This former mending room behind the clinic was where she intended to begin.

It wasn't a new temple. There were no guards or golden arches. A half-dismantled stocking rack still hung in the corner. The clinic had offered a three-month trial: two afternoons a week for people who wanted help with problems in their aid records. At other times, families could still mend clothes here.

"So you can't declare it an independent kingdom," Elena said.

"I was only planning an independent investigation."

"Good. We won't need border troops just yet."

Cecilia took the brass ruler Elena offered and moved the table away from the dispensary wall. Patients waiting for medicine shouldn't overhear other people's names and medical concerns.

At last, Adrian found a scrap of wood that fit beneath the short leg.

The table held firm.

"Our first successful reform," he announced.

By afternoon, a dozen or so people had crowded into the courtyard: clinic staff, several patients' relatives, two members of the Temple clergy willing to help, and Sabine Rowe.

Sabine set her chair at the edge of the group.

"I'm here to hear how you define your remit. I won't be endorsing your conclusions."

"There aren't any conclusions today," Cecilia replied. "Only what we intend to do."

Rather than stand on the wooden crate provided for her, she offered it as a footrest to a relative who had trouble walking.

"I'll continue serving as a charity volunteer, and I'll keep my faith. But the departments whose aid records we're checking won't be the only ones deciding whether those records are sound. I want to begin with receipts, subsidy acknowledgments, and identity corrections that patients choose to bring us, then apply for independent checks."

Someone asked whether all donations to the Temple were suspect.

"No. We start with specific entries where errors have occurred, then establish who did what. People willing to pray for the sick shouldn't have to plead guilty to a false report. And patients who need continued treatment shouldn't lose their next dose of medicine because a report says their care is complete."

Dr. Winter nodded from the doorway. She had brought corrected copies of the bed-identification records. Jonah's and Elliot's entries now matched the right patients; their doctors would continue deciding what medicine they needed. The public notice confirmed the corrections without revealing either patient's medical details.

"We used to carry our papers around without knowing who to ask," a relative said. "Can we get them corrected here now?"

"We can help locate the originals and support you in requesting a check. We can't change medical records for the doctors, or promise every application will succeed."

"But someone will come with us?"

"Yes."

For a moment, the courtyard was quiet.

Beneath the eaves, Mira tucked her red thread bracelet inside her sleeve, then stepped forward to write down when she could volunteer.

She put down Thursday only.

"I want to spend the other days with the children in the wards. We haven't put up their new sun drawings yet."

"Thursday is perfect," Cecilia said.

Nobody asked her to give this project every hour she had.

An elderly woman with a cloth bag was the last to speak. She couldn't read, and she worried that handing over a receipt meant agreeing to appear on the next fundraising poster.

Cecilia didn't give her a lengthy notice to mark with a thumbprint. She pulled up a stool and explained each thing they could do.

When she'd finished, the woman still gripped her bag tightly.

"I'll take it home and ask my daughter first."

"Of course. Come back next week, or just stop by for some water."

"No signature for the water?"

Adrian, arriving with the pitcher, immediately shook his head.

"That's the one service I'm fully qualified to provide."

She accepted a cup and finally smiled. Rather than look disappointed at losing a possible file, Cecilia found another chair for anyone who wanted to sit without an appointment.

Elena watched her.

"You used to look at everyone as though you were afraid you hadn't answered their question quickly enough."

"When people call you a saint, they're usually already waiting for an answer."

"And now?"

Cecilia glanced at the bag on the woman's lap.

"I'd like to let them finish asking first."

Adrian set down the pitcher and gave her a thoughtful look. Noticing it, she reached for the accounts ledger and picked up the cleaning cloth instead.

Elena cleared her throat.

"I don't think the accounts need quite that kind of cleaning."

Cecilia looked down and silently replaced the cloth.

Adrian was the first to laugh. He didn't laugh long before both women unanimously assigned him to wash the cups.

"I was only admiring how efficiently this place runs."

"Excellent," Elena said. "Take that efficiency over to the sink."

The budget was less grand than the speeches: two stacks of paper, a small lockable cabinet, postage, and a handful of travel allowances for patients' relatives. The clinic supplied the spare room. Neighborhood donations went through the bookkeeper, and Elena's one-time contribution appeared on the same page as everyone else's.

Cecilia signed as the person responsible for handling the funds. Ada and two family representatives would review the accounts each month. Anyone withdrawing their materials could take back the originals. Making a donation bought nobody privileged access to patients' medical information.

Elena raised her hand. Adrian instinctively straightened.

"May a donor request a replacement leg for the table?"

Laughter rippled across the courtyard.

"You may request one," Cecilia said. "But this leg has already been repaired."

She looked at Adrian, her expression softening.

"Very well repaired."

He suddenly felt he ought to check the wooden shim again.

After the meeting, a young clergyman stayed behind. He was afraid helping them would be seen as betraying the Temple.

Cecilia spoke with him in the doorway for a long time. She didn't urge him to resign or demand he choose a side. She simply showed him the public statement explaining which materials they intended to examine.

"Come if you want to. If you aren't ready, please keep bringing medicine to the people who need it."

Before leaving, Sabine accepted the first proposed list of records to check.

It included the charitable donation Elena's mother had earmarked for a particular purpose. Existing documentation supported its original designation for relief; subsequent transfers still required verification. Her mother's signature couldn't vouch for everyone who had handled the money afterward.

Elena personally placed the family's copies in the folder.

"Tell me even if you find something against us."

"I'll tell you what the facts establish," Sabine said.

"That's enough."

Adrian's real difficulty arrived after the visitors had left.

Holding a note, Cecilia asked whether he was free the following afternoon.

Elena was drying the remaining cups. Her hands paused, then resumed.

"It's about the lunch we discussed," Cecilia added. "You said we could arrange a time after the safety inspection. There's something personal I want to explain first."

Adrian glanced at the paper.

"Not that old book of social etiquette?"

"I've returned it. No quotations this time."

She turned over the note. On the back was a lopsided flower and the name of a bakery.

"I found a bakery that serves lunch, too. No archives, no betrothal checks, and no patients who need your help. I already told you this wasn't a thank-you. Today I think I know what I actually want to ask."

Only then did she notice how tightly she was gripping the paper.

"You don't have to answer today. I've realized that even if all those documents disappeared, I'd still want to know whether you prefer sitting by a window or closer to the fire."

Adrian didn't take the note as though he'd accepted a date.

"I remember the lunch, and I'd still like to find a time. Thank you for telling me how you feel. I want to think seriously about that, rather than leave you guessing what my agreement to lunch means. And I haven't worked out tomorrow yet."

"Of course." She exhaled softly. "When I practiced this, I only imagined you asking whether they had apple pastries."

"Is it too late to ask?"

"No. I checked. They do."

She smiled, the color in her cheeks finally showing.

Elena set the last cup aside.

"At least this pastry doesn't come with historical Calamity Assistance provisions."

Cecilia lowered her head and laughed harder.

On the walk back to the estate, Elena said nothing about the note. She discussed the cost of roof repairs, the need for a lamp at the clinic entrance, and the unfortunate color of Adrian's wooden shim.

He listened for a while, then stopped at the river railing.

"You don't actually like that piece of paper."

"I have nothing against paper."

"Good. We'd lose half our livelihood."

She looked at the water. Her mouth twitched.

After a moment, she said, "I'm jealous."

She didn't tuck the admission behind a joke.

"When she looks at you, I want you to look at me first. I know she can ask, and you can consider it. Knowing that doesn't instantly make me generous enough to deserve a statue in the square."

"Statues get cold in winter."

"Adrian."

"I'm listening."

She turned to face him.

"I won't use your work schedule to block an invitation, or cut back my support for the clinic. But we're seriously courting. I don't want to be so determined to seem gracious that I can't even say it hurts."

Adrian put his gloves in his pocket.

"Of course you can. I like you. Someone inviting me for pastries doesn't mean you have to start guessing about that again. But I don't want to brush aside what I haven't worked out by promising nothing will ever change."

"You could offer something a little more specific."

"I'd like us to finish the travel book we started. Tonight, if you want to."

She studied him, the guarded look slowly leaving her eyes.

"Where will we sit?"

"By the window. Somewhere close to the fire, too."

At last, Elena smiled.

"Greedy."

"I've only just learned how much thought this question deserves."

He offered his hand. She placed hers in it.

They didn't settle their whole future beside the river, but they walked home much more slowly than they'd come.

That evening, Cecilia returned alone to the borrowed room and put the next day's appointments in the cabinet.

She still had the note in her pocket.

She'd thought the warmth she felt when Adrian came to mind was a consequence of the dangers they'd shared. Today there had been no fire, no accusations, no ancient ritual. She'd simply watched him crouch down to repair a table and wanted to save him a chair that wouldn't wobble.

It was a small wish, entirely unrelated to a record from seven years ago.

She didn't rush to give it a name that would have to last forever.

Mira peered inside and asked what title to put on their first notice.

"The Saint's Independent Court?" she suggested mischievously.

"Aid Records Advice Room," Cecilia said. "Let's keep the name smaller than the building."

Mira ran off laughing.

Cecilia moved the lamp to the window. Light fell across the table. The scrap of wood sat firmly beneath its old leg.

Tomorrow, people would come with whatever papers they chose to share.

And at last, she could begin by asking, "What would you like us to help you with?"
