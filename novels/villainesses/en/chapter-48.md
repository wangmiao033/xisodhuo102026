# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Two: The Saint Won't Let Me Get Married
### Chapter 48: Being a Saint Isn't a Favor

Adrian opened the dining-room door on October twenty-eighth and found two breakfasts waiting for him again.

Elena brought over fresh bread and apple preserves. Cecilia set a small dish of honey biscuits at the corner of the table.

“Why are we competing again?”

“We aren't,” Elena said. “Martha told me you barely ate lunch yesterday.”

“I bought some mild biscuits at the clinic, the sort the patients can have,” Cecilia explained earnestly. “I picked up an extra portion.”

Adrian looked from one pair of eyes to the other.

“Then I request an independent breakfast review committee.”

Martha hid a smile. “Mr. Reed, you could simply eat.”

Cecilia passed him her suggested portions. Elena glanced at the page and found notes on exercise and sleep as well.

“Are you keeping clinical records of his breakfast now?”

“It isn't a clinical record. I'm worried he'll be too nervous to eat again.”

Adrian raised a hand. “Please respect the subject's dietary autonomy.”

Both women put down their pens, then laughed.

Cecilia's disciplinary decision would be announced at ten.

Unlike the previous two nights, she had slept. That morning she had folded her white robes neatly into a trunk and put on an ordinary coat.

“Why not wear your formal robes?” Adrian asked.

“I don't want to use my clothes as proof of innocence. Or make people think I've given up being a saint. I'll wait for the decision.”

Elena nodded. “That confession Oversight wrote for you doesn't get to define who you are.”

“Thank you,” Cecilia said softly.

Sister Anna and the other two reviewers were already seated when they reached the hall. Vera and Oversight's representative each confirmed that the certified comparisons obtained the previous day had been entered into the record.

Anna did not deliver a vague blessing. She read a decision.

First, Cecilia's disclosure of the identity conflict between C-19 and C-91 had been a necessary, limited notification intended to prevent incorrect care. The available evidence did not establish that she had disclosed the restricted original oracle to unauthorized people.

Second, the allegation of reputational harm lacked both a clearly identified violation and an applicable rule. Public criticism of the Temple was not, by itself, grounds to discipline her.

Finally, the unexplained differences between versions of the prognosis-completion summary required further independent investigation. This disciplinary committee would neither rule on the original oracle's truth nor identify offenders on behalf of the criminal authorities.

“Accordingly,” Anna concluded, “the disciplinary allegation that Miss Saintclair made unauthorized disclosures is not sustained. She retains her standing as a saint.”

Vera's hand finally relaxed on her papers.

Cecilia did not smile at once.

“Then may I continue helping with medical relief?”

“Yes, provided it does not involve the restricted records in this case. Your ordinary charitable-service privileges are restored. Independent investigative access to the originals must be reviewed and assigned separately, to ensure that an interested party does not have sole control of the evidence.”

Cecilia nodded. She did not claim that being cleared entitled her to open any file she pleased.

“I'll gladly continue cooperating under proper authorization and with independent witnesses.”

Oversight's representative objected that this would embolden the Temple's critics.

Anna looked up. “We are deciding Cecilia's conduct today. Not how quiet everyone else ought to be.”

The hall fell silent.

Cecilia slowly let out her breath. She was still a saint. Not because someone had buried a record to save face, or because she had signed a false confession. The committee had been required to determine, from evidence, whether she had committed a specific wrong.

After the ruling, Anna asked Cecilia and Vera to remain while she explained how the disputed documents would be handled.

“Clearing the accused does not make the material used to accuse her reliable. The unverified additions will be kept separately from the formal records. A different review team, with no role in this disciplinary decision, will investigate how they came to be.”

Vera asked whether Cecilia could still apply to examine R-074 material concerning her own name.

“She may apply,” Anna said. “An independent custodian must decide what can be disclosed while protecting the identities of other interested parties.”

“Can she obtain a list of the materials provisionally sealed today?”

“A custody inventory that protects other people's privacy, yes.”

Cecilia spoke. “I'd like Oversight to receive the complete inventory too. If somebody thinks I acted wrongly in future, they should at least be able to make their case from the same materials.”

Anna studied her. “You aren't asking us to remove documents unfavorable to you?”

“No. I just don't want anyone signing for me.”

The archive reviewer ducked his head over a brief laugh, then recovered his solemnity.

Adrian remembered what Cecilia had said when she first brought the historical betrothal: a name on a document did not establish that person's consent. Now she was applying the same principle to the identity she valued most.

After the hearing, a young novice hurried up. Adrian recognized the girl who had asked Cecilia in the reception hall whether she was still someone worth learning from.

“Your Holiness, can you still teach us?”

Cecilia crouched before her. “I'll teach you anything I can. But remember that teachers can be wrong too. If somebody tells you to write something untrue about a patient, you're allowed to ask why.”

The girl nodded hard.

Standing beside them, Adrian thought that ordinary reminder might matter more than the gavel announcing the decision.

Mira waited outside by the steps with Mercy Clinic's latest volunteer roster. Some children had also asked her to draw Cecilia a crooked little sun.

“Can you come back tomorrow?” she asked.

Cecilia accepted the paper. “Yes. I'll check with the clinic supervisor about what they need first. I don't want the ‘saint's return’ taking up the nurses' working hours.”

“Dr. Winter will make you wash your hands first,” Mira said, smiling.

“She makes me do that too,” Adrian put in.

“That's because you keep carrying the account books and bed linens together.”

Elena doubled over laughing.

As the novices gathered around, Mira quietly mentioned another concern. Since hearing Cecilia was under investigation, several apprentices had been afraid to report registration errors to the clinic.

“They thought they'd be suspended too if they spoke up.”

Cecilia listened carefully and asked her to submit the concern to the relief-training suggestion box.

“We can't promise that raising a problem will never cost anything,” she said. “But there should at least be somebody who helps them record it and protects the patient while an authorized person checks.”

“Will you run that training?” Mira asked.

“I hope so. If the committee doesn't let me keep working with the archives, I'll start with teaching people to record medicine collection lists accurately.”

“Are you planning to devote your entire life to forms?” Adrian asked.

Cecilia considered. “Not all of it. I'd also like to learn how to buy biscuits once without checking the price.”

“Then we'll go together,” Elena said. “Adrian can pay.”

“Why me?”

“Because you're so good at explaining voluntary choices to two ladies now.”

Adrian feared the estate's financial pressures were only increasing.

That afternoon, Vera brought a certified copy of the complete ruling. She reminded them that the disciplinary case and the separate investigation were distinct. Oversight could still lawfully investigate subsidy fraud, improper record custody, and the circumstances in which Mira had been prevented from leaving.

“We can't announce that everyone responsible has been found,” Hale said.

“But we've resolved something that could have ruined her life,” Adrian replied.

Cecilia sat by the window and took her saint's badge from its box. Instead of putting it on immediately, she wiped the dust from its edges with a soft cloth.

“I used to think this only meant people had placed their trust in me. Now I think it also means I'm responsible for speaking when I find something wrong.”

By evening, Cecilia had pinned the investigator's badge back onto her coat. Sunlight filtered through the leaves outside and caught its edge.

“Adrian,” she asked suddenly, “if I hadn't been able to keep it, would you still have come to listen to me today?”

Before he could reply, Elena said gently, “That's a question he can answer for himself.”

“Of course,” Adrian told Cecilia. “The person I first met was holding a file and telling us an old betrothal shouldn't be used to order people around. I met you before I met the badge.”

Her eyes reddened, but she smiled and nodded.

“I hope one day people will remember something about me besides being accused, or being cleared.”

“Then do things you'll want to remember yourself,” Elena said.

A strange relief settled over Adrian as he watched them. He had once divided the people in this world into game characters who would win, lose, or die. Now their wishes were far more complicated than their endings.

Elena sat beside Cecilia. “We can at least celebrate together.”

“With what?”

“Apple pie.”

“Not one you've baked yourself, my lady?” Adrian asked quickly.

Elena turned to glare at him. Cecilia laughed softly.

“I bought two kinds. One sweeter, one a little lighter.”

“This time I'll choose,” Adrian said.

“Do you have plans tonight?” Elena asked.

“We haven't finished the maps for our trip.”

“Then I'd like to ask you to the study. We can turn the page.”

He glanced at Cecilia. She was gathering her documents, with no sign of feeling excluded.

“Go,” she said. “I want to write to my mentor. To tell her what I've learned, rather than prove I did nothing wrong.”

She stood, then turned back to him.

“Yesterday you said an old betrothal doesn't amount to love today. I think you're right.”

“I do believe that.”

“Then I can't declare that I like you just because of that record.” She glanced at her badge. “If I ever do feel that way, I'd like to be the one who says so.”

Adrian didn't answer immediately. Elena let the silence stand.

Then he said, quite seriously, “You have that right.”

Cecilia smiled and left.

Later that night, Hale brought a summary from an old risk register recently received by the independent review authorities. It hadn't formed part of the day's disciplinary case. Oversight had included the old index when transferring its version catalogue.

The summary listed several people affected by special contract registrations. Some names were lawfully concealed. Adrian recognized one that wasn't.

*Cecilia Saintclair.*

Beside it stood:

*High-risk threshold date: October thirty-first. Not to be treated as a determined outcome before review.*

His smile froze.

Three days.

He remembered September twentieth printed on Elena's execution notice. This was no verdict and no death warrant, but it placed Cecilia's name beside a dangerously close date.

He handed the paper straight back to Hale. He did not announce that a prophecy had made her fate inevitable.

The lawyer stressed that the notation was neither a medical record nor a lawful execution order. It showed only that an archival system had flagged that day for review.

“It could concern a ceremony, settlement of an old contract, even an ordinary annual check. Until we verify its source, we cannot call it a date of death.”

Adrian nodded. In September he had actually seen Elena's execution notice. This document used a different classification and different language.

“But I still want to tell Cecilia as soon as possible.”

“Yes. Tell her what the record says, and explain what we don't know along with it.”

Adrian looked out of the window. He didn't need to hide something to protect her. Nor did he need to frighten her into hiding herself.

“First, find out who wrote this summary,” he said. “Then let her decide what she wants to do.”
