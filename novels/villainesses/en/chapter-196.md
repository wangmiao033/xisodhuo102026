# Chapter 196: There Were Still Gaps Between the Lines

The wooden bird vanished.

Adrian looked up and found it safely in Beatrice's hand. Sunlight filled the workshop on February 23, but a narrow slit in a screen allowed him to see only a strip across the table.

“I didn't see you take it.”

“You didn't see it burn either.”

She replaced it. The technician closed the screen. Beside them, an academy examiner drew two circles: bird present, bird absent. He didn't fill the space between with fire.

Luna reclined with her right palm cushioned. She wasn't staying for the entire examination. Beatrice would continue during her rest.

“If today's conclusion is that my bird can fly,” she said, “first establish whether your hand is under the table.”

The technician raised both hands. One held the bird. Adrian laughed, realizing he'd been staring at the empty positioning frame since arriving.

The subject's chair hadn't been brought in. Today's work concerned retained technical material, ordinary lamps, and new comparison plates. His left hand stayed in his sleeve. His travel book occupied a corner far from the frame.

A custodian opened the box containing February 14's newly made trial plate. Under oblique light, it retained several very faint gray traces. The three ancient objects weren't here; their old power unit remained sealed elsewhere. The examiner checked custody before moving his lens to the edge.

Adrian's back tightened at the sight. He remembered nausea. Looking at the wooden bird, he waited for his breathing to settle.

Luna noticed without asking whether another memory had arrived. His initial account remained closed in Beatrice's folder. The examiner had access only to the approved observation periods and equipment records.

“An unpleasant question first,” he said. “Has this examination promised a conclusion favorable to Mr. Reed?”

“No,” Luna said.

“If the original observation proves defective, will that finding be retained?”

“Especially that finding. Including anything bearing my signature.”

The gray line's endpoint occupied a little box in the original record. The technician had observed at agreed intervals. Power indicators were recorded separately. At Adrian's command and dropped card, he had disconnected immediately. The plate had darkened. Their first checks had concerned the stopping procedure and Adrian's condition.

“We know when power ended,” Beatrice said. “Now we must ask whether the observation continuously covered the effective area before that.”

Adrian reached toward the diagram, saw it was inside the examination space, and stopped. An assistant gave him a separate copy. He realized he'd treated its thin border like the walls of a whole room. Everything outside had escaped his imagination.

The technician restored the recorded positioning marks, initially using an ordinary transparent comparison plate of the same size. A gray stripe crossed its middle; a fine grid marked its edges. Lamplight from the original angle showed a continuous dark line through the viewing aperture.

“Turn it slightly,” Luna said.

The clamp moved along marked increments. One end faded, then shortened. The lamp remained lit. The stripe hadn't been erased. It was still on the plate but had entered glare at the aperture's edge.

From the side, the examiner could see it clearly. He replaced the overly uniform stripe with one varying in darkness. The pale portion near the edge vanished sooner.

Luna's left hand moved as though she meant to adjust the frame herself. She pressed it against the blanket and asked the technician to repeat it.

The second result matched.

For the third, the examiner concealed the direction of movement from the observers. Three people separately recorded the visible length. One reported a broken line. The side view showed an unbroken stripe, its faintest portion outside the effective viewing path.

“The earlier checks corrected the shifting tablecloth, frame positioning, and stop signals,” Beatrice said, consulting the old error record. “They didn't test visibility at the aperture's edge for traces of different intensities.”

Luna studied her signature.

“I made repeatable positioning look too much like complete observation.”

“You didn't write complete,” the examiner said.

“The diagram invites it. I read it that way today.”

She requested that her correction be attributed to her, rather than blamed on the technician who'd followed the observation intervals. The young man's grip on his pen finally eased.

The custodian placed the retained trial plate in an unpowered inspection holder. Only an ordinary viewing lamp moved. From the front, the rightmost trace appeared to have a clean endpoint. Oblique light revealed a still fainter tail beyond it.

No letters, brass handle, or image appeared. Only a shallow trace that the previous angle hadn't made readable.

Restored within the original recorded placement, the tail lay just beyond the effective viewing edge. The examiner changed seats and obtained the same result. The supposedly complete endpoint diagram had omitted something physically retained on the plate.

The assistant began joining the old endpoint to the newly observed tail with a dotted stroke. The examiner stopped him. He could mark where each observation had been made, but the space between them needed to remain unfilled. After a moment, the assistant started a fresh sheet and kept the abandoned drawing beside it. Adrian watched him write the viewing angle under each separate mark. The result was less graceful than a single continuous line. It also made it possible for the next reader to discover exactly where the observer had stood, instead of mistaking the drawing for the whole object.

Adrian's heartbeat accelerated.

“So afterward there was—”

He stopped himself. Luna let the unfinished sentence remain.

“A little more technical trace,” she said. “Nobody here has seen the afterward you're asking about.”

Disappointment came so quickly that it felt as though the examination had stolen an answer already his. Gray didn't tell him where the handle moved next. It couldn't give the people outside the passage a second sentence.

Beatrice placed the power-indicator record beside the observations. Their recorded periods didn't coincide completely. Nor were the gaps between observations filled. The new comparison established one way observation could fail: a trace near the edge could fade from view while the power indicator remained lit.

It didn't explain every blank or translate missing observation into seconds of historical events.

“At minimum, this is not a continuous record of the whole period,” the examiner said.

He turned the original diagram facedown. Its back was blank. Adrian had kept looking there for a road already drawn.

“I told the verification office I heard evacuation wasn't finished and still asked them to wait.”

“That statement can stay,” Luna replied. “What you actually heard needn't be withdrawn alongside an overconfident diagram.”

“I'm afraid I'll be relieved by this and refuse to admit anything again.”

She watched him, her voice lowering.

“Don't hurry to convict your future self. Answer the next specific question when it comes.”

Her rest period arrived. The examiner wanted another answer about the clamp angle. She asked him to put it to Beatrice first; she would respond after resting. Her assistant moved her reclining chair next door. Adrian offered to accompany her as far as the doorway.

“Will you read in the doorway?”

“I haven't become clever enough to turn a waiting chair into a lectern.”

“Excellent. The investigation is producing results.”

A loose window catch admitted a draft. Adrian fetched the assistant rather than standing on a chair to fix it. Luna watched him put down the book.

“No captain today.”

“All right.”

“And no story about the courage of stopping.”

He swallowed You did well and sat beside the door.

She regarded her bandaged right palm. Her left hand ached from repeatedly wanting to take up a pen. After a while she admitted she'd hoped to find a prettier clue, preferably without first acknowledging an omission in her own diagram.

“I know what you want to know. I hate not being able to hand it to you.”

“You gave me an answer I can use.”

“Only where you can't jump.”

“I keep falling there lately.”

She looked up. The teasing reply she'd prepared never arrived. Her shoulders relaxed against the cushion. In the other room, the wooden bird tapped the table. Examination continued without requiring every breath she took to supervise it.

“You can go. I want to close my eyes.”

“Leave the book?”

“And the bookmark. It can undergo an appearance review on your behalf.”

He tucked the centipede between the pages and closed the door smiling.

Beatrice completed the comparisons that afternoon. Adrian returned only for the conclusions he was entitled to receive, never occupying a trial position. The plate was resealed. The new tail diagram, original observation, and failed comparisons were retained together.

Before they packed away, a messenger arrived from the publication office. The objections submitted on the eighteenth had received an initial response. The testimony wording had been referred for review. For the old final account's principal attribution, the office requested material directly overturning its conclusion. It wouldn't yet promise withdrawal of the entire circular.

Adrian read standing up. He almost handed the messenger the new diagram.

The examiner's hand rested at its edge.

“What would you like me to write on it?”

Adrian looked at the trace that needed a different angle merely to become visible. It couldn't bear the weight of the whole notice. His desire to remove the notice had almost made him place that weight there anyway.

“What you've established.”

The messenger could deliver papers, not amend the reply. Adrian asked him to return during normal collection hours for a written response instead of waiting through a private discussion. Relieved, the man went out for water.

Beatrice retrieved the original objection and indicated fear being changed to confirmation.

“You don't need to disprove the entire history before this can be answered.”

Adrian retained that point. He requested individual answers to the identified changes in paraphrase. His objections shouldn't become a test in which he alone had to disprove a vanished world. The technical finding would accompany only the part it addressed, subject to its own review.

Could the diagram be titled No Complete Record? The examiner objected that it would seem to dismiss material they hadn't inspected. Eventually they settled on Gaps in the Equipment Record Examined Here.

An awkward, narrow heading, unsuitable for a stirring proclamation. Adrian read it twice and left it.

He approved a shareable explanation stating that the equipment record couldn't constitute continuous historical imagery, and that recollection still required independent sources. His private physical reactions weren't included. Nor would every interested inquirer receive his complete first account. The examiner signed his portion. After resting, Luna read and signed the technical scope.

Isabella had changed into the dress for tomorrow's appearance when she received the authorized page. She read it and observed that this would be much harder to explain than her original absolute assurance.

“But it's true,” Adrian said.

She nodded and crossed out the most prominent line of her speech with her right hand.

“Fenwick's reply has arrived.”

Rather than explain immediately, she asked her attendant to bring the complete letter. Half its broken wax seal still adhered to the paper.

Adrian looked at it. Tomorrow's uncertain ground already had someone preparing to withdraw his support.
