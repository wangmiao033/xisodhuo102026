# Chapter 83: That Carriage Wasn't Here to Collect a Patient

On the morning of November 28, Viola set a paper bag in front of Adrian, then immediately drew it half an inch back toward herself.

“This is for you.”

Inside was a walnut cake. Its edges were a little scorched, but the middle was intact. Someone had clearly chosen it with care.

Adrian glanced behind her. “Where are the others?”

“In the kitchen.”

“Then why does mine get a personal delivery?”

Viola still had her right hand on the top of the bag. She had prepared an excellent explanation, involving his remark yesterday that he liked soft chestnuts, and the tendency of walnut cake to harden as it cooled. Unfortunately, it had grown to resemble a briefing on campaign provisions.

In the end, she only said, “Because I wanted to give it to you.”

He took the bag. “Thank you. I'm glad you did.”

Her ears went red. By the window, Elena closed her ledger and considered the cake he had carefully placed on his plate.

“The one you chose is bigger than the one the landlady gave me.”

Viola turned at once, apparently intending to go to the kitchen and correct the injustice.

“Don't.” Elena was holding back a smile. “My position isn't so fragile that I need a larger walnut cake to defend it.”

“I could share,” Adrian offered.

“Eat your cake. Someone picked it out especially for you. You don't have to divide it up until everyone has exactly the same amount.”

Having said this, she reached over and took a walnut from the edge of his plate. When he looked at her, she ate it with perfect composure.

“I like walnuts.”

Viola finally laughed. The little paper bag didn't need to come with a promise, or proof that everyone had received an equitable share of cake. She sat down and discovered that the opening remark she had spent all night preparing was now entirely useless.

Cecilia missed breakfast. She was helping a nurse count clean linens at the clinic, and when she first heard wheels outside, she assumed the coal delivery had arrived.

The carriage had clean blankets laid inside it. Two men in gray coats unloaded a stretcher. At the entrance, the man in front produced a paper and said Bell had to be transferred to the military convalescent hospital immediately.

The neutral guard did not move aside.

“The only thing scheduled today is a follow-up interview here,” he said. “Any transfer has to go through Dr. Shaw.”

“There was a change last night. The roads are icing over. Leave it any longer and we won't get through.”

The paper bore a passage stamp from the supply office and a scrawled line of medical advice. The guard took it and sent his colleague for a nurse, remaining in the doorway himself.

Cecilia set down the linen basket. The supposed medical recommendation carried Dr. Shaw's name.

When Cecilia had left the previous night, the doctor had still been cleaning a frostbitten coachman's wounds. That memory alone could not tell her whether Dr. Shaw had signed a new arrangement afterward. Rather than argue, she asked the nurse to check directly with the doctor.

The man in front took a step toward the ward.

“We have orders to collect him.”

“Then please wait for the people who have orders to care for him,” Cecilia said.

She had not raised her voice. The man noticed the Temple emblem at her collar and hesitated for a moment. Then he reached into his coat.

The guard saw the cudgel first.

He threw up an arm to block the first blow. The end of the club struck the doorframe, scattering splinters at Cecilia's feet. The other man tipped over the stretcher, trying to force his way through the side door off the corridor. A nurse pulled a patient on crutches into the dispensary. Cecilia helped close the door and dragged the bench behind it across the opening.

She did not flood the entire clinic with light. There were injured people inside, and hot water, and people who had no idea what was happening.

A guard shouted outside. Then came the crack of the driver's whip.

Through the small window, Cecilia saw the carriage strike the stone post beside the courtyard gate. One wheel lurched crookedly beneath it. Another guard, who had remained in the courtyard, had already shut the outer gate. The driver jumped down and managed a few running steps before an arriving town constable tackled him.

The struggle in the corridor was brief. Even after he had been pinned, the first man managed to kick over a bucket. The second dropped a short knife and raised both hands.

When Dr. Shaw emerged from the consulting room, her expression was colder than the whitewashed walls.

“Who says I ordered a patient with a lung injury to ride in that carriage?”

No one answered.

From his room, Martin asked whether the guards had changed shifts.

Cecilia steadied herself against the doorframe and only then realized that her fingers were shaking. She waited for the nurse to say she could go in, then sat beside his bed. His original guards were still here, she told him. They had stopped an unauthorized attempt to take him away.

“Do I still have to go for the questions?” he asked.

“The doctor will rearrange it. You don't have to hurry for anyone right now.”

He turned his face toward the window. After a while, tears slipped down beside his nose.

When word reached the inn, Adrian still had half his cake left.

Viola rose. There was no sword at her hip. She looked at the door, then back at the constable who had brought the news.

“What do you need us to do now?”

“Bellamy wants Mr. Reed to confirm the handover of an earlier copy. Everyone else should wait until they're called.”

She slowly sat down again and let Adrian get his coat on first. Elena called for a carriage and went with him, rather than letting him run all the way there in his alarm.

The first thing Adrian noticed at the clinic was the wet skirt across Cecilia's knees.

“Hot water?”

“Cold. I wasn't burned.”

He sat beside her. For once, she did not immediately tell him there was something else she needed to do. Her hands rested in her lap, her fingertips still trembling faintly.

“I was so angry,” she said quietly. “He treated a patient like a parcel overdue for collection.”

“Scared, too?”

“Very.”

Elena took the clean towel a nurse offered and handed it to Adrian. He passed it to Cecilia. She wrapped it around her hands, then asked abruptly, “Could I lean on you? Just for a moment.”

“Yes.”

Her shoulder settled lightly against his. Elena paused at the end of the corridor, then went to find a change of clothes for her. Adrian stayed still, without turning that moment into a heroic promise. Outside the room, a guard was refitting the door latch. Each blow of the hammer made her shoulder twitch. Eventually, she stopped tensing at the sound.

“All right.” She sat up. “I still need to visit that patient who got such a fright this afternoon.”

“I thought you were going to say we had to uncover the entire conspiracy.”

“First we need a new bucket. The hole in that one is much too big.”

Her laugh was soft, but the life had returned to her eyes.

Bellamy called him in soon afterward. The false transfer order had already been numbered and sealed. Dr. Shaw denied signing it, and neither the previous night's entry log nor the clinic's medical orders contained a corresponding record. The genuine supply-office passage stamp belonged to a properly issued empty-carriage pass. Its original purpose had been to collect broken beds for return to the warehouse. Today's instructions to collect a person had been written on an extra layer of paper pasted over it.

There were no reserve medicines in the carriage. There were a coil of rope, a gag, and a rough map showing a route to an abandoned quarry. At first, the driver had called it a shortcut. The road map brought by the town constables showed that the convalescent hospital lay in the opposite direction.

“He still says his only job was driving.” Bellamy closed her notebook. “We'll investigate each of them separately. For now, we preserve what they brought and what they actually did.”

Adrian confirmed that he had returned his copy of the questions the day before. He had had nothing to do with arrangements for the ward and had never seen this order.

By noon, the first lead pointing further up the chain had arrived.

The carriage firm's booking named the office of Captain Cedric Morrow, at military supply. The silver paid in advance matched the firm's deposit receipt from the town bank that morning. Meanwhile, the garrison's issue register showed that the same office had collected three empty-carriage passes the previous night. One bore the number of the pass seized at the clinic.

These records existed in three separate places. They did not depend on one of the arrested men giving a name.

Morrow replied that he had approved nothing except the collection of furniture for repair and had not known anyone had changed its purpose. Ward required him to produce the names of everyone who had handled the passes, along with the two remaining originals. She also asked the town authorities to secure the carriage firm's accounting records and the relevant physical evidence.

Morrow did not confess on the spot. Nor did anyone declare every member of Major Voss's garrison a murderer merely because the paper bore a supply-office stamp.

But instead of the originals due with the review team that afternoon, all that arrived was a note promising to provide them the next day.

Ward read it and told her staff to retain the note as well.

That afternoon, Martin asked a nurse to bring Cecilia back. He thought the day's events might also stop his letter to his sister. He asked twice, then said quietly that he could make it shorter.

“Just say I'm alive. She'll know my handwriting.”

Cecilia collected the letter from the legal adviser, who had already verified the arrangements for getting it to its recipient, and placed it where Martin could see it. It gave no location for his room. There was nothing in it that waited on his admitting to anything. When he had finished reading, his finger lingered beside his sister's name.

“I used to complain that she made the soup too thin.”

“Would you like to add something?”

He nodded. For a long time, he thought about it, without producing any grand statement worth setting down.

“Tell her I want some of her soup.”

Adrian stood outside the door, listening to the pen move across the paper. The carriage's visit had not transformed Martin into a brave witness. It had only made him want to go home. But he let the legal adviser leave the questions he might be asked tomorrow, saying he would see whether he could answer after he had slept.

Outside the room, Cecilia discovered that Elena had brought her dry clothes. Elena handed her the parcel and inspected the still-damp hem of her skirt.

“Were you planning to wear that through an entire winter of relief work?”

“I forgot.”

“I thought you might. The dress inside is mine. The sash may be a little long.”

Cecilia looked through the parcel and smiled. “You chose the most comfortable one.”

“The easiest to wash. Your buckets are dangerous.”

Once she had changed, the sash did indeed turn out to be a little long. Cecilia tied a neat bow in front of the mirror, then turned to ask Adrian whether it suited her. He gave her a careful look and said the color was lovely.

Elena raised an eyebrow. “When I wore it last month, you said it looked easy to walk in.”

He slowly turned toward her, discovering that the same dress could come to collect a debt twice.

“The color has always been lovely.”

“And you mention that now?”

“Now I finally have a chance to make amends.”

Both women laughed. A quiet cough came from Martin's room. Cecilia stopped laughing at once and listened for the nurse's reply. Only after she knew there was no need to go in did she straighten the bow again.

This time, she picked up the parcel of wet clothes without Elena reminding her.

When Adrian returned to the inn, Viola was sitting near the door, shelling walnuts. She worked with a small wooden mallet at an astonishingly slow pace, making sure before each tap that the movement would not pull at her left arm.

“I wanted to do something useful,” she admitted.

“Are these for me?”

“For everyone.”

He glanced down at another small dish. The kernels in that one were especially whole.

Viola looked at it. She could not quite maintain her claim to impartiality.

“That dish is yours.”

He sat and took the mallet. “Then I'll crack the next batch. Tell me where to put the pressure.”

The first walnut looked as though it had survived a siege.

Viola stared for a moment, then laughed so hard she had to cover her mouth with her right hand. Adrian swept the shattered pieces into his own dish and sincerely acknowledged that the walnut was not to blame.

She showed him how to use the edge of the mallet, brace it against the crack, and tap gently. The second walnut split in half. She was even happier about it than he was.

When Elena returned and saw the pile of shells, she asked whether they intended to open a very slow bakery.

“Our quality is still inconsistent,” Adrian said.

“At least stop practicing on the landlady's table.”

She slid a thick wooden board beneath their work, then sat beside him. When Viola went to fetch more dishes, Elena asked in a low voice, “Seeing her lean on you hurt a little. But I want to know something else more. Were you badly frightened today?”

“When I heard, yes. My legs only started shaking after I got there.”

Elena reached beneath the table with her uninjured hand and took his.

“Then sit with me for a while later, too. I don't want the story about the captain dropping things into his soup today.”

“What would you like?”

“You choose. Preferably something where no one needs rescuing.”

He promised. Her hand stayed in his a little longer before she let go to take the dish Viola brought back.

At dusk, Owen knocked on the inn's door. His expression was grim.

Two groups of knights were standing at the supply warehouse entrance. One carried Ward's evidence-preservation order. The other, acting on instructions from the garrison's duty officer, refused to let outside investigators in. Neither side had drawn swords. Neither would be the first to move.

Viola set down the mallet.

“Who's there?”

Owen gave several familiar names. When he finished, she did not send him to summon followers for her.

“Report this to the colonel first. Don't let anyone decide whom to draw a sword on tonight just because someone asks whose side they're on.”

By the window, the bag of unfinished walnut cake had gone completely cold.

She folded down its top and left it for Adrian, then put on her coat to find out what orders those knights had actually received.
