# Chapter 183: Keep the Anchor on Shore

On the morning of February 11, Adrian found a shoe outside Luna's workroom.

Rolled cloth filled it. Its lace ran around a table leg to a wooden block. He stopped without touching anything.

“You're trapping visitors now?”

“It's a model.” Her right palm remained bandaged. “A trap wouldn't have such ugly labels.”

The block said Shore. Beside the shoe: Boat. He asked why the boat had already sunk.

“My assistant overfilled it.”

The young assistant, putting away a ruler, protested that she'd requested more weight. Luna conceded so promptly she might have prepared for it. Adrian laughed, and entering became easier.

None of the Temple originals was here. Three objects remained in independent custody. The table held authorized partial images, measurements from the repaired mirror mount, and an ordinary wooden frame Luna had made. The old energy cartridge remained sealed. Nothing connected to anybody's hand.

Beatrice, already present, placed signed conditions on the corner without sliding them toward him.

“Today we reconstruct external positioning. Not your memory.”

“I understand.”

“You look as though you hope I'll say they're practically the same.”

He couldn't deny it. Yesterday's three pages lay beneath a book at home. He understood every word and couldn't remember the person beside the signature. He wanted a straight road to someone he could acknowledge or reject, rather than circles around his name.

Luna asked her assistant to loosen the lace.

“Now it moves. When you pull the boat, shore shouldn't come too.”

She nudged the block with her uninjured hand, then let the assistant finish. Both block and shoe moved.

“If an unverified record becomes our shore, and your reaction proves the record, everything moves together. Someone will announce that the boat has been correctly placed all along.”

“So you don't need my hand?”

“Your eyes and patience today. Keep your hand for holding cups.”

External anchors could provide repeatable positions and sequences for comparisons, Beatrice explained. Material assessment remained incomplete. Any later personal-memory trial required another plan, authorization, and medical assessment. They couldn't turn today's paper over and continue on its back.

There was no shortcut hidden in the neatness of the signed conditions. He had wanted someone to describe a single next step, something he could complete and then be finished with wanting to know. Instead, the people at the table distinguished what they could do from what he hoped it might mean. It was frustrating precisely because he could see why they were right.

He sat. A small brass button lay near the frame, entirely unlit.

Luna asked him to choose something already on the table. Not his scar, not an image of the originals, and not whatever looked most magical to oblige her.

He chose the button.

“Why?”

“It doesn't have to explain who I am.”

Her smile briefly stilled. The assistant marked its position on paper. Reconstruction began with disappointing little things: the same angle, two readings, a plain sheet substituted and removed. A mounting strip obscured a reference line; they removed it and measured again.

The first results disagreed.

Adrian looked at Luna. Instead of announcing recognition, she inspected the tablecloth. Moving a chair had pulled it sideways.

“That's why we start with a button that won't protest its innocence.”

They removed the cloth. Adrian collected the nearest coaster, touching only everyday objects Beatrice permitted them to move. The next deviation diminished without disappearing. Both failures stayed in the record; a tidier third attempt didn't erase them.

After one set, Luna leaned back. She didn't disguise fatigue, and the assistant didn't put the next set before her during the break.

“Get what you mentioned,” she told Adrian.

He produced the travel book. Last time its captain had lost his hat. Now, ashore, he discovered it on a fish seller.

Luna laughed, then frowned while adjusting the pad beneath her right hand. Adrian stopped.

“Continue. Pain doesn't require turning the room into an infirmary.”

“You can stop me.”

“I will. Particularly if you give the fish seller the captain's voice.”

He tried again, making the fish seller even more solemn. The assistant laughed behind the shelves. Luna didn't show him her closed private notebook; he didn't ask. He finished their agreed two pages and put the book away. Helping him hadn't made her own work another document she owed.

Afterward Beatrice checked the frame against an authorized image. A shallow mark resembled two joined characters under slanting light. Luna had the transcription covered. Each independently recorded the shape before comparing answers.

None matched exactly. He looked from one sheet to the next, resisting the urge to choose the answer that seemed most like a useful discovery. The shapes were still there in the image. What they meant had not become clearer because three people had sat down determined to help him.

Adrian had written Door. Thinking about doors, about confinement, he'd let his pen decide ahead of his eyes.

Luna wrote Corner. Beatrice drew lines without naming them.

“I can't return your past today,” Luna said. “But I can avoid putting what frightens you most into it.”

That made him want to stay more than a claim of success would have.

Before dismantling the model, Beatrice moved his chair and placed the assistant behind a screen. Adrian should call a halt whenever he wished.

“Why the screen?”

“Watching your face, people may think they already know what you can bear.”

The assistant moved ordinary picture cards, nothing historical. Halfway through the third, Adrian said stop. The assistant heard, but finished the movement before releasing it.

Without reproach, Luna had the card covered again.

“We've found another problem.”

“I didn't want it to fall.”

“Then the card must be allowed to fall. The person shouldn't have to wait.”

They added a shallow tray. Adrian stopped the next attempt at the first card. The assistant immediately let go; it landed with a small sound. Adrian almost apologized for wasting time, then swallowed the apology.

It was such a small interruption that he might have dismissed it outside this room. A hand completing a movement, a person saving a card: nobody had raised a voice or refused outright. Yet he had felt himself waiting after he had already said he wanted the waiting to end.

Asked to describe the difference, he said the first time everyone had heard him and still thought another action mattered more. The second time, he believed his voice changed something.

“Write that,” Luna instructed. “Not that he behaved well.”

The assistant corrected his first line. He also explained that hearing behind the screen was difficult: a passing trolley had nearly swallowed the last syllable. They changed seats and proposed a falling-card signal requiring no sustained grip in addition to spoken words. It was a suggestion for a later protocol, not permission to test Adrian's endurance with today's model.

Looking at the pear pictured on the fallen card, Adrian suddenly wanted one.

“Your experiment has side effects.”

Luna pushed an unpeeled pear toward the edge for the assistant to pass him. That particular effect had been observed extensively, she said, although sweetness wasn't guaranteed. It was sour enough to make him squint. The assistant laughed. Laughter didn't make the error unimportant. It let the person who'd admitted it remain in the room.

By the morning's end they had a reproducibly positioned external frame, recorded deviations, and several unreadable shapes. No memory, no verdict, no old world illuminating the room.

Elena stood by the corridor noticeboard. She hadn't entered to wait or requested an advance report. Collecting her own consultation acknowledgment, she'd found a spare moment. Would he walk to the corner with her?

Yes.

Below, workers carried a long mirror upstairs. They waited on the landing, their reflections divided by its wooden frame. Thinking of the old signature, Adrian looked away, then worried that looking away punished her.

“I didn't remember anything.”

“That's a result too.”

“Are you relieved?”

She waited until the workers passed and they could speak without blocking anyone.

“A little. Not because I want you never to know. I'm afraid afterward you won't want to see me.”

His fingers rested against the cold railing.

“I'm afraid I'll demand you guarantee you've never done anything bad.”

“I can't guarantee things I don't remember.”

“I know. I hate knowing.”

She neither withdrew nor pulled him away. Outside, a coal cart rattled past. Waiting for it, he remembered treating other people's correct answers as solutions. When the answer didn't come, he'd assumed they weren't willing enough to help.

This time he could only admit he didn't know what wouldn't hurt to hear.

At the corner Elena took two scarves from her bag. One was hers; he'd left the other in the hall. She offered instead of wrapping it around him. He wound it twice and trapped the end inside his coat.

“Ugly?”

“Very determined.”

“That isn't an answer.”

She smiled and indicated his caught collar. He tried again without much improvement. Could she help? Yes. The adjustment was brief, neither a reconciliation ceremony nor a touch to his left hand.

Their standing together didn't accelerate the afternoon assessment. Beatrice sent its results: existing images and measurements sufficed for positioning; surface layers couldn't yet be reliably dated. One passage referred to a festival sequence, another to a western evacuation passage. Before comparing memory, they needed to learn whether different records meant the same things by those words.

Cecilia awaited her own application for festival registers. Viola had asked about old battlefield evacuation routes, not agreed to visit dangerous ground. Isabella proposed checking royal calendar amendments, but had to identify the scope of her request like anyone else. Adrian's impatience couldn't turn three inquiries into evidence at once.

He replied that he wanted to learn more about a limited trial. He signed no consent form that didn't yet exist. Wanting an answer was real, but it was not a blank space into which anyone could write whatever method came next. He reread the request carefully before folding it. Before any contact with personal memory, he wanted to know what he'd be shown, who could see the results, and who would actually stop the apparatus when he said stop.

He didn't give Elena the letter to check. She didn't ask for it. The separate sheet stayed in his hands, as her own papers had stayed in hers at the storage room yesterday.

That evening Luna's assistant returned his forgotten book with a shoe. The model was dismantled, Luna said; the owner could reclaim his boat. Adrian recognized the spare shoe the cobbler had returned last night, which he'd left outside her workroom.

The Boat label remained inside. He kept the little paper and set the shoe on the floor.

Tomorrow might still bring terrible news. At least today nobody had dragged the shore into the water to arrive sooner.
