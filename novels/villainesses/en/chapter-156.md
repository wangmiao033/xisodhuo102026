# Chapter 156: Someone Stays After Sunset

Isabella first removed the notice announcing a royal summons.

Two guests had already arrived that afternoon, January twenty-sixth. The attendant couldn't see the problem. It was the palace's usual form; he couldn't even find a different border.

“Use a blank sheet,” she said.

“Just reception?”

“My name. People may ask questions. They may also leave.”

Right-handed, she filed the discarded notice. Gauze still covered her left wrist. Adrian held back the windblown door curtain without proposing a more impressive phrase.

This was a private consultation with nobles, not a royal assembly or newly constituted council. Each invitation explained the proposed discussion of registration review. Attendance promised neither support for restoring her succession eligibility nor loyalty. Absence wouldn't make someone an enemy.

Of nine people invited, five had accepted. One now wrote that a family member had a fever.

“I hope it's true,” she murmured.

“You hope someone in his household is ill?”

Startled, she pressed her forehead. “I hope he isn't avoiding me.”

“More reasonable.”

She wished the patient a swift recovery without requesting a physician's certificate. Then she removed the unused cup rather than letting an empty chair stand trial all afternoon.

Elena arrived last with a summary of her personal objection, not her father's voting seal. Baroness Merriwell followed, settled her cane, and asked whether agreeing to anything would require a show of hands.

“No,” Isabella said.

“Good. My shoulder is troublesome today.”

She sounded amused, but her gaze rested on Isabella's wrist. The princess left it cushioned instead of hiding it to appear vigorous.

Two nobles proposed the straightforward solution: guarantees from reliable families should obtain exceptions for those immediately affected. General rules could wait.

Knowing Elena and Adrian were courting, one suggested Rosenberg backing would be easiest. Why turn this into a question about every ordinary person's betrothal?

Elena's cup stopped halfway to her mouth.

“Some named people lack that sort of family.”

“Those who have one can guarantee them.”

“Guarantee what? Whom he may love, or who pays for a loss?”

He didn't take offense. People with assets could more readily demonstrate their ability to compensate consequences. That wasn't unreasonable in every other kind of contract.

Isabella let him explain rather than immediately calling it a sale. Then she asked about losses that property couldn't buy back.

She didn't display her wrist for effect. Some losses fell on the person bearing them; a guarantor couldn't consent in that person's place.

The noble glanced at her sleeve and ceased speaking solely about silver. He still preferred pursuing a practicable path first. He wouldn't sign a joint declaration that might be read as opposing all safety review.

“No such declaration is here,” Isabella said. “Read it before deciding, if one is written.”

She hadn't won the support she wanted. Nor had she driven away a person merely for refusing to promise more than he would do. Someone remained willing to listen, although that was less than she had imagined when writing the invitations.

Merriwell asked whether technical material explained what compatibility assessment could actually measure. Adrian read the brief public conclusion Luna had authorized him to share. The guests weren't recipients of her private research records.

Existing data couldn't rank all six people in a certain order of safety. Risks along some paths didn't mean personal choices could be derived from load figures.

“That doesn't mean no combination is more dangerous,” a guest observed.

“No,” Adrian agreed.

“You won't improve your own case much that way.”

“They'll ask me about the original tomorrow.”

“Perhaps today,” Merriwell said.

An attendant brought Isabella a reminder: half an hour remained before the final report delivery round. She read it openly, rather than reprimanding an interruption before guests.

Her limited coordination of old-project records expired at sunset. No extension decision had arrived. This meeting couldn't prolong the small badge's life.

A guest asked what these discussions were worth if she lost her coordinating role.

Isabella pressed the cushion edge. Adrian saw her knuckles tighten. She had asked herself the same question, without wanting it spoken in that voice.

“Are you here to discuss registration rules, or the badge I hold?”

“Power gives discussion results.”

“I'm not asking you to pretend it doesn't matter. But I cannot invite you with powers I haven't received for tomorrow.”

Merriwell moved her cane closer to her chair, signaling no intention of leaving.

The other guest stood. Another engagement awaited. He would receive specific proposals, but made no promises. Isabella called his carriage to the normal collection point. Nobody delayed returning his coat.

Those remaining didn't thereby become loyal allies. Isabella couldn't count the chairs still occupied and turn their number into votes. Each person had stayed to discuss a different part of the problem, with different reservations still intact.

They narrowed their differences to things they could actually submit. Merriwell would personally ask for clear ordinary-review deadlines and remedies. Another guest would ask which losses financial guarantees covered, rather than letting guarantees become arranged partner selection. Elena insisted personal choice couldn't vary by assets, while accepting that genuine risk needed assessment.

Three positions didn't merge into a single sentence. Isabella recorded what each would undertake, let them read it, and omitted unanimous support for the princess's proposal.

Near the end, someone asked why Adrian still hadn't named his eventual choice.

He put down the technical conclusion.

“Their rights to choose don't begin when I supply an answer you find satisfactory.”

The guest shrugged, calling it evasion.

Elena looked at Adrian rather than answering for him. Isabella nearly declared private questions forbidden, then stopped. Preventing the asking wasn't a power that made questions disappear.

“You may dislike his answer,” she told the guest. “We won't supply another on his behalf.”

The consultation ended on time. Guests took their separate texts. No declaration, no cheers for restored royal power. Hard sweets and a wet coaster remained.

“Have I invited everyone to an indigestible afternoon tea?” Isabella asked.

“The sweets were good.”

“You ate one.”

“Quite hard. It needed time.”

She finally laughed. Saying farewell to Merriwell by the door, Elena looked back and smiled too. His conversation with Isabella didn't transform the afternoon into betrayal.

The hall keeper brought the account for refreshments and the room. An attendant proposed sending it with old-project document expenses. Isabella refused. This private consultation wasn't covered by that budget. Two things happening on the same day couldn't make one purse responsible for both.

She requested entry against her private expenses at the agreed price. The keeper noted another pot of hot water but charged no supplement; it fell within the guest allowance. She read the figures back against the original quote rather than assuming the extra water must have produced an extra charge. Then she checked and signed right-handed, without hiding her embarrassment at nearly mixing accounts beneath an extravagant tip.

Adrian found her fallen pen cap.

“Am I terribly foolish? Even holding a meeting without authority seems something I must learn again.”

“I didn't know how they charged for hot water either.”

“You needn't share every admission of ignorance.”

“Only that one.”

Smiling, she cleared the table. The coaster hadn't dried. She left it on the tray instead of enclosing it in an official report.

Then came the old-project progress statement. The corrected return text and full payment register still hadn't all arrived. Eight people remained missing. Separate authorities were examining the replacement catch and questions about inspection and the display card. She listed recovered records, unanswered requests, and continuing contacts, including her own responsibility for restricting earlier inquiries.

Today's betrothal consultation couldn't become evidence of performance under an unrelated appointment.

An attendant helped with her coat. Adrian accompanied her to the royal receiving window. The report was filed first; the silver-edged badge followed. Separate acknowledgments covered both. The extension application had been forwarded, but the duty clerk couldn't predict a decision.

“If families come tomorrow, is there still an ordinary contact point?”

He checked the arrangements. Searches and existing preservation requests didn't vanish with her appointment. Outstanding matters could be pursued under their original references.

She listened without forcing a promise that eight people would appear tomorrow. The distinction mattered to the next family arriving at the window. Her own right to coordinate might stop, but that must not be misreported as the disappearance of every existing case and contact.

At sunset, the little piece of metal she had so often touched lay behind the counter.

On the steps, her right hand entered the empty inner pocket. She withdrew it irritably.

“It weighed so little. Why is its absence obvious?”

Adrian didn't say other things mattered more. That would sound like consolation prepared in advance.

“You always remembered where it was.”

“Yes.”

After standing in the wind awhile, she asked whether he must hurry home.

He checked the time. He could walk to the carriage house ahead, but tonight he and Elena would discuss their separate material for tomorrow.

Isabella accepted without extending the walk into a loyalty test.

Beside the carriage house was a warm waiting room. An old man who minded umbrellas was repairing a board game with a chipped corner. Waiting for her carriage, Isabella asked to look.

He offered the pieces but told her not to move the table with her injured hand. She complied. Right-handed, she arranged what seemed a beautiful opening. By her third move, she had blocked her only retreat.

Adrian laughed before he could prevent it.

“You may point things out,” she said.

“I wanted to let you notice.”

“Kindness needn't be so punctual about it.”

The old man took back one of her moves, allowing another attempt without charging a price only a princess could afford. Considering carefully, she chose a less impressive edge square and left room for what followed.

The old man watched the pieces rather than her face. She could study the next move without having someone announce that her improvement had restored faith in her future. Nobody called it a metaphor for government. The carriage arrived, and the unfinished game remained unfinished.

At home, Merriwell's additional note awaited her. It clearly defined what the baroness would continue asking. It addressed Isabella, not the holder of a particular badge. She set it on the table beside the empty pocket of her coat.

One note didn't mean power no longer mattered or that everyone had chosen to stand behind her.

Before dinner, she asked whether the others wanted to discuss their separate attendance tomorrow. Elena was already in the sitting room. Luna agreed to stay briefly. Viola brought the confirmed route; Cecilia had a passage she wanted to revise.

Six chairs were drawn out. None marked a presiding seat.

Standing beside hers, Isabella realized that her pocket was empty, yet people still wished to hear her finish speaking tonight.
