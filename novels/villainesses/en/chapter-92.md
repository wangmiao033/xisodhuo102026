# Chapter 92: The Witch Wants to Know What's Under the Glove

On December 9, Luna entered the breakfast room with a boiled egg and set it in front of Adrian.

“Compensation.”

He looked at the egg. “What did I lose last night?”

“Half a minute of your heartbeat. I used it for a joke with insufficient evidence.”

Elena looked up. “One egg is enough?”

“If you require two, I'll need to reconsider the cost of speaking.”

Adrian picked up his spoon and gently cracked the shell. “You can keep talking. Just don't let the pointer answer for me.”

Luna smiled. She had been about to ask who was allowed to answer for him, then noticed that Elena had not looked away. Instead, she poured herself some tea.

Cecilia's unfinished strap still lay on the table. The witch examined it and offered to register it as a new type of filter if Cecilia wished to donate it to the tower.

“I still intend to tie it around a flask,” the saint said.

“Then the flask will need to demonstrate a considerable will to live.”

Viola laughed so hard she nearly dropped her bread into her soup. Cecilia wasn't offended. She passed the knitting over and asked Luna to identify the place most likely to unravel. Luna pointed out a loose stitch, then realized she had already begun a different sort of unpaid consultation over breakfast.

The wider table was on the tower's second floor. No strange bottles stood by the window, only a plant with yellowing leaf tips.

“Is that poisonous?” Adrian asked.

“No. Just neglected.”

Luna fetched a watering can, moistened the soil, then cleared away two empty cups. At last, there was room to spread the drawings out. Ivo's letter of introduction lay beside a public directory bearing the institution's registration number. The visitors could check what the tower did and find out who was responsible for receiving complaints.

She began with the stone.

The original remained in municipal custody in Greymarch. They had brought only authorized drawings, dimensions, and site records. Comparing those with her preservation supplement, Luna pointed out three matches and two areas she could not make out clearly. The adjacent curved grooves were consistent with the mounting arrangement for paired carrier plates. Tracings alone could not date the stone, however, or establish that the document sleeve's lining had come from the same place.

“Here. Whoever drew this might have mistaken a broken edge for an incised line.”

Viola leaned closer and found her own notation of the drainage channel's direction. “There was mortar still covering that part. The foreman said they had to wait for the person responsible for its custody before removing it.”

“Ask them not to hurry, then. A side view first would help.”

Luna circled the area on her own working sheet. She did not ink a correction directly over their original observation.

Next came the November 24 test record. After reading the responses from the blank and the artificially marked comparison specimen, she asked whether the operator had waited for the indicator to return completely to zero after power was cut before lowering the screen.

Cecilia turned to a later page and showed her the time recorded there. Luna nodded and changed the little cross beside her question into a dash.

“Why cross it off?” Adrian asked.

“Because the record has answered it. Keeping it would make my list of questions look more impressive. It would also send you on the same errand twice.”

She said it casually. Cecilia gave her a more attentive look.

Plenty remained unanswered. The paired traces on the inert material indicated an associated response under particular conditions. The results said nothing about its application to human beings or whether it had once been modified. Luna did not give their fatigue and patches of light the grander meaning of proof that one person had already borne someone else's calamity.

She took a restoration log from the shelf and opened it to an installation drawing of an old registration plate. There was a gap between two inserts, with fixing teeth of different depths marked beside them.

“The same housing can hold different things,” she said. “You need to establish what was in it. You can't fill the inside with whatever frightens you most just because the box looks familiar.”

Adrian's fingers stilled at the edge of his glove.

He had come because there were so many gaps he couldn't fill. A line of lore easy to skip in a game now required real people to sit around a table and ask their way through it.

Luna noticed the movement.

“I saw an old scar on the back of your left hand when you were washing last night,” she said. “Some of the questions in these materials concern you personally, don't they?”

Elena did not answer for him. Viola shifted her chair a little so that he would not have to speak across someone else.

“Yes. But the scar isn't from Greymarch.”

“When did you get it?”

“I can't give you a reliable date.”

Luna's pencil paused. “All right. I'll write ‘unknown’ where something is unknown.”

After thinking for a moment, he explained that touching certain old objects had brought unusual sensations in the back of his hand. These were his own impressions. No instrument had recorded them, and they could not substitute for evidence in any case file. He did not assemble the incomplete images he remembered into a past that sounded certain.

The witch's smile faded. Her concentration became more apparent than her teasing.

“Pain? Heat? Something like a tug?”

“Not always the same. I need to separate what I actually remember first.”

She nearly asked a third question. Then she looked at his teacup and rose to add hot water.

“Then I won't race you to an answer. Interesting things usually make me impatient. It's a failing of mine.”

“I thought researchers were supposed to be particularly patient.”

“That's how they describe themselves when they write the report.”

Adrian laughed, and so did she. But her gaze still returned occasionally to the glove covering the scar. She plainly wanted to know more. Admitting to a fault had not suddenly cured her curiosity.

While they waited for the water to cool, Luna took a brass lamp base from beside the shelf. A repair-return tag clung to the bottom. Two notches on its side had been ground down in error. It looked too ordinary for a player to glance at twice.

“Is that an old contract device too?” Viola asked.

“The housing is. It was later adapted as a streetlamp mounting. Someone repaired it according to a different model and ground this part flat.”

She showed them how the two notches had originally carried weight separately. Since the alteration, one end kept slipping outward. The town had written to complain of a haunted lamp bracket and asked her to come exorcise it. She had sent back a drawing of a new support.

“Were they disappointed?” Adrian asked.

“The reply said the brass cost less than hiring people to sing hymns for three days. They included two jars of preserved fruit.”

Cecilia looked up. “I hope they didn't keep charging for the hymns.”

“Apparently it became voluntary singing after dinner.”

Luna put the base back and ran a finger over the damaged grooves. She wanted to know how old things had worked for reasons beyond their mystery. Sometimes, understanding one very dull support meant a person walking underneath it wouldn't get hit on the head.

Adrian looked at the repair tag. It gave him another reason to want to keep asking questions.

Near noon, the caretaker came to report that the new bearing for the lift was the wrong size. The supplier could deliver another only the following day. Luna read the delivery slip and immediately frowned.

She was about to ask them to wait, then noticed that the tea on the table had gone cold for the second time.

“We'll continue this afternoon. Have lunch. I need a word with someone who regards dimensions as a suggestion.”

On the way downstairs, Viola asked Adrian whether he wanted her there that afternoon too.

“I'd like all of you there,” he said. “But I don't want you to keep postponing your own work because of me.”

“I'll take the letter about my follow-up examination to the posting cabinet first. Then I'll come back.”

She went to do it rather than treating guard duty outside his door as the only proper use of her leave. Cecilia asked to borrow the public volume of the restoration log. When the caretaker brought out the lending slip, she checked the return date instead of treating sainthood as an exemption from registration.

Elena and Adrian stopped at the little window in the guesthouse.

“Do you want her to look?”

“I want to know what the scar is. I haven't worked out how I want to investigate it yet.”

She nodded, her fingers resting on the sill for a moment.

“I was jealous this morning. She sat down with that egg as though she could make absolutely anything sound easy.”

“And afterward?”

“Afterward, I thought some of her questions deserved asking. Both things can be true.”

Adrian smiled. “I can want to understand an old scar and want to have lunch with you without a drawing on the table.”

“Just one lunch?”

“We can start with this one. I'll ask you properly for the next one too.”

At last, she reached out and smoothed the turned-up edge of his scarf. The colors looked warm this time, and there was no meat juice on it. After examining it, she asked whether he had finally learned to take it off before eating.

“I had a good teacher.”

“She deserves a compliment.”

“She looks lovely today.”

Elena raised her eyes to his, a smile slowly forming.

“That isn't specific enough.”

He thought about it. “You didn't pretend you weren't worried,” he said quietly. “And you didn't agree for me. I like the way you looked at me and waited for me to finish.”

She had evidently been preparing another objection. Instead, she lowered her eyes. A vegetable cart rattled past outside. When the wheels had gone, she slipped her hand into his right hand.

They stood that way for a little while, until someone downstairs called that the soup was getting cold.

That afternoon, Luna had indeed exchanged the papers for a new set.

They were not a contract. There was no blank space awaiting Adrian's signature. She called them a proposed plan, sketching a table first, then marking the equipment she intended to place around it.

“I'd like to examine your scar,” she said.

This time, no joke softened the request.

Her first suggestion was a visual examination in ordinary light: measuring its position and dimensions and recording the sensations he described. If there was still a reason to proceed, she would then consider passing low-intensity inspection light through an isolation medium to see whether the nearby medium underwent any recordable change. She explained that this later step was entirely different from the previous evening's pulse wheel. It might cause discomfort or localized warmth. It might also tell them nothing at all.

No instruments had yet been placed on the table. The exact power limit and stopping conditions had not been written down. Luna acknowledged that equipment checks and a physician's assessment would have to come first. Not coughing on a journey did not establish fitness for an experiment.

Cecilia asked whether any original historical contract would be introduced.

“Not under the current proposal,” Luna said. “You've brought only authorized copies in any case. I want to understand what I'm looking at before discussing whether there's a reason to add another medium.”

“Then why does this say that further necessary procedures may be added as observations develop?” Viola asked, pointing to the margin.

Luna looked at the wording she habitually used.

“Because I don't yet know what the first step will show.”

“It doesn't tell us who decides on the second step either,” Elena said.

The room fell quiet. Luna regarded her arrows. For the first time, she seemed to see that what looked to her like a convenient route forward might look to the person across the table like a corridor with no marked exit.

She turned the paper back toward herself, then paused. She did not simply cross out the sentence and pretend that solved the problem.

“I want room to adjust,” she said honestly. “Perhaps more than you're willing to give me. We need to discuss that.”

Adrian lowered his gaze and read the entire page.

Outside, the lift was still out of service. Workers were carrying boxes up the neighboring steps one at a time. The first thing he had seen Luna do on arriving was pull the braking lever. She really would stop when danger appeared. But liking what he had seen was not enough to decide who should judge the dangers of an experiment.

He laid the proposal flat. His hand was still gloved.

“First, I want to understand what this line usually means here.”

Luna took the pencil from behind her ear and settled into the chair at the other end of the table.

“Then let's start there.”

There was no signature on the paper. No inspection light came on beside the table.

This time, the witch waited for him to finish his question.
