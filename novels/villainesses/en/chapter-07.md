# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume One: A Betrothal Written in Blood
### Chapter 7: Lady Elena Would Never Fall for Me

Adrian Reed would later swear that he had never intended to give an interview.

He'd only gone to the gatehouse to deliver some papers.

Then someone shouted from the other side of the iron fence.

"Mr. Reed! Are you and Lady Rosenberg in love?"

Adrian stopped.

Several reporters had gathered outside, each wearing a newspaper's badge on his hat.

The gate guard waved them back.

"Keep your distance! This is private property!"

"Just one question, Mr. Reed!"

"Who initiated the kiss?"

"When's the wedding?"

Adrian considered sticking his head inside the folder he was carrying.

"Yesterday was an accident," he said.

Every reporter lowered his head.

Pens scratched furiously.

One of the younger journalists dragged over a little wooden stool so he could take better notes. When Adrian glanced at his notebook, he saw the words *Duke's daughter boldly declares love* already written across the page.

"I didn't say that."

"But our readers want to know," the young man replied.

"Then your readers should want to know what actually happened."

"So what happened?"

"My shoe caught her dress. We nearly fell."

The reporter considered this gravely.

"I see. You kissed her to save her."

Adrian stared.

Compared to these people, the Judicial Office's forged evidence was positively straightforward.

Then he remembered Elena's instruction.

*Do not answer questions about my love life.*

Too late.

The reporter with the enormous mustache leaned forward.

"Has Lady Elena fallen for you, then?"

"Of course not!"

His answer came out rather louder than intended.

"Lady Elena would never fall for me. She's a duke's daughter. I'm a servant. We don't have that kind of relationship."

A moment of silence settled over the gate.

Then the pens started moving even faster.

"So she's in love with you, but you don't return her feelings?"

"No!"

"You've rejected the duke's daughter?"

"That isn't remotely what I—"

"Mr. Reed."

Someone behind him cleared her throat.

Adrian turned around with the mechanical slowness of a man approaching the gallows.

Elena stood on the front steps in a pale-green day dress. She was smiling.

The roll of documents in her hand, however, had been squeezed almost flat.

It was the sort of smile that reminded Adrian of the execution notice hidden in the jewelry case.

"Are you finished?" she asked.

"Almost?"

"Then come to my study."

She turned and went inside.

Martha, standing beside the steps, shook her head.

"Is it too late to run?" Adrian whispered.

"You were brave enough to challenge the prince himself."

"That was a completely different level of danger."

"Quite." Martha nodded. "The prince didn't actually take a sword to you."

The study door closed.

Adrian decided to confess before Elena could begin the interrogation.

"My lady, I know I was wrong."

"Do you?"

"I shouldn't have spoken to the reporters."

"Anything else?"

"I shouldn't have shouted."

Elena set down her documents.

"You think this is about the volume?"

"Then...?"

"When did I ever say that I could never fall for you?"

Adrian blinked.

A bird chirped twice outside the window.

Even the wildlife seemed to be laughing at him.

"But you asked me to clear things up yesterday."

"I asked you to make it clear that the kiss at the ball was an accident. I did not ask you to decide who I might or might not fall in love with."

"You're a duke's daughter."

"You mentioned that yesterday."

"And I'm a servant."

"That too."

Adrian opened his mouth.

Nothing useful came out.

Elena's voice softened.

"Do you think I choose who to care about based entirely on their family name?"

He shook his head.

He'd only really come to know her over the last two days. She checked evidence instead of accepting convenient answers. She got frightened and refused to hide behind someone else's decisions. And when she was nervous, she made jokes about his shoes.

What gave him the right to speak for her?

"I'm sorry," he said. "I was trying to keep the gossip from hurting you. Instead, I made it sound as though I understand your feelings better than you do."

Elena looked at him for a long moment.

"At last. The correct answer."

"So you're not angry anymore?"

"Who said that?"

"Then how do I make it up to you?"

She opened a drawer and took out a thin sheet of paper.

"Start by reading this."

It was a resupply order.

Three boxes of blue sealing wax. Two rolls of ceremonial ribbon. Blank stationery. Delivery to the east warehouse at three in the afternoon.

"We're buying more sealing wax?"

"No."

"Then why write an order?"

"You said we needed a way to investigate Henry without arresting him."

Adrian's eyes brightened.

"A planted order."

"One that doesn't make him think he's being singled out."

They spread the paper across the desk.

Adrian's first idea involved staging a warehouse leak, transferring several crates, and making Henry discover an altered purchasing record.

Elena stopped him halfway through.

"Do you always take the longest possible route between two points?"

"A complicated plan is harder to see through."

"A complicated plan involves more people who can make mistakes. One planted document is safer than five."

Adrian thought about that.

"You're right."

They settled on a single false resupply order.

It used the Rosenberg household's ordinary template, with one deliberate error in the supplier's name.

The real shop was the **White Heron Stationers** on South Street.

The false order named **White Swan Stationers**.

A harmless-looking error.

A useful one.

Martha would place the order in the folder Henry reviewed every afternoon. If the false supplier name appeared anywhere outside that folder, they'd know exactly where the information had traveled.

"That's a classic bait document," Adrian said.

"I know."

"You do?"

"Do you think noble families have survived for centuries without ever having to investigate a leak?"

He held back a smile.

Elena noticed anyway.

"Something to say?"

"Only that you'd make an excellent investigator."

"And you?"

"The person you yell at when the investigation goes wrong."

"An accurate job description."

Adrian raised one concern before they finalized the order.

Nobody was actually to request three nonexistent boxes of wax from the supplier. The document was meant to trace information, not lure Henry into a theft accusation.

Elena approved.

She pressed her personal seal onto the retained copy.

"This isn't a purchase authorization," she said. "If someone tries to collect goods against it, we'll tell the shop not to release anything."

"Then how do we find out where the order went?"

"By recording who's coming and going through the gatehouse. Not by chasing Henry around shouting *traitor*."

"You really have thought this through."

"Admitting that occasionally won't affect your wages."

Adrian looked down at the rose badge on his coat.

"Am I actually being paid?"

"Let's see whether you survive the Judicial Office hearing first."

The Rosenberg employment contract was proving remarkably suspenseful.

Martha returned. They checked the deliberate error, prepared two numbered cover sheets, and established who would keep each copy.

"One goes in Henry's folder," Elena said. "The other stays in the study. Don't mix them."

"Understood, my lady."

"And nobody is to withhold Henry's pay or search his quarters simply because we're suspicious."

Adrian looked up.

That time, Elena had said it without prompting.

She noticed his expression and looked away.

"Moving a document doesn't mean someone knows what's written inside it."

"I know."

"You said something like that before."

"So you remembered."

She didn't answer.

But Adrian was beginning to realize that the woman who claimed not to trust him listened to him rather carefully.

After Martha left, silence settled over the study.

Elena sorted documents.

Adrian lingered at the desk.

"Was there something else?" she asked.

"About what I said at the gate."

"Another apology?"

"No. I wasn't saying that I'm too low-born to speak to you. I just... you're already being targeted by the prince. I didn't want my name making things harder for you."

Her pen stopped.

"You're remarkably fond of deciding what counts as a problem for other people."

"I'm working on it."

"It's all right if that takes time."

"Really?"

"As long as you keep trying."

Was that encouragement?

Adrian wasn't entirely sure.

Then he asked something significantly more dangerous.

"What kind of person *do* you like?"

Elena looked up.

He regretted it immediately.

That sounded like something a suitor would ask before proposing.

"I mean—for future reference. In case reporters ask again. I don't want to say the wrong thing."

She watched him for a couple of seconds.

"First, I don't like people answering on my behalf."

"Noted."

"Second, I don't like people who break their promises."

"Also noted."

"And third..."

She paused.

"I don't like people who are always looking for a way to run."

Adrian stared.

"Is that aimed at me?"

"Consider it a reminder."

Then she smiled.

"Don't worry. You didn't run this morning."

The afternoon passed with the Rosenberg household carrying on as though the previous night's scandal had been a minor inconvenience.

Maids counted supplies left over from the ball. The kitchens prepared refreshments for visiting lawyers. Workers pushed carts through the outer grounds.

Only the reporters lingering beyond the gate betrayed how thoroughly the household had become the capital's latest obsession.

Adrian was assigned to help with inventory near the east warehouse.

He couldn't watch Henry directly without drawing suspicion.

So he spent half an hour counting rolls of silk.

On the twenty-seventh roll, a young warehouse attendant came over.

"Mr. Reed?"

"Yes?"

"Is it true Lady Elena kissed you last night?"

"It was an accident."

"Ah. So you're keeping it quiet for now."

Adrian looked at the silk.

Some misunderstandings were clearly beyond the reach of ordinary language.

"Has Mr. Henry been here today?"

"Just now." The attendant pointed to the office. "He took some orders inside."

"Is he still there?"

"No. Went out toward the yard."

The folder was where it belonged.

Its seal had been opened.

The false order was gone.

Adrian didn't touch the remaining papers. He sent the attendant to fetch Martha so they could check them together.

"The order with the wrong shop name?" he asked.

Martha went through the folder.

"Missing."

"Anything else?"

"Not a page."

Someone had taken precisely the document they'd planted.

But why?

A shout came from the yard.

"Mr. Henry! You forgot to sign!"

No reply.

Adrian hurried toward the rear gate.

A gray carriage was already rounding the corner.

The gatekeeper was struggling with his register.

"Was that Henry's carriage?"

"No. It had a temporary Judicial Office pass."

"Did Henry get in?"

"Carrying a parcel. The driver said he was collecting royal purchasing papers."

Adrian felt his stomach tighten.

Martha instructed the gatekeeper to record the exact departure time.

The carriage, he remembered, had a recently repaired metal band on the right wheel. Its roof lacked the brass lamp usually fitted to Judicial Office vehicles.

The pass itself had seemed genuine, but the driver hadn't given a name.

"So we don't know whether it actually belonged to the Judicial Office," Adrian said.

"Only that someone used its authority to get through our gate." Martha added a note. "That's more useful than guessing who owned the horses."

They went to Henry's office.

The cupboard stood half open. Papers lay scattered across the floor. His teacup was still warm.

By the drawer lay a broken silver sealing handle, its fresh fracture catching the light.

Adrian crouched without touching it.

"Martha, could you note where that was found?"

"You think it's connected to the wax?"

"Possibly. We need to check before saying anything certain."

Martha wrapped the piece in paper and had two attendants witness its storage.

There was also a faint red mark along the desk's edge and some gray grit on the window ledge.

Adrian had both noted.

He refused to decide what they meant just because they looked suspicious.

Elena arrived a moment later.

She surveyed the room, then turned to him.

"Where is he?"

"He left in a carriage using a Judicial Office pass."

"Why didn't you stop him?"

"We had no grounds."

"Good."

Adrian blinked.

"Good?"

"At least you didn't drag him back and force him to answer questions."

"But maybe our planted order frightened him off."

"Or maybe he realized someone was watching him."

She paused.

"Do you think he's running?"

Adrian glanced at the unfinished tea.

"If he planned to flee, why leave his personal belongings behind?"

Elena's expression changed.

Martha spoke quietly.

"You think someone summoned him in a hurry?"

"Too much of a hurry to let him decide whether he wanted to go."

A message arrived from the gatehouse.

The gray carriage hadn't followed the route listed on its pass. It had left through the south gate and turned toward the river docks.

Elena shut the ledger.

"Prepare a carriage."

"You're going yourself?"

"We found him. Shouldn't we be the ones to establish what's happened?"

"My lady, aren't you afraid it's dangerous?"

"Yes."

She looked at him.

"But I dislike the idea of someone taking my deputy steward away without telling me why even more."

Adrian grabbed his coat and followed her.

Before leaving the office, he glanced at the retained copy of their false order.

*White Swan Stationers.*

The misspelled name sat there in plain sight.

Someone was carrying that deliberate mistake toward the river.

Adrian made sure the gatehouse recorded where their own carriage was headed. If something went wrong at the docks, at least someone would know where to start looking.

Elena noticed.

She nodded without a word.

As the carriage rolled out of the rear gate, she asked, "What you said to those reporters this morning. Did you actually believe it?"

"Which part?"

"That I could never fall for you."

Adrian nearly dropped his notebook.

"Are we really discussing that now?"

"I'd rather hear your answer than read the newspapers' version."

The wheels rattled over the cobblestones.

He thought for a moment.

"I don't know you well enough to say anything is impossible," he answered. "What I do know is that you're willing to come with me to find someone who might be in danger."

Elena looked out the window.

"Better."

"Better how?"

"At least you didn't make up my mind for me."

She fell silent.

But she shifted slightly, leaving more space for him beside the window.
