# Chapter 135: Not to Make You Stay

On the morning of January 10, Isabella was not sitting at the head of the reception table.

She stood beside a basket of old clothes, watching a woman fold clean coarse cloth into a rectangle. Cold water had reddened the woman's fingers. Only on her third fold did she notice the princess still standing.

“Your Highness needn't wait. I'll finish this first.”

“I can wait.”

The words seemed to sound awkward even to Isabella. She sat in another chair.

They were in the back room of Cecilia's office. The front room still received people seeking help. No one had emptied the benches for a royal visit. As Adrian entered, someone outside was complaining that a relief letter had the wrong house number. Someone else needed directions to a pharmacy, not that form at all.

Cecilia went to find him the right desk and asked the three of them to wait.

The woman folding clothes was Clara Senn, a laundress in the capital. Four years earlier, her brother Milo had been hired to accompany equipment for an old project as a porter. He had never returned. She kept his employment departure slip, but the latest answer to her inquiries said personnel matters had been transferred with the old case. The current desk considered the matter closed.

“The desk was finished,” she said. “That isn't my brother coming home.”

She put the cloth in her basket before taking the slip from an inner pocket. Two old handkerchiefs protected it. Fine thread repaired one corner.

Adrian did not reach first.

“May we see it?”

“Yes. Don't copy the address onto anything you put up outside. It isn't that I'm afraid of visitors. I don't want the landlord saying I'm inviting trouble again.”

Isabella looked up. “People have visited before?”

“To ask questions. No one beat me. They asked the same thing, then said it wasn't their responsibility. The landlord thought I owed money.”

Whatever Isabella had prepared to say remained unspoken.

Cecilia returned with a new acknowledgment for Clara. Today's submission was a copy voluntarily provided for checking; the original would go home with her. Search inquiries would pass through the office. She need not tell her story again to every visitor.

“Nor are you being asked to support a candidate,” Cecilia added.

Clara glanced at Isabella, nodded, and unfolded the handkerchiefs.

The departure slip said neither that Milo was dead nor why he had disappeared. It recorded his age as an adult, the work for which he was hired, the receiving desk, and one transfer location. Later replies threaded several offices together. The last reduced a man still unlocated to a matter referred onward.

Cecilia recorded verifiable facts separately from the family's account. Isabella did not demand that royal be removed, nor promise Milo would be found tomorrow.

“I can first request a fresh examination of the reason given for closing this,” she said.

Clara asked what it would cost.

“There is no charge for that request.”

“Then can I still go to work this afternoon?”

More clearly than any seal, the question made Adrian understand that an hour spent sitting here was not merely an hour to everyone.

“Leave a time when we can reach you,” Cecilia said. “You don't have to stay here waiting.”

Noticing a patched coat in the basket, Isabella asked whether winter-clothing assistance should be arranged first.

Clara looked up, abruptly wary.

“That isn't mine. It's a customer's. I didn't say I needed clothes.”

Isabella stared.

“I only wanted—”

“I know you want to help.” Clara folded in the coat's edge. “Someone offered me rice last time. I accepted. At home, I found the paper said my living arrangements had been provided for. When I asked again, they told me to wait.”

Cecilia asked her to identify that reply and added a clarification: relief and the search must be handled separately. Adrian looked at the clean laundry. He did not explain Isabella's good intentions until Clara was obliged to be grateful.

“You should receive the rice,” Isabella finally said. “If you meet the ordinary conditions for assistance, it isn't payment for not asking again.”

“I only want to know the last place he went.”

“We'll start there.”

Clara nodded and relaxed. Before leaving home, she told them, Milo had repaired their leaking bucket. Badly. It leaked again the next day. At that, she unexpectedly smiled.

“He always said he'd fix it when he got back. No one puts that in an old project file.”

Adrian stopped writing. It was not an identity-verification question or something they could put on a street notice to make it more moving without her permission. He laid down his pen and listened before asking one final question about the slip.

Clara wrote Evening as her available time and put away her original. Before leaving, she checked the cloth in the basket again, as though remembering others were waiting for that too.

Isabella stood and held the door.

Clara thanked her. She did not fall to her knees or promise to trust the princess because of it.

When the door closed, Isabella took another summary from her document bag.

“This passed through my office six weeks ago.”

Adrian said nothing.

“Not every original. A summary of unresolved old-project petitions. Eight adults, six households, all with someone we could contact in the capital. The office summary said they had been referred onward and reopening was not recommended. I had it marked No further reports to me required.”

She turned the page to her own instruction.

Cecilia did not offer the excuse that she had been busy.

“Did you see that the families were still asking?”

“It was in the summary. Half a line.”

“Half a line was still there.”

“I know.”

Isabella's voice hardened. Her jaw tightened, and Adrian expected an explanation of how much she had to manage. Instead, she continued.

“I saw who might take charge of the royal projects first. Later, when the old equipment summary became questionable, I wanted people with enough authority to help me explain it. What you had all accomplished would make my application look more credible.”

She looked at Adrian.

“I didn't propose a betrothal only because I thought we could get along. I wanted your name beside mine, somewhere other people couldn't easily remove it.”

Carriage bells sounded twice outside.

Adrian waited until they passed before asking whether, had he accepted, ordinary access to records would have required her permission.

“I would have thought we should discuss it first.”

“What if I wanted to publish a finding that harmed your position?”

She did not answer immediately.

The hesitation explained more than an elegant qualification could.

“I might have asked you to wait. Until it wouldn't damage my arrangements.”

“So you didn't only want another person able to speak the truth.”

“No.” Her fingers slowly tightened on the bag. “I wanted to decide when he spoke too.”

Cecilia glanced at the old instruction and asked whether today's admission was intended to remain among the three of them.

Isabella raised her head.

“Do you want me to stand outside and confess to everyone?”

“I want the people affected by your decision to know which step you set aside. Not for him and me to receive a secret and become responsible for believing you've changed.”

Isabella said nothing. Adrian understood that she might still have hoped, in inviting him, that finally being honest would make him more willing to stay.

He did not want comforting her to turn that expectation into another indistinct bargain.

“Thank you for telling me. I still need to see how this is handled.”

“I knew you'd say that.”

“You were hoping a little that I'd say something else.”

Her smile was thin. “I've embarrassed myself enough today. You needn't point out that part too.”

“Then I'll spare you the extra sentence.”

She looked at him and suddenly laughed properly. Cecilia remained serious. When the laughter ended, she placed a blank sheet before the princess.

There was enough space to explain what Isabella had received, what instructions she had given, and which matter she would request be reviewed. It could not replace the supervisor's investigation of the original office's responsibility. Neither did it require an ending guaranteed to satisfy everyone.

Isabella took up her pen. At I assumed referral meant closure, she stopped and struck out assumed.

She had not seriously asked whether they meant the same thing.

That had been no one else's order.

When she finished, she asked that the statement go to the responsible office and readable copies be provided to the affected families. Cecilia checked its destination rather than allowing it to disappear among ordinary requests for help.

The discussion stopped revolving around Isabella.

All six households had records at different desks. Leon's request covered only two cases. Cecilia's office could help each family verify contact arrangements, not decide for them whether their names should appear in a public search notice. Nor could it declare all eight people murdered.

Isabella wanted her explanation released alongside the list.

“At least people will know we aren't hiding it.”

“Clara only agreed to let us examine the slip today,” Adrian reminded her. “She didn't agree to prove anything for you.”

Isabella closed her eyes and withdrew the publication time she had already arranged.

“Ask them first.”

Cecilia had the usual reception staff contact the families whose capital addresses were already on file, arranging meetings at convenient times. The original office must verify petition numbers and current status. Isabella could request and pursue those checks, not complete every identity verification herself that moment.

One household had already refused to publish its address. Another did not want even a name discussed among neighbors again. Their wishes were retained as given, not labeled uncooperative.

Before noon, Isabella prepared to leave. People still filled the front benches. She stopped at the door and asked Cecilia for a blank search application, so her office would not always see only an abbreviated half-line.

Cecilia gave her one and warned that a form was no promise of being heard.

“Someone has to read it.”

“Including me,” Isabella said.

Only outside did she turn to Adrian again.

“I had meant to ask you to lunch.”

“Had meant?”

“Now it sounds as though I'm waiting for you to grade my performance today.”

He considered. “You can ask. I've already promised to spend lunch with Elena today.”

Disappointment checked her expression. She contained it within courtesy rather than turning it into another assignment.

“Then I'll ask another time.”

“All right. Ask, not reserve.”

“You even annotate another time.”

“A newly acquired professional habit.”

She shook her head at him and went to her waiting carriage alone.

Adrian did not immediately follow. Cecilia was putting away Clara's copy. He helped return the cups to their tray.

“You were stern,” he said.

“I was speaking to my former self too.”

She looked up, her eyes quiet.

“An old betrothal once made me think I could answer for you before anyone else. Now I'd rather ask.”

“What would you like to ask?”

“If you go to see the preparations for the public search tomorrow, may I walk with you? Not as your witness. Simply because I'm going too.”

“Yes.”

She smiled, then pointed at the tray he held too high.

“But the cups needn't go upstairs. There's no kitchen there.”

He looked down and laughed, turning in the right direction.

Someone called Cecilia from the front room. She answered and first placed the copy in its numbered compartment. Adrian watched her leave. He did not close an imaginary completed-case file on a name that still had no answer.
