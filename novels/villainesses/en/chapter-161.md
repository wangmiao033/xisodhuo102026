# Chapter 161: She Won't Sign for the Past

Cecilia did not tear up the form.

On the afternoon of January 28, she covered its signature boxes with clean paper, leaving only the requested acknowledgment visible. Adrian watched her read it three times.

“Will different paper improve the wording?”

“I’m afraid I’ll see the spaces first and start imagining them filled.”

She spoke softly, her ears pink.

The registration liaison counter had suggested a possible classification. It promised neither recognition of marriage upon signing nor a compulsory answer that day. But continuing relationship on the attachment reached much further than the cover letter’s convenient verification.

Cecilia uncovered the boxes. Their printed names stood close together, without anyone first having to ask permission.

She had imagined their names together. That wish wasn’t ugly. What hurt was the shortcut borrowing it.

“Would you like me to come?” Adrian asked.

“Yes. Don’t answer for me.”

“I’m named too. I can ask about my part.”

She packed the letter and her questions, not a denial already written for him. Refusing representation by the past did not entitle her to decide all his words now.

At the counter, they waited behind an elderly couple arguing over spelling. The husband maintained that his old document’s missing letter didn’t make him someone else. His wife said it meant explaining years of receipts again.

Cecilia glanced at Adrian.

“They fixed your name yesterday.”

“I hope the captain hasn’t the same problem.”

“He probably can’t find his ticket at all.”

She smiled and waited until the couple had finished before presenting her letter.

The clerk checked its reference. This was a classification inquiry, not a ruling or a secret route already approved by a superior. Existing-relationship examination could locate corresponding material faster. Applicability still required review.

“Does existing mean historically recorded, or continuing now?” Cecilia asked.

He initially said they were related. She let him read until he reached confirmation of a relationship not terminated, then indicated the line.

“Please read that sentence.”

He did, frowning. The form had been designed for ordinary continuing relationships. It might not fit a case involving only historical registration. Matching names did not make it applicable.

“So signing means more than saying an old page contains our names.”

“It confirms the continuing status you are declaring.”

Adrian looked at his box. Narrower than yesterday’s partner field, it demanded more.

He acknowledged no current valid betrothal and had authorized no such application. The clerk recorded that. Did Cecilia wish to retain a classification request in her own name, or first check the historical text?

She chose the text.

Deciding to refuse was no reason to stop caring what the old record actually said. It still concerned their repeated appearance in the same risk index. It wasn’t wastepaper to be ashamed of.

At the designated reading table, a custodian produced the permitted certified copy and the previously verified status statement. The original remained with its custodian. Today’s letter hadn’t placed it in her hands.

Cecilia checked quotation boundaries and page numbers. Her extract omitted a note; the custodian had her copy it onto plain paper, not annotate the certified page. Adrian held down the blank sheet’s curling corner until she finished.

R-074 had a verified lapse even under its original system. Historical survival and present validity were separate. Continuing on a new form did not erase the old status statement or settle every remaining question of signatory identity and interpretation across periods.

“The record exists,” Cecilia said slowly. “That doesn’t mean everyone named in it owes the same yes today.”

The custodian confirmed only the documentary status within his remit, not whom she ought to love.

A young reader at the next table recognized her and asked hesitantly whether the newspaper was true.

Cecilia closed her notebook without hiding the historical copy.

“The part saying I’m married to him now is false.”

“So the old betrothal is false too?”

“I haven’t said that. It’s historical material we must keep examining. It cannot prove I have a present claim on him.”

The reader glanced at Adrian, apparently finding this answer less convenient than true or false. Reminded to keep quiet, he looked down again.

Cecilia remembered first receiving the copy. She had hoped sufficiently old words could give her inexplicable dreams a stable answer. The paper remained; there were more questions now. She would neither mock the woman who had wanted to believe nor require Adrian to repay something on that woman’s behalf.

Her pencil rolled toward the edge. Adrian stopped it without picking it up to supply her conclusion. She put it away and thanked him. The custodian did not record that quiet thanks, but afterward she could read the next line again.

She studied the page a long time. She wasn’t accustomed to every disappointment. She simply knew another person couldn’t be required to bear them before she would let go.

Before leaving, she confirmed the scope and cited pages of today’s examination. She requested no destruction of the archive and did not equate rejecting a current classification with declaring the past nonexistent. Other inquiries continued through their existing procedures. No single signature could furnish every answer.

The liaison clerk still had her form.

“You may defer your decision. If applicability is confirmed, this category could secure earlier examination.”

Cecilia glanced at the waiting people. She knew what waiting meant. The restriction’s original deadline was tomorrow noon; ordinary review might still have no findings then.

Valuing freedom had not made her stop wanting to escape these papers sooner.

“I won’t apply as a continuing betrothal.”

“Then we can’t hold a priority place under that classification.”

“I understand.”

She requested the reason be recorded: the material supported a historical record, not a current betrothal or present consent. She withdrew neither her existing submission nor her request for specific risk explanations. Adrian separately confirmed his own boundaries. The statements formed no new contract.

The clerk marked the form not applied for, retaining the letter and corrective explanation. They did not sign its original confirmation boxes. Cecilia received a filing receipt, not a seal rewarding the right choice.

Outside, her folder-holding hand felt cold.

Adrian offered a rest. She wanted the nearby chapel, open that afternoon, where sitting required no purchase. He agreed without asking whether she still believed in the emblem at her chest.

Two women folded old altar linen inside. An attendant cleaned a lamp. Cecilia set the folder on her lap and watched a line of light through the window. Adrian sat beside her, leaving space for her coat.

“I did imagine it,” she said. “Signing first. Having a place I wouldn’t have to compete for.”

He didn’t hurry to assure her she had never done wrong.

“That would make answering what you really wanted to ask very difficult.”

“I know.”

She touched her emblem without removing it. Her faith had taught her to offer support freely; she still wanted to believe that. She rejected a past document taking the place of the willingness of the person beside her.

One woman tugged a tangled thread tighter, complaining about her eyes. Cecilia offered help. Setting her folder aside, she used an ordinary needle to loosen the knot. Adrian held the far end of the cloth so its weight wouldn’t tighten the thread again.

Recognizing her, the woman asked whether she was in trouble, then immediately said she needn’t answer. She had only noticed how long Cecilia sat there. A little trouble, Cecilia admitted, though not the kind that needed solving now. The woman took back the freed cloth and said this bench always caught the afternoon sun.

Those minutes proved no superior purity of faith and took none of gossip’s sting away. Cecilia had merely finished a small, possible task. Sitting down again, she found her fingers less cold.

The attendant offered another lamp. She said there was enough light. He went to the next row without awaiting a sermon.

Cecilia smiled faintly.

“Now there’s something else I want to say that doesn’t belong at a counter.”

Adrian turned toward her.

“I still want you to like me. Refusing that form doesn’t mean I’ve decided to become nothing but a correct witness.”

“I understand.”

“You don’t have to give me another place now.”

He nodded. After a while, he asked whether she would like to check yesterday’s correction together. Not to inspect her fulfillment of a duty. He wanted to see whether the paper they had struggled to position was still there.

She asked whether he, too, regretted losing the faster opportunity. He did. He had regretted it yesterday already. He wouldn’t pretend waiting had suddenly become pleasant just to make her feel better today.

Oddly, that relieved her. If every right decision had to come wrapped in happiness, she would have needed to conceal even her disappointment. His answer left room for the less admirable-looking part of her afternoon, without changing what she had chosen.

“Then we can dislike queues together,” she said.

“No signatures required for that.”

She agreed, but sat another moment before rising. Neither hurried the quiet into its next useful purpose.

The correction remained in the printer’s window. An almanac advertisement beside it obscured nothing. Cecilia read it before handing the counter a short statement for publication in its exact terms: she claimed no present relationship rights from the historical betrothal and had not sought recognition of current consent through a continuing-betrothal application.

She attached none of Adrian’s private words. She did not declare an end to her affection. The counter accepted it within the permitted scope, with no guarantee that every person in the street would read it.

At the hat shop, the old broadsheet had moved to a corner of the window, but remained. Cecilia looked without quickening her steps.

“Go in?” Adrian asked.

“If they haven’t corrected it tomorrow, I’ll ask again. Today, I’m going home.”

She sounded tired, with a little lightness at choosing when to stop.

On the way, she asked whether he had found her difficult that day. After consideration, he said the elderly man in the queue had been harder to persuade.

“That isn’t very high praise.”

He admitted it, then said he had wanted to spend the afternoon beside her, not because she offered the easiest answers.

She held her folder closer. This time it didn’t hide her smile.

In the entrance porch, Viola was checking evening access arrangements with an on-duty guard. Someone had transformed her explanation of protecting companions into a lifelong oath to Adrian. People waited outside for her confirmation.

Viola glanced at her supported left arm, as if wondering how a hand forbidden to carry a basket had acquired responsibility for someone’s entire life.

Cecilia couldn’t quite keep from laughing. Viola blushed.

“That wasn’t what I meant.”

“I know. You may have to say so yourself.”

Adrian hung up his coat and looked outside. The last daylight touched the steps. They needed no additional betrothal there. They needed someone to clear the blocked path.
