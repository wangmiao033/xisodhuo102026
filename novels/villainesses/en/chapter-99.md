# Chapter 99: The Plates Don't Need to Marry

On December 13, Luna placed two small wooden boxes on the breakfast table.

Elena moved her jam dish out of the way.

“If they're joining us for breakfast, they'll have to pay separately.”

“They need better names.” Luna lifted the lids to reveal two thin ceramic plates. “A and B have no romance.”

“Unwashed Dish One and Unwashed Dish Two,” Adrian suggested.

She looked at him. “You always manage to renew my appreciation for technical numbering.”

She left the plates in their boxes, and nobody reached to touch them. The labels on the lids read Newly Made and No Personal Binding, with the material batches listed beside them. Plate A, P-1, and Plate B, P-2, consisted of fresh ceramic and an artificial gray-silver coating. Their etched lines followed the drawing of a historical betrothal carrier in a public restoration manual. The design contained no individual's name, signature, or fate record.

After reading the labels, Cecilia asked why the plates had come to breakfast.

“I wanted to catch my experimental partner before he finished eating.”

“Friend,” Cecilia corrected earnestly. “Over breakfast, he can simply be a friend.”

Adrian raised his cup. “And this friend would like to eat the last slice of toast.”

Luna closed the boxes and spread her hands, conceding priority to the toast. Elena pushed the butter toward him, then offered him a small piece of particularly hard crust from her own plate.

“You said yesterday you liked it crisp.”

“This has developed the ability to defend territory.”

“You can decline.”

He took a bite and nodded thoughtfully. “Suitable for the eastern gate.”

She laughed and shared the softer half with him. Across the table, Luna watched an exchange that required no etched lines to complete. Her two new specimens suddenly seemed rather dull.

The west workshop was cold that morning. The administrator had checked the delivery route, and the sand beyond the door was still dry. Viola came for a short visit, explaining that her left shoulder felt looser than yesterday, but that she would leave before lunch to post the confirmation for her medical review. She would not attend the afternoon operations. Luna removed her name from that session's attendance box without asking her to manage another half day.

The sign on Bench Three read W-3. The ceramic plates sat on separate stone mounts, two handbreadths apart. No concealed wire ran beneath them. The only link between their holders was a narrow ceramic bridge coated with paired conducting lines.

“Let's start with this morning's question,” Adrian said from behind the protective screen. “What are we looking for?”

“Whether this particular structure can carry a measurable load from one side to the other.”

Luna set a removable warm reference block beside the apparatus. It had been heated slowly in a constant-temperature water jacket, using an ordinary, verifiable heat source. The lines on the bridge would receive only a capped excitation from the calibration lamp, which consumed the energy stored in its small cartridge. Temperatures would remain well below anything that could damage the ceramic. Nobody's hand would be used to find out what happened.

She pointed to the older receiver gauge, R-4, on the right, then produced a sealed liquid thermometer.

“One reads the instrument's response. The other independently measures Plate B's temperature. First we'll check both at low and high points with a steady reference block. We wait until the readings stop changing before writing them down.”

Tessa delivered the repaired fine spring and signed only the component handover sheet. Luna thanked her without adding a request for one free finger observation. The woman asked whether anything else would need repairing after lunch. Told there would not, she left to follow her own plans.

Before bringing in the heat source, Luna fitted the correct bridge and switched on only the calibration lamp. They recorded the background glow and temperature produced by the excitation itself. Once stable, both sides remained close to room temperature; the slight change could not account for the temperature rise they would be looking for. She covered the lamp, removed the bridge, and waited for the apparatus to return to its starting state before admitting the reference block. Otherwise, warmth generated by the bridge's own energy use could be mistaken for a load arriving from the other end.

For the first check, the bridge slot remained empty. The reference block was slowly brought against the back of Plate A. Its reading rose, while Plate B stayed close to room temperature. An insulating screen prevented the source from radiating directly onto the right-hand plate. A blank plate at the same distance provided a control.

They waited for steady readings, removed the heat source, and covered the lamp. No light crossed the space between the mounts.

For the second check, they installed a bridge with its pattern facing the wrong way. The result was much the same. Adrian saw Luna's mouth flatten slightly. She plainly wanted those fine lines to prove themselves useful.

“What if nothing happens the third time either?” he asked.

“There'll be less to write today.”

“An earlier lunch?”

“You make the worst outcome sound remarkably tempting.”

While they waited for everything to cool, Luna handed Cecilia the public manual and pointed to the explanation beside the drawing. The page described the carrier structure of one kind of old registration case. Further on, it listed a variant used for guarantees on goods. Betrothals were one kind of document the design had served. The material did not understand love.

“So something similar could be fitted to a freight agreement?” Elena asked.

“The local structure might be the same. Its use, terms, and actual connections are separate questions,” Luna said. “Paired doesn't mean either person has said yes.”

Adrian noticed an ink streak beside her nose. He pointed to his own face, but she misread the gesture and lifted her chin.

“Finally realized the observer is worth observing too?”

“I've realized the observer's ink has gone astray.”

She touched her face and added half another streak. Adrian offered a clean handkerchief while Cecilia directed her to the correct spot.

“Left. A little lower.”

Once her face was clean, Luna folded the handkerchief and asked whether she could return it after washing it.

“Of course,” he said. “It doesn't have a paper due.”

She bent her head and laughed for a while, her shoulders loosening. They stood around an unlit experimental bench and listened to a delivery cart pass along the new route outside. Waiting had not stolen a stretch of time they would later have to recover from someone's body.

Only after the equipment had cooled did Luna fit the bridge whose pattern matched the etched lines on the two plates. The administrator checked the labels again. Cecilia read out the position of the blank control. Elena stood on the other side and verified that the reference block was the same one, no hotter merely because the researcher hoped for a better result.

When the calibration lamp came on, the gray-silver lines on the bridge glimmered a faint blue. It hardly looked like the legendary light that could bind two people's fates together.

The first visible change appeared in the liquid thermometer beside Plate B.

The little red column crept upward, leaving the fine line that marked room temperature. The receiver needle gradually followed. Under the same heat source, Plate A's stable temperature rise was slightly smaller than it had been with the bridge absent. Plate B was slightly warmer than the blank control.

Adrian did not reach for it. He heard no voice from an old memory.

He saw two ceramic plates and a reading that someone else would need to be able to understand.

“The right one's getting warmer,” he said.

“We still need to rule out heat leaking across from this side,” Luna replied.

She was clearly pleased, but had the administrator record the screen's distance. After the whole assembly cooled, they exchanged the plates together with their mounts from left to right. The reference block stayed against Plate A and the thermometer followed Plate B. Keeping the same spacing and supply limits, they performed another steady-state check. The change followed the paired carrier plates. The blank, made from the same material but without matching conducting lines, did not show the same temperature rise.

Finally, they withdrew the ceramic bridge while the readings were steady. Plate B stopped gaining temperature. Its remaining heat dispersed slowly where it was. Breaking the path did not make that heat miraculously disappear.

Luna watched the red column's slow descent for quite some time.

“It isn't removing it,” she said softly.

“Then what's it doing?” Viola asked.

“This carrier structure takes part of the thermal load to the other end. That end actually has to receive it.”

She wrote the conclusion down, then added its limits: these materials, this pairing, low-intensity steady conditions. They still needed to correct for differences in heat loss before estimating a precise proportion. Subtracting one needle's reading from another would not establish that they had accounted for everything.

Cecilia looked at the title on the public manual.

“So historical betrothals might contain a structure that transmits loads this way.”

“The carrier design in this particular book does,” Luna said. “That doesn't mean every betrothal does. And it certainly doesn't mean signing a name on paper can send one person's calamity to someone else.”

“But what if it originally carried something other than this little bit of heat?”

Luna did not answer too quickly.

“Then that's another thing we would have to prove.”

Adrian watched Plate B, still warm. Once, in a story, he would have accepted shared burdens as a lovely line of dialogue, as though somebody volunteering to stand beside him would automatically divide the pain in half. The temperature marker at the other end told him that stepping closer did not make anything vanish.

But neither did it say that only one person must always stand there.

As soon as that thought formed, he separated it from what they had measured. A hope could point the way. It could not pass itself off as a reading.

Viola's chosen leaving time arrived. She checked that the apparatus was off, handed responsibility for the outside doorway back to the administrator, and took her envelope toward the front courtyard.

Adrian saw her to the door. She raised the envelope as though worried he might think she was slipping away.

“I'm doing another important thing.”

“I know. Remember to keep the biscuits in a different pocket.”

She laughed and actually checked.

As they finished the morning's work, Luna wanted to read the qualified conclusion aloud for a third time. Her stomach made a noise first. The workshop fell silent for an instant. Cecilia then suggested, with perfect seriousness, accepting this reproducible signal as grounds for starting lunch.

“You're cruel,” Luna said.

“We have independent witnesses,” Adrian replied.

They labeled the source box Cooling and secured the cartridge in its isolation case. That morning's result had not altered the receiver plates sealed after the earlier scar observation, much less extended permission for the examination that had already ended. Luna took only the drawings and readings from the new ceramic pieces. The administrator would secure all the equipment in the proper order.

On the way to the dining room, Elena caught Adrian's sleeve.

“What were you thinking when you looked at Plate B?”

He did not tell her it was nothing.

“That if we ever find a way to share a burden, we have to know what someone would actually be receiving.”

“And?”

“That I don't like you being hurt. And I don't want that sentence to make your decisions for you.”

Elena studied him. Her fingers moved slowly from his sleeve into his palm.

“Then start by learning to ask the whole question,” she said. “For now, I'd just like you to have a meal with me without calculating temperature differences.”

“What if your soup's too hot?”

“I'll blow on it myself. You can tell me how clever I am today.”

“You're very clever today.”

“Too quick.”

“I don't need to repeat the control.”

She turned her face aside to laugh and led him down the corridor. That entirely ordinary warmth appeared nowhere in Luna's records.

Toward the end of lunch, the witch finally resisted spreading out her readings for a third time and took up a blank proposal instead. She had eaten half a slice of bread. The other half still lay under her drawing, apparently forgotten.

“This morning we applied the load slowly and took it away slowly,” she said. “What if the real load arrives all at once? Can these instruments tell us what's happening quickly enough?”

She proposed their first controlled pulse trial for that afternoon. They would still use only Plates A and B, with no person or historical betrothal record connected. A limited-charge cartridge would supply a small pulse lasting no more than six seconds. The ceramic bridge could be withdrawn at any time. The spring shutter and dry heat-sink channel would stand ready independently.

Adrian asked her to mark who controlled each stop on the plan. He chose the independent release behind the protective screen. Cecilia would watch the liquid column, Luna would read R-4, and the administrator would handle the supply and workshop exit. Elena would record both readings and the time. Anyone could call a stop without first convincing the person holding the pen.

“If the readings differ by more than one small division, we stop immediately,” Luna said. “If they haven't settled to baseline beforehand, we don't begin.”

“Unexpected heat or smells mean stop too,” Elena added.

“After stopping, leave everything in position and let it cool before investigating,” Adrian said. “We don't switch it back on to finish a line of numbers.”

Luna touched her pen lightly to her lips and looked at him.

“Do you know this is the least romantic afternoon invitation I've ever heard?”

“You can always ask me for tea separately.”

Her pen stopped. She turned the plan toward the others, but the tips of her ears had gone quietly pink.

“Let's do this properly first,” she said.

Outside, the afternoon bell had not yet rung. On Bench Three, the two ceramic plates were slowly cooling.

The first pulse had not begun.
