# Chapter 189: You Didn't Cancel It for Me

Adrian knocked before dinner.

“I can talk now.”

Elena rose without approaching. The little table was cleared, both cups empty. He didn't want tea. She didn't order any or bring out a softened honey biscuit, as though sharing food could make them swallow the afternoon's silence too.

“Let me finish first.”

She nodded.

“You didn't intercept my letter or cancel my appointment. I know. That's not what I'm saying.”

Rehearsal hadn't kept his voice gentle. His independent notice had arrived properly. But her earlier shareable information offered an additional window for questions. She'd promised to tell him, then postponed his distress until his choice was gone.

“I might have said I didn't want to read it that evening. Now I can't even know that.”

She studied the cup. “You were sitting on your bed, looking so unwell.”

“And?”

“I feared knocking again would make your own room feel like nowhere you could hide.”

You don't understand almost escaped him. But he'd wanted quiet. She'd seen genuine exhaustion. A mistake didn't require an entirely false motive to count.

“You could have asked whether I wanted rest.”

“I know.”

“Didn't you know yesterday?”

She looked up, pale. “Yesterday too.”

That angered him more, but left no argument with somebody ignorant of the rule. He stood beside the chair before sitting. She didn't reach for him because he had.

“I wanted a better moment. Later I feared admitting I'd already waited would make you think I was still arranging things for you. So I kept waiting.”

Protection wasn't the whole story. Fear of admitting the first mistake had joined it. She spoke quietly, more completely than merely saying she'd worried.

Adrian watched a repaired crack in the tabletop. Too many repairs today: even this made him wonder what was original, what added later to improve appearances.

“I don't want to check arrival times whenever you bring me news.”

She nodded.

“But now I will.”

Her fingers tightened, then loosened. “Then check. I can't demand that you immediately stop.”

“I'm not looking for a new occupation.”

After silence, she proposed a visible arrival slip for responses she'd promised to share. He would decide when to read their contents. Not all her private correspondence, not supervision of her life: information already agreed to be shared. More than a promise to remember.

“You can reject the arrangement.”

“Let's try. Don't make Martha responsible for our arguments.”

“I'll deliver it myself.”

He agreed without offering forgiveness. An untested procedure couldn't purchase restored trust in advance.

Elena fetched the unused supplementary-question sheet. She didn't sign for him. She drafted her own acknowledgment of delay, asking whether today's questions could enter the next search. The repository would decide acceptance and timing. She couldn't write the problem repaired by wishing it so.

“Don't say I was ill.”

She crossed out her first line and rewrote: I failed to communicate shareable information promptly.

She didn't simply sign the crossed-out sheet and send its uncertainty to the repository. She took a fresh page, keeping the working draft beside it, and copied only what she could properly say. Adrian watched the pen stop before his name. She wasn't asking the official to treat his reaction as the cause of her delay, or to correct the record by making him responsible for it.

The error remained on the draft, not the formal letter. Watching her change sheets, he no longer needed to argue whose name the responsibility belonged under.

His chest didn't ease.

“I want to walk alone tonight.”

“A carriage?”

“No.”

“Should dinner be kept?”

“Yes. Don't wait to eat.”

At the door he heard his name and turned, braced for don't leave like this.

“I'm very upset,” she said. “Not to make you stay.”

He nodded. The words didn't detain him or require pretending that leaving cost nothing. He heard that she was hurt and left anyway, as they had agreed he could. It wasn't punishment arranged to make her chase him. Nor did his need for a walk mean that the person behind him had stopped mattering. He carried both facts down the stairs.

Downstairs, Martha was closing the correspondence book. Had the delivery stub found its place?

“Yes. Thank you.”

She didn't ask about their conversation. He paused, realizing his use of her information might have made her fear she'd caused their quarrel.

“You didn't say anything wrong.”

“I told the relevant person what had arrived.”

“Yes.”

The ordinary sentence tightened his throat. Coat over his arm, he listened without using thanks to end her opportunity to speak. He didn't want her as a witness on his side, or hesitating next time over whether domestic peace required silence. They would handle their communication themselves, he added. She needn't choose sides.

Her shoulders relaxed. Where was the knife-repair ticket? The kitchen still needed its small knife; two unhappy people shouldn't mean cutting everything with a blunt blade tomorrow. He found the ticket and agreed to collect it the day after tomorrow. If plans changed, he'd say so, rather than leave a promise for somebody else to interpret.

He almost returned upstairs to tell Elena, proving he'd begun doing better. Instead he put on his coat. Not every decent small act needed immediate presentation for her judgment.

The porter asked his destination. Nearby, home for dinner, no escort. Ordinary departure records had a purpose. He gave a general direction without making refusal of surveillance mean nobody could know he'd gone.

Across the road, someone crouched with a loose lead, persuading a reluctant dog home. Adrian looked away before making even that ordinary scene evidence in his recent argument.

Lamps were newly lit; shops closed half their shutters. February 16's wind carried coal smoke and beans cooking. He sought neither a tavern nor somebody who would guarantee he was completely right. At the temple's public side gallery he merely wanted a bench.

Cecilia was putting borrowed hymnbooks away.

“What brings you here?”

“Sitting.”

“We still allow that.”

She indicated a bench without declaring he'd come to confess or guessing his quarrel. One loose-stitched book went aside so tomorrow's reader wouldn't shed pages halfway through.

“Can I help?”

“Pass that ribbon. You aren't earning the bench.”

It proved too short. She found another and laughed. Daytime solemnity hadn't improved her knots.

Work finished, she asked to sit at the other end. He agreed. Eventually he described the delayed information and his anger without reciting Elena's most private words as testimony for a friend's verdict.

Cecilia didn't appeal to good intentions.

“You can remain angry. Tomorrow you can be differently angry. You needn't select one eternally correct response tonight.”

“I'm afraid I'm handing her the old file's anger too.”

“Then distinguish what you're discussing now. Distinguishing it doesn't erase today's mistake.”

In the worship room, two voices missed their harmony and began again. Did faith make evenings like this easier to navigate?

She considered it.

“Sometimes it reminds me I needn't make the worst choice first.”

“Only that?”

“Today that's quite a lot.”

As a child, she'd thought truly faithful people didn't fear. Wearing white, she'd found people increasingly expected her to be fearless on their behalf. She'd sometimes complied with excessive certainty, believing a steady voice could hold everyone up.

“And now?”

“I'm afraid part of those records is true. Afraid somebody will use a part to decide everything. Afraid I'll close my eyes because I don't want to admit an institution I trusted did wrong.”

For the first time she said it this directly to him, without hiding personal fear inside concern for others.

He didn't answer that a saint would never do that. His comfort shouldn't replace the burden she'd just set down.

He watched her face, aware that she had answered him as a person rather than performing certainty for the man on the bench. Telling her she couldn't fail would have sounded generous. It would also have denied the very possibility she'd trusted him enough to name. He had disliked being asked to believe reassuring words unsupported by evidence. She deserved better than receiving those same words from him.

“I can't guarantee otherwise for you.”

“I know. That's better than telling me I'll certainly manage.”

Her tired smile didn't demand an embrace in payment for honesty. She still liked him, still welcomed his choosing the bench beside her. Neither gave her ownership of every unhappy evening he might have.

For a while they listened rather than continue the conversation. Adrian could hear the point at which the singers had been missing one another, and the small adjustment that let the two voices meet. Cecilia didn't use the sound as proof that all difficult things eventually resolved. She waited until the phrase ended before returning to what he'd asked about faith.

The singers finally found their harmony. What Cecilia still valued in faith, she said, was treating a frightened person as a person—not requiring proof of harmlessness before offering water and a chair.

“Not declaring you innocent,” she added.

“I understand.”

He touched the folded note in his coat: wet ash, brass handle, the unseen voice. He'd avoided linking the fragments lest they become an entire story. Now he understood that refusing an entire story needn't mean pushing away the sentence he'd heard.

“If I admit one mistake, will people use it to prove I did everything?”

“They might.”

His chest sank. Cecilia didn't append a guarantee.

“Then I still have to explain what I actually know.”

She nodded. “Let others be responsible for cutting your words apart. You needn't anticipate them by cutting away your whole voice.”

Before he left, she offered leftover bread. Dinner waited at home, he said. She kept it for breakfast. Accepting food wasn't proof her comfort had worked.

He walked home more slowly, avoiding a puddle and scraping mud from his soles. Still angry, he could also recognize hunger.

Elena wasn't at the dining table awaiting his verdict. Martha reheated a meal. Elena had eaten and left a note: the letter had been delivered, and goodnight, with no response required tonight.

He didn't go upstairs to embrace her and rename the quarrel a misunderstanding. On another slip he wrote that he was home, dinner was good, and they would talk tomorrow.

Then he unfolded his brass-handle notes. The first line was no longer perhaps none of it is true.

He wrote: I heard the warning and still said, Wait another moment.

The space below the sentence was harder to leave than it looked. He wanted an explanation ready beside it, something that would make the act understandable before anybody else could react. He also wanted to cross out the entire line and postpone the question again. For once he did neither. The paper could retain what he had written without requiring the rest of the past to arrive tonight.

He left the next line empty. Tomorrow he would write only what he could genuinely take responsibility for.
