# Chapter 181: He Wouldn't Agree

“I don't believe it.”

Adrian said it three times. The third was at the lunch table. The soup had cooled. Martha waited near the door without offering to heat it yet again.

Elena didn't put the spoon into his hand.

“Which part?”

He looked up sharply. “You want to divide it into parts?”

Her face paled. His voice was too loud. Outside, someone stacked firewood. The house hadn't stopped needing wood because a record said world termination. That made him absurdly angry, as though ordinary life meant nobody had understood.

“I don't believe I did that.”

“I haven't asked you to admit it.”

“But you haven't said it's false.”

If she called it entirely false, he'd feel better. He knew that, which made him want to force the words from her.

“I hope it's false,” Elena said. “I can't invent evidence we don't have.”

He pushed back his chair. Its scrape announced an ending, but he couldn't immediately leave. What if his place at the table no longer existed when he returned?

Elena half rose, then sat.

“You can go out. I won't have somebody follow to demonstrate concern.”

“I'm not running away.”

“I didn't say you were.”

He fastened his coat incorrectly, undoing the second button outside. The cold of February 9 struck his face. Initially he walked without knowing where he meant to go.

At the corner, the registration agent's shop was open. The man trimmed wrapping paper for a customer; his receipt lay nearby. He'd defended preservation while resisting the retention of his own copies. Adrian had admired his ability to hold two judgments at once.

Now any sentence not entirely on Adrian's side sounded like the enemy's. He stood outside the window, watching the man work, and remembered how easy it had been to appreciate that distinction when the frightening sentence didn’t contain his own name.

He didn't enter to solicit comfort. At the secondhand bookshop he bought a sheet of ordinary writing paper and used a corner table with permission. I wish to withdraw, he wrote.

Withdraw what?

The preservation request? Permission for further reading? His name from the excerpt? His hand stopped. His own paper suddenly seemed harder to understand than the old plate.

The bookseller offered ink. Adrian had some. The man went to another customer without peering at the page because Adrian looked distressed.

That ordinary courtesy finally lowered his shoulders.

He crossed out the words without tearing the sheet and wrote: Request verification of the material underlying the attribution.

His pen stopped again. Something worse waited behind it. What if verification didn't establish a falsehood?

He folded the paper and headed home, buying two warm rolls on the way. He miscounted the payment. The baker returned the extra coin. He almost laughed, couldn't, and simply thanked her.

Inside the gate, Viola occupied her usual resting bench, left arm supported. Was the bread intended to prove he'd eaten?

“Martha will demand further evidence.”

“She is exacting.”

He took another chair. Viola didn't need new medical permission to become endlessly available. Her rest would end on time; she could listen during these remaining minutes.

“A correct order once put you in a situation where every choice seemed wrong,” Adrian said. “How do you know your explanation afterward isn't an excuse?”

She considered it.

“I can't know just by liking myself.”

She didn't equate his record with her recall order. Hers had been genuine. She'd received it, answered, sent four riders back, and remained with the last wounded wagon under circumstances she actually knew. Those facts could be checked without first deciding she was good.

“My name is on the line.”

“A name begins the question. You have no corresponding memory. You can't borrow mine.”

She glanced at his folded paper. What had he written?

He chose to show her. She pointed out that the request didn't specify what needed checking. She didn't rewrite it as prove my innocence.

“What if I don't like the result?”

“Then I still want you to let me hear what happened, rather than decide in advance that I couldn't bear it.”

Her caregiver came to remind her of the time. Viola didn't wave her away or promise that no truth could ever alter any relationship. She pushed Adrian's roll back toward him and told him to eat his own share.

He thanked her without asking her to stay longer.

In the study, Luna listened to an assistant reading the morning observations. She hadn't activated equipment or offered her injured hand for a test that would instantly clear him. Would he like to hear his part?

“Do you think I did it?”

She looked up. After a pause: “I don't know yet.”

This time he didn't leave.

“That hurts to hear.”

“I know. Saying I knew you hadn't might make me sound more like the person you want to hear. It wouldn't be what I know.”

He sat with the bread on his lap. For a moment he'd wanted the record to stop existing. Luna didn't rebuke him in the name of research. If the plate accused her of burning everyone's homes, she might not have sat as steadily as she had today.

“Your fear can't complete the plate. Neither can my curiosity.”

She asked the assistant to read the uncertainties: incomplete source, damaged date, unidentified signature. None automatically proved falsity. Then the actual observations: two layers and legible sentences. Distress didn't justify removing those either.

Adrian added three specific requests to his paper: the sources cited by the original account, the complete preceding and following page sequence, and who had made the primary attribution on what grounds. Name, account, and causation couldn't replace one another.

He didn't demand a guarantee that inquiry would leave him faultless.

Luna quietly asked about tonight's travel story. They could cancel without making it another debt.

“I'd like to read. Probably badly today.”

“The captain doesn't always wear his hat properly.”

He finally smiled. She indicated the cup-handle drawing at the edge of the book, not as proof she'd chosen a man who could never do wrong. The comma still looked as if it were escaping. He was willing to open the book tonight.

Downstairs, Isabella was separating tomorrow's appointments onto differently colored paper. Blue ink marked her right fingers; her injured left wrist wasn't helping.

“I might not be suitable for chess tomorrow,” Adrian said.

“Another appointment, or you don't want to see me?”

“Because I might be…”

The words caught. She put down the papers rather than supply a rescuing phrase.

“We can cancel if you'd like. But don't announce on my behalf that reading that line must make me unwilling to sit opposite you.”

“Aren't you afraid?”

“Yes. And angry. I dislike being given such an enormous conclusion without the preceding pages.”

For once, he didn't hear another person's fear as a forecast of departure. Isabella wouldn't use a chess game to demonstrate unconditional trust, she added. New actual danger would require reconsideration. For now she still wanted to play. He could say directly if he didn't.

“I do. I'll tell you in advance if verification conflicts.”

“Good. You still owe me that move.”

“I thought you might move pieces while I was away.”

Her eyebrow rose. “If I wanted to win, why choose something so easily discovered?”

He froze for a moment. She laughed first, calling it a poor joke. The board was unchanged. He laughed too. Tired afterward, he no longer felt laughter itself was evasion.

She resumed her work instead of requiring his company through all of it. At the doorway he watched her wipe blue ink onto scrap cloth, not particularly elegantly, until her fingers were clean. He treasured the smallness of something that would never enter a final account.

In his room, Adrian flattened the purchased paper. The crossed-out withdrawal remained beside the new request. He didn't discard the first sheet to rewrite himself as brave throughout. It belonged in a private folder, not masquerading as an application he'd actually submitted.

Toward evening, the official excerpt arrived. He checked it against what they'd read. Gaps hadn't become fluent sentences. His lack of corresponding memory and refusal to confirm the attribution appeared beside the relevant passage. He read his new request before signing and gave it to the messenger.

Cecilia brought an authorized excerpt from an associated catalog. Did he want it now? She didn't spread it over the dining table and force everyone to learn it together.

Yes. He asked Elena to come, specifying she could refuse.

When she entered, he stood.

“At lunch I wanted you to guarantee an answer you didn't know.”

She held the door handle rather than immediately absolve him.

“You were frightened. You also shouted at me.”

“Yes. I'm sorry.”

She nodded and only later sat. Apology didn't turn the cold lunch into a tender memory. Nor did the hurt erase their existing affection.

Cecilia laid out the catalog. It wasn't the termination plate's complete text. This separately maintained historical cross-index pointed to an earlier judicial questioning record. Adrian Reed appeared again. The requesting party for the referral was Elena Rosenberg.

Elena's hands stopped on her lap.

“It says I delivered you to the Judicial Office.”

“The index says that,” Cecilia replied. “We haven't seen the original. The citation needs verification.”

Adrian's first reaction wasn't accusation but an unfair relief: finally his wasn't the only name there. Recognizing that made him feel sick.

Elena lost color. She didn't explain that another version of her must have been coerced, or that she'd certainly meant to save him. The referral language resembled a family inquiry-request form she knew. A familiar format couldn't authenticate a signature or an event. She was describing the wording she recognized, not claiming she remembered writing it. Neither the fear in her face nor the familiarity of the form could supply the missing original.

“I don't remember this,” she said. “I won't invent a reason to make tonight easier.”

He remembered her saying I hope it's false and finally understood what that answer cost the speaker too.

“I don't want to force you to.”

Furnace smoke thinned into the evening outside. Their names lay together on paper neither liked. Neither folded away the other's half.

Elena pointed to the source reference.

“Tomorrow I'll request the original. Not merely ask to remove my name.”

Adrian nodded. He still didn't believe the attribution. At last he could hold that alongside another statement: he would investigate, including what he didn't want to see.

After dinner he kept his promise and read Luna two pages. He misread something in the first paragraph; she corrected him, he tried again, and nobody used it to discuss his fate. When the captain finally recovered his hat, Adrian paused before turning the page. Tomorrow's original still waited. Tonight's two pages had actually been read.
