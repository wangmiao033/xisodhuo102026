# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Two: The Saint Won't Let Me Get Married
### Chapter 49: The Saint's Death Is Marked on the Calendar

On the morning of October twenty-ninth, Adrian insisted that Cecilia be given a dangerous notice herself.

He had seen something like it the month before. Then, he had hidden a risk record bearing his own name for half a day, until Elena asked him in the conservatory why he thought he could decide what other people had a right to know.

This time he hid nothing.

Hale placed the old risk index on the table and began with its title and source.

“It's called the *Special Contract Risk Observation List*. It is not an execution order. An independent archive reviewer found this entry among the sealed-record inventories submitted by Oversight. October thirty-first is marked as a ‘high-risk threshold date,’ but no specific consequence is given.”

Cecilia saw her name and went still.

Elena sat beside her. She didn't take the paper away.

“You don't have to read it,” Adrian said. “You can if you want. I wanted you to know what we found before anyone started telling you how to react.”

Cecilia drew it toward her. “I want to read it.”

The writing in the signature field wasn't hers. This was a list of subjects under observation, compiled by a records department. It was not a contract in which she had agreed to bear a risk.

“October thirty-first,” she said quietly. “Two days.”

“Yes,” Hale replied. “But we don't know what the risk is. The old system sometimes used ‘threshold date’ for contract reviews, ritual renewals, or the observation of irregularities. We'll check the definition first.”

Adrian remembered a Temple side quest in the game. A woman in holy orders had vanished while praying in the bell tower. The screen had displayed a single line: *Fate's account is settled.*

He'd once assumed the writers had skipped a scene. Now he wasn't even sure whether the woman had been Cecilia.

“I saw something similar in a story,” he said. “But I don't know whether this list concerns the same thing.”

“You want to warn me there might be danger,” she said, “without presenting a guess as something that's happened.”

“Yes.”

She nodded seriously. “Then we'll investigate together.”

Some of the tension left his shoulders.

Hale contacted the archivist responsible for the list's transfer. The man agreed to provide public definitions, but would not disclose other people's names without authorization. They requested only Cecilia's entry and the ceremony catalogue for October thirty-first.

That afternoon, they visited the public scheduling hall on the Temple's outer grounds.

It was nothing like the locked Old Contract Records Archive. Schedules for blessings, relief work, and memorials covered the walls. Elderly visitors checked remembrance dates; families registered prayers for patients. A child was pointing to his mother's name in a book.

Adrian had expected somewhere like this to feel less threatening.

Then he found October thirty-first.

*Calamity Remembrance and Renewal of Blessings. Four p.m. Bell Tower Inner Court.*

The officiant was Cecilia Saintclair.

She stared. “When did I agree to this?”

The receptionist consulted the booking ledger. “The Temple Coordination Office submitted it two weeks ago. A qualified saint usually presides.”

“My investigative access is suspended,” Cecilia said. “That doesn't necessarily prevent me from leading prayers. The problem is that nobody asked whether I wished to.”

“What happens if she doesn't attend?” Adrian asked.

The receptionist indicated a note. “The next duty cleric takes over. No individual is to be compelled to participate.”

Hale requested an extract of the schedule. Nothing on the page could force Cecilia to appear.

But why did her name appear on both the risk list and this event?

To establish what force the old list carried, Hale took them to consult a published commentary on the former law.

Its definition surprised Adrian. A high-risk threshold date had originally reminded archivists to reconfirm a person's willingness to bear a risk. It neither scheduled an execution nor declared a death.

“So it might have been intended to prevent transfers without consent?”

“Yes. It could even refer to a required manual check,” Hale said. “But we'll need the technical regulations from the period to establish how the old mechanism actually operated.”

Cecilia found an annotation in the appendix: *The subject must be notified before the threshold date and permitted to withdraw.*

“Why haven't I received formal notice?”

Hale copied the passage. “That's a procedural question we can raise. Whether notice was delivered, and who acknowledged it, should be traceable.”

Adrian had wanted to erase Cecilia's name from the list. Now he wondered whether protecting her meant restoring the notice that the entry had required someone to give her in the first place.

They continued tracing the event's organizers. The application originated with the Old Contract Records Office's Maintenance and Settlement Coordination Group. Only a departmental code identified the handling officer. The approval field read: *Reserved under customary practice.*

“Could somebody be luring her into the inner court?” Adrian asked.

“Or an administrative office could be reusing an old roster,” Hale replied. “We need to compare last month's officiant and establish whether there is an actual danger.”

Cecilia opened the historical schedule. “My mentor presided last year. She's well, and there wasn't an accident.”

“Then why is there a risk flag now?” Elena asked.

“I don't know.”

Cecilia studied her name, then laid her hand on the page.

“I want to establish whether this ceremony could affect other patients or volunteers. I can't ask them to cancel the entire event just because my name is on it.”

“But you don't know whether you're in danger,” Adrian said.

“Which is why we need to find out.” She looked up. “If I protect myself by letting an uninformed cleric take my place, how is that different from somebody secretly assigning the risk to me?”

Elena nodded slowly. “You can refuse to participate. That doesn't make somebody else an automatic substitute.”

“Exactly.”

Adrian had no answer. He had already been planning to get Cecilia out of the capital for three days and find some excuse to hide her. Now he realized that might simply put somebody else in her place.

If the ceremony's rules were the problem, removing one person wouldn't change them.

He suggested checking its actual steps and equipment.

The receptionist supplied the public program: flowers, remembrance, prayers of blessing, and a maintenance demonstration of an old mark lamp. Such demonstrations ordinarily used blank media with no personal connections.

“A mark lamp?” Adrian asked.

Cecilia's expression changed. “The Old Contract Records Office sometimes displays fate marks at memorials, to show that people touched by calamity are remembered. Regulations prohibit connecting those displays to a living person's individual mark.”

“Then we need to check which medium they're using this time,” Elena said.

Hale requested verification by a qualified independent technician that the equipment involved no personal samples supplied without consent. The Coordination Office did not answer immediately.

As they left the hall, sunlight flashed across Cecilia's investigator's badge.

Two days felt very short to Adrian. Short enough to be frightening. Long enough, perhaps, to do something they could verify.

A novice records clerk from the Coordination Office quietly told them the ceremony had originally been planned with several clerics sharing the officiating duties. After October twentieth, the program had abruptly changed to list only Cecilia.

“Who changed it?” Elena asked.

“I don't know. I only know the revised program's reference number.”

She didn't ask him to guess. Instead, she asked Hale to use that reference to request the public scheduling history.

The response arrived that afternoon. Two programs did exist. The later one had been issued by the Maintenance and Settlement Coordination Group, not by the officiant herself.

Cecilia included this in her objection.

“Even if it's ordinary scheduling, someone should ask whether the person being scheduled agrees. We can request that the ceremony be reorganized, rather than leave one person responsible for it.”

She also proposed that any necessary public remembrance use only blank mark media, unconnected to anyone.

Watching her, Adrian began to see something beyond his fear: an approach they could actually take.

In the carriage back to the estate, Elena held his hand.

“You've been quiet today.”

“I've been wondering whether Cecilia should leave the capital.”

“You can suggest it. You can't force her.”

“I know.”

“You're afraid for her. And you're afraid there's one more person in your memories who's going to die.”

He nodded. “And if I persuade her to run, another cleric might get pushed into her place.”

Elena rested her head lightly against his shoulder. “We can look for a way that doesn't require anyone to take that risk.”

Across from them, Cecilia gazed out of the window. She heard, but didn't interrupt. Only when they passed the tower did she speak.

“If I ever develop particular feelings for you, I'd want those to be my choice too.”

Adrian turned. “What brought that on?”

“I've just discovered that even a ceremony can be assigned to me without anyone asking.” Her smile was a little sad. “I don't want a betrothal or a risk list to write my whole life.”

Elena reached over and took her other hand.

“Then keep writing it yourself.”

Cecilia blinked.

The sight of them holding hands almost made Adrian forget that he was the one caught up in the historical betrothal.

That night, he stood alone in the garden, looking toward the bell tower half a capital away.

When he'd first awakened on September seventeenth, he'd had only one thought: stay out of the story's bad ending.

Now he understood that the greatest danger wasn't always ignorance of the plot. Sometimes it was believing that knowing the ending entitled you to choose for everybody else.

Elena found him with a cloak around her shoulders.

“Planned another escape route?”

“Not this time. I'm trying to think of a way nobody has to hide.”

“That's more like the man I want to court.”

He looked at her. “Doesn't helping Cecilia bother you at all?”

“A little,” she admitted with a smile. “But I don't intend to disguise that feeling as an order.”

Adrian gently kissed the back of her hand. He didn't try to explain it as part of his job.

The tips of Elena's ears reddened.

“You did that of your own accord.”

“Yes.”

“Then I'll remember it.”

She took his hand and led him out of the night wind.

Once they were back inside, Hale received a supplementary record. On the equipment list for October thirty-first, the Maintenance and Settlement Coordination Group had requested a “historical linked-mark transfer ring.” Its listed purpose was a “temporary demonstration of risk balancing.”

That phrase appeared nowhere in the public program.

“A transfer ring?” Cecilia went pale.

“You know what it is?” Adrian asked.

“Only from annotations to ancient rites. In theory, it was once used to transfer risk marks between two registered media. Modern rules prohibit connecting it to a living person without their consent.”

Hale circled the equipment number.

“We still don't know whether it can actually do that. It might just be a historical exhibit. But tomorrow we must request a technical review.”

Adrian looked at the calendar.

October thirty-first.

He could no longer dismiss it as an ordinary day. But he chose not to call it the date of the saint's death.

She was alive. She argued, laughed, and stepped forward when someone needed help.

He would not let a risk list put an end to those things before their time.
