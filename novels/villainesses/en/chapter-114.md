# Chapter 114: The Witch Borrows His Hands

When the thin paper arrived, Luna Ashcroft looked at her right hand.

Securely bandaged, it showed no intention of performing miracles. She reached left-handed for the paper knife. Adrian Reed turned the handle toward her before she touched it.

“What do you need cut?”

“A long slit. A straight one.”

“That sounds like a lack of faith.”

“Quite the opposite. I have to trust you now.”

Only afterward did she notice Elena Rosenberg watching across the table.

“With the paper,” Luna added.

The technical recorder coughed and gave Adrian a cutting board. The tribunal had authorized paper explanations and comparisons with submitted documents. No original contractual artifacts, sealed specimens, or energy-storage plates were in this reading room. Even if the witch claimed a kiss could explain the world, its purpose would first require registration.

Adrian began cutting. Luna laid out copies of the December 17 tracings beside the maintenance sheet Tessa Quinn had just verified. The independent examination report had arrived that morning after leaving the tower the previous day. Within their authorization, the restorers had inspected wreckage in the accident's dissipation box. They hadn't touched C-17 or tried to restore the destroyed D-17.

The report recorded metal connections in the broken control base, with drawings from each side. The main stop lever interrupted the probe-light supply. A bypass remained closed between the side discharge plate and the control base until the sacrificial ceramic neck broke. Why coupling had crossed the isolation before the accident remained under investigation.

Luna read that qualification twice. She didn't cross it out.

“Tessa saw the external connection. The restorer saw inside,” Adrian said.

“Only the agreement between them establishes that I didn't invent this path to explain the accident.”

Luna flattened the paper and clumsily drew two parallel lines left-handed. The first curved too much. Before she could fetch another sheet, Adrian helped reposition it with a ruler.

“May I borrow your hands?” she asked.

“Yes. What exactly for?”

She looked up, smiling openly. “Hold down the bottom sheet. You're warmer than a paperweight.”

“But paperweights don't request breaks.”

“Then you can resign whenever you like.”

Elena set down an actual paperweight. “For when he's resting.”

Luna eyed the brass disk, tempted to comment on its timing. Cecilia Saintclair first removed a little dish of paper scraps, suggesting that the paperweight deserved proper employment too. The recorder bent over his writing, his shoulders twitching.

Luna didn't prolong the joke. She asked Adrian to mark a short stroke on each of two sheets, then covered them with the slit paper.

“This isn't a revealing experiment,” she told the recorder. “The paper explains positions. A similar-looking model cannot prove the original necessarily behaves this way.”

Her left finger shifted the upper layer. One stroke moved through the slit while the other remained, resembling a single line dragged into a double image.

“We called it afterglow. But after D-17 lost illumination, its displacement didn't involve every part fading together. The surviving tracings show the outer edge receding while an inner section continued using the same notch as a reference.”

Adrian remembered the fleeting corridor, then kept that recollection within its limits. Those two breaths couldn't supply the notch with mysterious significance.

“What supports the notch?” the recorder asked.

Luna indicated three independently drawn records and their timed readings. The notch occupied similar positions in tracings made during illumination and afterward. Those few timed readings were all they had. They were not enough to prove that every part of the layered image had remained stable throughout.

“Now S-48.”

Its authorized copy also described two separately fading layers, but from a different old contractual casing. She'd called it a minor coating defect: once the top layer faded, the other seemed misplaced. Her premature conclusion lay beside proof that her later correction had been received. Nobody at the table offered to tear away the embarrassing portion of her younger self's work. The conclusion and the correction both remained there to be examined.

“Two media of different provenance may each have one layer that changes with external conditions and another retaining a reference position.” She realigned the paper. “For now, I'm calling the latter an anchor layer. It could align an old trace during readout. That wouldn't mean the day it was inscribed had happened again.”

“Nor that a promise made that day has just come into force,” Cecilia added.

“Correct. But you must explain the law and ritual texts. I can only say these records provide no evidence of a new inscription.”

The church advocate spoke for the first time. If it was an anchor, what did it anchor: material, memory, or an unfinished vow?

Luna didn't immediately answer. Outside, someone shoveled snow, rested the shovel against the wall, then picked it up again.

“At present, I can only propose that the material retains a reference position. I don't know why it lasts so long, or whether both materials share a manufacturing process. Certainly Mr. Reed's brief vision cannot establish that his body is part of the equipment.”

Adrian released the sheet. The paperweight held it firmly. Nothing terrible escaped when he withdrew his hand.

The advocate looked at his left hand. “Should the scar be included for comparison?”

“Today's authorization covers existing materials,” the recorder replied. “Any application for additional bodily examination requires separate grounds and conditions.”

Adrian rested his hand naturally in his lap. He didn't have to volunteer for an unexplained experiment to prove he wasn't frightened.

“I'll describe what's happened,” he said. “Please record what we don't know as unknown.”

Luna looked at him for a moment, then qualified her explanation: a partial principle for future testing, not proof of reversed time or repeated lives.

The recorder asked her to reset the paper and demonstrate from the other side. The slit caught on a ragged lower edge and refused to move.

Before reaching out, Adrian looked at her. She nodded. He lifted the sheet and trimmed the crooked corner.

“You cut it originally, didn't you?”

“Yes. So I'm repairing it.”

“I was going to criticize your craftsmanship.”

“You can save that for lunch.”

She laughed and passed the model to the recorder. In another pair of hands, the marks still aligned. It didn't prove an anchor layer existed. It let everyone identify where her explanation worked and where it didn't, without first believing she was a genius.

By the midday recess, she was paler. Gathering the paper, Adrian found an unused sketch beneath it: a pointed hat, two legs, and an exceptionally wide smile.

“An anchor layer too?”

“Something to do while I waited for paper.”

“Looks like you.”

Luna took it. Seeing that he meant the smile, she couldn't keep her own mouth from lifting.

“Your taste is dangerous.”

“Should it be sealed?”

“I'll keep it under observation.”

Elena called them to lunch. Luna tucked away the little figure quickly, as though merely filing an irrelevant draft.

Lunch was stew. When the waiter asked who wanted pepper, Adrian began answering, and three voices simultaneously said not too much. A beat late, Luna raised her unoccupied left hand.

“May I cast my vote for myself?”

The waiter struggled to determine which lady supervised which bowl. Finally, he placed the pepper in the middle and retreated.

Viola Blackwood moved her bowl farther away. “We frightened him.”

“I only said Adrian's still recovering,” Elena said.

“So did I,” Cecilia replied.

Luna looked at Viola. After a silence, the knight admitted she'd meant Luna. The doctor hadn't recommended eating herself into a sweat immediately after a dressing change.

“So someone at this table cares about me.” Luna pressed a hand to her chest.

“Yes,” Adrian said. “So slow down. The bowl's hot.”

Her left hand stopped short of its rim. His yes was too ordinary to leave an opening for teasing. She picked up her spoon and blew on the stew.

Halfway through lunch, Elena produced a small paper from her sleeve pocket and asked whether Adrian had lost something. It was the bookshop receipt he'd tucked inside the travelogue the previous night, with a page number scribbled on it.

“You even kept this?”

“It fell outside your door. I couldn't let the proprietor mistake your scribbles for accounts.”

Luna looked up, visibly tempted to apply her paper model to the receipt. Elena immediately pinned it to the table edge.

“There are no old vows on this one.”

“There's last night's promise to read,” Adrian said.

Elena looked at him and relaxed her fingers. She returned the ordinary scrap of paper, her voice softer.

“Then remember today.”

He folded it into his pocket. Cecilia didn't compete for that particular paper. She passed the breadbasket, advising them both to finish eating before the stew cooled.

That afternoon, the tribunal declined the church advocate's proposed wholesale transfer of research materials. Provenance still required investigation. The provisional two-layer explanation established neither an exclusive church right to research nor the academy's possession of safe methods. The relevant laboratories remained sealed. Authorized copies could undergo limited review by multiple parties.

Luna signed her explanation. Her left-handed letters were untidy, but her wording was harder to misunderstand than before.

Before returning the documents, the recorder produced the independent report's final page. Beside the cracked ceramic neck was a string of old specification symbols. The restorer believed they resembled those on an earlier batch of return-flow components and recommended consulting comparable load tables.

“Why does this say load cycles?” Adrian asked.

Luna leaned closer without taking the original. “Because they encountered something that wouldn't leave too.”

“Magic?”

“Possibly. Or a residual load we haven't yet identified.”

She indicated a circled column. It concerned whether discharge was complete between readings, rather than the size of one input. She requested the old tables with their provenance. She wouldn't guess how badly those blurred figures might injure a person.

Back at the guesthouse that evening, Elena opened the travelogue's second volume beside the fire, deliberately leaving a place for Adrian. He sat and asked which passage she wanted.

“The one we didn't read yesterday.”

“I thought you might punish the captain by making him board his ship again on page one.”

“He doesn't answer to me.” She opened the book, looking at Adrian. “Neither do you. But I remember what you promise.”

Smiling, he took the book. First, he drew the curtain against the draft on her colder side. Then he began. The captain finally met the person who'd refused to open his letters, only to discover that not one sentence of the speech he'd rehearsed for three chapters was useful.

At another table, Luna listened while sorting the day's paper left-handed. She did not secretly add another line to improve her explanation, and she did not redraw a hypothetical sketch as though it depicted an already proven truth. The pointed-hat figure went into her notebook with its broad smile showing.

Viola placed the paper box within easy reach and asked what they'd actually found that day.

Luna watched a fragment of ash slowly fall in the hearth.

“A position that makes old traces harder to lose,” she said. “But keeping something doesn't mean there's room to hold it.”
