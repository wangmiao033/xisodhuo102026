# Chapter 163: You Can Stop Here

The first page of Luna's statement contained only two lines: its title and a warning that this was not a recruitment notice.

On the morning of January 29, the student making her fair copy asked whether giving the warning such prominence might suggest her research had stopped.

“Some of it has.” Her right hand rested on padding. “My hand hasn't recovered for the sake of the layout.”

Across the table, Adrian checked the passages he permitted her to quote. Only two invited scholarly visitors and a printer's copyist would attend. There would be no demonstration. Hartwell had agreed to check sources, not guarantee every technical judgment.

Her draft said the participant understood the risks.

Adrian crossed out understood.

“I've heard your explanation. That doesn't mean I understand everything.”

The student's pen stopped above a beautifully copied line.

“Specify what you've heard and what remains unclear,” Luna said.

She did not take his pencil away or laugh that everyone used customary wording. The choice stayed in his hands. Adrian circled two passages he could confirm, then covered the description of his scar. A public discussion didn't require a drawing of his hand.

“This doesn't go to the printer.”

“All right.”

The answer came so promptly that he looked up. Her candor looked faintly uncomfortable.

“I practiced last night. Not adding but afterward.”

When the visitors arrived, the student displayed an old blank application template Luna had authorized for publication. It contained no names or medical details. Continuous observation had once seemed a wonderfully convenient phrase.

A member wearing a gray cravat asked whether asking again at every step would destroy continuity.

“Some observation shouldn't be continuous.”

He indicated the final permission: adjustment as the researcher considered necessary.

Luna studied it. Blaming someone else would have been easier.

“I wrote that. I imagined adjustments remaining within the safety limits I understood. But those limits were in my head, not in the participant's hands.”

He offered the example of an unconscious patient. Luna distinguished emergency treatment from research that could wait. An emergency required its own grounds for assistance, not an opportunity to collect unrelated information.

The student hurriedly added a small note permitting emergency exceptions.

“That isn't what she said,” Adrian interrupted.

Everyone looked at him. He separated the two points aloud. Comparing them, the student erased the note. Luna didn't praise Adrian as a promising assistant. She gave him the revised version to check. He read before nodding.

The printer's copyist wanted an example ordinary people could understand without three pages.

Luna pointed at a cup.

“I ask him for water. He agrees. Coming to the table doesn't mean he has agreed to show me an injury.”

“And in research?”

“Explain what you'll observe, how, and what could happen. Unknown isn't the same as harmless. A changed method, a new purpose, or sharing private observations requires another question. When someone says stop, stop whatever can be stopped. Any consequences that can't immediately be halted must be explained beforehand.”

What if stopping wasted everything already spent, the man in gray asked.

“The person proposing the research must accept that possibility. You can't produce an exit fee at the end.”

She wasn't being breezy. An inquiry about instrument maintenance costs lay under another folder. Stopping cost money and disappointed colleagues. It could leave a promising explanation permanently unfinished. She had not magically ceased caring about any of those things.

“Would you regret it?” Adrian asked.

“Very much.”

“Put that in too.”

She slowly smiled. “It might stop sounding like a statement.”

“It would sound more like you.”

Her next left-handed word went crooked. The student studied his inkwell with great discretion. Nothing interesting happened to it, but he kept watching until Luna reminded him another page remained.

The other visitor worked in materials custody. Must all previous records be destroyed? Luna refused a promise they might be unable to keep. Material under lawful preservation and unused private notes required different explanations. Published copies could not all be recalled by declaration. None of that authorized further collection or unrestricted circulation under the name of preservation.

Adrian thought of old papers with memories longer than his own. Their continued existence must not deprive him of decisions today.

“My public statement ends here.” He offered the circled passages. “Put that beside my name, not on a different page.”

The copyist read it back. Adrian signed beneath the approved quotation, not in the template's participant box.

A bell rang outside, well before noon. Viola passed through the side corridor, her left arm still supported. She hadn't come to provide security. She gave Adrian a small note to read when convenient.

Ten minutes, it said.

Below: Sitting under the covered walkway. No walk. No gate inspection.

He nearly laughed. Looking up, he found her blushing.

“The physician hasn't changed my permitted range. So I changed the invitation.”

“Two this afternoon?”

“If you'd like.”

He would. She wrote the time as seriously as if ten minutes deserved respect too. Luna didn't crane her neck. After Viola left, she merely asked whether her own handwriting was harder to read than a knight's.

“You had a student rescuing yours.”

She laughed, settled her hand on its cushion, and called a break. Explaining principles didn't turn pain into a reason to skip rest.

During the pause, the student gathered discarded copies for the fire. Luna stopped him.

Beneath the broad consent wording was a passage Adrian hadn't reviewed. It described his hesitation as momentary nervousness.

“Was that your observation or my answer?”

The student said he had wanted the sentence to read smoothly.

“I might have been unwilling. Not every pause is waiting to be persuaded.”

Luna kept the page on the table. An unconfirmed fair copy must not circulate as an established record. Nor did she blame only the student: the original annotation was hers and sounded as though she already knew. Before both visitors, she removed the judgment, retaining only the pause and absence of an answer. Then she asked whether Adrian would permit even that anonymous example to be published.

He read it twice and declined.

The copyist lowered his ready pen. Luna didn't explain his refusal for the room. She returned the page to its private folder. They had lost an excellent example and gained a stop that didn't have to become a demonstration.

Before the final check, the man in gray asked an unpleasant question. Would admitting defects in old wording undermine confidence in every previous result?

Luna was quiet.

“Review what can be reviewed. Fear of examining the past doesn't require me to keep writing the future wrongly.”

He would acknowledge attendance, not endorse the entire statement. She accepted that distinction. His presence did not make the attendance sheet a joint declaration, and she did not ask the student to alter its heading after he had agreed to sign. The custody specialist identified two ambiguous sentences; the student corrected them. Hartwell's source check later arrived with its own explicit limits.

Adrian personally compared the printer's copy with the one remaining on the table. The drawing of his scar stayed in its folder. Public ethics had not become a way to publish another body.

A registration messenger arrived shortly before noon.

Conversation stopped. He carried no new seven-day order. The notice confirmed that the original measure would expire at its stated time. Individual risk inquiries and objections continued. Any further restriction required new grounds and the corresponding notice. Expiry was neither a declaration that every technical danger had vanished nor automatic approval of pending applications.

Adrian read every line and checked the reference and date. The messenger recorded the actual delivery time without asking for the name of an assessment partner.

When the clock struck twelve, nobody leaped up to announce a betrothal.

Cecilia offered Adrian bread and asked whether lunch might now be possible. Only then did he notice the uncapped pen still in his hand.

That afternoon, the approved statement went to the scholarly reception office and printer through the usual messenger. Luna did not demonstrate endurance by traveling injured. Later receipts identified a researcher's statement, not a new rule already adopted by the organization.

At a quarter to two, Adrian was still checking page counts with a messenger. Viola asked whether their time needed changing rather than waiting silently for him to guess.

“Five more minutes,” he began. Then he saw the remaining receipts. “No. I can't promise that. Quarter past two?”

She considered and agreed, going to rest first. She did not stand beside the work until embarrassment forced him to abandon it. Nor did she pretend the revised time was the one they had originally chosen. It was a change they had both actually heard and accepted. No penalty was entered. His acceptance hadn't become a requirement to remain available at any moment. Adrian watched her go, grateful that she had asked a plain question rather than leaving him to work out whether the corridor had become another place where someone was waiting unhappily.

At a quarter past, he found two chairs and a small plate of sliced pear under the walkway.

“You're punctual,” Viola said.

“That sounds like an inspection.”

“I'm practicing not sounding like that.”

He sat. She selected the ugliest piece of pear. Asked why, she explained that she had tried one slice with her right hand, then asked the kitchen to finish. She couldn't claim the kitchen's work.

Adrian requested a trade. Her expression became suspicious; perhaps he meant to demonstrate his noble care of an injured woman.

“This piece is bigger,” he said.

She laughed. She told him how, as a child, she had mistaken the supper bell for an assembly signal and summoned companions still holding spoons. He admitted once earnestly waiting for transport that never used that road. She didn't turn that little recollection into a demand for his entire history. She didn't ask where he came from, only how he got home. By walking a long way, he said. Hungry the entire time.

“So you like punctuality?”

“I like knowing someone will tell me when a plan changes.”

When ten minutes passed, she looked at the clock and then at him, reluctantly. He had five more minutes if she wanted them, but she needn't extend her activity for his sake.

She chose to go inside. Once standing steadily, she invited him to come another time. Today's pleasure wasn't an obligation stored against the future.

Inside, Isabella was reading a private letter. Someone would support a further proposal if she left room to discuss a political marriage. She turned the letter with her right hand, careful of her bandaged left wrist.

“Before tomorrow, he needs to know there is no such opening.”

Adrian stopped. She looked up without first asking what he would pay for how much support.
