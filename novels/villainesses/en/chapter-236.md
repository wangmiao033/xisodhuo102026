# Chapter 236: Don't Agree for Me

On April 4, Viola placed an empty envelope on the breakfast table. She had kept the private note herself. What she chose to share was this: an old colleague wanted her to consider a special commitment to public protection, alone, because she understood duty better than most. It wasn't a threat or a formal order. Its tone was almost considerate.

“He said there was no need to upset anyone else.”

“You can't—” Adrian began.

Viola looked at him. His cup touched its saucer. Only last night he'd moved chairs so the women could see each other. This morning his fear nearly reclaimed the middle.

“I'm afraid you'll be persuaded to bear it alone. Not saying I'm entitled to forbid you thinking.”

She nodded without immediately forgiving his first words. With her right hand, left arm still supported, she reclaimed the envelope. She'd already answered: public protection could be discussed; a secret commitment to spare friends' feelings couldn't. She wasn't requesting his permission to refuse. Could they actually prevent one another from being isolated?

That wasn't the same as putting five names on one sheet. A shared heading wouldn't tell them what happened when someone spoke to one woman without the others, offering an apparently kind way to spare her friends. They had to answer that situation, not merely declare that they stood together and hope the declaration did all the work.

They met beside the academy's small courtyard, not at yesterday's examination table. Luna could attend briefly; others arrived around their duties. Adrian initially divided equal speaking periods. Cecilia reminded him of her afternoon shift. She couldn't surrender a day for a symmetrical arrangement.

“Ask who needs to leave when first,” he conceded.

“Better than assigning how I use my day.” She offered a darkly baked cake. Would he accept that fully disclosed risk?

He broke it, finding a soft center beneath the singed edge. She took half too. Care for him wouldn't replace what she wanted to say.

Before everyone arrived, a relief-hall worker sought Cecilia. A mover had a family difficulty; could she arrange help? She stepped outside, neither admitting him to the private discussion nor dismissing ordinary work because they were deciding the world's future. Was the missing work carrying or registration? She asked the established shift contact to check a replacement.

Adrian offered himself. Cecilia asked what he'd already promised for the afternoon. This meeting and preparing his own response, he said.

“Don't cover an old promise with a new one. This isn't a test of whether you'd carry things for me.”

Embarrassed, he listened as the worker laughed. Someone could exchange an actual shift without occupying two places to look important. Another adult already assigned carrying work agreed to change periods, provided evening relief came earlier. Cecilia requested confirmation of that relief. Willingness hadn't completed every arrangement.

Back inside, Adrian asked whether she'd seen through his wish to impress.

“Not entirely. I like your wanting to help. Not discovering afterward that each yes leaves someone else waiting. I can like the intention without accepting the arrangement.”

She placed her cup at her own chair. Refusal hadn't carried her farther away. She remained here, unwilling to substitute an appealing answer for something possible.

Elena arrived last with courtyard soil on her shoes. She wiped them outside and asked what she'd missed. Cecilia explained the shift exchange, not making their conversation a secret someone must compete with.

Protection caused the first argument.

Viola wanted anyone invited to make a private dangerous commitment to be able to bring an independent witness or chosen companion. Isabella feared their statement would be interpreted as refusing legitimate individual questioning. Cecilia distinguished testimony from consent to bodily risk. A need for separate questioning didn't authorize an examiner to decide the latter.

“What if one of us genuinely wants to accept a risk?” Elena asked. “What do the others do?”

Luna shifted her shawl without involving her injured palm.

“Persuade her. Ask what she knows. Offer alternatives. Don't write yes for her. Don't lock her up to prove respect.”

Adrian wanted a different answer—a promise that nobody would ever go, something that closed like a bolt.

Elena asked whether he was turning fear into rules for everyone again.

“Yes. I know I can't. I still dislike that answer.”

“You can dislike it. I dislike your having dangerous choices of your own too.”

Nobody laughed. They left the discomfort there instead of covering it immediately with alliance. Adrian's dislike hadn't vanished because he'd acknowledged it. Elena hadn't offered to make it vanish by surrendering a decision. For a while they simply sat with the fact that caring about another person didn't provide a convenient way to remove every choice that might frighten them.

Isabella proposed a royal notice routing all invitations through her, then stopped herself. Special coordinating authority no longer belonged to her. Personal concern couldn't create a new power. Even with authority, she'd become another door everyone had to pass.

She turned the sheet over. Each person could choose contacts, decide whether to share an invitation, and how much. Actual danger or coercion required the appropriate protective authority; a circle of friends couldn't replace that process.

“I hate how much slower this is than one notice.”

“I hate not being able to stand in every doorway,” Viola said.

Luna hated that her hand wouldn't let her spread the drawings faster. Three different frustrations together showed Adrian women who wouldn't stay merely because he'd found a lovely speech.

Luna made her choice first. She would keep designing and examining alternatives, including unfavorable results. Her injured hand and magical strength weren't untested final patches. A genuinely assessable future proposal required a new explanation, not today's sharing together converted into permanent consent.

Viola would help protect routes ordinary people could use and coordinate only escorts and witnesses she actually had authority to arrange. Honor wouldn't prohibit withdrawal. She wanted reminders when she again confused endurance with the only worthwhile contribution.

Cecilia would serve, explain, and question. A saint's title wouldn't choose sacrifice for others. She didn't control the entire Temple, and sincere faith didn't guarantee her colleagues would agree.

Isabella would expose her privileges, resource sources, and decisions to examination. She couldn't promise the kingdom's agreement or convert royal responsibility into five private debts. Her right hand held an ordinary pen while her left wrist rested in its support. Shorter sentences were harder to take back.

Elena spoke last. She would help find verifiable resources and ensure employees and lenders knew their use. Her house's name couldn't consent for them. Loving Adrian didn't prove she deserved survival more—or sacrifice sooner—than someone else.

“I want to live. My reason needn't be better than yours.”

Adrian's chest tightened. After so many qualified phrases, a sentence without an attachment. He didn't immediately replace its subject with I want you alive too.

“So do I,” Cecilia said first.

Viola rested her right hand on the table. Luna requested her last dictated sentence read back. Isabella folded the unfinished royal notice. There was no new magical contract on the table, no ceremony making their bodies part of the model. Each statement belonged to its speaker. None supplied an answer for every future circumstance. What mattered was that they had actually said the choices aloud, heard the differences, and refused to hide those differences behind a unanimous-looking list. They hadn't sworn never to disagree. They refused to be separated into bargains in which one uninformed sacrifice bought the others' safety, leaving survivors believing it had been the only way.

When Adrian's turn came, he didn't volunteer to replace them. He would share supported dangers and failures, not conceal bad news. When tempted to agree first, he'd pause and let them question him. He would retain his own refusal, not treat being alive as a debt owed to the women.

Luna considered him. “Read the last part again.”

He did.

“Harder to memorize than a promise to win.”

Afternoon wind opened the door and sent loose pages toward the courtyard. Adrian rose. Viola instinctively moved too, then stopped at the pull on her supported arm. Sitting again, she told an assistant not to run. The pages weren't near the pool yet.

Elena stepped on a blank sheet, Cecilia retrieved another, and Isabella held the door with her right hand. Luna remained seated, pointing out the last page behind a flowerpot rather than standing to prove she wasn't a burden.

Adrian brought the papers back and smiled. Not at five women revolving around him. Nobody had to become the bravest person to retrieve paper. Viola noticed, indicated her water, and changed an emerging command into a request to bring it closer.

“That I can do.”

“Refusing wouldn't be treason.”

They laughed. Elena folded the blank sheet beneath a wobbling chair leg. It didn't become treasured evidence of an alliance's birth. The chair stopped wobbling. Someone could sit comfortably again. That small practical use was enough for a sheet on which nobody had written anything important.

At Luna's departure time, the meeting stopped demanding immediate answers from her. She left the portions she'd agreed to share and arranged later questions. Leaving early wasn't acceptance of everything still to be discussed. Cecilia went to her shift, asking whether tomorrow's meal arrangement stood. Adrian would check his hours first, not ask her to keep a place for uncertainty.

Alone with him afterward, Elena asked whether being corrected by five people had hurt a little.

“A little.”

“Still want to come?”

“Yes. Better than only hearing what I want.”

She approached and asked to embrace him. He agreed. This was a question about an embrace here, between the two of them, not a reward for answering the group correctly. He wanted her close and could say so without promising away tomorrow. Lowering his head, he didn't turn her arms into treatment for every fear. She patted his back once and admitted she was afraid too.

They stood together, then separated to clear the cups. She hadn't promised never to worry him; he hadn't demanded a riskless life as the price of their relationship.

That evening, Viola's old colleague sent an acknowledgment. He accepted her refusal of a private commitment but reported a limited meeting tomorrow, held personally by the High Priest, concerning current protection and the timetable for alternatives. The established liaison verified the invitation. Its conditions allowed an independent recorder and designated companions.

No deadline number, no recruitment already begun, no authority over their bodies created by an invitation. Adrian forwarded it within each person's agreed sharing scope, accepting for none of them.

In his own diary he entered willing to attend; personal and recording arrangements to be confirmed. Beyond the window darkness settled. Viola had taken away the empty envelope from breakfast. It had never belonged to him.
