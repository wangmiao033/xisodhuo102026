# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Two: The Saint Won't Let Me Get Married
### Chapter 55: The Saint Isn't Choosing a Fated Husband

On November fourth, Cecilia handed Adrian an apron.

Embroidered across its front was a white dove so plump that its neck had disappeared.

“This is my uniform?”

“It belongs to the clinic. You can choose a different one if you don't like it.”

The other apron had two doves.

Adrian quietly tied on the first.

The independent interviews wouldn't begin until that afternoon. First, they had come to Mercy Clinic to sort clean bed linens. Dr. Winter restricted them to chores the volunteers had been trained to perform, and the dispensary keys stayed with a nurse. Yesterday's heat-transferring copper discs hadn't accompanied them. All they had to move today was some cloth washed almost colorless.

Cecilia showed him how to match the ends of a long sheet. Adrian took hold confidently, gave it a shake, and engulfed his own head.

The corridor went quiet. Then somebody laughed hard enough to cough.

“Mr. Reed?” Cecilia asked through the cloth. “Do you need rescuing?”

“If this is a Temple ceremony, I refuse to participate.”

Laughing, she lifted a corner. A tuft of his hair stood straight up, and a cotton thread clung to his forehead.

She raised a hand, then stopped close to his face.

“May I?”

Seeing what she meant to remove, he nodded. She picked away the thread. She didn't take the opportunity to straighten his collar, but as she withdrew her hand, she forgot to look away.

Adrian looked back at her.

“Is there another one?”

“No.”

“Admiring the dove, then?”

“It isn't as handsome as you.”

The thread slipped from her fingers.

She looked down for it. So did Adrian. The floor offered no possible way of distinguishing one cotton thread from another, yet they searched as earnestly as if they had lost the original document that could prove their innocence.

Mira passed with an empty basket and paused.

“They'll sweep this afternoon,” she pointed out.

Both of them straightened. Cecilia's ears were still pink when they reached the next ward.

Mrs. Merritt, the elderly woman in the bed beside the window, was waiting for someone to fetch her shawl from a cupboard. She knew nothing about noble family trees and cared nothing about an old betrothal. When the two volunteers entered, she simply asked Adrian whether he could fix a window latch.

The window wouldn't close properly. A draft kept the flowers beside her bed trembling.

After two attempts, he found that the wooden frame had swollen with damp. Forcing it might trap someone's fingers. He asked the nurse to arrange for a carpenter and tucked folded cloth along the gap for the time being.

“I can keep the draft out for now,” he said. “Please don't put me on the maintenance staff.”

Mrs. Merritt drew her shawl comfortably around her.

“You're better than my husband. He could fix it, but first he'd have to tell me how good he was at fixing it.”

Cecilia smiled as she replaced the water in the vase.

“You must miss him.”

“Of course. I even miss his dreadful singing.”

Mrs. Merritt glanced at her, then at Adrian, still checking for drafts.

“When I was young, I thought someone worth loving ought to arrive in a blaze of light. Later, I realized it was quite something to find a man who'd let me finish a sentence when I was angry.”

She smoothed the edge of her shawl.

“The singing really was dreadful, though. Best ask for a song before you choose.”

Cecilia turned to Adrian. “May I save that part for later?”

“I'd prefer you dropped it permanently.”

She turned toward the flowers to hide her laughter.

Elena arrived before noon with the donation records the accounts office had copied. At the door, the plump white dove interrupted her businesslike expression.

“It suits you.”

“Please don't say you're going to buy ten.”

“Just one,” she said slowly. “To hang somewhere you'll see it whenever you refuse to rest.”

Adrian untied the apron, folded it, and returned it to the shelf. Watching him, Cecilia unexpectedly wished the morning didn't have to end.

He hadn't made a single claim about saving anyone's fate. He got tangled in sheets. He admitted he couldn't repair a window. He pleaded quite earnestly to be spared singing.

The figure she had followed in her dreams for years had never contained any of those details.

The man standing here did.

“I still want to attend this afternoon's interview,” she said.

Elena looked at her. “We weren't going to suggest you run away.”

“I know. I just wanted to say it myself before I went in.”

Before they left, Elena borrowed a bench in the clinic's rear courtyard and put the copied accounts into a buckled leather satchel. Adrian sat beside her, holding the mouth of the bag open as the leather kept curling inward.

“Did the duke sleep well?” he asked.

“Not especially. The accounts office sent the renewal authorization to his study last night. He looked at it for a long time.”

“And you?”

“Not especially either.”

She fastened the buckle but didn't stand.

“This morning, though, I saw the first year's heater receipts again. It made me want to visit the relief home. Not to prove anything about Mother. I'd just like to know whether the heaters are still being used.”

“We could go when it's convenient for them.”

“I mean visit, Adrian. Not open another file.”

“I'll try not to ask for the equipment number.”

She laughed, some of the strain leaving her face. A tuft of lint clung to his cuff. She plucked it away more briskly than Cecilia had, but afterward she too left her hand where it was.

“You're different today.”

“I wore a dove.”

“That isn't what I mean.”

She studied him, apparently deciding whether to finish the thought.

“You did something that didn't need somebody to be dying for it to matter.”

Adrian was quiet for a moment.

Since arriving in this world, he had feared that the slightest pause would make him miss some lethal turn in the plot. Yet what he remembered of this morning was a draft at a window, flowers beside a bed, and the saint searching the floor for a piece of cotton.

“I like mornings like this too,” he said.

Elena took his hand without squeezing it. Neither of them hurried to stand.

“Then leave some room for them.”

The wind pushed the courtyard gate open. Mira peered around it, saw them sitting together, and swallowed whatever she had meant to call out. She left a document bag with an unfastened clasp beside the gate and tiptoed away.

Adrian looked at the bag.

“I think she misunderstood what we were doing.”

Elena didn't release his hand.

“She understood perfectly. We're holding hands.”

This time, he could find nothing to correct.

Vera was waiting for Cecilia outside the interview room. Did she want to change the scope of testimony they had settled on that morning?

Cecilia shook her head. She would describe the relief registrations she had handled, the charity's working practices, and her reasons for questioning the additional demonstration. She wouldn't offer incomplete dreams as proof.

The notebook containing those dreams stayed in her bag. She took her work records inside.

Sabine conducted the fact-finding interview. Sister Anna attended as a representative of the independent review committee. Opposite Cecilia sat Dorian Mercer, his gloves folded on his knees, his cuffs more immaculate than anyone else's.

“Miss Saintclair, do you believe those receiving a blessing should begin by distrusting the person conducting it?” he asked.

“I believe the person conducting it should know what they're asking others to accept.”

“Are you repudiating the Temple's traditions?”

Cecilia looked at the supplemental wording that waived medical verification.

“When did this sentence become a tradition?”

Mercer didn't answer at once. Sabine directed him to address the specific document rather than ask a witness to account for their entire faith.

Eventually he acknowledged that his office had forwarded the supplement. He claimed to have checked only its format; the contents came from reports compiled by his staff. Its author remained unidentified.

“Is there an approval form bearing your authorization?” Vera asked.

“I'll check.”

From the back row, Adrian heard the familiar words and understood for the first time how differently they could weigh in different mouths.

Cecilia submitted her volunteer log. The entry in question asked a doctor to verify the patient numbers. It contained no instruction to stop providing care.

She also acknowledged that, in the past, she had copied *Blessing complete* into the standard forms without checking every patient's subsequent medical arrangements.

Vera didn't interrupt.

“Does that mean you knew every record was untrue?” Sabine asked.

“No. It means I treated finishing the ceremony as though I'd finished everything I was responsible for.” Cecilia gripped her pen, her fingertips paling. “I now believe those have to be separate.”

Mercer leaned back a little.

“If the public hears that, they'll conclude blessings are meaningless.”

“A patient asked me to change the water in her vase today,” Cecilia said. “Knowing the water wasn't a miracle didn't stop her from thanking me.”

Sister Anna looked up. She didn't smile, but she wrote the sentence in the margin of her notes.

When the interview ended, Cecilia read her statement and signed it. She specifically asked to retain the passage acknowledging her past oversight. She didn't want the record reduced to sentences that helped her case.

Outside, the first thing she did was lean against the wall.

“Did I say too much?”

Adrian unscrewed the lid of her water flask. He didn't look to Elena for permission before offering it.

“You said what you wanted to say.”

“Some people might be disappointed.”

“I'm not.”

She raised her eyes.

He met them and finished the thought. “What you said makes it easier for me to trust your answers.”

Cecilia closed her hands around the flask. Suddenly she understood why she had kept looking at him after picking away the thread.

She wasn't in a hurry to give the feeling a suitably solemn name.

But she wanted to fold sheets with him again. Preferably without Mira watching next time.

When Mercer emerged, he handed the group an event notice. Tomorrow, the North Cloister relief hall would hold a public blessing intended to reassure the community. He would oversee verification of the program.

The notice expressly stated that only conventional consecrated optical lamps would be used. The historical transfer ring would remain sealed. There would be no personal risk registrations and no connection to living people's marks.

“We can't leave well-intentioned believers with nothing but fear,” he said.

Cecilia had no objection to an ordinary blessing. She asked whether independent safety staff would be present.

“Of course.” Mercer pointed to the permit stamp.

After he left, Elena turned the notice over. A small line on the back had been printed in different-colored ink.

*The side door will be temporarily closed during the illumination to prevent drafts from disturbing the lamp.*

“Is that door an exit?” she asked.

Cecilia had visited the hall. As she remembered it, there were only two ways out: the front door and the side door.

Hale didn't send them off with the notice to force a lock. He contacted the municipal safety inspector responsible for that district. By early evening, Bess Ward had arrived at the meeting room just outside the clinic, carrying a copy of the original permit.

Its wording was clear. Both exits must remain passable throughout the event. Ventilation must not be blocked to improve the lighting effect.

“I didn't approve this addition,” she said, comparing the two.

The ease Adrian had felt that morning slowly drained from his stomach.

“Can it be stopped now?”

“I'll serve a correction notice immediately. We'll inspect the hall before admission tomorrow. If the conditions aren't met, the event can't open.”

Cecilia handed over the notice and offered to show her the hall's usual entrances and exits. Elena asked the relief organization to hold off bringing guests with limited mobility until the inspector had confirmed the arrangements were safe.

They couldn't close the building by personal decree. They could hardly wait for the lamps to light before asking whether the doors still opened, either.

Dusk had settled on the steps by the time they left the clinic. Adrian glanced back to find Cecilia lingering at the door.

“What is it?”

She seemed to have thought carefully about her answer.

“When this is over, I'd like to take you to lunch. Would that be all right?”

“Will there be historical archives?”

“No.”

“Will I have to sing?”

“That part is negotiable.”

He laughed. “Let's find a time once the safety inspection is finished.”

Elena, standing beside the carriage, had heard every word. She didn't answer for him. Instead, she pulled the end of his scarf out of the carriage door.

“Try not to trap yourself here. You still owe me your company on tomorrow's ride.”

He took the scarf she offered, and her hand with it.

What needed protecting tomorrow was already written on the permit.

It wasn't the beauty of a lamp's glow.

It was every person's right to walk back out after they went in.
