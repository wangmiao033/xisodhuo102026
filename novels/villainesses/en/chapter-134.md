# Chapter 134: Even a Prince Fears Someone Answering for Him

Leon was not waiting beside the throne.

On the afternoon of January 9, he borrowed an ordinary reception room overlooking the practice yard. Two young guards were rehearsing disarms outside. One failed to catch a wooden sword. Its clatter sounded more honest than the teacups inside.

The prince glanced out and asked an attendant to close the window.

“I don't want this conversation interpreted as siblings privately dividing power.”

Isabella removed her gloves. “So you've invited two witnesses and a clerk, and intend to leave the door open.”

“Something you never agreed to last night almost reached two offices today. I consider caution worth learning.”

Adrian sat near the door. Elena sat beside him and entered her name in the attendance record before declining the statement Leon offered.

“We aren't here to prove that nothing involving you ever happened.”

His hand stopped. “I didn't ask that.”

“Then remove the second paragraph.”

The statement said no proposed proxy had been confirmed and Leon had not agreed to serve. The next paragraph claimed the document had no connection to any of the prince's affairs.

He could speak to the first. The second reached far beyond what he knew.

Leon took back the paper, irritation finally showing.

“Miss Rosenberg, you always find the least convenient place for courtesy.”

“Someone once arranged my execution. My idea of where courtesy belongs is rather narrow.”

Silence settled.

Adrian did not tell her it was all in the past. He remembered their October meeting, Leon's admission about the red-bordered warning, and the confidentiality agreement once prepared for him. A new sheet of paper could not sweep those things into a column marked Unrelated.

Leon set down his statement.

“I only ask that you don't treat this proxy draft as an order from me.”

“Then produce what supports that,” Isabella said.

He had the clerk fetch his office's retained dispatch copy. It was not last night's draft. It was a January 7 inquiry to the secretariat: could candidates designate contacts to receive ordinary succession notices so their absence need not delay delivery?

The final sentence reserved withdrawals, substantive responses, and changes of authorization for confirmation by the candidate concerned.

Adrian read it and turned to the delivery counterpart. Its reference and date were complete. It remained a copy held by Leon's office, however, not independent evidence merely because it had been placed before them.

“Does the secretariat have the corresponding receipt?”

“I've requested a check,” Leon said. “You can wait for the answer. You needn't agree now.”

Isabella's expression changed as she read the exclusions.

“You wanted easier receipt of notices. Someone made it permission to withdraw things for me.”

“They may have had another source.”

Leon answered quickly. He plainly disliked the possibility that his ordinary inquiry had begun this, but did not insist the papers could have no connection.

“I wasn't at the banquet. That doesn't mean I know where everyone else was.”

“We haven't asked you to,” Isabella said.

“You might later.”

The unpleasant answer sounded sincere. His tea remained untouched. A fingertip rubbed the cup's handle as though he were calculating which room might someday hear each of his words repeated.

Adrian suddenly thought that Leon did understand fear.

He usually had enough power to make someone else feel it first.

Another wooden sword fell outside. Leon frowned. An attendant moved to reprimand the guards, but the prince raised a hand.

“Leave them. Let them practice.”

While they waited, Isabella asked why he had insisted on inviting Adrian. A statement limited to Leon's own actions needed no outside witness.

The prince looked directly at him.

“Because they've already put your name after hers. I need someone to know I didn't ask you to lie for me today.”

“That still doesn't mean I'll support you in other matters.”

“I know you're good at that sentence.”

“People often hope it only applies once.”

Leon gave a humorless laugh and took an old correspondence index from his folder. One field bore the existing reference for the red-bordered warning. He had not brought the text, and Adrian learned no new secret from it.

“In October, I told you some warnings about old records hadn't begun with me. I thought handing the danger to people called experts would at least keep it from becoming my responsibility.”

“You handed over my situation too,” Elena said.

“Yes.”

The single word did not lighten the past.

Leon did not ask forgiveness. He returned the index to its folder. He still limited what he would discuss to a few correspondence records, unwilling to commit unverified suspicions to a statement. He was offering dispatch and receipt records relevant to this proxy, not a complete confession exchanged for immunity.

Adrian could hear the calculation in that choice.

Calculation did not make verifiable evidence worthless. Nor did providing evidence suddenly make its source trustworthy.

The reply arrived later than the afternoon refreshments. The secretariat confirmed receipt of the January 7 inquiry. Its complete text matched Leon's copy and contained no request to make substantive responses on Isabella's behalf. The supervisor would compare it with the draft's preparation materials, but had not established a source relationship.

Leon exhaled and immediately picked up his original statement.

Elena raised her eyes.

He stopped. This time he crossed out no connection to any of my affairs himself, replacing it with neither requested nor accepted the proxy authorization described in this draft. Whether a staff member had cited his inquiry remained to be checked.

“Is that enough?”

“Enough to record what you can say now,” Adrian answered.

The clerk transcribed it. Leon checked and signed. Then he placed his office's correspondence register for this proxy arrangement under the supervisor's preservation review, rather than selecting only the page that favored him.

“No unrelated private correspondence,” he emphasized.

The supervisor's written request already limited the scope. The clerk circled three relevant items in the index and left a counterpart acknowledgment. No one carried away the entire document chest.

Isabella looked at her brother's signature.

“You're afraid they'll fill a blank with you too.”

“Aren't you?”

“Yes.”

For a moment, he had no answer.

They did not reconcile like children. Adrian did not even know whether their childhood had contained days that description would fit. But Isabella did not mock his fear, and Leon did not make last night's danger a joke about her fitness for power.

Once the papers were put away, the silence became harder to manage than their earlier testing of each other.

Finally, Leon indicated the attachment to his inquiry.

“The delivery question didn't come from nowhere. Several missing-person petitions were returned to my affairs desk last month, marked settled elsewhere. I don't administer that project, yet the families asked me twice.”

Isabella went still. “What kind?”

“People seeking workers who accompanied an old project. They have royal departure slips. Neither the workers nor any acknowledgment explaining their whereabouts has returned.”

He did not produce a list containing every secret. He offered two return indexes with household addresses covered, retaining petition numbers, return dates, and closure summaries for authorized staff to retrieve.

One verification note described folds and a stitched repair in an original. That was not how the prince's office repaired its paper.

“Was the family's document like this?” Adrian asked.

“A record made when they showed it at the desk. The original was returned.”

“Have you checked that they really received no answer?”

“No. I asked the responsible office to explain. Its reply said the matter had been transferred into an old case and candidates' desks shouldn't receive duplicate petitions.”

Isabella pressed her lips together.

Adrian turned toward her. She offered no immediate explanation. Instead, she asked whether Leon could request a check by petition number without first releasing names. Families seeking people must not be rewritten as her supporters.

He agreed to request the records, not to arrange a public statement for her.

“I don't want to sign up for another future undertaking on someone else's behalf today.”

“This time, you don't have to,” she said.

Elena slowed as they left. Adrian stopped too and accompanied her to the side of the corridor.

“When he said yes, for a moment I wanted to ask why he couldn't have said it then.”

“You can ask.”

“I know. Today, I don't want to give him the rest of the afternoon.”

She held out her hand. Adrian took it and waited for her to choose a direction.

Finally she pointed toward the palace gates.

“They should still be selling pastries. We missed ours yesterday.”

“Weren't you going home to the family accounts?”

“The accounts won't escape if I leave them fifteen minutes. You might.”

“Do I look ready to run?”

“You look ready to stay for everything waiting for an explanation.”

His smile slowly faded. He walked down the steps holding her hand. She was not asking him never to help anyone. Before he surrendered another entire afternoon, she had saved a small portion in which they could enter a pastry shop.

The window held none of the palace's elegant cream towers. There were apple rolls and darkly baked honey biscuits. After serious consideration, Elena chose the variety least likely to shed crumbs.

The first bite fell onto her skirt.

Adrian turned away. She nudged him with her elbow.

“Laugh. Not every error of judgment requires a written explanation.”

He finally did, bringing her a clean little plate rather than brushing crumbs from her skirt himself. She tidied them, sat by the window, and pushed another biscuit toward him.

Outside, a woman selling sewing supplies disputed with a customer whether a particular thread was dark blue or gray-blue. They listened for a while. Neither hurried to adjudicate.

“Why didn't you take his paper at first?” Adrian asked.

“Once I accepted it, he could more easily treat me as someone who had agreed to review it for him.”

“You did read it later.”

“Yes. Then I chose to.”

She rested a hand beside her plate and looked at him.

“I don't want refusing one prince to turn every refusal afterward into a war. Sometimes I still can't tell whether I want to stay or simply refuse to be the first to retreat.”

Adrian did not immediately praise her unfailing courage. He considered, then moved his plate a little nearer hers.

“What about here?”

“Here, I want to stay.”

“So do I.”

Her expression softened. A little later, she admitted the supposedly crumb-free biscuits tasted no better than the others. Next time, she could choose the apple roll she actually wanted.

Adrian topped up their ordinary hot water. Until their agreed fifteen minutes ended, neither mentioned the prince's statement. The time was short. That did not mean it could only become worth having after everything else was finished.

Isabella had not followed them. In the portico, she called back her attendant and changed the message urging them to attend the palace the next morning. She wrote a specific invitation instead: on the morning of January 10, she wished to explain an old petition she had received.

When it reached Adrian, she had added a final line.

If he was willing to listen, she would begin with why she had set that summary aside.
