# Chapter 271: This Morning Was Still This Morning

Adrian woke to bargaining outside.

The vegetable seller blamed last night's detour for higher transport costs. The kitchen buyer insisted half the basket was crushed and couldn't be priced as good produce. Neither mentioned destiny. Adrian listened before reaching for his bedside cup.

April 29. The water had cooled, his scar remained, and last night's dust still marked his coat.

Elena sat by the window with a message from the daytime liaison office. Seeing him awake, she asked about his headache, then whether he remembered last night's promise.

“I didn't offer to hire an entire restaurant, did I?”

“You said you'd ask me out today.”

“Then I'm asking.”

She closed the letter. Despite plainly having waited, she lifted her chin. “Breakfast first. Hungry people have unreliable judgment.”

He studied the porridge. Would finishing it prove that liking her was a considered decision? She put the spoon in his hand. Another sentence, she warned, and breakfast would be colder than last night's bread.

From outside, Martha asked whether eating porridge also required independent witnesses. Blushing, Elena invited her in. Adrian nearly laughed his first mouthful back into the bowl.

Last night hadn't cured the world. Afterward, they opened the message.

Two nearby stations confirmed continuing protection, a locked-out central preparation linkage, and no instruction to proceed. Dangerous sections of one home's outer wall still needed removing; its occupants couldn't return. The riverside households were sheltered, the scraped resident cared for. More distant replies remained in transit. Sunrise hadn't colored the entire liaison map green.

“Can we say the kingdom is temporarily spared a reset?” the daytime liaison asked.

Adrian didn't answer immediately. With the engineering supervisor, he examined the central overnight record. The only chain able to issue continuation signals for this round's regional preparation linkages remained physically parked and isolated. Local stations couldn't initiate a complete reset independently. That fact applied throughout the kingdom without pretending they had personally inspected every lamp.

Whether disaster had eased in each locality required local evidence.

The notice ultimately announced temporary nationwide reprieve from this reset, immediately explaining the central suspension, continuing local protection, and unchanged precautions. It wasn't an order to be happy. As the first nail went in, a man asked why his shop couldn't reopen if everything was suspended.

Adrian recognized the husband who'd resisted leaving his business. He carried shoes for his wife and looked nothing like someone awaiting a hero's wisdom.

“The wall still isn't stable,” Adrian said.

“Then what good is your paper?”

Adrian indicated the next line: temporary accommodation continued; return required site inspection. Still angry after reading it, the man asked whether an ordinary route was available to retrieve his accounts.

The people who could answer were at reception. Isabella and the duty staff confirmed an existing accompanied retrieval period, without inventing a royal carriage. The man finally admitted he'd gone to his wife's lodging last night. Watching the shop alone was too cold. He hadn't become a reform supporter. He'd simply agreed at last to bring her shoes.

Returning from seeing him out, Isabella opened her right hand toward Adrian.

“He asked if I could have the wall repaired immediately.”

“What did you say?”

“I can press the site supervisor for an answer. I can't press mortar into pretending it's dry.”

She glanced at her supported left wrist. Today's most satisfying achievement, she said, was that nobody had made her sign an all-purpose guarantee of tomorrow's accomplishments. She went to the next person without waiting for Adrian to pronounce her grown.

A little girl stood on tiptoe to inspect the notice's date. Would tomorrow still be tomorrow? Her mother tucked a ball of thread into her basket and bent to ask why.

“They said we'd start again. Would my birthday start again too?”

Not under that procedure now, her mother said. But they still couldn't live at home. Her birthday would be in borrowed accommodation. The girl considered that, then asked whether they could bring the height marks from the doorframe. Her mother's mouth moved without forming a smile.

Adrian didn't promise to save the whole door. He asked the people arranging retrieval whether an ordinary tracing could be made during an existing safe entry period. If its position allowed, paper and charcoal would suffice. They wouldn't dismantle structural supports or extend a dangerous visit.

The mother accepted that answer. Even remembering this year's mark would help. Her daughter held her hands apart to show how much she'd grown—an absurd distance. Beside them, Elena solemnly suggested that at that rate, the roof should apply to move next year.

The girl laughed. Her mother finally did too. They didn't yet have the tracing. Their wish had someone real to ask, rather than being packed into a hollow promise of nationwide safety.

Walking away, Elena touched Adrian's elbow. He could remember that laugh without immediately using it to justify a decision. He nodded. She herself kept looking back until the pair entered the staffed reception door.

That afternoon, Adrian visited Luna as arranged. In a window-side resting chair, her right palm freshly bandaged, she looked slightly better than last night. Her assistant opened the travel book where they'd stopped.

“How far today?” Adrian asked.

“Until the author stops calling getting lost exploration.”

“We may need another book.”

She laughed and told him to sit. She'd heard about both cylinders' consumption and the unresolved technical questions. He wasn't to prove his design while reading today. She'd chosen this rest, and he read what she'd chosen.

The traveler climbed the wrong hillside for sunrise and discovered, when the sun appeared, that he faced west. Listening, Luna suddenly asked whether Adrian had imagined holding everybody's hands to prevent the world starting again.

After a pause, he admitted to a similar thought.

“Fortunately, you didn't. My hand would have objected first.”

“I was going to say fortunately people helped.”

“Also true. Don't turn the people helping into a row of safety ropes attached to you.”

He agreed and continued. After half a page, she said it was enough, without using his gratitude to lengthen his visit. As he rose, she asked whether he'd willingly come again—not because he owed her one.

“Yes. I'll ask what time suits you.”

She looked aside. Of course he must ask. Otherwise he might arrive precisely when she was complaining about bitter medicine. That shy moment belonged to her; it didn't need a shared future borrowed from someone else to become precious.

In the courtyard, he found Viola. Her brief passage briefing had reached the daytime supervisor. Now she sat eating an ordinary baked potato, left arm still secured. Too hot to hold, it made her adjust its paper wrapping twice, less confidently than she'd directed evacuation traffic last night.

“Need support?”

“You have permission to lend me a plate.”

He fetched one instead of peeling the entire potato for her. Finally able to set it down, she sighed. Nobody had invoked her honor against her need for rest today. That felt better than being called fearless.

Adrian sat awhile, listening to how the threshold ought to be repaired. She still wanted to protect people. Her ears still warmed at his praise. Her arm didn't need to heal immediately to prove this life worth keeping.

At dusk, Cecilia brought news of the riverside apprentice. His sister was settled; the cat remained missing. Patrols wouldn't endanger themselves dismantling supports beneath unsafe roof beams for it. He'd offered her half a pastry he'd saved, asking her to keep it until the cat came back and they could celebrate.

“I told him I couldn't keep it long. It would spoil.”

“Was he angry?”

“He said to eat it now. When the cat came back, we'd find another.”

She divided the half pastry into small bites for those present. Fear of loss didn't become a neatly completed fable. Adrian received a few crumbs and found their sweetness remarkably concrete.

On the morning of April 30, several more distant reports arrived. Their departure times differed; the farthest still contained older observations awaiting updates. None of the reporting stations had received a new central continuation signal. Some protective loads eased; others remained high. A road was closed, a warehouse roof damaged. Verification of the full human toll remained incomplete.

The kingdom hadn't been carried away by this reset. That was an established, enormous result. The engineers had never concluded that nationwide disaster was gone.

Adrian didn't stay at the liaison desk until every part of the world answered him. Leaving his actual address with the next shift, he went with Elena to a palace side hall.

There was no victory ball. An affected reception center had borrowed the room for distributing everyday supplies. Distribution was finished, and the manager permitted a short visit that wouldn't obstruct cleaning. The ballroom door stood open. Afternoon light reflected from its distant floor, with the almost excessive brightness Adrian recognized from September 17.

“Don't look at your feet,” Elena said.

He realized he was inspecting her hem. She glanced down at today's practical skirt, then looked up with an unguarded smile.

“You looked exactly like that then. As though you remembered every step.”

“I remembered the game. Real skirts don't follow walkthroughs.”

She laughed. They'd once worked so hard to explain that the accidental kiss had meant nothing to either of them. Later he'd even declared publicly that her ladyship couldn't possibly like him. Remembering that clumsiness still heated his neck.

“I didn't fall for you because of the kiss,” she said.

“I know.”

“But when you said I couldn't possibly like you, I was furious.”

“Because I answered for you.”

“And because you sounded so certain.”

He lowered his head, laughing, and offered another apology, no longer heavy. She straightened his collar. No bow tie remained for her to tighten. Her fingers lingered.

“Shall we dance today?” he asked.

“There's no orchestra.”

“Two steps will do. We don't need to screen a witness from anyone.”

She checked the space the cleaners had already cleared and agreed. They turned through half a slow circle on that small patch of floor, hearing a bucket knock softly against the boards. He didn't tread on her skirt. She didn't lose her balance. When they stopped, he still held her hand.

“May I kiss you this time?”

She looked at him, then smiled again.

“Yes. Don't blame an accident.”

He kissed her lightly, briefly enough that a cleaner by the door barely had time to look politely away. Afterward she didn't demand that anyone take responsibility, and he didn't mistake survival for lifelong consent. Two people had simply chosen to draw close.

Leaning beside his shoulder, she asked whether today's date passed inspection.

“Not one new archival document. Far beyond expectations.”

A liaison appeared at the door at that exact moment. Elena's expression as she looked at Adrian was worth preserving.

“I didn't look for this one,” he said.

The messenger didn't intrude between them. He handed the formal notice to the daytime contact, who established its urgency. No immediate departure was required. Oswin had petitioned the independent adjudicating body for a public trial. He would attend personally and demanded questioning of both the suspension decision and responsibility for the old system.

Elena read it, folded it again, and said, “We'll hear him tomorrow. We still have ten minutes.”

Adrian took her hand again. Those minutes repaired no wall and won no coming trial.

They truly belonged to them.
