# Chapter 184: Before the Bells

On February 12, the temporary service window had a shorter queue than Adrian expected.

Not because everything was settled. It was raining. Someone had brought the wrong papers; others wanted to know where last week's submissions had gone. Cecilia steadied a box slipping from an elderly man's bag without taking all his documents.

“The left-hand desk tracks submissions. You needn't apply again.”

She stepped aside. Apparently he'd already tried the wrong queue. Not again visibly eased him.

The week's trial report hung on the wall. Received, returned, and awaiting verification were separate; receipt wasn't completion. One entry admitted inadequate staff for two kinds of access requests on a particular afternoon. An unwelcome result, but not replaced by Everything proceeding smoothly.

Cecilia wasn't staffing the window today. Her appointment concerned old festival registers. Coinciding with this report didn't make it permission to enter another office.

Outside she found a crooked button in her pocket.

“You said the pouch opening was too tight. I unpicked it.”

She handed over the repaired pouch. Adrian thanked her, about to ask whether she'd stayed up late. Daylight work, she anticipated; no pricked fingers either.

“Not every gift needs you to worry.”

“All right.”

“It doesn't mean I'm no longer hurt, either.”

Rain fell from the umbrella. He paused, acknowledging that. She looked toward the junction, leaving her admission somewhere it needn't immediately be caught and corrected.

The festival office between two chapels smelled of damp paper and extinguished candles. An elderly registrar checked Cecilia's application and produced two bound volumes. No underground secrets: few people wanted records of furniture, lamp oil, and clock corrections. The dullness was almost a relief after the storage room. Adrian could look at a column without finding his name waiting at the end of it. Yet these were still accounts written by particular people for particular purposes, not a complete view of everything that had happened nearby.

They sought the sequence of the Festival of Return mentioned in the old material. Later summaries, the registrar explained, grouped preparations, early prayers, and collection of lamps beneath the main celebration's date.

“So the festival name needn't mean the day itself?” Adrian asked.

“Perhaps the day someone accounted for it.”

One word could accommodate several days, like a borrowed coat fitting different wearers.

The first volume had repaired corners. Cushions supported it; they mustn't force the spine. Cecilia paused at an entry.

Bells brought forward.

The north bell was undergoing repair. The west bell substituted for the third summons; timings were verbally relayed to duty stations. Adrian remembered the fragment After the third bell in an old image. An apparently precise phrase might indicate the wrong place.

He started copying. Cecilia first asked which festival the amendment covered. Part of the heading was missing; the following page's binding obscured a number.

“We can't lend it to the day we're looking for.”

The registrar softened a little. A neighboring supply ledger offered a matching repair order. They could establish a substitution, not that it occurred during the event described by the three objects.

Adrian crossed out Was advanced and wrote: Advance arrangement exists; date comparison pending. Uglier, and more honest. The canceled words remained visible beneath his line. He wanted to erase them properly, then decided the correction belonged there. The speed with which he had turned a possibility into an answer was part of what they were trying to notice, not something a clean copy should conceal from him.

A loose sheet in the second volume recorded the cost of removing festival banners. Wearing thin gloves, the registrar unfolded it. Two endorsements used different year systems: one after the Return, another the old royal reckoning.

Cecilia didn't announce two worlds. The registration guidance indicated that Temple and royal calendar changes might not have coincided. Precise correspondence required the amendment table. Neither woman tried to force a single date from the two endorsements. Adrian had thought a date was one of the safer things a record could offer. Now he could see that an apparently ordinary number might require as much explanation as the ominous terms surrounding it.

“Isabella is applying for that.”

“Then send her our question, not our preferred answer.”

They recorded page locations, damage, and comparisons needed. Permission covered specified lines; a donor's private note beside them remained shielded. Adrian didn't peer around the cover. He already feared his name becoming everyone's right to inspect him. Fear couldn't justify removing someone else's boundary.

Near noon a young courier arrived carrying three newly bound rolls. Hearing their question, he said his grandfather had described flags hanging for days. Perhaps that was testimony.

Adrian looked up. Perhaps was tempting. A living recollection sounded closer to an exit than damaged margins.

Cecilia asked whether the grandfather wished to discuss it. The young man hesitated. His grandfather was dead; he'd heard it as a child.

She thanked him without entering it as eyewitness evidence. She noted the district and banner lending place as a family-reported lead.

Embarrassed, the courier thought he'd caused trouble.

“No.” Adrian cleared space for the rolls. “Usefulness shouldn't make your grandfather sound more definite than he was.”

The young man finally lowered his burden. His grandfather had also complained that flagpoles obscured a bakery sign, mixing bread shopping with the celebration. That ordinary detail stayed with Adrian. A vague festival might contain traceable rents and shop locations without every household story becoming prophecy.

Banner records belonged elsewhere. The registrar's note wouldn't authorize taking them. They received the custodian's name and application procedure. Leaving, the courier moved an umbrella stand out of the way. Nobody pressed him for something more astonishing.

Cecilia drew a line between pages they'd seen and leads they hadn't pursued. Was she afraid Adrian would copy too quickly again?

“Hoping too hard isn't only your problem.”

The room grew colder. The registrar provided a small hot-water jug, well away from the volumes. People investigating bells forgot clocks didn't keep time by themselves.

“Someone has to climb up.”

“And admit when they're slow,” Cecilia said.

The woman laughed, pointing out the clock tender's entries: wind, worn rope, two quarters' delay. No testimony against Adrian. A day made solemn by later accounts had also contained almost-late lamp oil.

He remembered his morning impatience with queues. He didn't want every delay to become conspiracy, or old records dismissed as harmless confusion.

Confusion wasn't automatically guilty or innocent, the registrar said. They needed the particular copying and use, not to blame every bell.

Before leaving, Cecilia requested both discrepancies appear in the formal response. Someone suggested Date of festival incorrect. She declined. They hadn't established which date was wrong; clarity didn't justify making the evidence commit an error first.

Outside, rain had stopped. A child picked at wet candle string with his shoe while his mother urged him out of the way. Cecilia moved it near the drain without lecturing him about order.

“You've been quiet,” she told Adrian.

“I'm afraid I'll ask whether any page simply says I didn't do it.”

“You may want that. Don't make somebody else's paper say it.”

Neither cold nor wrapped in consolation. He liked her ability to say it, and knew liking this moment wasn't a promise he could offer her.

At the steps they separated. She had a former parishioner to visit, not blank time she'd reserved for him. He offered a carriage detour. No, it wasn't far; she wanted to walk.

As he said goodbye, a bell test sounded inside.

One low stroke, then another unusually close. His left hand curled. No image, no voice from the past. He'd spent days waiting for his body to answer; expectation colored an ordinary sound.

Cecilia noticed and offered a seat. He declined, then changed his mind.

She sat beside him without taking that hand.

“Does it count as anything?”

“You heard a bell and became tense. Beyond that, I don't know.”

The third stroke sounded clearer. A worker called upstairs that the second rope still rubbed against the frame. Not fate awaiting interpretation, just an awkward afternoon repair.

Adrian slowly opened his hand. No new mark lay in his palm. He had almost asked her whether the absence meant anything too. The urge to turn every sensation, and every missing sensation, into a clue was exhausting. She had not pretended to understand more than she did, and for a little while he could sit beside her without producing an explanation for his own breathing.

Cecilia waited until he breathed steadily, then rose. She still had someone to visit. He didn't demand she stay as proof of adequate care.

On his way home, he bought ordinary waxed thread and had it delivered for her sewing box. The note said Thank you, not payment for guilt or an appointment for tomorrow.

That evening Beatrice received their findings. A later trial shouldn't simply replay the newly discovered sequence of bells. Strong suggestion followed by declaring tension a memory was yesterday's moving shore again.

Adrian agreed but asked how the past could be found without any prompting.

He had not asked because he thought the safeguards foolish. He could picture the mistake clearly enough: hear the pattern, wait for fear, then name the fear something grander. What frightened him was how much of his own wish for an answer might cooperate with that mistake.

Her reply was brief: not an absence of conditions, but specified conditions, separate comparisons, and acknowledgment that what emerged might come entirely from today.

Luna added that she wouldn't frighten him with bells and call it success. She'd slept after finishing work; her right hand still couldn't lift anything heavy. Tomorrow wouldn't make her a witch able to solve everyone's problems.

He smiled and read that line to Martha as she brought dinner. Good, she said. Omnipotent people were usually the worst at eating on time.

Halfway through his meal he found the repaired pouch in his coat. Its opening really was easier now. He put it on the table, filling it with neither evidence nor unspoken apologies, only a coin for tomorrow's journey.

The thread and pouch could remain ordinary things, even on a day spent inspecting the meanings people had attached to other ordinary things. He did not need to make Cecilia's careful stitches another sign about his fate, or to pretend gratitude settled what she had said beneath the umbrella.

The festival registers hadn't proclaimed his innocence. They supplied people and dates to question. After the third bell was no longer something apparently unassailable, but words requiring someone to establish who heard it, and where.

He filed the formal response separately from his scattered impressions, calling neither the truth at last.

Nothing in that distinction made the waiting pleasant. It did give him a better question to carry into the next room.

That already changed their next step.
