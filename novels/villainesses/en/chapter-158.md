# Chapter 158: I Fill In This Blank

On the morning of January 27, Adrian discovered that the opening speech he had rehearsed all night felt less reliable than a button.

He checked his collar for the third time. Elena took a small mirror from her bag.

“It’s straight.”

“I know.”

“Checking whether it’s defected?”

He laughed, and his shoulders lowered. She left the mirror with him until he had decided that this ordinary, rather tired face would do.

The side entrance’s handrail had been repaired. No delivery cart blocked the passage. Viola sat against the waiting-room wall, her left arm supported, and indicated the duty staff with her right hand. Her drawing had become work other people were doing. Someone was actually sitting in its chair.

Luna hadn’t come. Her limited memorandum was among the submissions; requests for more answers did not automatically cancel her rest. Isabella waited under her own name, without her silver-edged badge. Her left cuff was loose. She lifted her right hand in a small gesture of encouragement.

Cecilia lowered the quotations she was checking.

“Had breakfast?”

“Yes.”

“Good. Don’t let them starve you into agreeing.”

Elena coughed softly. Cecilia said she would offer her the same advice, which made all three laugh.

Inside, the cramped room swallowed the laughter.

The hearing table wasn’t high, but papers concealed half the clerk’s hand. Two rows of observers sat in arrival order; later visitors waited. No family’s attendants displaced anyone. The reviewing officer defined today’s scope: examining the grounds submitted by both offices and hearing the affected parties. They would not adjudicate anyone’s feelings or consider a new betrothal no one had applied for.

Adrian laid out his statement. His name stood below the heading, without a family crest.

Yesterday’s consultation form lay in his folder too, its proposed assessment partner blank. He had turned it over last night, hoping that would help him sleep, then turned it back. The paper hadn’t followed him to bed, but explaining the empty space was still his task.

The clerk pointed at it.

“Missed a field?”

“No. I’ll explain.”

The clerk didn’t fill in a dash. He separated the unsigned form from Adrian’s signed statement. Keeping a space empty required effort, Adrian discovered.

The registration representative had brought more information. Ordinary objections remained available during the seven-day restriction, but technical staff were limited. Assessing a pair against a known historical connection would narrow the necessary records and speed initial screening.

“How much faster?” Adrian asked.

“We could arrange a preliminary check today. Ordinary review must wait for an available appointment. We cannot promise complete findings before this temporary period ends.”

Adrian had thought he was refusing a sentence. Behind it stood waiting, more carriage fares, perhaps another morning at this table.

The reviewing officer confirmed that declining priority would not withdraw an accepted objection. It would not secure the same speed, either. Elena’s fingers stopped at her paper’s edge. She supplied no answer for him.

The Temple’s representative indicated a publicly cited historical entry. An old connection was not a current marriage, but could provide a starting point for evaluating risk. Cecilia’s questions had been received; the issuing authority still considered preventive arrangements necessary.

“There’s a person’s name inside that starting point,” Adrian said.

“Technical examination requires a specific subject.”

“If I’d freely decided on a particular contract, yes. I haven’t. Your faster entrance asks me to accept a partner before examining why I can’t choose one myself.”

An observer shifted. The reviewing officer cautioned him against describing a safety assessment as forced marriage. The representative had made no such claim.

Adrian gripped his pen and resisted interrupting.

“Then record what it actually requires. I haven’t said anyone will drag me to a wedding today. I’m declining to let this priority procedure choose where my relationship begins.”

Reading that back, the clerk rendered it as refusal of technical examination.

“I’m willing to answer questions about specific risks,” Adrian corrected. “Please include that part.”

The officer ordered the whole passage reread. This time both sentences remained.

The representative turned to Luna’s memorandum. Insufficient evidence did not prove every possible pairing safe. Adrian agreed. He had made no such claim. The old tables supported neither a romantic ranking nor a guarantee of safety. Naming someone in public could fill neither gap.

“Stating whom you currently favor might help narrow the question.”

The room went very quiet.

Adrian could feel Elena sitting behind him. The walk outside the bookshop and the travelogue she had lent him still existed without being entered into this record. He would not summon her to the table to prove he deserved a choice.

“I’ll tell the people concerned what I’m willing to tell them. I won’t trade someone’s name for faster processing here.”

“So you designate no one?”

“Not in this application. Please don’t record that as having no feelings for anybody.”

Someone in the back laughed, then cleared their throat. Adrian’s face warmed. Defending freedom sounded grand. Preventing a roomful of people from misunderstanding him—and leaving him to explain afterward—felt considerably more familiar.

Elena looked down, smiling slightly.

When her turn came, she did not announce indifference to his answer. She stated her own grounds and requested separate explanations for risk restrictions and partner selection. Her family could provide guarantees; that should not be what qualified her to choose. She acknowledged that incomplete technical knowledge might require a clearly bounded wait.

“Explain the reason, duration, and grounds for challenge. Don’t use our urgency to obtain agreement to a different question.”

Her skirt brushed the chair as she sat. Adrian noticed how tightly her hands had been clasped.

Someone spilled a little ink. The clerk reached for blotting paper, and several people moved their documents. Adrian caught his own sliding folder without grabbing the neighboring stack. Even sentences apparently deciding a future had to wait for wet paper to be cleared away.

Before the recess, the officer asked whether he needed to consult those assisting him. Elena nodded slightly when he looked at her: he could decide whether he wanted to, not take her nod as a seal on his answer.

“I’ve decided.”

Waiting still frightened him. He did not change his answer.

Allowed to address the cited passage, Cecilia read its requirements of knowledge and willingness without rejecting an entire faith. The Temple representative acknowledged that a historical registration did not itself establish current consent, while maintaining its potential technical relevance. Both positions were recorded. The disagreement survived that single admission.

During recess, Adrian discovered he hadn’t read a line of his second page.

Passing him in the corridor, Isabella asked whether he regretted losing today’s initial screening.

“A little. I hate having to come back.”

“So do I. But I can’t hurry it along for you today.”

She didn’t touch the place her badge had occupied. She merely turned over her waiting number. No other authority was hidden beneath it.

“Could you tell me whether the water here is always this cold?”

“On that subject, I have equally thorough qualifications.”

When they reconvened, the officer separated the disputed issues from practical arrangements. Adrian declined preliminary paired assessment and continued under ordinary review. The registration office would separately notify him of specific information requirements and available appointments. His refusal could not be entered as withdrawal of his objection or acknowledgment of a historical betrothal. Luna’s memorandum was accepted, not treated as comprehensive proof of safety.

When the clerk returned his copies, one sheet had stuck to another. Adrian counted them and discovered that the page recording ordinary review was missing. He went back and waited while the clerk found it beneath the blotting paper. Only then did he put everything away.

“Are you worried about proving what was said today?” the clerk asked.

“That, and getting home and remembering only the parts that made me angriest.”

The clerk glanced at him and smoothed a bent corner. Adrian read the arrangement again. It had brought him no immediate result. He still disliked it, but making the day look more like a victory was no reason to forget the price he had accepted. He checked the page number once more before closing his folder.

No ruling on the merits of the temporary registration restriction was issued that day.

The seven-day period still ended at noon on January 29. What followed required an authorized decision and formal notice. Nobody quietly extended it through an unresolved proposal or announced it had already ceased.

Adrian requested another reading of his statement’s final paragraph. Only then did he sign to confirm the record’s accuracy, not his agreement with the original measure.

As they left, an observer asked Cecilia whether the historical entry concerned the saint’s old betrothal. A guard told him not to block the doorway.

“The public quotation explains that it creates no present marriage,” Cecilia answered. “If you quote it, include that.”

The man nodded, still writing. Adrian couldn’t see his notes. An excited expression wasn’t proof of tomorrow’s rumor.

Outside, Viola remained in her chair. After hearing what had happened, she asked whether they wanted the side exit. Several people waited with questions at the front.

“The side,” Adrian said. “I want lunch first.”

She asked the duty staff to confirm the route, without converting his choice into compulsory escort. Elena went with him. Beyond the courtyard, she requested her mirror.

“I forgot.” He found it in his pocket.

“I noticed. Your collar really is crooked now.”

Before he could fix it, she asked if she might. At his nod, she pulled out the folded corner. Her fingers brushed the warmth of his neck and withdrew.

“I wanted you to say my name in there.”

He looked at her.

“But not like that.”

His palm was still slightly damp when he offered it. She took his hand anyway.

“Elena. I want to have lunch with you today. Somewhere other than the registration office.”

Her eyes brightened. She glanced across the street.

“There?”

“If they have a table.”

“If not, we’ll choose somewhere else. No need to starve until evening proving the first choice was correct.”

Only a table near the door remained, cold whenever it opened. She moved her chair half an inch to leave him clear of arriving customers. Once the soup came, neither discussed the hearing.

He told her how the travelogue’s captain had finally discovered he was aboard the wrong ship—and first congratulated the cook. Elena tapped her spoon against her bowl, laughing.

“A fairly persuasive reason to stay.”

She broke the bread and kept the crisp edge she preferred. Perhaps the best thing about today, Adrian thought, was that she didn’t need to give him everything she liked.

Someone outside called the news from a newly printed sheet. The door muffled the words. Elena looked up. Adrian shook his head and finished his hot soup first.
