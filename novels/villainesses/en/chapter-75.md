# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Three: The Knight Says She's Responsible for Me
### Chapter 75: A Crown Can't Testify for Anyone

As the lamplight moved across the iron edge, the whole outline finally appeared.

It was a small crown. Three short lines enclosed its central point, with the narrow horizontal stamp used for ordnance acceptance beneath it. The incisions were much deeper than the black paint applied afterward.

Viola didn't reach for it.

“A Royal Arsenal acceptance mark,” she said. “I saw shipping racks of the same pattern during training. But the year needs checking. Don't rely only on my recognition.”

Edith set the lamp securely and asked the craftsman not to move the long rack yet. First she sketched its location, orientation, and the patch where the paint had flaked. She also had the guard captain examine it independently and recorded the dismantled old crossbow parts still sitting in its cradles when it was discovered.

Standing in the outer passage, Adrian saw the crown, and a name that would have been all too easy to say flashed through his mind.

He kept the name to himself. He wouldn't let it leap past everyone along the route who had yet to be investigated.

“Does this establish that it once passed through the Royal Arsenal?” he asked.

“Or that someone is misusing the mark,” Edith said. “We establish authenticity first. Even if it's genuine, we still need to ask how it left the arsenal, whether it was sold as surplus, who collected it, and who received it after that.”

Viola nodded. “Losing the lawful trail of standard-issue equipment doesn't mean every officer who ever used it signed the orders that came afterward.”

As she spoke, her eyes stayed on the crown. Adrian knew this wasn't a dispassionate statement of common sense.

A shipping band of iron had already come loose beside the rack. The same crown mark was legible in its impressed surface, with a small code beside it: RW-6. The investigator asked the craftsman to indicate where the band had originally been secured, sketched the break, and numbered and sealed the small item. It wasn't anyone's personal trophy. Once removed, it would remain in the review team's custody.

The paper sleeve from the gatehouse was collected during the same round of cataloging. On its reverse, the same RW-6 appeared inside a folded edge. Below it, only a tiny remnant remained of a torn-away date field.

Not a complete transport record. Not a signature sufficient to make someone confess.

But for the first time, a piece of iron and a paper sleeve connected this decommissioned post to another set of accounts they could investigate.

Adrian explained to Edith the parts he recognized. The outer sleeve used Northroad Freight's delivery-acknowledgment dimensions, with a settlement field left at one side. Such sleeves usually held a delivery note and its receipt confirmation together. This one was empty. Its appearance alone couldn't establish what had originally been inside.

She recorded “usually” too, rather than deleting it to make the account look neater.

Watching the wax harden around the evidence bag's seal, Viola quietly asked, “What if they say the rack was sold long ago?”

“Ask them to produce the sale and delivery records.”

“And if they don't have any?”

“Then that's a gap to investigate. It isn't yet a charge for us to fill in on their behalf.”

A gust rose outside. Only a broken rope remained at the top of the old flagpole. It lashed the iron ring, over and over.

Viola must have heard that sound many times before. Adrian saw her look up, as though remembering a flag that had once flown there whole.

After checking the warehouse threshold, the craftsman called the guards over to help secure the rack's loosened feet. Edith turned to a fresh sheet and arranged to have the remaining objects checked for dangerous mechanisms. Each would be cataloged after the professionals had established it was safe. She didn't authorize Adrian to go inside and lift the entire rack in search of a larger answer.

He consequently acquired time to eat half a piece of bread in the outer passage.

Viola smelled the herbs in it and turned. He opened the paper wrapper, leaving half within reach for her to take herself.

“We still need supplies even when we aren't training.”

“You could simply say it's left over from lunch.”

“I'd hate it to hear that and lose morale.”

At last she smiled a little, took a small piece, and stood beside him eating slowly.

The crown was still in her eyes, like a stain that couldn't be rubbed away. When she finished, she gathered the crumbs into the paper rather than letting them mingle with the site.

“The first time I was issued a regulation sword, I barely slept all night,” she said. “Not because I was afraid of fighting. I was afraid I wouldn't get it clean enough the next morning, and the sergeant would take it back.”

“And afterward?”

“Afterward, I learned that the sergeant didn't care whether I could polish it enough to see my face. He cared whether I'd point it at someone I shouldn't.”

Adrian looked at her empty hip.

“That still applies today.”

She nodded. After a moment, she looked at his face again.

“You said just now that you liked the sunshine there.”

“Yes.”

“I was just thinking about it. Perhaps I don't always have to find somewhere that will make you feel safe. I could first ask where you'd like to go.”

He didn't rush to offer her some faraway destination.

“Next time, you could meet me at the bakery. I'd like to choose something sweet that doesn't require my teeth to prove their courage.”

She took that quite seriously. Only afterward did she catch on and look up. “The chestnuts weren't actually hard yesterday.”

“The tea had considerable strength of will.”

This time she almost laughed aloud, then immediately glanced at the investigator, busy cataloging. Without raising her head, Edith said they could talk quietly, provided they didn't seal the bread bag among the evidence.

Adrian silently moved the two paper bags farther apart.

That was when they heard the sound.

It came neither from upstairs nor from the departing footsteps of whoever had fled. Three uneven scrapes sounded through the repair room's little window, followed by a very low cough.

Viola put down her bread immediately.

She didn't kick the door open alone. She called Edith over and pointed out where the sound had come from. The guard captain promptly took men around to the other side, prepared for the possibility of someone armed inside. The craftsman moved the planks covering the window ledge, letting light into the low room.

A man huddled beside the wall, ash in his beard and both hands raised to shield his face.

“Don't close it,” he said. “Don't put the boards back.”

Edith crouched outside the window, gave her name and investigative authority, and told him she would have someone open the door.

“What's your name?”

“Martin. Martin Bell.”

“Are you hurt? Is anyone else in there?”

“My foot… They locked the door. I haven't seen anyone else.”

He extended his foot. His swollen ankle had already stretched the top of his boot tight. The attendant came to the window, looked, and asked him not to force himself to stand. The lock was outside. The craftsman went for his tools while the guards secured the passage.

At the clink of tools, the man suddenly grabbed the window ledge.

“Have you come for the ledgers?”

“We've come to inspect the warehouse. Right now, we're getting you out,” Edith replied.

“They said I'd taken wages.” He shook his head, his throat so dry he could barely make a sound. “I only watched the yard. I'd always watched the yard here. They hadn't paid me everything. I asked about it.”

His account came in fragments. Several times, he tangled the same sentence. The investigator didn't finish it for him and turn it into a complete deposition. She only asked whether he knew of any danger outside the door. He shook his head, but his eyes kept moving upward.

“The ledgers are upstairs. In the gray cabinet. What came in, what went out, it's all written there. Don't say I told you.”

Adrian looked up toward the end of the second-floor gallery.

It was the very cabinet they had seen on entering the yard. Thick spines showed behind its half-open door, the outermost bundles tied with cloth tape. They were too far away to read.

Viola asked the craftsman whether they could go up. He tested the exterior stairs and platform with a pole, then shook his head. The added section's load-bearing capacity was uncertain. It needed at least one additional support. The guard captain sent someone to the carriage for a long rope and forbade anyone to rush up first for the sake of gaining a few steps.

Hearing footsteps, Martin shrank back into the corner again.

“Don't let the man in gray come back.”

Edith didn't promise that nobody would ever hurt him again. She asked the guard by the window to stay where the man could always see that someone was there. Then she asked whether he would let the attendant examine his foot once the door was open.

After a long hesitation, he nodded once.

The guard beside the window handed his long-handled weapon to a companion, freeing his hands so the man inside wouldn't flinch every time he turned. He hadn't left his post. He simply made his movements slower.

Martin kept staring at the badge at his waist. At last he looked away, toward an overturned wooden bucket in the yard.

“My things are under the bucket,” he said.

The guard asked what they were.

“A brush. Two socks. They aren't theirs.”

Edith had another person check. Sure enough, there was a small oilcloth bundle. It contained no secret letter that could solve the case, only old socks, a clothes brush missing several tufts, and half a cake of hard soap. She recorded the result of the inspection, had the belongings kept separately, and told him he could check them once he was out.

Staring at the bundle, the man's eyes suddenly reddened.

“They used to call me sir, too,” he said softly. “When they wanted me to help receive deliveries.”

Viola's fingertips stopped on her bracer. Adrian didn't ask how that form of address had later disappeared. He watched Martin grip his collar and try to redo the button he had put through the wrong hole. His hands shook so much that three attempts all failed.

“You can take your time with that once you're out,” the attendant said through the window. “Nobody needs you standing neat and tidy right now.”

Martin finally released his collar. When the tools sounded outside the door, he still trembled, but he no longer retreated into the darkest corner.

Adrian crouched and handed the attendant his water flask. He didn't use that mouthful of water to buy more names or ask whether the man knew who wanted Viola dead.

Martin hadn't even worked out who the woman knight in front of him was.

“Did you see those racks being brought inside?” Edith asked while they waited for the tools.

The man glanced toward the warehouse. “I saw them. I received two loads. Early in the month, when it wasn't this cold. Which day… I'd have to think.”

“Do you know the people who delivered them?”

“I couldn't give you their names. Some I'd seen before, some I hadn't. The one who had me sign for them said I just needed to put my thumbprint where he pointed.”

“That can wait,” the attendant reminded them. “His foot first.”

Edith closed her little notebook. Those few sentences were preliminary statements awaiting verification. They couldn't drag the head of an arsenal straight to this window.

As Viola listened, her expression gradually darkened. The ledgers in the paper cabinet might preserve the dates of arrivals and departures. They might also reveal participation Martin was reluctant to admit. Either way, they were closer to the answers they truly needed than a statement that royal equipment was here.

She examined the outer wall for places to set a support, then stopped at the corner.

The drainage channel there led downhill. Its stone edge was slippery, and behind a narrow gap an old footpath ran out of the yard. She remembered it from before. But when revising the map, she had marked only the exits vehicles could use and hadn't properly confirmed whether a person could still get through here after the alterations.

She told the captain immediately. He sent someone to look and didn't let her pursue down it.

Adrian heard her say quietly, “I should have checked here earlier.”

He didn't rush to deny it for her. The route she had identified needed to be recorded, and whether it had affected the earlier escape also needed investigation.

At last, the craftsman's tool bit into the old lock with a dull sound.

Just then, a faint crackling came from somewhere upstairs. At first Adrian thought it was frozen timber under strain. Then he saw a thin gray wisp escape through a gap in the records-room window.

Edith immediately ordered everyone away from the exterior stairs. The captain called for water and blankets.

Whoever had fled through the back yard had left no explanation. Nor could that gray wisp tell them when or how the fire had begun.

At the shouts, Martin lunged toward the door. His injured foot couldn't bear his weight, and he fell back beside the wall.

Viola turned and ran toward the repair room. Her right hand went instinctively to her hip. Finding it empty, she immediately grasped the short pry bar the craftsman offered instead.

Adrian didn't follow her into the smoke. He retreated to the upwind corner of the yard and saw the boards beside the second-floor paper cabinet beginning to blacken.

The thick dispatch and receipt ledgers were still there.

And the man beneath the window was repeating his own name, over and over, as though being remembered could keep him from being left inside this room that no longer served any purpose.
