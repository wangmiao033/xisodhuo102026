# Chapter 276: Arrival Was Not the Same as Being Heard

On the morning of May 5, Viola didn't use the adjudicating body's front steps.

She paused at the ramp while the cloth beneath her left-arm support settled properly. Carrying his own document bag, Adrian waited until she said she could continue. A young military attendant recognized her and saluted. Her right hand moved by habit, then lowered.

“I'm here to testify today. Please attend to your own work first.”

Flushing, he guided the waiting delivery cart to its unloading place. She neither criticized his salute nor used it to recover lost command. Inside, she admitted she'd almost asked how many people were on his shift.

“At the break, you could ask whether he's tired,” Adrian said.

“That doesn't sound very knightly.”

“You can ask wearing knightly clothes.”

She laughed, her shoulders easing. Today's collar sat lower than ceremony prescribed to avoid her support strap. After much consideration, she'd decided a strip of cloth needn't prove her fitness to testify.

The military custodians' boxes waited inside. The order and receiving copy examined on March 14 lay separately. The original attached roll remained incomplete; five people in the shortened counterpart couldn't simply become the five women here. Newly delivered journey logs, transfer counterfoils, and receipt registers had been traced from that earlier carriage expense and unanswered medical inquiry. Each custodial route was explained independently.

Viola didn't begin by displaying her sword. She asked for the medical inquiry to be placed where people could see it.

Its answer field had been blank. They hadn't declared that the army therefore refused all care. A station record subsequently found through the same journey reference showed a convoy stopping for an injured person, with medicine and delay entered. It corresponded to this journey, without restoring the person's name or making one stop representative of all escorts.

“Include that too,” Viola said.

The retired military clerk recognized her. He'd previously argued that soldiers couldn't wait for everybody's debate. Now he attended under notice, with an opportunity to state his position and answer. His fingers visibly relaxed at the medical record.

“At least they weren't beasts indifferent to whether people lived.”

“It supports someone stopping to provide care,” Viola replied. “We must also examine whether the person receiving it could refuse to continue.”

The room quieted. A stop for medicine mattered. It remained a real act of care, even if the next page asked an uncomfortable question about where the same wagon had gone afterward. Viola waited while the custodian brought that page forward.

The order required timely collection despite requests for review. Objections would follow the person without awaiting independent response. This journey's transfer counterfoil carried the receiver's acknowledgment and corresponding vehicle completion entry. A separate intake register assigned accompanying objections to the next shift's reviewing desk. The verifier compared the actual entries through common time references, establishing sequence without inventing precise minutes from worn marks.

Transfer had finished before the appeal reached anyone authorized to answer.

This was more than an issued order. In at least this journey, its prescribed sequence had occurred. The person's identity, subsequent fate, and each implementer's knowledge remained limited by missing pages.

The retired clerk asked to show his old traveling satchel.

The chair confirmed it was his ordinary possession, not case evidence, before permitting the illustration. Undoing its straps, he exposed two compartments: the larger for convoy rolls, the smaller for requests received en route. Against jolting, the small pouch was tied shut. At a station, its contents went together to the clerk.

“That's how I collected them. I delivered them wherever the cart arrived. I didn't deliberately throw them away.”

Viola didn't accuse him of forgetting his conscience. She asked him to put an irrelevant blank sheet inside and tie it as usual.

He did, with a practiced knot.

“If the paper said, I don't wish to be taken to the destination, who would see that before arrival?”

His hand remained at the pouch.

“Unless separately instructed, opening it on the way wasn't my responsibility.”

“It arrived safely. The person arrived too. But the sentence had no opportunity to change where the person was going.”

He began to object, then asked whether every soldier should decide independently to disobey.

She wouldn't accept only those two extremes. The satchel didn't convict every soldier; careful custody didn't establish that an objection had been heard. Who prescribed transfer before consideration, who could change it, and who received actual opposition required examination of specific material. His bag illustrated a practice. It couldn't independently prove every occurrence, any more than the fact that an appeal had been carefully folded could demonstrate that somebody with authority had considered it in time.

The verifier separated demonstration from firsthand testimony, asking which details he had experienced and which were familiar custom. The clerk sat, paler now, but didn't take back the blank sheet. He loosened the knot, staring as though something he'd tied all his life needed explaining for the first time.

A driver in the audience asked whether a vehicle number on the counterfoil meant investigators would next pursue everyone who'd driven it. His father had worked for the army. Now he could no longer clearly finish a story.

Viola asked him not to announce his father's address. The chair explained that documented journeys and individual responsibility still required specific connections. A vehicle number couldn't convict every driver. Relevant information could go through protected questioning, without publicly pressing a frail man to complete an answer.

“But you've already put it out here,” the driver said.

Viola didn't respond by asking to withdraw every established fact. Publicity brought genuine fears, she acknowledged. The archivist should include inquiry limits and existing channels against harassment. These records weren't permission to hunt unrelated relatives. Still dissatisfied, the man wasn't called an obstacle to truth.

After he sat, she studied the dispatch column. Once, she'd believed that clear orders put people in the right places. Now she also had to hear how accurate statements could frighten others.

A military representative asked whether her November delay at the border demonstrated that soldiers could sometimes stop of their own accord.

The November 14 recall was authentic, she answered. She'd signed a delayed acknowledgment, sent four riders ahead, and escorted the final wagon of wounded. The later review didn't prove everyone possessed identical latitude or cancel her obligation to explain her actions. Being right once didn't entitle her to decide how long others could wait.

“Do you still seek to protect the army's honor?”

“I want protecting people not to require a soldier gambling all their honor every time.”

Her left shoulder tightened afterward. The support chafed; dull pain climbed along her back. Seeing the next questioner's raised hand, she almost nodded.

Adrian didn't call a halt for her. He moved the little clock she'd agreed to keep on the table into her sight, well short of her hand.

She checked it and requested a break.

The question was brief, its author protested. The chair first asked whether she could continue. She couldn't; her support needed adjustment. The question would wait. Brevity didn't carry it past a stop already spoken.

Leaving felt harder than taking the stand. Eyes followed with concern and impatience. An attendant gave the assistance she requested. Adrian carried the thin folder she permitted him to take and followed into the rest room.

Only after the curtain closed did she exhale fully.

“I almost said it was all right.”

“Fortunately, you said something else.”

“I don't like everyone looking at my injury.”

He set down the folder while the attendant adjusted her support and explained the day's remaining activity limits. Viola wanted to return briefly to finish, without extending testimony into the afternoon by determination alone. She confirmed that arrangement with the chair herself. Adrian chose neither departure nor persistence for her.

The postponed question was still waiting when she returned. Without an alternative, could the urgent reasons for transfer support necessity? She requested full submission of the order's cited genuine hazards, not their removal. Necessity still required examination against available choices and actual deadlines. A soldier couldn't establish everything by repeating that nobody had time.

She read back the limits of her firsthand account and ended her testimony.

The retired clerk waited outside. Could a different order have changed things then? It might have changed the conditions of execution, she replied, without guaranteeing every consequence. He nodded. He could supply another receipt index and would let custodians investigate, rather than present memory as newly discovered evidence.

What she accepted was a submission route for his index request, not an ending in which everybody was forgiven.

Before the afternoon rest, Adrian recalled their agreement to find an afternoon for tea without discussing routes.

“Nowhere far today,” she said at once.

“The little courtyard. Its supervisor says the empty table is available for a quarter hour.”

Her face gradually reddened. “You asked already?”

“I asked whether there was a table. Now I'm asking you. I didn't agree for you.”

“Then yes.”

Weak leaves in ordinary hot water wouldn't earn a dedicated chapter in a travel book. Viola held the cup by its handle with her right hand; a steady shallow tray supported it below. She'd intended to choose somewhere with street musicians. But she still found herself checking which door would be easiest to leave through.

“I may not manage to stop thinking about routes entirely.”

“You may think about them. They needn't become an assignment I must complete on schedule.”

She laughed, looking at his little cakes. Their circles differed, one edge burned. She selected that one. Did a knight feel obliged to handle the more dangerous food?

“The other resembles a circle you drew.”

“You've heard too?”

“No need. I've seen your route maps.”

She laughed first, entirely forgetting to look dependably composed. Unable to defend himself, Adrian ate his crooked circle. They discussed an old training-ground acquaintance's overfed dog and the dress she'd like when her injury allowed. They set no recovery date and didn't turn pleasant company into another marriage contract.

At the agreed end, she put down her cup herself. Nobody had arrived to hurry her. That didn't mean quietly stretching what her body had allowed.

In the outer passage, Luna's assistant carried the afternoon's document box past them: original records from two nonhuman trials and transfer papers for unpowered preserved items. The military order showed how people had been delivered. Next, people would see how far the proposed alternative had actually traveled.

Viola didn't follow inside. She asked the assistant to tell Luna she'd read the public material later. She wouldn't remain today merely to make the five women complete.

Adrian accompanied her to the arranged resting place, heard her own goodbye, and returned to the hall. The retired clerk had taken his ordinary satchel away. The transfer counterfoil remained in the case.

Someone had carefully preserved an objection. Someone had still been delivered somewhere they didn't want to go. Publicly placed on one table at last, those facts could no longer use arrival to testify for each other.
