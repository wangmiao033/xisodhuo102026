# Chapter 142: First, Give Them Somewhere to Speak

Isabella wanted a table large enough.

Adrian first asked who would be willing to sit at it.

Lunch had been cleared on the afternoon of January 16. The reconsideration deadline lay before her. Three days sounded generous until every question required a custodian's confirmation or a witness's own answer. Her urgency could not compress those things into a sheet of paper.

“I want them to explain before everyone. The transfers, the disappearances, the altered summaries, today's decision.”

“Who is everyone?”

She looked up. “Should I keep being examined in little rooms?”

“No. But if we begin with everyone, we may end with only the people who know how to issue invitations.”

Elena did not soften his words. Viola had gone to her room after completing her route notes. Luna had not returned; Cecilia had her own work. Three people could not decide for five women, five places, or an entire missing-person list.

Isabella put down her pen.

“Then propose something.”

Instead of rising to make a speech, Adrian took a blank sheet and wrote the smallest question he could: how could people concerned examine and answer differences between public summaries and original records?

“Start here.”

“What about my succession eligibility?”

“Challenge that explicitly in a separate application. If every family must prove they support you before discussing their missing relative, this table will still belong to the throne.”

Her jaw tightened. She had lost that position only this morning. Now he was asking her not to make regaining it everyone's first concern.

Elena asked which institution they intended to hold the hearing.

Adrian looked at her.

“You said public as though you were opening a window. You should ask whose courtyard is outside.”

He looked down at his one-line proposal and laughed.

“All right. I don't have a table yet.”

“I noticed.”

They first went to an ordinary application window, not the noble assembly.

Isabella wore no surrendered dispatch token. The clerk gave her a queue number under the usual procedure. Two applicants preceded her: one correcting an address, the other asking about festival traffic.

The princess sat on the bench and listened to someone finish a matter unrelated to her.

The man asking about roads carried a clinic delivery slip. He feared reaching the bridge on the seventeenth only to turn back. The clerk showed him that morning's new coordinated reply. The old guard would remain until replacements actually arrived. Vehicles would be guided in batches along a confirmed level route. The clinic had checked its transport times. Notices were being replaced.

Recognizing the problem they had raised earlier, Adrian requested the public portion. Military and civilian offices had each recorded confirmation. The previous misunderstanding had not been erased.

The man asked whether he needed to queue somewhere else.

“This combines the instructions.”

At last, he relaxed and put away his slip.

Isabella watched him leave.

“I lost the power to issue requests this morning. That was completed anyway.”

“It had reached the people responsible,” Adrian said.

She slowly unclenched her hands.

When their turn came, the clerk did not promise a hearing. Public deliberation required assembly procedure and participation by relevant offices. This window could register and forward applications and preservation requests. It could not fix a meeting date. An eligible assembly seat would have to support placing a matter on the formal agenda.

“Her status isn't sufficient?” Elena asked.

“Under the suspension, she can't invoke her former coordination authority to put it there herself. She may seek reconsideration. Another qualified sponsor may bring an independent matter.”

Isabella looked at her queue number without crumpling it.

They took the form to a side table. Adrian replaced convene immediately with accept this application and explain the conditions for public consideration. Isabella disliked the slow words. She did not ask the clerk to turn them into an order she no longer had the power to issue.

As they prepared to leave, a plainly dressed woman stopped them.

She recognized Isabella. Holding a copy of the missing-person notice tightly, she would not give her name. She wanted to know whether people who supplied leads would be summoned to testify for the royal family.

“Who told you that?” Isabella asked.

The woman retreated half a step.

Adrian signaled for a moment and moved clear of the doorway.

“You needn't tell us who. What do you want confirmed?”

“I want someone found. I don't want to stand on a noble's stage.”

Isabella slowly withdrew her first question.

“Registering an inquiry isn't agreement to appear. If someone demands that, ask the receiving office to record it. You may ask the purpose before deciding.”

“If I don't appear, will you still look?”

“Yes.”

The woman kept watching, apparently waiting for the condition.

Isabella neither took her paper nor invited her to the palace for a private discussion. She asked the clerk to explain the existing inquiry and subsequent contact arrangements, and listened. Having answered once did not entitle her to hurry the woman into belief.

The woman left her question with the office without naming herself to the three visitors. As she left, she gave Isabella a very small nod.

Adrian saw the princess's expression change.

“She doesn't want to hear me prove myself.”

“The person she wants found matters more to her.”

“I know.” Isabella paused. “I only really knew it just now.”

In the carriage, Elena laid the draft across her knees and asked whether they planned to use the woman's words in their public presentation.

Isabella began to say they illustrated the problem well. Then she stopped.

“We didn't ask her.”

“Then describe the procedure we need to change. Don't identify her or borrow her fear to give us weight,” Elena said.

Adrian distinguished voluntary attendance, written submissions, and restricted identity checks. People could offer one part of a factual account without endorsing a political position. Authorized neutral custodians could verify material unsuitable for publicity. Listeners would hear the scope and grounds of that verification, not receive private files.

Isabella had another objection.

“Our opponents will say we're hiding evidence.”

“Let them identify what requires checking, and let the chair determine an appropriate scope,” Elena said. “We can't tear down everyone's front door because we're afraid of an accusation.”

Back in the residence's sitting room, they did not send the application immediately.

Adrian requested contact with people able to explain each source, first asking whether they would be named as prospective invitees. Hartwell replied through the ordinary channel: he could explain custody procedures, but attendance needed his superior's arrangement. The fiscal office would receive specific questions, not pronounce final judgment on the hundred and twenty coins before verification ended. The military office offered only a written reply excluding unrelated deployments.

None amounted to wholehearted support for Isabella.

She read each and kept them in the proposal.

Toward evening, Cecilia sent her observations from her office. The festival date could be explained publicly; private relief material required separate limits. She would answer for the liturgy she had checked, not admit or deny unknown matters for every Temple employee.

After her medical appointment, Luna reviewed the diagram account in the next room. Restrictions on her right hand remained. She would not endure prolonged uninterrupted questioning. Instead, she proposed written questions in advance and provision for breaks and stopping. Isabella did not summon her into the sitting room to demonstrate sincerity.

Walking between the rooms, Adrian discovered that a meeting allowing people to refuse really was less tidy to prepare than a summons.

Before dinner, the draft acquired a workable structure.

First verify the material, then question people separately. Those whose responsibility was discussed would have a chance to answer. Objections and corrections would also be recorded. Necessary protection could address immediate safety without waiting for the entire debate. Public sessions would use approved copies; originals remained with their custodians.

Isabella insisted on allowing affected people to ask why the disappearance leads had gone unpursued for years.

Adrian agreed, then asked her to include the royal offices among those who might answer.

She watched her pen and added them herself.

“I might not win a hearing like this.”

“Yes.”

“You could at least pause before answering.”

Elena smiled. “You might think we were choosing something pleasant to say.”

Isabella laughed too, then reread the page.

The application did not declare her representative of five women. Five column symbols did not testify for people whose connection remained unestablished. Her eligibility appeal was separate. The proposed factual hearing explained the links without making restoration of her status a condition for any examination to begin.

Adrian signed as drafter and as a person able to describe specified firsthand observations. Once submitted, his own words would face public questions. He would no longer merely stand behind others pointing out discrepancies.

Elena saw his hesitation.

“Afraid?”

“A little.”

“You can still remove your name.”

He reread his defined scope, shook his head, and finished the signature.

She did not call him brave. She placed blotting paper beside him and waited for the ink to dry.

That evening, the three returned to the receiving window with their draft. The night clerk registered only the preliminary application and preservation request, supplying a reference number and a notice of missing requirements. A formal hearing had not been approved. No date was set. The necessary sponsoring seat remained unfilled.

Isabella took the acknowledgment, more tired than when she received her suspension, but more certain of what it said.

“Next, someone willing to put this on the agenda.”

“Someone willing to hear both sides,” Adrian corrected.

“I heard you.”

On the way back to the residence, Elena did not immediately discuss that seat. She asked where they had stopped in the travel book.

“The captain got home and discovered he'd been given the wrong door key.”

“You were so sleepy yesterday that you read the same passage twice.”

“Tonight, I can start at the right door.”

She moved closer until their shoulders touched and quietly agreed.

Only in the entrance hall did she place an unaddressed sheet in her document folder. Adrian saw the Rosenberg crest and asked whom she intended to approach.

“Father and the lawyer first. Which seats can sponsor a matter, and who might hear a case that doesn't promise us victory?”

“You don't have to ask favors because I suggested the plan.”

“It isn't for you.” She looked at him. “I don't want that woman able to ask questions only from a doorway either.”

She closed the folder without claiming anyone's support.

Adrian asked what would happen if her family refused.

“I'll listen to why. They have people to protect too. Wanting something doesn't let me hand them its costs.”

“And then?”

“Then I'll decide what I can bear myself.” She held his gaze. “You needn't hurry to think that part through for me.”

He nodded slowly, withdrawing the hand he had reached toward the folder. Instead, he took the coat she had removed.

Someone upstairs called them to dinner. Elena put away the still-unaddressed sheet, then reached for his hand.

This time, no appointed seat awaited them.

There was only an opening someone would still have to win.
