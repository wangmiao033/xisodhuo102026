# Chapter 224: The Sentence She Didn't Finish

On March 23, Cecilia began reading an old oath in the meal service's back room. Halfway through, she stopped herself.

“Until my duties are fulfilled…”

The woman washing dishes by the window looked up. What counted as fulfilled? Finishing today's bowls, or waiting until nobody in the capital was hungry?

Cecilia examined the closing lines. She couldn't find an answer.

This wasn't a sinister contract newly discovered in a locked cabinet. The text had long accompanied ordinary service inductions. Most people understood it as a solemn expression; shifts were registered separately. But if they hoped to learn about voluntary commitments from it, previous kindness didn't erase the parts someone else could borrow.

“Let's leave that sentence unread for now.”

The deacon responsible for the rota frowned. “You're canceling the ceremony?”

“I don't have that authority. I'm studying a text, not issuing new orders here.”

She asked for the actual rota rather than substituting Adrian's notes for the people expected today. Her lost opportunity to lead the recitation hadn't returned merely because their inquiry had advanced.

Adrian sat on a stool so small his knees nearly reached the tabletop. After studying him twice, Cecilia asked whether he had selected the least comfortable seat to demonstrate equality.

“I didn't look properly when I came in.”

“Good. We needn't debate a new virtue first.”

She requested an ordinary chair and drew back the tablecloth on her side. Their laughter loosened the deacon's shoulders. It didn't answer the original question.

First they checked for a withdrawal provision. The oath had none, but the service instructions on its reverse described handover notification and replacement requests. Some newcomers heard only the front and never received the back. The deacon explained that experienced helpers instructed new ones. Anyone needing to leave simply had to say so.

The dishwashing woman dried her hands. “Last year I asked to change a day. You asked whether I'd lost faith in the Temple.”

His expression shifted. “I was asking whether you needed help.”

“I needed to patch my roof. It rained all day.”

She wasn't shouting. Adrian felt he needed to sit particularly still, resisting the temptation to turn her into evidence for Cecilia's elegant victory. She would continue working here, with this man, after the visitors left.

Cecilia asked permission to record the incident for their research. After consideration, the woman allowed an anonymous description: someone had understood a shift-change question as an examination of faith. She didn't demand a public confession. She wanted her next change of shift discussed without first defending what she believed.

The deacon promised to ask about the practical difficulty instead. She didn't immediately declare everything forgiven. She asked him to enter today's missing replacement in the rota first.

They resumed work until an argument sounded at the back door. A young helper wanted to leave. A server stopped him, insisting he'd agreed to assist with meal deliveries. He pointed to the time they had discussed: he had promised only until noon. His father needed him at their shop afterward.

Adrian stood, nearly saying, Help finish first. Steam under the pot lids, people at the queue's end, and unfilled meal boxes made the sentence terribly easy.

Cecilia reached the door ahead of him.

She asked each person what they remembered agreeing, without first deciding who was selfish. The rota said morning assistance. A finer hand had added until deliveries are complete, but nobody established when or by whom that extension had been communicated. The original registration sheet confirmed noon.

“We can't keep him through those later words,” Cecilia said.

“What happens to the boxes?” the server asked.

Cecilia looked at them. Saying freedom wouldn't make them disappear.

There was already an afternoon shift. One member had arrived early and was waiting in the yard for a key. The deacon went to ask whether she could begin packing now, without treating the request as an answer already given. The supervisor changed delivery routes: nearby meals needing heat went first; the distant route would run later, with its receiving point notified.

No extra hands appeared from nowhere. With the supervisor's agreement, Adrian accepted a count he could finish before leaving, not a promise to deliver across the city. Cecilia stayed to fill boxes. Her planned afternoon transcription moved back one session as a result.

Before putting on his coat, the young helper asked whether they no longer wanted him coming back.

The server said nothing. After a pause, the deacon told him to return for his next registered shift. Ending this one wouldn't be recorded as abandoning his post. Still uncertain, the young man examined his registration once more before leaving.

Cecilia put her unfinished research somewhere dry and washed her hands before taking the serving tongs.

“You're supposed to meet Luna today,” Adrian reminded her.

“I'll change the arrangement so she doesn't wait. Her working longer can't make up my time.”

He carried that precise message, without adding that Cecilia had sacrificed herself for everyone. She had chosen work she wanted to do. Others should also see what it displaced instead of concealing the cost inside praise.

When serving quieted after lunch, the deacon asked Cecilia to fix the oath. Since she understood it better, couldn't she leave a new version for everybody?

She shook her head.

“Today's questions don't represent every service. Certainly not a future experiment Luna may propose. The responsible staff can clarify this rota now. Formal changes to the oath have another procedure.”

“We can't leave everything blank.”

“We can stop using it to answer things people haven't agreed to.”

She asked that the morning arrangement and actual extended work be recorded separately, including her own additional time. He didn't write saintly duty beside her name. It wasn't only other people who needed better language.

Before leaving, they asked an elderly tailor willing to help to examine the service instructions. He read little and usually heard documents aloud. Cecilia asked him to explain in his own words what agreeing to come tomorrow morning would mean.

He would come, he said. If the shop needed him, he would send his granddaughter instead.

Cecilia didn't immediately congratulate him on understanding. The instructions required notice if he couldn't attend, after which the rota supervisor would seek a willing, suitable replacement. They didn't authorize him to give away someone else's morning.

Considering this, the tailor laughed. His granddaughter probably wouldn't obey anyway. He tried again, this time offering only his own time.

Adrian recorded a wording problem, not an old man's stupidity. Make other arrangements had seemed perfectly clear on first reading. Asking someone how revealed the different things they had heard.

Cecilia invited the tailor to identify the hardest sentence. He selected no long word, but extend as needed. Whose need? The kitchen needed him; so did his shop. He wanted to help, not discover whether he could go home only after those places finished arguing.

The deacon brought another sheet and wrote, in front of him, that this service period would not automatically extend. Additional invitations would be asked separately. He read it back. The tailor nodded without signing up.

“I'll check tomorrow's work first.”

“Of course,” Cecilia said.

After he left, Adrian wondered whether this counted as an unsuccessful check, since it hadn't obtained agreement.

“He knows what he needs to consider,” she said, looking at the unsigned page. “If we only wanted the sheet filled, there was no need to ask him to read it.”

They retained the page as language research. His identity wouldn't be passed to the model project without separate permission. They had no new volunteer, but one more sentence someone could understand.

Walking back to the academy, Adrian carried an ordinary meal box for her. He assumed it was something saved for Luna. Cecilia informed him it was her dinner.

“Do you think saints don't get hungry on the road?”

“I thought you'd eaten.”

“Lunch doesn't forbid looking forward to dinner. No conflict of faith there.”

He laughed and adjusted his grip. Then she asked whether he would sit with her in the small garden tonight. No contracts to discuss, no messages to carry. Just some time together.

Adrian hesitated. She noticed. She knew his relationship with Elena, she said. She knew that her earlier admission of affection hadn't received an identical answer. This invitation wasn't compensation demanded for a morning packing meals.

“I still want to ask. You can answer seriously without treating me as a case likely to suffer damage.”

He explained that he'd already arranged to see Elena tonight. He could sit by the academy entrance with Cecilia until the next bell, now. That wasn't a promise to reserve every evening afterward.

She looked disappointed, then nodded. “Now, then. Don't slow the bell to make me feel better.”

They sat on a bench beside the steps, reached along level ground, with people passing nearby. Cecilia opened the box. It contained a flatbread of almost unrecognizable shape. She had folded it badly while turning it. The taste had survived; the outline didn't deserve rescue.

Adrian asked for a bite. She handed him an ordinary small knife and let him cut a corner. Sharing food acquired no invisible betrothal. They talked about a new cat near the kitchen without promoting it into divine intent. When the bell rang, Cecilia closed the box herself.

“Leaving on time means you took that answer seriously.”

The evening discussion became a written review. Having received the accurate message, Luna kept her planned rest instead of adding a session to accommodate late arrivals.

Adrian copied three separate expressions from Cecilia's work into the model's appendix: permission to receive an explanation, permission for limited preparations, and permission for a specific operation. They were research language, not forms issued to any prospective risk-bearer.

Reading them, Isabella observed that three separate sheets could still be pressed upon someone by the same questioner. She had offered seemingly optional arrangements as a princess. She knew separate paper didn't necessarily make the air easier to breathe.

Viola asked who could notice a hesitation without immediately explaining it away on the researchers' behalf.

“Not only us,” Cecilia said.

She didn't point toward people receiving food. Accepting a meal today wasn't an application to witness their research. Nor should the young helper be recruited to prove reform correct because of an argument that morning.

Viola thought of ordinary works staff, retired personnel, and citizens able to decide for themselves. She could propose bounded witness invitations, making clear that witnesses wouldn't guarantee a model's safety or consent for someone else. Whether that arrangement could work depended on their questions too.

Adrian noted the next step without filling in a list of names. Before leaving, Cecilia adjusted the knot on her prayer cord. The old oath remained in its folder, not torn apart in anger.

“Will you still say it?” he asked.

“Some sentences. Before finishing today's sentence, I want to know whom it keeps, and for how long.”

She hadn't lost her faith. Wanting to preserve it hadn't turned the morning into an unimportant misunderstanding either.

A delivery cart sounded at the corridor's far end. The delayed route now had a receiving acknowledgment. The meals had arrived cooler than usual. The supervisor recorded that shortcoming and planned a different rota next time. Respecting one person's departure hadn't made every arrangement the best possible one.

Cecilia read the acknowledgment and released a quiet breath.

Tomorrow needed better work. For now she took the box to her own desk and ate the misshapen bread.
