# Chapter 127: No Magic Needed Today

At breakfast on January third, Luna Ashcroft solemnly announced that today she intended to earn her living with her eyes.

“I thought you generally used your mouth to eat,” Adrian said.

“This is why you shouldn't write research introductions.”

She picked up the thick-handled pen with her left hand and reviewed the examination's scope. Her right palm remained dressed and supported. Last night's medical review had not lifted the prohibition on casting or exerting that hand. A palace invitation did not entitle anyone to add an exception to the doctor's instructions.

Across the table, Elena was finding her a bookmark that would not roll away. Cecilia was at her office that morning. Viola was taking her planned rest. Nobody would damage an injured left arm again to stand guard over an ordinary lamp.

“You really aren't bringing the enormous magnifying glass?” Adrian asked.

Luna gave him a look. “You won that argument in the mountains. You needn't celebrate again in the capital.”

She packed only approved structural drawings, her thick-handled pen, and her glasses. C-17 remained in its tower cabinet. D-17's remains were not concealed at the bottom of her luggage. She was going to examine the royal archive's own empty contract wrapper, K-9.

At the door, Elena handed Adrian a packet of biscuits.

“Eat during the break. Don't wait until you see the clock.”

Luna held out her left hand.

“There are some for you,” Elena said, giving her the packet.

“I was going to say you're becoming our quartermaster.”

“Then finish your lunch before I become an inspector.”

They smiled at each other. Adrian took the document bag, judging silence his most valuable contribution for the moment.

In the palace conservation room, Beatrice Wren was adjusting an ordinary optical microscope.

She appeared to be around forty, with close-fitting cuffs. No crystals, circuit plates, or power boxes sat on the table. A shaded lamp and reflector directed oblique light across the work surface. The flame stood well away from the original, where it could not heat the paper.

“No separating layers, sampling, or solvents,” she said. “I move the support and adjust the focus. You observe from the permitted positions. Anything we cannot see today remains unseen.”

Luna regarded the microscope with a trace of regret.

“I generally request another angle.”

“You may request it. Whether I can provide it is a separate matter.”

“I think I'm going to like you.”

“Don't commit yourself yet. Many people change their minds after my third refusal.”

Adrian laughed. Luna looked at him without denying it.

Hartwell checked K-9's seals, object entry, and removal log. Beatrice transferred the entire fitted support to the table. The original stayed flat. Nobody caught an edge and lifted the lining.

Isabella arrived before observation began. She brought no gift box and asked for no promise of findings suitable for her council meeting. She simply stood outside the marked boundary.

Before focusing on the first area, Beatrice showed them an ordinary piece of lining paper used in teaching. Two almost identical lines changed under a different light angle. One was an impression; the other was merely a fine fiber lying on the surface.

Luna asked how long she had practiced that kind of judgment.

“In my first year I thought I'd mastered it. In my second I discovered I ought to redraw my first year's diagrams.”

“And after that?”

“I learned to put the uncertainty in the drawing.”

Luna turned over an old working sheet, leaving herself more room for new observations. She did not treat the teaching paper as a same-age, same-material reference for K-9. It simply reminded her not to let one viewing angle decide an entire shape.

Isabella asked why they had not first summoned an artificer capable of magical imaging.

“Because ordinary light can answer part of today's question,” Beatrice said. “Additional methods carry additional costs. Making an examination look more worthy of the palace isn't a good enough reason.”

The princess did not enjoy the answer, but she did not insist. Luna raised an eyebrow at Adrian, as though pleased not to be solely responsible for being the room's most difficult person.

He studied the teaching paper instead of certifying that conclusion.

Under magnification, the wrapper abruptly lost its mysterious appearance.

The fibers were uneven. Its gray-silver layer resembled worn frost. Tiny brush marks marked the adhesive beside the repair strip. Adrian had expected something grand in an ancient contract. What he saw was a corner someone had once repaired with care.

“What do you see?” Luna asked.

“A line that looks like a tear.”

“Good. Don't turn it into the end of the world yet.”

He moved aside. She sat in the chair already adjusted to the correct height, her right hand returned to its cushion. Her left touched only her own paper as she marked the observation position.

Beatrice changed the light's direction without increasing its brightness. Raised fibers beside the break cast short shadows, then cast them the other way. No writing moved by itself. Light was meeting an uneven surface.

Luna studied it for a long time.

“The thin layer breaks beneath the protective strip here. Could we examine the edge where the strip has already come away? Without lifting it farther.”

After checking the support, Beatrice allowed another viewing angle through the existing opening. No blade went beneath it. Nobody tried a fingertip against the material.

Luna asked Adrian to turn her diagram halfway around. She drew one crooked line with her left hand, then a worse one.

“Please don't judge my profession by this.”

“I'm trying to decide which line is the table edge.”

“That's the line you're not meant to comment on.”

Beatrice offered her own position sketch. Luna thanked her and marked the area she meant. Her left hand no longer had to fight a battle between precision and pride.

The later protective strip overlapped the broken layer. Several fibers lifted from the old break were trapped beneath its adhesive. The strip itself had not broken along the same line. This localized damage therefore predated the outer repair.

“It was dismantled?” Isabella asked.

“At this point, the lining has broken and locally separated from the layer beneath,” Beatrice said. “The relatively regular edge merits further examination. Today we cannot rule out earlier wear or damage during an old repair, much less identify someone who deliberately peeled it away.”

Luna changed Removed in her notes to Local break and delamination.

It was a small correction. Adrian remembered how much she had once enjoyed a beautiful, complete explanation.

The fourteenth leaf's repair note said the existing lining had been retained. It did not say the lining was intact. The composite summary had nevertheless turned a limited outer repair into a completed overhaul.

An ambiguity in the papers had finally met the physical object. The meeting still did not produce a verdict against whoever they chose.

The next area lay along older impressions near the wrapper's inner edge.

Two shallow parallel traces remained, one on the thin layer and one on neighboring backing paper. Beatrice recorded dimensions and direction. Consulting the existing structural drawings, Luna explained only that the arrangement was compatible with older two-layer recording structures.

“Compatible,” Isabella repeated.

“Not proof that it held two people's vows. Nor can we turn two lines into two occasions of use. Shifts, repairs, and impressions can produce similar edges. We have no legible names, no complete record, and we have not activated it.”

Adrian's left hand rested quietly on his knee. Nobody placed his scar under the microscope or asked him to supply missing information with his body because they had found parallel lines.

The princess looked at the marks drawn on paper.

“Then what have we actually gained today?”

Luna lifted her head from the eyepiece.

“We know not to describe it as structurally intact and cleared for use. We know the outer strip covers earlier localized damage. And we know there are old impressions worth preserving, rather than wiping away in cleaning.”

“That sounds like less than I need.”

“More than we actually knew yesterday,” Adrian said.

Isabella did not immediately argue.

At the agreed stopping time, Beatrice covered the lamp and returned the tray to its protected position. Luna was still studying one impression. Her left hand lifted the pen a little, then lowered it.

She did not request one tiny extra test.

Away from the table, Adrian noticed a red mark beside her right brow where she had leaned against the eyepiece. He handed her warm water without reaching to rub it.

“Your eyes earned their wages.”

“More reliable than writing left-handed.”

“Your questions were valuable, too.”

She looked down at her sketch before raising her eyes.

“You're rather practiced at compliments today.”

“I've had some practice recently.”

She guessed with whom. Her smile paused, then returned naturally.

“I'll accept that one for its stated purpose.”

He smiled as well. He did not compensate for her brief disappointment with a promise he could not honestly make.

At midday she wrote the report in the outer room. Beatrice checked each physical observation while Hartwell documented the return and resealing. Luna placed her interpretation of the historical structure in a separate section, rather than making technical inference sound like something the conservator had directly observed.

Near the end, she realized that her right hand had cast nothing and no ancient record had awakened for her. Yet she had still produced something the next reader could use.

She had expected such a result to feel disappointingly small.

Instead, the satisfaction was slow and solid.

The biscuit packet lay beside her. She checked the clock and took one before Adrian could speak. He swallowed his prepared reminder. With considerable satisfaction, she invited him to eat as well.

Isabella received the combined consultation report that afternoon. W-24 had been assembled from records of different dates. The summary's renewed-use conclusion lacked support in the approvals presently available. Temple routing, military forwarding, and physical repair each established a separate, limited part of the history. Originals remained in royal custody; future citations had to retain sources and qualifications.

She did not demand that the uncertainties be removed. Beside them, she listed the material still to be requested.

Then a messenger arrived from the council secretariat.

The new notice had a reference number, a delivery acknowledgment, and an identified issuing office. Isabella sent her attendant to the secretariat's duty desk with the duplicate to verify it. A familiar emblem on the seal did not make it automatically trustworthy.

While they waited, she read the text twice.

The preliminary succession council had been moved from January twelfth to January sixth.

The stated reason was the consolidation of fiscal and royal-affairs business. Candidates could submit sourced summaries and applications for witnesses or advisers with a defined remit. A detailed agenda would follow separately.

This was not a coronation. It would not decide that day who took the throne.

But nine remaining days of preparation had suddenly become three.

The attendant returned with the matching duty-register reference and confirmation. The notice genuinely came from the secretariat. It was not a private note exploiting the princess's name to hurry someone into danger.

Isabella laid it flat on the table. For once, she did not immediately announce the next step.

Adrian thought of the covered lamp and Luna lowering her pen. A shortened deadline would not turn the unseen parts into answers.

“I need you to help me explain this clearly,” the princess said at last.

“We can each explain what we've actually established. Specific attendance and material will depend on the agenda and the applications.”

She looked at the report, which nobody had polished into a victory, and slowly nodded.

Luna put her pen in its case. Her hand did not glow. It merely closed the lid gently.

Tomorrow's garden remained where it had always been.

But from today, they could no longer afford to let a handsome summary finish speaking on behalf of what nobody knew.
