# Chapter 93: This Time, the Witch Has to Wait for an Answer

Luna turned the proposal toward herself. Her pen hovered beneath the words *additional procedures as necessary*.

“Usually that means changing a filter, extending the count, or increasing the power if the response is too weak,” she said. “It doesn't mean turning you into a frog. The kitchen has already rejected the frog-keeping budget.”

Adrian didn't let the joke carry the question away.

“Who decides what's necessary?”

“The operator. Me.”

“And if I say stop, but you think another moment would be valuable?”

Luna looked up. Outside, a worker hauled a crate of potatoes onto the steps. His labored breathing reached them through the window. The afternoon of December 9 hadn't stopped simply because the room had fallen quiet.

“I would stop.”

“I believe that's what you intend. But the paper doesn't say it. It only says you can decide what to add.”

He pulled his glove snug and pushed the proposal back to the middle of the table.

“I'm not taking part in an experiment on those terms.”

Luna's pen tapped the ink bottle. She steadied it without immediately arguing.

Elena straightened. Viola lifted the hand that had been holding down the diagram, leaving the paper entirely within Luna's reach. Nobody hurried Adrian to find a more agreeable reason for refusing.

“Do you mean this proposal,” Luna asked, “or have you decided against an examination altogether?”

“This proposal. I still want to know why the scar behaves strangely. I understand you can't predict every result before you start. But wanting an answer doesn't mean I've agreed to every step that might come after it.”

She studied her arrows.

“If we hold a conference every time I need another filter, the opportunity may pass.”

“Then we let it pass.”

It was harder to say than he'd expected. How often had he imagined that one sufficiently clear clue would lead him out of the story someone kept rewriting? He hated missed opportunities as much as anyone at the table. That only made it more important not to hand his urgency to somebody else and let it become a reason to choose for him.

Luna watched him for a moment, then laid her pen down.

“I don't like that answer.”

“Neither do I. It's still my answer.”

Cecilia moved a cup of cold water away from the edge of the paper.

“The three examples you gave aren't the same thing,” she said. “Does changing the filter change where the light goes? Does a longer count mean more exposure, or simply watching afterward? Could more power make discomfort arrive faster?”

Luna took a fresh sheet and sketched a lamp, a shutter, and a receiving plate. She stopped midway through the second line.

“Exposure time and reading time need to be separate. I took a shortcut there.”

“For whose convenience?” Elena asked.

Luna glanced at her. “Mine.”

The tension didn't disappear. But it had become something they could point to on a page and discuss.

Elena asked whether Luna could guarantee the experiment wouldn't hurt him. Luna shook her head at once.

“No. Even ordinary light can irritate damaged skin. I can explain known risks, limit the energy, stop, and arrange care. A promise of no possible harm would make you feel better. It would also mean I shouldn't be doing the work.”

Elena raised her chin, evidently displeased to have her worry taken apart like a faulty instrument. After a moment, however, she crossed *absolutely safe* out of her own notes.

“Then don't use the words low intensity as a substitute for an explanation.”

“It's very difficult to be charming when you keep making reasonable demands.”

“You can save the charm for occasions that don't involve shining lamps at people.”

Adrian finally smiled. “Better for everyone's eyes.”

Luna gave him a wounded look, then brought a wooden box from beside the cabinet. First she explained that there was no energy cell inside. She opened the lid so they could see the empty socket. The box contained a spring, a shutter, and two handles.

“This cuts off the beam. I'm not examining you. I want to show you a practical problem.”

She waited for his nod before using the ordinary light from the window. A narrow strip of brightness fell across a sheet of white paper. A red handle stood by her right hand; the second handle faced the opposite chair.

“Pull yours and the shutter closes. Mine does the same.”

Adrian sat in the other chair. He could reach the handle with his right hand without extending his left. But when he pulled, the whole box slid half an inch before the shutter fell.

He looked at her.

Luna had seen it too. Instead of saying she'd be more careful during the real experiment, she pulled the box back and inspected the base.

“The mounting clamp isn't fitted. I've always held this demonstration model down myself.”

“So you thought it was easy to stop.”

“And you had to catch it first.”

She called for the administrator to bring a clamp. The apparatus stayed on the table; discovering the problem wasn't treated as a reason to put it away. When the clamp arrived, Viola checked the table edge and asked the administrator to try the release twice in ordinary light. She kept her injured left arm still, testing the fastening with her right hand.

“It will need checking again before use,” she said. “Different people, different positions. Today's demonstration won't cover that.”

Luna regarded her. “Do you inspect the furniture on dates too?”

Viola's ears went pink. “Only bridges.”

“That inspection was necessary,” Cecilia said earnestly.

Elena turned toward the window. Adrian heard the laugh she couldn't quite hide, and his own shoulders finally loosened.

When the laughter faded, the questions remained.

They began with the things easiest to overlook. If Adrian changed his mind tomorrow, could they still stay at the guesthouse? Would the consultation on their drawings continue? Who would pay the physician who had already been asked to visit?

Luna's first answer was that of course she wouldn't evict someone for declining. Elena spread the ordinary visitor price list beside the proposal. It covered rooms, not examinations or use of the equipment.

“We can't settle the bill with of course.”

Rubbing her forehead, Luna went to consult the administrator. The plant by the window trembled in the cold air. Adrian narrowed the opening. From the stairs came a discussion of medical bags and the carriage needed for a house call. He began to understand why refusing something could be more complicated than saying no. You also needed to know that, afterward, you could still sit in the same chair.

While Luna was gone, Cecilia opened an earlier set of notes to a line about dizziness, added after the event. That day, the recorder had been occupied with numbering the equipment. It wasn't until dinner that anyone had asked why she looked pale.

“This time, I'd like the uncomfortable person to speak first,” she said quietly. “Even if everybody is looking at a very beautiful light.”

Viola moved a footstool out of the way, leaving room to stand. “And nobody blocks the exit. Nobody has to prove they're brave.”

Looking at them, Adrian felt a prick of guilt. “You've given up another afternoon for me.”

“I was going to unravel this,” Cecilia said, holding up her knitting bag. “It still hasn't decided whether it's a kettle strap or a fishing net. At least your problem is reasonably clear.”

“My letter about the follow-up examination is posted,” Viola said. “And sitting here gives my arm a rest.”

Elena tapped her notes. “I'm learning how to refuse a beautifully written bad clause. That will be useful in procurement contracts. You don't have to count every choice we make as another debt you owe.”

She wasn't smiling at that last part. He held her gaze, then nodded. Instead of another general apology or thank-you, he asked a specific question.

“What if I decide later that I don't want the record used anymore?”

Cecilia didn't answer for Luna. They wrote it down and waited. Below, the administrator called that the afternoon mailbag was about to be sealed. Luna told him to hold it, then changed her mind a moment later and said to send it on schedule.

When she returned, she explained.

“I was going to send the academy an equipment scheduling application. We haven't settled anything here. Other people's calendars shouldn't make the decision first.”

Elena glanced at her empty hand. Luna tucked the unsent application into her notebook rather than setting it before Adrian as a reason to sign quickly.

“As for records…” She read the new question. “What stops being used after withdrawal, and what must be retained if there's a safety incident, should be clear beforehand. I'll check the rules tonight. Tomorrow you won't have to rely on my memory.”

Luna then spread out the ordinary written quotation she had brought back. Their accommodation would continue unchanged, and the drawing consultation would proceed within its previously agreed scope. The physician's visit was a separate service, with its cost stated in advance. Experimental materials would be charged separately; there would be no discount conditional on agreeing to every examination. Adrian could consult the physician alone, or do nothing at all.

“The kitchen won't demand its egg back either,” Luna added.

“The one I ate?”

“We don't possess that technology. I'm profoundly grateful.”

Adrian read the quotation and slipped it into his notebook. That wasn't agreement to an experiment. He agreed only to hear the physician's advice in the morning and asked Luna to write a first step that could end without obliging him to take a second.

“You need to ask before an ordinary visual examination too,” he said. “Coming to the tower doesn't make my hand an exhibit.”

Luna looked at his glove, then away.

“Noted.”

This time, she actually wrote it down.

At dusk, Elena walked with him across the little courtyard between the tower and the guesthouse. The worker had finished moving the potatoes and was sitting by the door peeling a boiled chestnut. The lift still bore its out-of-service sign. Nobody had removed it simply because evening was coming.

“Did you want to refuse for me?” Adrian asked.

“Yes.” Her answer was immediate. “Especially when she said the opportunity might pass.”

“You didn't.”

“You were speaking. And if I'd cut in, all anyone would remember was a duke's daughter forbidding an examination of her footman.”

She turned her face toward him.

“I did very much want to burn that paper.”

“Then she'd have had to write it again tomorrow.”

“She has to write it again anyway.”

He laughed and offered his right hand. “Look at it that way, and you've saved the tower a sheet of paper.”

Elena considered his hand before putting hers in it. She held him lightly, avoiding strain on the tender new skin. They walked slowly. At the porch, neither immediately let go simply because they had reached a place where they could.

“I am a little afraid,” Adrian admitted. “That I'll say no and never get another chance like this.”

She didn't promise to find every answer for him.

“Then I'll help you remember why you said no today. When you get impatient, you can come back and look.”

She raised her eyes, her voice softer.

“And I'd like you to remember that I'm waiting for you tonight. Not for an examination result.”

He gently settled her hand more securely in his. “I'll be there. With the captain who's already lost three compasses.”

“If he loses a fourth, we're choosing another book.”

After supper, he took his promised seat beside her. The fire wasn't too hot. On the page, the captain finally asked for directions, only to be directed to a shop selling compasses. Elena had to pause for so long that she couldn't get through the next sentence.

Then someone knocked.

Luna stood outside holding a rewritten description of the first procedure. She had brought neither a lamp nor the little pulse-counting wheel.

“I was going to say I guarantee this is the least romantic invitation you've ever received,” she told him. “Then I remembered guarantees haven't been popular today.”

Adrian accepted the pages. “What's the invitation?”

“To read it together tomorrow morning. You can agree to read without agreeing to do.”

Elena half closed the book and looked at the first page. There were now two signature spaces at the bottom: one for the participant, one for the researcher. Luna's responsibilities, including her obligation to stop, had a place of their own.

“You have to sign too this time?” Elena asked.

“If we reach an agreement.”

Adrian didn't pick up a pen. He set the pages where there would be good light to read them in the morning.

Luna watched him do it and, unexpectedly, didn't hurry him. Looking down, she discovered she was still holding a spare pen. With a small smile, she put it back in her sleeve pocket.

For the first time, the witch went quietly downstairs without the signature she had come for.
