# Chapter 130: One Receipt Cannot Make Two Journeys

There were no gilt-framed portraits in the fiscal reading room.

Only a clock, ticking loudly. Adrian suspected it existed to remind anyone hoping to linger past lunchtime that clerks also needed to eat.

On January 7, Hester Blythe placed six certified copies before him and turned the permission sheet so he could read it.

“This covers the allocation approved for examination yesterday. Other taxpayers' names, the complete collection registers, and unrelated payments are outside its scope.”

“We weren't planning to copy the national accounts.”

“The last person who said that immediately asked whether he could see half of them.”

She was about forty, with worn gray sleeve protectors and spectacles hanging from a cord. She distributed blank paper to Adrian and Elena, then sat opposite them. Nothing in her manner suggested that royal figures were sacred.

“Twelve hundred received. Three hundred sixty for storage, two hundred forty for transport and escort, one hundred eighty for conservation. Four hundred twenty remaining. The sum is correct. If you want an addition error, this will be a short day.”

Elena glanced at Adrian. “At least you won't demonstrate bad arithmetic to Her Highness.”

“I adjudicated water service yesterday. I'd rather not expand my jurisdiction.”

Blythe looked up, wondering whether they were serious. Elena offered no explanation and copied the dates and footnotes.

This was not W-24's four-year-old receipt account. It concerned capital expenditure on old records during the November just past. Its use to support a larger funding request remained a separate question. Two entries mentioning an old project did not turn years of expenses into one transaction.

The first apparent duplication was almost too obvious.

Storage showed two payments of one hundred eighty silver coins: the same warehouse, the same recipient, the same registration date. Adrian was about to circle them when Blythe directed him to the back of an attachment.

One covered October, the other November. October's acceptance paperwork had arrived late, delaying settlement. The receipt numbers differed, and payment dates matched the recipient's independently retained counterparts.

“So the three hundred sixty doesn't pay twice for November.”

“It pays for two months. Criticize an unclear summary if you like, but don't evict October from the calendar.”

Elena moved that entry into the explained section of her notes. “This belongs in the supplementary report too.”

Blythe's expression softened. Evidently, she had met enough people who took away errors and left their explanations behind.

The hundred eighty for conservation also reconciled. Six ordinary labor and material charges added up correctly and stayed within the allocation. Adrian had half expected a familiar name to emerge. Instead, he found a smith paid to replace hinges on old document chests.

Chests still needed hinges, even while people argued about the throne over the papers inside them.

By the time they reached transport, the clock had struck eleven.

The summary described two hundred forty as transport and escort settled. The particulars showed two entries of one hundred twenty, registered on November 14 and November 28. Both cited receipt RC-61.

“Could it have been apportioned?” Adrian asked.

“It could.” Blythe placed the payment entries together. “Which is why the receipt number alone won't answer it.”

Elena stopped his hand before he added the figures.

“This is expenditure. What is that column?”

Allocated, awaiting collection, settled: separate headings ran across the page. If a payment had merely moved from pending to settled, adding the two columns would create money on their own paper.

Adrian realigned the dates and entries. Both amounts sat under settled, and both counted toward monthly expenditure. They were neither opposing entries nor a balance carried onto the next page.

“Different payment references,” he said. “P-81 and P-96.”

Blythe nodded but did not immediately label them duplicate payments.

From the custody cabinet, she retrieved a settlement counterpart covered by the same permission. The fiscal office had received it from the carrier at month-end; it was not another summary copied by the same clerk. RC-61 recorded one ordinary transport of sealed documents on November 12, costing one hundred twenty silver coins including escort. It was marked paid, with no outstanding balance.

Adrian looked back at escort in the summary.

Something already included had acquired a separate, reassuring label.

“What if the other hundred twenty covered a second journey?”

“Then there should be another route record, delivery acknowledgment, or independent basis for the charge,” Blythe said. “None is attached.”

“It might have been filed elsewhere,” Elena pointed out.

“Yes. But the summary can't omit it while claiming all its supporting vouchers were verified.”

For the first time, anger entered Blythe's voice. Not because they had questioned her office, but because someone would eventually have to explain the blank space behind that tidy conclusion.

A junior employee knocked to ask how many lunches the reading room needed. Blythe glanced at the clock and proposed fetching the correction register while they waited.

Elena asked whether it was on the same floor.

“Yes.”

“Then it can wait until after lunch. The palace wanting these papers is no reason you should go hungry.”

“Her Highness has the banquet tomorrow evening,” the young man volunteered, then blushed at his interruption.

Adrian took the order sheet. “Another reason to eat today.”

Lunch was stew and dark bread in the next room. Blythe took her portion to her colleagues rather than trade unofficial conclusions with them. Adrian and Elena sat near the window. Pale pressure marks showed on her fingers after hours of writing.

“Cold?” he asked.

“Tired. Don't give me your soup. You only have one bowl.”

He put his bowl back and fastened the loose draft panel beside the window instead. When he returned, half her bread lay on his plate.

“You just said we each had one portion.”

“I said soup. Such a careful listener at hearings, and now you miss words.”

He laughed, cut the bread, and returned the softer half to her.

After a while she asked whether he had felt put on the spot when Isabella offered him water yesterday.

“A little. Then it became funny.”

“I hadn't meant to compete in front of everyone.”

“But you wanted me to take your cup first.”

She looked down at the steam without denying it. “Childish?”

“A little.”

She immediately raised her eyes. Before she could speak, he added, “I like you bringing me water. Not because I need anyone to defeat another cup.”

She was quiet, then broke off a piece of the soft bread.

“Next time I could simply say I want to sit beside you.”

“That makes the right answer easier.”

“And what is the right answer?”

He moved his chair closer, leaving room for her spoon.

She smiled, her shoulder touching his. This unimpressive lunchroom had become more useful than an entire argument about court etiquette.

That afternoon, the correction register eliminated another possibility. P-96 had no cancellation mark, and the summary was not an obsolete version prepared before a correction. The surviving entries retained both amounts as settled, totaling two hundred forty; the independent receipt counterpart supported one service costing one hundred twenty.

“Can we write that expenditure was overstated by a hundred twenty?” Adrian asked.

Blythe considered. “Write that the present vouchers support one hundred twenty, with another hundred twenty lacking independent support. Actual disbursement and who collected the second amount require the original payment instruments and receipt confirmation. Don't make a ledger discrepancy tell you whose pocket the money reached.”

Adrian crossed out the beginning of embezzlement.

Elena did not laugh. She also replaced the Crown paid twice with possible duplicate expenditure against one receipt. Then she identified a separate mismatch: the summary called P-96 escort, although RC-61 already included escort in the first amount. This was more than matching numbers. The wording needed to be preserved too.

They submitted both points, along with the resolved storage issue, to the duty supervisor. He did not let them take the original books. He checked page references, registered their preservation request, and placed the relevant payment instruments, receipt counterpart, and correction pages within the review's preservation scope.

“You cannot tell the carrier he collected twice,” he said. “We'll make a formal inquiry and check any collecting agent. If missing attachments appear, their submission time will also be recorded.”

After he left, Blythe remained seated. She turned over the initial summary and showed them the transcription signature.

“My name. I copied from an internal sheet marked verified. I didn't retrieve the receipt counterpart.”

She did not ask them to omit that. Instead, she added the internal sheet's reference to her explanation.

“Why tell us yourself?” Adrian asked.

“Because my signature won't disappear before the next person asks.” She put on her spectacles. “I'm afraid of losing this desk. But I don't want to believe that staying behind it long enough makes every mistake somebody else's.”

Elena promised no rescue by House Rosenberg. She asked whether Blythe had an ordinary route to respond and supplement the record. The clerk said the supervisor had arranged it. She separated the transcription she was responsible for from verification she could not claim on another person's behalf. Her writing stayed clear. Only at the end did her grip loosen.

She signed her statement, including Isabella's prior reliance on the insufficiently verified summary. She wasn't taking responsibility for the discovery because she trusted the princess.

As they left, she handed Adrian an acknowledgment.

“This doesn't mean the case is finished.”

“I only want to finish investigating the half slice of bread that remains unaccounted for.”

Elena took the acknowledgment and filed it. “A private gift. No refundable balance.”

Blythe finally laughed. She kept her own receipt without covering the gaps on the desk. Sunlight touched her worn sleeves. Adrian wondered whether the hardest thing to preserve today was not a hundred twenty silver coins, but someone's willingness to write one more line beneath her own name.

That evening, they delivered their limited findings to Isabella in a palace outer office. Her first question was not whether the hundred twenty could hurt Crowe. She asked whether the four hundred twenty balance therefore changed.

“The books still show four hundred twenty,” Adrian said. “We can't add the disputed amount back as though we've recovered it.”

She nodded slowly, setting aside whatever she had intended to say.

Ellery then brought the next evening's seating arrangements. The New Year reception had been scheduled on December 27, not invented as a trap for an accounting investigation. Isabella would receive invited representatives; Elena and Adrian had been added to the guests. They could attend or answer the invitation in writing.

“Are we expected to present the accounts?” Elena asked.

“No. The fiscal review isn't complete. I won't trade a dessert for an early verdict.”

They accepted. The original books and important counterparts stayed at the fiscal office, not in anyone's formal coat. The palace had received only a progress statement without sensitive payment-recipient details.

In the carriage home, Adrian touched an ordinary button at his cuff and remembered he would need evening clothes.

“Will you check them?”

“Your collar?”

“All of it. I'd rather not arrive looking like an attachment requiring correction.”

Elena turned toward him, unable to hide her smile.

“Of course. Tomorrow you only have to stand still. I'll do the looking.”
