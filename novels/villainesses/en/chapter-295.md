# Chapter 295: A Blank Is Not Someone's Name

On June 3, Elena straightened Adrian's collar outside the assembly and found a furniture dealer's note tucked inside it.

“Explaining bed sizes to the legislators?”

“After the hearing.”

She extracted it. Two chairs, said the bottom line. Where was the bookcase? He indicated the reverse. There he'd written: Don't buy another cabinet that won't go upstairs.

“Who is that about?”

“A remarkably farsighted future household.”

Laughing, she folded it into her folder. Their serious discussion of a shared future had reached the question of turning a cabinet on narrow stairs. This wasn't their wedding. Nobody could settle every future disagreement in advance.

Names were called inside. Elena would answer questions about family marriage provisions; Adrian would address historical contracts being treated as personal promises. They entered separately. The furniture note wasn't evidence.

Isabella was already seated, left wrist cushioned. Before her lay the committee's May 18 recommendation and the subsequent comparisons of marriage provisions. Qualified sponsoring members had combined them into a formal repeal bill. The chair announced its scope, the previously published hearing arrangements, and the opportunity for objections.

The princess's pass still said invited witness. She hadn't gained a vote.

A regional registrar rose first. On an old form, beside the adult participant's signature, was a space for the head of the family.

“If the person refuses, the family head signs. Our directive permits provisional registration until the person comes around. Does that survive?”

The sponsoring member said the new text prohibited substitution. She remained standing.

“But that directive isn't in the annex. We receive plenty of new laws. The paper in our drawer always says it's a special case.”

Elena studied the worn form. Her excellent upbringing had taught her how to make an unwilling person appear unreasonable without sacrificing anyone else's manners. Here that gracious pressure occupied a space just wide enough for a seal.

Given permission to speak, she referred to the submitted comparison of family rules. Marriage approval and property administration had been placed together. A pending inheritance dispute couldn't turn an adult's refusal into provisional consent. Property claims could be heard separately; marriage and bodies couldn't serve as collateral.

“Including House Rosenberg?” someone asked.

“Yes. My surname shouldn't become an exception in a footnote.”

She neither answered every question about her father's accounts nor withdrew her May 10 statement. The chair ordered a further comparison of conflicting directives and family clauses while preserving ordinary property remedies. The revised text would return for examination. Applause couldn't supply missing pages.

At lunch, Agnes found Isabella with a paper stained by soup. After her earlier testimony about housing threats, someone had asked whether an expiring subsidy could ever be stopped.

“I can't promise everyone a free house forever. But someone moved an expiry date to the day a person refused to sign.”

Isabella directed the verifiable account to the responsible staff without disclosing the family's address at the hearing. The drafters distinguished assistance ending under its original lawful conditions from withdrawal used to compel marriage or designated burdens. Disputes wouldn't vanish. Affected people would have grounds to challenge actual coercion without waiting for the word compulsory to appear on paper.

On June 6, the emergency exception returned.

The elderly member no longer wanted the High Priest alone to certify necessity. Instead, an emergency order could suspend withdrawal of consent until the facility found it convenient.

Adrian's fingers curled at convenient. He didn't touch his scar. Nobody needed another bout of muffled hearing to prove a refusal mattered.

Called to answer, he described agreeing to a limited observation and stopping when its stopping condition occurred. If accepting once allowed others to extend it indefinitely, only the first sentence of the agreement was true.

The engineering supervisor explained that safe exit did require planning. Withdrawal wasn't achieved by pulling the wrong wire. But safe procedures must be explained beforehand, bounded, and independently reviewed, not become an excuse for continued exposure without end. No human burden-sharing trial was currently approved. An unapproved possibility couldn't justify demanding irrevocable access to everyone's body.

“What if insufficient people volunteer?” the old member asked.

Isabella waited for permission.

“Then there aren't enough. We reduce the plan, raise money, and admit what we can't guarantee. We don't fill the empty places with names we've already chosen.”

After debate, voting members rejected the exception. Clerks checked the votes individually and preserved dissent. Isabella's hand remained flat on her desk.

The funding review was less satisfying. Seals and two relief shifts required payment this month. Additional distant monitoring remained unfunded. One supporter proposed combining everything under reform expenses to produce an impressive total.

“Writing it together won't put another seal in the storeroom,” the supervisor said.

Verified ordinary maintenance allocations continued. A separate assembly vote approved a capped supplementary allocation, with the treasury confirming its available source. Unfunded expansion awaited later budgets. Wages weren't rewards attached to sacrifice and couldn't disappear with designation authority. The protective main bridge continued operating. The fixed barrier left by May's dismantling stayed in place.

That evening Elena waited below the steps, nudging a pebble with her shoe. Did Adrian need to leave before breakfast tomorrow?

“Nothing scheduled in the morning.”

“Then I'd like to sleep late without hearing you whisper outside my door that I might be ill.”

He agreed so earnestly that he reached for his notebook. She laughed and stopped the pen.

“Ordinary life isn't another duty roster. You can sleep too.”

He took her hand without immediately heading for the carriage. “I thought that when everything ended, we'd naturally know how to live.”

“The furniture dealer will go out of business first.”

She retrieved the note. No bookcase by the window; she wanted to sit there. He also wanted daylight. A second chair was permitted opposite hers. They disputed two feet of space before agreeing to inspect the actual room. Neither had to surrender to an imaginary floor plan.

At the public text check on June 8, the registrar brought another problem. To save paper, the new form squeezed both parties' confirmations into one box. Witnesses received more space.

“Print this and clerks will still hand the pen to whoever speaks loudest.”

The embarrassed copyist explained the space available. Adrian thought it a small matter until Elena turned the form toward him. If she filled it in and asked him merely to make a mark, where would his independent answer appear?

He passed over a blank sheet. “Use another page. We can calculate the printing cost.”

They didn't rehearse their wedding on it. Two willing adult clerks tested the explanation separately. One thought withdrawal requests still required a family head. The drafters pointed to direct personal submission and corrected the misleading reference. The answers stayed separate. A witness verified the process, not another person's decision.

Elena watched the family-head box move aside.

“I used to imagine a splendid wedding. Rarely a registrar asking me to leave so you could answer alone.”

“Would you mind?”

“I'd be impatient. But I want you to come back and tell me you said yes yourself.”

Their hands met briefly beneath the table. She released him so the page could continue to the copyist.

On June 10, the revised bill completed its clause-by-clause consideration and final vote.

Isabella didn't immediately rise at the result. She found the provisions requiring an adult's specific, informed, revocable consent, then the prohibition on designating sacrifice through status, marks, or historical contracts. Both remained. May's petition had become an adopted bill. Formal royal approval and promulgation still followed.

A young member offered her the presentation packet. Couldn't a princess accelerate the seal?

She indicated the official messenger. “I can ask about progress tomorrow. I can't sign for that step.”

Success hadn't restored her restricted qualifications. Submission, text verification, and formal Crown approval proceeded through the authorized offices. A checker found the effective date missing from one copy intended for sealing. It was returned for correction. Isabella wanted to hurry them. Instead, she asked how long the new comparison required.

On June 12, the formally approved and promulgated law reached the assembly's notice office.

The Abolition of Coercive Marriage and Status-Based Sacrifice Act. Effective June 15.

Adrian stood before it for a long time. Existing preservation, suspension, and revocation orders continued under their own scopes. The kingdom-wide prohibition now had a definite date; nobody pretended every distant town had already received it this morning. Delivery records identified outstanding acknowledgments for follow-up. Registration offices prepared to discontinue conflicting fields without destroying the historical forms.

The regional registrar arrived. Folding her old form, she collected the verified replacement and asked where to send someone challenging an earlier registration. The desk supplied the actual receiving office. Existing disputes, appeals, and individual proceedings hadn't been canceled by celebration. Nor did the new law decide Evelyn's, Leon's, or Oswin's remaining personal liability.

A man carrying a market basket leaned toward the notice. He read June 12 aloud, then stopped at the second date.

“Which one do I tell my daughter?”

The registrar moved aside so he could see both lines. “Published today. Effective on the fifteenth.”

“She wants someone to examine an old registration. Do we wait here until then?”

The desk clerk asked whether he wanted directions to the existing receiving office. He did. She wrote its address and opening hours on a separate slip, then asked him to read it back. He confused the east entrance with the entrance facing east. The registrar sketched the archway beside the words.

“That one. I passed it twice.” He put the slip inside his basket, away from the damp vegetables.

“Your daughter can ask about her own record there,” the clerk said. “This desk can't decide her case.”

He nodded and left. Elena, standing beside Adrian, followed the basket with her eyes.

“We should have brought a drawing of the furniture shop.”

“I know that entrance.”

“You also knew which pocket held the measurements.”

He checked. She held up the folder before he finished.

Outside, someone shouted that danger was over.

Isabella turned. The law removed authority to command designated people to bear it, she explained. Actual warnings, repairs, and unresolved risks remained.

“Then what are we celebrating?” the disappointed man asked.

She glanced at the registrar holding her new forms.

“Next time someone says no, she won't have to find a reason that somebody else's seal answers for them.”

He fell quiet. The registrar ran a finger over the new page and suddenly smiled. She wouldn't attend a banquet, she said. There was an afternoon carriage to catch and two days of work waiting at her window.

Isabella stepped aside. Then she joined the members waiting to discuss a further proposal on royal oversight. She offered Adrian no private invitation, only asked the recorder to confirm the next material-delivery time. He agreed and watched her enter a room where people would still oppose her.

Elena pressed the furniture note into his hand. They finally reached the shop before closing and discovered that the cabinet they'd spent so long arguing about had sold.

“A limited national tragedy,” the dealer said. “There are cheaper ones.”

Elena glared at Adrian's suppressed smile, then laughed herself. Together they measured an ordinary replacement, depth first, then height. Blank space remained on their paper. Neither hurried to fill it with the other person's future.
