# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Three: The Knight Says She's Responsible for Me
### Chapter 72: The Knight Asks for a Place at Dinner

On November 19, Greymarch's honor review began once someone had carried out a stool with a broken leg.

Adrian Reed had imagined that a colonel presiding over such proceedings would at least bring an imposing chair. Helen Ward merely had someone level the table, then tucked her traveling gloves into her coat pocket.

She had arrived from the regional fort the previous evening and hadn't changed her mud-spattered riding boots. Gray showed at her temples. Her voice was a little hoarse, yet it silenced the people arguing over seats in the back row.

“Sit comfortably before we begin. Loyalty won't be judged by how long anyone can remain standing.”

Viola Blackwood, halfway out of her chair, slowly sat down again.

Her uniform was immaculate. Her hip remained bare. Adrian looked across from the witnesses' waiting area. She acknowledged him with a nod, without forcing a smile to reassure him.

Some seats had been made available to residents authorized to attend. A woman by the door had brought her sewing. Remembering that this was a military proceeding, she hurriedly put her needle away. Ward noticed and only asked her to keep the thread from rolling into the aisle.

Two officers from other garrisons sat beside the colonel. Major Voss, who had imposed the suspension, occupied a separate table. He would not preside over the review of his own decision. Town officials recorded the handling of the civilian documents. Ada Finch did not take a military board member's seat.

Ward established the day's scope: examine the grounds for suspension, hear Viola's response, and determine which documents could still establish authentic orders or actual shortages. A full military hearing on the treason allegation would require further preparation.

“My sword has been deposited,” Viola said. “I have the receipt.”

The clerk accepted a copy. Nobody exchanged it for permission to reclaim the weapon.

The first witness was the courier who had delivered the redirect on the fourteenth.

His boots were polished, but he walked stiffly. The clerk read the local report's summary: upon receiving the order, Blackwood had refused to return her patrol to Greymarch.

“Are those the words you heard?” Ward asked.

“She didn't return immediately.”

“I asked what she said.”

The courier wet his lips.

“She said the last stretcher wagon was still on the road. She would escort it to the relief point, then return. She told me to take that message to the major.”

Corporal Owen Hart testified next. The evacuation convoy was already traveling along the lower river road, but its rear wagon had not reached the guarded relief section. A wheel had become stuck. Viola had acknowledged the instruction in writing, explained her delayed return, and sent four riders back with the messenger to reinforce the town. She and the remaining escort stayed with the wagon. The riders who remained with the rear wagon reached their town-defense positions later than the major wanted, though the last wagon had entered Greymarch before dusk.

Between the two accounts, the simple word “refused” became more specific. It also grew heavier.

“So the delayed instruction did reach you,” Ward said to Viola.

“Yes. It ordered the patrol back to reinforce Greymarch. I received it.”

“The substituted November 4 annex doesn't erase the choice you made on the fourteenth.”

“I know.”

Someone shifted indignantly in the back row. A sergeant gestured for quiet, without roughness. The woman with the sewing clutched her bag but said nothing.

Voss submitted the defensive arrangements in place at the time. Restricted details passed only among the board members. When he explained his concern to the residents, he did not indicate specific positions on the map.

“I didn't know whether there was a second attacking force. We'd taken in wounded people and villagers. We were short of men at the gates. I recalled the patrol because I didn't want this town to burn too.”

Viola looked at him. “I knew you needed the men.”

“And you still delayed.”

“Yes.”

She didn't turn toward Adrian as though a glance could distribute her responsibility among them.

“People in the last wagon couldn't walk. We could still hear horses beside the road, and the escort coming to meet us hadn't arrived. I judged that withdrawing the guards would leave the wagon exposed. I sent four riders back first and kept the remaining escort with it, reporting what I was doing. That was my decision.”

Ward asked whether she believed saving lives excused her from explaining the consequences for another defensive position.

“No. I can explain the danger I saw. I'm willing to have any better deployment options examined. Reaching town in the end doesn't mean nothing could have gone wrong along the way.”

This time, Voss did not answer at once.

Viola reached for her cup and found it empty. The pitcher stood in front of Voss. He pushed it around the corner of his table, and the clerk poured for her. She thanked the clerk, then glanced at the major. Neither of them used the water to dismiss their disagreement, but the air in the room eased a little.

As she drank, Adrian realized how stiffly he was sitting. He leaned back and relaxed the hands pressed against his knees. There were answers he couldn't give for her today.

Her own fingers tightened beneath the table. Saying these things wasn't easy for her. She had simply declined to let someone else conceal the difficult parts.

Lieutenant Rusk testified next.

He began by acknowledging that he had demanded the supply wagon's prompt release on the eleventh and had doubted the Rosenberg visitors' purpose. Then he put the depot's receipt on the table.

“After inspection, I supervised the transfer of the dry provisions. They reached West Slope Depot. Stopping the wagon that day did not cause its entire load of grain to disappear.”

“Was the vehicle genuinely unsafe?”

“The axle bracket was cracked. It exceeded its declared weight, and there was ballast in the crates. We had to brace it again before unloading could continue.”

“Despite that, you objected to inspection?” Ward asked.

“I was concerned about reduced rations.” Rusk paused. “I hadn't seen what lay beneath the sacks. My concern was real. So was the problem with the wagon.”

In the front row, Elena did not seize the opportunity to study his expression. When she gave her commercial transport records to the clerk, she identified only the crate number and transit dates authorized for inspection. Her injured hand rested quietly inside her shawl.

The Northroad Freight purchase records went into a separate investigation file. They warranted pursuit. They could not yet tell the people in this room who had planned the entire attack.

Next, the dispatch register delivered the previous day was placed on the evidence table.

Two clerks read the retained issuing counterpart and the delivery acknowledgment separately. Then they showed the board the substituted local annex. Ward examined the binding record and the issuing officer's reply before asking Viola which version she had received.

“The one requiring all remaining grain to be removed, using the high bridge.”

“That version will no longer be accepted as an authentic order.”

Someone in the back row let out a clearly audible breath.

Then came the six hundred pounds of grain.

Tobin Quill gave his evidence seated, his injured hand supported on a folded coat. Ruth Fen read the quantity from the town weighhouse receipt. A military clerk checked the commercial consignment number. Twelve sacks, fifty pounds each. Still one consignment.

When called to the table, Adrian produced only the comparison sheet he was authorized to retain. He pointed out the two summary columns to the clerk without claiming to have found every missing sack.

“If both entries refer to the same shipment, the second can't establish that an additional shipment went missing.”

“Can you prove where each of those twelve sacks eventually went?” one board member asked.

“No. What I can establish is that the same consignment number appears twice in this total. Its movements still need to be checked individually.”

Stepping away, he caught his foot on a low stool. Viola's hand rose, then fell again. The nearer clerk steadied the stool. Adrian regained his footing and gave her a small nod.

Ward conferred quietly with the other two officers. Their pens moved, stopped, and moved again.

During the wait, Cecilia arrived from the infirmary with Dr. Shaw's signed account of the patient's admission and the handover of his guard. Cecilia verified only the portions of registration in which she had participated: the wounded man had received supervised treatment, and his guards had not been withdrawn for dressing changes. She did not claim that Ellis's identity had been established or offer his wounds as proof that his account was true.

“Treatment did not constitute release,” Ward confirmed, filing the document with the records concerning his admission.

Cecilia joined Adrian and pressed a little parcel into his hand.

“You left your bread at the infirmary this morning.”

“I thought I'd donated it.”

“The donation register says bandaging cloth. Your breakfast isn't quite that useful yet.”

He bent his head to hide a smile and noticed Viola looking at the parcel too. Cecilia produced another and asked the nearby clerk to give it to her during the break.

“I brought two. Knights can't live on honor.”

Before noon, Ward read the day's determinations.

The local November 4 annex had been substituted. It could not be used to establish that Viola had disobeyed the authentic order. Until the relevant movements were verified, the duplicated six hundred pounds could not be presented as an established additional theft for which she was responsible.

The military would investigate the order's passage separately. The town would continue preserving the civilian transport records. Checked copies would be made and retained independently, rather than leaving everything in the same receiving office.

The deployment decisions during the evacuation on the fourteenth, the other grain discrepancies, and the attackers' origins remained under investigation. The command suspension imposed on the seventeenth continued. Viola would remain in town during the review, with access to an adviser and the right to submit further evidence.

A little tension left her shoulders.

No scabbard returned to her hip. Nobody proclaimed her innocent. But the paper that had directed villagers over a damaged bridge could no longer give orders from behind an authentic seal.

Outside, the woman with the sewing stopped Viola and offered her a wooden button.

“The old cloak you put around someone that day lost a button. I've sewn on a new one. The cloak's still drying, but you can have the spare I bought.”

Viola took it, her fingertip resting on its polished edge.

“Are you all right?”

“Drafts in the room where we're staying. Better than the road.” The answer was practical. “When we can go back, perhaps you'll help us look at our gate.”

“When escort visits are allowed.”

“All right.”

The woman went. Viola put the button in her pocket, much more gently than she had put away the receipt for her sword.

By evening, the inn smelled of stew. Adrian and Elena sat by the window, finally finishing the page they had left two days earlier. Her hand had not fully healed, making it awkward to turn the pages. He held the spine open while she chose the passages she liked.

“You spoke well in there today,” she said quietly.

“I nearly lost to a stool.”

“I intend to leave that part out.”

The travel book's next chapter described a market beside the sea. Elena touched an illustration of a shell and asked whether he'd ever seen one that large.

“Only small ones that merchants described as large.”

“Then we'll have to see for ourselves sometime.”

“I'd like to.” He answered faster than she expected. “When things here allow it. And when your hand's better.”

She looked at the rather crooked illustration and murmured agreement, leaving the bookmark on that page.

He looked at her and drew his chair closer. She stopped reading. Her shoulder touched his, and her gaze lingered on the same line for a long time before she turned the page.

Cecilia returned from the infirmary and went to wash her hands. Viola stood in the dining room doorway, looked at their table, took half a step, and stopped.

“May I have dinner with you tonight?”

Adrian looked up.

She produced no duty roster. She didn't explain which chair offered the best view of the stairs.

Elena pulled the empty chair out a little. “Of course. Though there's no patrol report tonight. Only the landlady's grievances about the price of carrots.”

“I've heard those,” Viola said.

“Then you're well prepared.”

She was smiling when she sat down. Adrian pushed the bread basket within her reach. Soon, four bowls of soup stood on the table.

They had barely tasted it when a waiter brought a meeting request bearing the joint seals of several noble houses. A delegation of border nobles invited Viola to the assembly building the following morning. They hoped she would provide a written account “that safeguards the border's reputation and can command common agreement.”

Suggested wording had already been prepared. It could, the letter assured her, spare her a great deal of trouble.

She read that line twice.

“You can look at it tomorrow,” Adrian said.

Viola folded the letter and set it beyond the reach of her soup bowl. She neither accepted nor asked the waiter to carry back a hasty refusal.

Then she picked up her spoon again.

She had asked for this chair herself.

For a little while, she wanted to enjoy sitting in it.
