# Chapter 86: The Princess's Envoy Asks Her to Sit Down

On December 1, Viola was coming down the inn stairs in civilian clothes, without rank insignia on her shoulders, when she noticed an unusual silence below.

A woman in a gray-blue traveling outfit sat downstairs, melting snow still clinging to the brim of her hat. Two attendants stood behind her. A dispatch case rested beside the table, and the landlady held a bill, uncertain whether to interrupt.

The woman looked up at Viola and moved a chair aside.

“Miss Blackwood? Please, sit. I've taken your usual breakfast table.”

Viola instinctively straightened.

“You are…?”

“Alice Fenwick. Appointed by the Crown to assume responsibility for the cross-jurisdictional investigation.” She removed her gloves. “At the moment, though, I only want to know whether this pot of tea is included in the room charge.”

The landlady recovered at once.

“It isn't.”

“Then please add it to my bill.”

Adrian was halfway across the room with a plate when he heard this. At least, he thought, the special envoy understood Greymarch's truly important rules.

Fenwick did not open her dispatch case at the breakfast table. She asked Ward's liaison to take her to the council building and left an attendant to arrange the lodgings. Before departing, however, she glanced at the bread in Adrian's hands.

“You're Reed?”

“Yes.”

“Those three freight comparison charts are clear. We may need you to explain your sources this afternoon.”

“I'll be there.”

She nodded and left. Elena passed behind him and set her portion of jam beside his plate.

“At last, someone who only praises your charts.”

“You sound pleased.”

“Until she starts praising how dependable you are, I am.”

Viola laughed. Then Elena asked whether she would like some jam, and she sat down. For once, the arrival of an envoy from the capital did not make her feel that she needed permission even to finish breakfast.

Over the previous day, the people injured at the bridge had all received treatment. The guard would keep his leg, though he still needed leave to recover. The escort struck by the shield remained at the clinic as well. Morrow was being questioned in an authorized holding facility, with independent personnel recording the interviews. The contents of his leather bag had not been spread across the inn's dining table. Authorized staff had received and checked each item.

The people of Greymarch, meanwhile, had quickly invented seven different versions of the south bridge incident.

In one, Viola had overturned both wagons with one hand.

“I didn't even overturn a table,” she said.

“The table will be relieved to hear it,” Adrian replied.

Another claimed that she had reclaimed her own sword and led her former troops to storm the supply warehouse. Hearing it, she almost set down her fork to go and correct the story. Elena held down the sheet of rumors on the table, without touching her hand.

“The committee will publish what actually happened. Finish your bread first. Does everyone who exaggerates your strength get to command your breakfast?”

Viola considered that, then went on eating.

At the council building, the transfer began with verification of the commission.

Fenwick presented the formal papers bearing the royal seal and the joint endorsement of the Military Council. Their identifying number matched the registration acknowledgment that had arrived earlier along the regional post road. Ward checked the scope of the commission. Magistrate Ada confirmed that civil seizures and arrangements for patients would remain with the appropriate authorities. Only then did they sign the handover sheet.

Ward's report, sent on the twenty-second, had requested broader authority to investigate military supply offices and carriers across regional boundaries. The N26 material received afterward had followed in successive supplements. The capital had authorized the special investigation's takeover on the twenty-fifth. Fenwick had departed on the twenty-seventh, meeting regional auditors along the way.

She had not heard that someone had swung a sword at the bridge and instantly rushed out from the capital.

“Princess Isabella proposed to the Military Council that the relevant royal procurement records also be included in the examination,” Fenwick said. “My commission comes from the Council and the Crown. The princess's personal trust cannot rewrite anyone's testimony.”

Elena looked up and remembered those words.

Including royal records meant that border bookkeepers would not be the only ones answering questions. Fenwick did not use that fact to declare the Crown guilty. She required the outlying warehouse to preserve its existing rack-loan approvals, repair returns, and packaging circulation records, with each person's conduct examined against their actual responsibilities.

Viola's military hearing would still be decided by the three-member committee that had already heard the testimony. The judicial observer who had accompanied the envoy would oversee the procedure. New investigative materials had to be equally available to the questioning officer and Viola's legal adviser. Arriving in the princess's dispatch case could not turn them straight into a judgment.

That afternoon, Morrow's lawyer raised the first objection.

He acknowledged that the leather bag had been seized at the south bridge, but argued that the private payment ledger might contain other people's accounts. A name on the cover did not establish that Morrow had arranged every entry. Fenwick had the clerk record the objection, then asked the investigators to produce the independently retained records already verified the previous day.

The town bank's records showed a withdrawal on November 12 from a military supply revolving account under Morrow's management. That same day, Northroad Freight's bookkeeper had entered an identical amount, describing its purpose as additional escorts for N26. The divided payments in the private ledger matched those two original records.

The supply cashier recognized the withdrawal slip he had completed. He pointed out one alteration that Morrow had told him to make in person. He also volunteered that, at the time, he had seen only the apparently legitimate description of temporary freight protection and had not asked why a separate night bonus was being paid.

“That was a question I failed to ask,” he said. “I can't pretend now that I had already spotted the problem that day.”

Next came a counterpart of the instructions, recovered when Northroad's office was sealed. This original had remained with the carrier. It was not the ledger burned at Alder Watch. It directed the men assigned for the fourteenth to intercept the evacuation wagons along the lower river route, destroy the remaining grain, and wear the issued blue-cloth identifiers. Afterward, the grain was to be reported lost to an enemy interception, and the patrol officer already named was to account for the failure to protect it.

The courier confirmed that he had collected a sealed packet from Morrow's office. The carrier's steward confirmed how duties had been assigned after its arrival. Both men's own responsibilities remained under investigation, so their statements were checked item by item against the office dispatch register, the returned seal-use record, and the payment receipts.

At last, the order was more than a story heard from a captive.

Morrow's lawyer requested a handwriting examination and challenged whether the counterpart might have been created afterward. The committee authorized a licensed document examiner to inspect the paper, signatures, and traces left by the sealed packet. It also summoned the independent custodians of the relevant registers, allowing both sides time for further questions the following day.

Sitting nearby, Viola slowly loosened her fingers.

Which roads the wagon had taken on the thirteenth, how the attack on the fourteenth had been financed, and why the carriage had come to the clinic on the twenty-eighth still required answers from different people. But the lines once used to point toward her were taking on a different shape among real people and real payments.

Adrian did not see a results screen from Crown of Fate.

He saw the supply cashier wiping sweat from his forehead. He saw the clerk replace a worn-out pen. He saw Major Voss finish reading the counterpart and remain with his head bowed for a long time.

These things weighed more than a game's victory fanfare. They were also more likely to let a living knight walk out of this room.

After the meeting, Fenwick asked Adrian to stay and explain the sources behind his charts. Elena waited in the corridor. After a while, Viola joined her.

“Aren't you worried she'll take him to the capital as a clerk?” Viola asked.

Elena glanced at her. “I am. Are you?”

Viola nodded honestly.

“Good. At least today there's only one person who can pretend not to understand.”

Adrian came through the door just then. Seeing both of them turn toward him, he slowed for a moment.

“Did I miss an important decision?”

“No,” Elena said. “What tea would you like this afternoon?”

“Ordinary tea is fine.”

Viola turned away, unable to keep from laughing. He had no idea why even choosing tea felt like giving the wrong answer, but he followed them down the corridor anyway.

On the stairs, a clerk stopped Viola to confirm the time she would be needed the next day. Elena and Adrian waited for her by the courtyard gate.

“Did Fenwick ask whether you'd like to keep working for her?” Elena asked suddenly.

“She asked whether I'd be willing to put the charts into a set of copies suitable for handing over. I agreed to finish that part.”

“I meant afterward.”

“No.” He looked at her. “If she does, I'll decide whether I want to, then talk to you. I won't pretend I never want to do anything just because you're jealous today.”

Elena lowered her eyes and touched the edge of her cloak. It took a moment for her smile to return.

“That was a rather annoying answer.”

“But you were asking about the future.”

“Yes.”

She looked toward the street. A candle seller was picking up a sign blown over by the wind. Someone beside him steadied it, and the two went on haggling. Once, she had imagined the future as a long table where she could assign everyone a place. Now, she could not even write down where the person beside her would want to go next.

“I hope you want to be with me,” she said.

“I am with you.”

“I know. I wanted to hear it today anyway.”

Adrian held out his hand. She placed her uninjured one in it. They stood where they were not blocking the way. An old woman passing through saw them, smiled, and walked around without asking anything.

That, somehow, made Elena self-conscious. Her fingers did not let go.

When Viola came out, she saw their joined hands. She paused on the steps for half a heartbeat, then walked down and asked whether they could go back by way of the tailor's. They agreed, and all three turned into another street.

No one pretended the moment had not happened. No one required her to retreat a great distance simply because she had seen it.

At the tailor's shop, Viola stopped in front of the window.

Two narrow hair ribbons were displayed inside, one dark green and one gray-blue. She had passed this shop many times. Today was the first time she seriously considered how they would look with her clothes.

Elena followed her gaze and asked whether she wanted to go in.

“Just to look.”

“That's usually how shopping begins.”

The shopkeeper brought a small mirror. Viola could not comfortably lift her left arm, so, after asking, Elena tried tying the dark green ribbon in her hair. Adrian stood by the door, attempting not to look like a supply clerk waiting for a weapons inspection.

“What do you think?” Viola asked.

“It's lovely.”

“The dark color isn't too severe?”

“Severe wasn't what came to mind.”

She looked at him in the mirror. “What did?”

Adrian paused.

“That you should ask questions like that more often.”

Her eyes stayed on him. Elena untied the sample ribbon and asked the shopkeeper for a new one, then quite naturally chose a second ribbon in gray-blue.

“You're buying one, too?” Adrian asked.

“Of course. I didn't come here solely to make someone else prettier.”

Smiling, the shopkeeper wrapped it for her. Viola paid for her own. On leaving, however, she did not put it in her pocket. She asked Elena to tie it into her hair properly.

Back at the inn, Cecilia was writing a reply beneath a lamp. Her eyes brightened at the sight of Viola, and her compliment was entirely direct. Then she handed two winter-clothing collection slips to the volunteer who had come for them and finished giving the necessary instructions before returning to the conversation.

“Do you like it?”

Viola touched the end of her hair. “I do.”

“Then that's good. I once bought one everyone said suited me and never wore it again.”

“Why?”

“Too scratchy.”

Adrian laughed. So did Viola, but she stopped him when he turned to fetch the teapot.

“I didn't buy it to make the hearing committee like me better.”

“I know.”

“Or because you said I should ask more often.”

He nodded and quietly waited for her to continue.

She pinched her sleeve between the fingers of her right hand, then slowly released it.

“Sometimes, the person I'd like you to see is just an ordinary girl who wants pretty ribbons and tart candied apples. Not a knight who brings a duty or a danger with her every time she comes to you.”

Adrian looked at her. The familiar tightness in his chest had a different cause now.

“I saw her today,” he said. “I liked the questions you asked me very much.”

She blushed, but her eyes brightened.

“Then next time, I'll ask again.”

Outside, lamps were being lit one by one. Viola returned to her chair and tasted the fruit tea Cecilia recommended. Its tartness immediately wrinkled her nose.

Adrian pushed the sugar bowl toward her, without suggesting that someone who liked tart apples was obliged to like every sour thing in the world.

She added one spoonful, tasted it, then added another half.

The final military decision on December 3 still awaited her. Before then, though, she could already decide exactly how sweet she wanted today's tea.
