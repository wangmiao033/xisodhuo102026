# Chapter 199: The Door Missing from the Map

Miriam's first line went the wrong way.

At least, Adrian thought so. Arriving at the surveying room fifteen minutes late by agreement on February 26, he found the original drawing already sealed and a copy spread out. Her western passage bent downward, almost opposite to the street plan they'd previously obtained.

He didn't turn the paper for her.

“Where did you start?”

“The desk. Where I copied names.”

“Not the entrance?”

“I didn't work sitting on the entrance.”

The examiner lowered his face over a smile. Adrian smiled too, then crossed out his intended question about incorrect direction. He'd always looked inward from the building's official entrance. She looked outward from where she put her feet every day.

The surveyor added orientation on transparent paper without altering her lines. Aligning the receiving room with an old works plan brought the bend to a courtyard corner now divided by a later wall. The new street map called it West Laundry Yard. Earlier works registers called it the service area of the Western Relay Court.

Hale's gatehouse and transport information had reached only the outside road, not distinguished these neighboring yards. Miriam's low passage opened beside an old unloading slope. Ordinary carts neither used it nor fitted through it.

“I said there was a door here. Not there.”

On the current plan, she indicated an uninterrupted wall.

People still worked in the yard. Entry required management permission and arrangements around workers carrying wet cloth past steam vats. Narrow access reduced the visiting party by two. After receiving the route assessment, Viola kept her left arm in its sling. She could check routes from the level outside observation platform. The interior measurements belonged to field staff.

“Will you think I'm no use outside?” she asked Adrian.

“You'll check whether they've moved west to north.”

“I may check you first.”

Her caregiver settled her cloak. Viola held a wind-lifted map corner with her right hand. The joke passed; she stayed where she could safely remain. One field visit hadn't healed her.

Miriam hesitated on the coach step. Metal scraped inside the yard. Her hand closed around the door.

Cecilia waited below without asking whether she was seeing anything.

“Just iron moving,” Miriam said first. “I know.”

“You can know and still wait a moment.”

Adrian's chest tightened at wait. Inside, a worker had dragged an empty rack clear. No old brass handle appeared. He separated the two events and asked the driver to leave the supporting strap beside the door in place while Miriam decided when to descend.

When she did, she first borrowed a chair. Courage didn't abolish sitting. Her leg had no new injury, she explained. She disliked being watched at such moments.

The manager brought the repair register. Authorized inspection covered the wall, drains, and disused relay room, not the operating laundry furnace. He initially pointed to a new doorway. Everyone had always used that entrance.

Miriam indicated the wall behind the drying poles.

“I don't know where you go now. I came out there.”

Workers voluntarily moved their empty baskets from the wall. The manager first recorded the working time affected by inspection, preventing historical inquiry from quietly becoming unpaid labor. Rough stones emerged. At the right, one mortar seam was narrower than its neighbors.

Nobody ordered the wall smashed. The surveyor found the blocking plan in the repair register, checked its dimensions, and opened an existing inspection cover beside the drain. Through a short mirror he examined a sealed low arch. A slanted grate remained behind it, one of three crossbars bent.

Miriam stood well back.

“Don't put the lamp above it.”

The lamp bearer stopped. Pale, she explained she meant the old lamp. Today's lighting should follow the surveyor's instructions, not move because of her remark.

The lamp remained safely secured. She returned to the chair without forcing another look. The field staff sketched the reflected view and sent it outside for comparison with her previously sealed drawing. Two independently drawn routes finally met behind a stone wall.

Adrian crouched outside the permitted boundary, imagining an injured person narrowing her shoulders. The old world ceased to be an abstract termination inscribed on fragments. He couldn't see her fire. He could see how much room an ordinary body needed to pass through alive.

A laundry worker asked whether the yard would close.

“No such decision today,” the manager answered.

“Don't just bring people in, then leave us guessing tomorrow morning.”

Adrian stood. He asked the manager to post the actual inspection boundaries and usable areas at the entrance without disclosing the witness's name. The man would obtain the site supervisor's confirmation before writing it. A visitor couldn't guarantee safety for every worker.

The approach to the relay room was narrower. Miriam didn't enter. From outside she indicated where the noise had originated: first the floor shook beneath her feet, then the impacts came overhead. She didn't know who operated anything inside. She hadn't watched central transmission stop.

The works examiner compared her position with the building's levels. Several transmission channels met at that room. Its old function supported nearby protective wards and routing. It wasn't the highest control chamber of the entire system. A disused inspection opening revealed a disconnected metal mounting without indications of present power.

Adrian wanted a closer look. The examiner stopped him: the floor hadn't been assessed for weight-bearing. A clear restriction with a reason and scope. Adrian stepped back rather than demand special entry because he might have visited before.

Authorized staff extended measuring rods from the doorway, measuring the vacant mounting and channel spacing without touching broken internal components. Surviving grooves, old repairs, and Miriam's independently identified exit matched. The vague western-passage incident could now be located at this relay court, rather than the open ground near the neighboring veterans' club.

“So it started here?” Adrian asked.

The examiner didn't nod. An authorized extract from an old inspection described local damage spreading from the relay room toward the western passage. That supported locating the center of this particular catastrophe here. It couldn't establish this room as the source of every disturbance throughout the kingdom. Upstream faults appeared on another page. Today's permission didn't open the entire volume.

Adrian looked into the dark doorway. Finding a center didn't produce a single cause.

Miriam lost interest sooner than the others. Could she see the receiving-room window? The manager led her there. White cloth now hung behind it, admitting more light than she remembered.

“I thought the desk faced the yard. There was clouded glass between.”

Should the surveyor alter her first drawing?

“No. Add that I found something today I may have remembered wrongly.”

Her guarded forefinger stopped an inch from the sill. She touched nothing. Nothing manifested. Watching her fingertip, she said she'd always hurried away after work, complaining that the room was stuffy. She'd never imagined wanting to establish whether its window had existed.

Cecilia stood beside her without reciting anything. Eventually Miriam turned away herself. Time to go.

Outside, Viola had circled a slope no stretcher could negotiate. Why did the old transfer entry describe it as passable? The works examiner explained that the entry referred to pedestrians, not every evacuation vehicle. One apparently open route divided into two very different usable widths.

“An incomplete evacuation may involve more than people moving slowly,” Viola said.

Adrian recorded the question. In games he'd treated escape as one uniform action. If the button lit up, everyone could leave. These walls and slopes offered no such convenient button.

They returned Miriam to the corner she chose. She asked them not to include the shop's name on the shared plan. The examiner stored contact details separately. The map retained only information needed to investigate the site.

“Now you've found it, will you open it again?” she asked before descending.

“Not today. Not merely because I want answers.”

She studied him, then nodded. She hadn't decided he'd never make a mistake. This answer could at least be checked against what happened next.

That afternoon Luna heard the field report and had the assistant separate the unlettered technical drawing of the vacant mounting from people's routes. She hadn't visited the cramped yard. Her right palm rested flat; her left-hand pencil indicated one baseline requiring another check, leaving written notes to the assistant.

The earlier cutoff comparison suggested the previous trial film’s gray trace could fade from the observation window prematurely when displaced from its effective optical path, while the power indicator remained lit. The new positioning frame had passed repeatability checks with ordinary samples. Geometry from the vacant mounting could help compare external references. Showing Adrian a diagram labeled with the answer, then waiting for him to remember it, would achieve something else entirely.

“I saw the doorway, passage, and inspection opening. Record that before the trial.”

“All of it. You might dream about them.”

Did that mean he could never try again?

“It means we need to know what has already entered your eyes.”

A physician's reassessment had been arranged for the new limited trial application. Whether tomorrow's trial proceeded still depended on his actual condition. No increase in strength, no opening the old power case, no historical objects connected. He could have an answer about the place and still know nothing more about his hand.

Before leaving, he offered the travel book. Luna considered it, then asked about the laundry worker complaining about closure instead of the captain.

“He made more sense than the captain.”

“Hardly difficult. The captain can't manage a hat.”

Adrian laughed and described white cloth blowing across the measuring rods, forcing the examiner to wait while workers secured it. Luna listened without requiring every detail to point toward catastrophe. Besides retaining an old wound, that yard still housed people who needed today's washing to dry.

Before dinner Elena brought an arrival slip. Her supplementary questions had produced permission to inspect old judicial-handover material on March 1. One original receiving counterfoil had been added to the previous scope. Its contents hadn't yet been examined.

“I don't want to read the long instructions now.”

“All right. The deadline for additional questions is on the outside.”

He read and remembered it. She hadn't used his opportunity for him. Prompt delivery didn't make the coming inspection easy either. Would he like breakfast together before tomorrow's trial? Yes, although he might not talk much.

“You seldom have much to say about porridge.”

“I have opinions. I haven't found the appropriate receiving office.”

She smiled, left the slip, and took her own book away. He didn't follow her asking for a guarantee that March 1's papers would contain nothing bad. What she could offer today was timely knowledge of when that door would open.

At dusk he put the new plan away. It hadn't drawn a hand shutting down a device for him. It contained a low opening somebody had actually escaped through, a slope no stretcher could cross, and a catastrophe's center that finally had a name.
