# Chapter 255: The Royal Name Was on the Paper Too

On the morning of April 18, Isabella found an extra sentence at the end of her declaration: guaranteed upon royal honor.

She pushed the page back with her uninjured hand.

“Today concerns precisely why that isn't enough.”

The copyist flushed. A princess surely needed a suitably solemn conclusion. Rather than punish him, she requested the original wording aloud, without assurances she hadn't given. Her supported left wrist rested beside a paperweight. Nobody needed to kneel and hold the page still as a demonstration of loyalty.

Yesterday's reference number hadn't unlocked the palace's entire guilt overnight. Within existing lawful access, archivists retrieved the corresponding expense page and its checking ledger. The royal household had paid for a transfer-pattern revision. The receipt matched the old calibration plate's catalog number. Payment alone couldn't establish who understood the eventual consequences.

What kept Isabella awake was the attached settlement note. Referring to designated-bearer records they already held, it said the adjustment would maintain the established five places without requesting fresh agreement from their occupants. Dates, marks, and custody routes had been checked. Individual responsibility for some signatures still required investigation, not a princess completing a trial in the street.

The limits belonged in the same declaration as the accusation, not in a quieter correction issued after everyone had gone home. She included them where listeners could hear and copy holders could find them. Taking responsibility for disclosure also meant refusing the satisfying leap from a checked payment to every possible intention. Another sheet held ordinary people's names. Public copies retained only necessary references. Her willingness to acknowledge royal wrongdoing shouldn't send the city to question those people's families.

When Adrian arrived, she was practicing her opening.

“As a member of the royal household—” She stopped. “It sounds as though I'm announcing lower taxes.”

“You could begin with what you've brought.”

“What if nobody listens without the title?”

He didn't rush to deny it. Her position would bring some listeners and make others distrust her. She needn't pretend the advantage absent, only avoid adding its weight to what the documents proved.

She asked him to sit and play a hostile listener. He hadn't settled into his chair, he protested, and she was already assigning roles. She laughed, then asked properly. Willingly, he agreed, but he'd choose the ugliest question.

“Your usual standard of service, then.”

Her new opening identified three checked historical records and their relationship. Adrian asked how admitting ancestral wrongs would prevent today's lights failing. It wouldn't, she answered. But if somebody called an old choice untouched divine will, these papers showed human decisions had always helped designate the victims.

“What about you?”

She took time to answer.

“I've used royal status to make other people accept my arrangements. Speaking truth today doesn't mean I was never wrong.”

Adrian remembered her treatment of marriage as a negotiating instrument. She didn't ask his eyes to confirm forgiveness or turn remorse into an injury he must comfort. He said she could choose how much of herself to discuss, but apparent candor shouldn't expose somebody else's private experience.

She struck out a paragraph involving him. The result wasn't a prettier confession, only one that occupied less of another person's life.

The announcement took place on covered steps outside an ordinary archive reading hall. Permission covered a public explanation while the entrance and return window remained open. Isabella hadn't regained special authority. Access, copying, and notices went through the current duty staff. She even waited behind a man researching his father's property record.

Recognizing her, he hurried to step back. She asked him to stay: she'd dislike somebody announcing a princess was more urgent just as he reached the important page. Adrian suppressed laughter until she whispered whether she'd learned to queue like a political reform.

“You still need to lose your number.”

She held the paper slip more tightly.

More listeners came than there were chairs. Staff kept seating clear of passageways; late arrivals could listen at the side or read copies afterward. A former court adviser accused her of hanging royal dirty laundry in the street to assist people determined to destroy everything. Somebody else shouted that, since she admitted it, the entire royal family should bear the calamity.

Isabella didn't begin by rebutting the second man to establish her own victimhood. She explained where the three records corresponded: expenditure, an old decision, responsibility still undetermined. Somebody complained she was slow. Instead of raising her voice, she asked the copy holder to identify the unclear line.

“This payment came from royal accounts. Responsibility cannot all be shifted onto the Temple. The related record explicitly maintained five places without asking their occupants again. It isn't five people saying yes.”

The adviser did not deny the payment. Instead he argued about the circumstances under which it had been made. That distinction mattered: a dispute over justification was not the same as a dispute over whether the record existed. Isabella let him finish before answering. He called it a necessary crisis decision. Isabella acknowledged genuine danger then, and disaster records which should also be checked openly. Royal guilt wasn't grounds to destroy them. But danger couldn't erase a choice from history or permit descendants to repeat it without renewed proof.

An elderly woman asked whether her brother's record was included. He'd been taken for supposed temporary assistance when young and returned unable to work properly for a long time. She had no papers with her, only a remembered year and location.

Isabella didn't promise this was his file or demand public medical details to strengthen her statement. She asked the actual inquiry clerk to explain submitting a lead privately. The woman still declined to leave her name. She received ordinary inquiry hours and required materials so she could decide whether to return.

That was harder than announcing royal compensation. No approved fund existed to hand out, and personal shame couldn't price every historical wrong. Known requests could reach the responsible office with a receipt, not a promise that today settled them.

A young man pushed forward and tore his copy in half. Paper fell at Isabella's feet. Did a princess confess her family's wrongdoing, then use honesty to remain a princess? Her shoulders tightened. Her supported wrist shifted.

Two steps away, Adrian didn't scold him on her behalf. Safety staff watched the man's hands without charging forward in the absence of an attack. Isabella looked down at the pieces. She couldn't decide whether people trusted her, and wouldn't exchange the declaration for a contract preserving her power.

“Why only now?”

“Because before, I didn't want to look this clearly. You don't have to accept that as an excuse.”

She continued to the next concrete question rather than demand applause for suffering visibly enough. The man stayed without tearing anybody else's paper. Eventually he took another copy. Nobody knew whether his opinion changed.

Near the end, an unsigned question demanded every noble who'd known. Isabella had it read aloud before explaining that an expense page couldn't establish each member of an entire family's knowledge. Accused again of protecting her own kind, she didn't hide uncertainty behind her cup. Individual inquiries would take time, and evidence might be moved meanwhile. She had therefore also requested lawful preservation of the relevant original accounts. An authorized office had accepted the request; it wasn't her personal order to seal every private residence.

A clerk displayed the receipt and explained where subsequent progress could be checked. A driver who'd shouted earlier asked what happened if he couldn't read. Without a pitying look, Isabella requested the actual spoken-inquiry hours and checked somebody would be on duty. She repeated the time and asked him to say which day he'd come. Only when they understood one another did they move on.

Adrian remembered how easily he'd once said people would understand. Even a receipt somebody unable to read could actually ask about required another person to remain and answer. Isabella put the arrangement into the handover rather than abandoning it for her next impressive speech.

Afterward, full checked material entered formal custody. Public copies included verifiable references and unresolved matters. Private names weren't posted; original pages weren't fought over on the steps. The adviser's written objection was accepted too, not discarded because he opposed them.

Isabella's voice had grown hoarse. Taking water, she asked whether Adrian had wanted to say something kind on her behalf. Yes. Why hadn't he?

“You weren't here to win an argument everyone carefully protected you through.”

She watched the water tremble. “I used to think silence meant you weren't on my side.”

“And now?”

“Perhaps you're waiting for me to find my own footing.”

She added that she'd still welcome help with her shawl. Respect didn't require doing nothing. Tied or draped, he asked. Draped: today she didn't want to resemble a document which required a knot to become official.

That afternoon they delivered ordinary supplies to temporary lodging. A displaced family needed a dry blanket. Reception checked the actual stock; a princess arriving couldn't generate another set. Isabella helped read sizes. She even reversed a pair of children's shoes, then accepted Adrian's correction and changed them around.

The child asked whether she was the lady who'd had paper torn today. Yes. Put it higher, he advised seriously, and people couldn't reach it.

“Sometimes people need to be able to take the paper. Protecting paper isn't why we write it.”

Apparently deciding adults were peculiar, he carried his shoes to his mother.

The child's advice was sensible if the only aim was to keep paper intact. It was less useful if people needed to examine what stood upon it. Remembering the copies people had carried away from the hall, Isabella walked quietly afterward. Adrian didn't describe one blanket delivery as a princess finally understanding ordinary people. Nor did she request an exclusive retreat for two. They left at the ordinary shift change, carrying sizes still needed for the next actual delivery. The missing items remained missing; their presence hadn't magically completed the work.

Luna's message arrived that evening. Following medical advice, she'd done no further reading of traces. Her assistant read the public copy aloud. Luna sent one sentence: don't use yesterday's magical expenditure to prove something she hadn't read today.

Isabella laughed and instructed the messenger to tell her every missing word remained missing. They hadn't borrowed her name to fill the gaps.

The final letter came from Evelyn Vale. It didn't promise every secret or request a declaration of innocence first. She said the publicly identified old revision number had another correspondence in her transfer index, involving the execution hall. She was willing to explain with custody witnesses present.

Isabella handed Adrian the letter. The royal name hadn't disappeared from the paper. Somebody else who had once carried out orders must now decide how to keep signing hers.
