# Chapter 98: The Knight Guards a Chair

On the morning of December 12, Viola stood outside the west workshop without a drawn sword.

She was carrying a broom.

When Adrian approached, she was probing the thin ice beyond the door with the end of its handle. Dirty water from the previous night's coal delivery lay beneath it. A cart stood sideways on the slope, its rear blocking the last half foot of the door's outward swing.

“Is that today's most dangerous enemy?” he asked.

“Currently in first place.”

“Who's second?”

She glanced down at her left shoulder, which she had tensed out of habit, and did not attempt to move the cart.

“Still under observation.”

Arriving at the research tower had not ended the fourteen days of convalescent leave that began on December 4. The wound on her left forearm was healing, but lifting anything heavy still pulled at it. She put the broom down and asked the administrator to find a porter to move the cart and spread dry sand over the path.

Luna arrived carrying her drawings to discover that a chair with a back had appeared beside the door.

“Your guard post?”

“A place to rest,” Viola said. “I agreed to help look after the site. I didn't agree to stand until dinner.”

Adrian shifted the chair half a pace closer to the wall, clear of the walkway, and asked whether she could see both doors from there. She sat down to check and nodded, looking as serious as if she were inspecting a scabbard.

“You really intend to have a knight guard an experiment sitting down?” Luna asked.

“If you'd rather I guarded the chair, I can do that too.”

Luna looked at her, then laughed.

The workshop had a stone floor. Bench Three stood against the inner wall, its legs secured in fitted slots. Beneath the west window ran a dry heat-sink channel filled with broken stone and heat-resistant sand, with no connection to a drain. Luna had an assistant lift the cover and check for collected water. Then she laid a ceramic bridge piece on the bench.

Pulling it out would separate the two specimen holders. A spring would close the source shutter without anyone having to maintain a spell. The removed energy cartridge went into a separate lidded isolation box, where residual heat could dissipate into the stone channel.

“It has to stop even if I'm not standing beside it,” Luna said.

She asked the administrator to try it himself.

The bridge jammed halfway out.

Luna frowned and crouched beside the guide slot. A small bead of fresh protective coating had hardened along the edge and caught the handle. Instead of asking for a harder pull, she dismantled the piece and sent it to Tessa to be smoothed. They would test it again afterward. Moving at all was not the same as stopping reliably.

From her chair, Viola watched the administrator pull the handle a second and third time. The little ceramic piece dropped into the padded catch beneath it. The spring snapped shut, each time making a sound softer than a sword leaving its sheath.

“I like that sound,” Viola said.

“More than my voice?” Luna asked.

“It generally means you've finally stopped.”

Adrian laughed aloud. Luna raised her pen as though she meant to add him to the list of her attackers, then merely circled a space on the inspection sheet.

The morning began with an unpowered evacuation rehearsal. Everyone walked the route from their intended position. Adrian called the stop, the administrator disconnected the supply, Luna left the operating station, and Viola checked that nobody was coming in through the outside door. There was no smoke and no sudden noise designed to frighten anyone.

The rehearsal stopped at the west exit anyway.

A flour delivery driver was pushing a narrow handcart into the yard. Finding a group of people in his way, he raised his delivery slip in bewilderment.

“They told me this way was quicker. The lift in the front yard is still being checked.”

Luna was about to tell him not to use this entrance during experiments when Viola asked, “Where do you usually deliver?”

“The kitchen. Going around the east means the steps. I can't get this cart up them.”

Viola looked down at the plan. On paper, there was a handsome empty passage. In reality, it was the route that brought the kitchen its daily food.

“We can't just write No Entry here and call it finished,” she said.

Luna looked at the two sacks of flour. She crossed out the heat-source check scheduled before lunch and first let the driver complete his delivery. Adrian went with the administrator to inspect the eastern side. There were indeed steps, but a level passage ran beneath the southern eaves. A row of empty crates was all that blocked it.

The porters agreed to move the crates. The administrator arranged the new delivery route and had someone from the kitchen walk it. They left enough space outside the west door to turn freely, and marked the new entrance on the delivery slip.

“A different driver tomorrow needs to be able to find it,” Viola reminded them.

“Draw a map. Don't just write Ask Someone,” Elena said.

She had finished dealing with a commercial-audit reply in the visitors' room and come to see how they were getting on. She redrew the kitchen, making it considerably more recognizable than Luna's shoe-shaped rectangle.

Luna studied the result. “Does your house accept apprentice draftsmen?”

“Learn to draw a door first.”

“My doors are at least more even than my slices of pear tart.”

There was no cart at the west door during the final rehearsal before lunch. Adrian went outside, then looked back. Viola remained in her chair, directing the last assistant toward their assembly point in the courtyard. She had not charged ahead of everyone or been obliged to support anything heavy with her own arms.

He suddenly understood what she was guarding that day: a path that other people could use without her carrying them through it.

While they waited for lunch from the kitchen, Viola took half a stick of white chalk from her cloak pocket, followed by a biscuit wrapped in paper. The biscuit's edge was white too.

“I thought I'd separated them,” she said.

Adrian found two clean paper bags and set the spoiled biscuit aside. He did not suggest that blowing on it would make it clean.

“Your new protection budget needs a biscuit tin.”

“A quartermaster would send that request straight back.”

“Then put it on your own shopping list.”

She looked at the two bags for a moment without speaking.

As a child, she had imagined that a knight kept everything important behind a shield or inside a scabbard. Now that she could not carry a shield on her left arm, she was examining for the first time how to keep a food cart from colliding with people trying to get out.

“If I go back to the garrison, I want the kitchen and clinic staff to walk the routes with us,” she said. “The people drawing the maps shouldn't decide on their behalf how easy the steps are.”

“You'll receive some very specific opinions.”

“More useful than a report that says Everything Is Normal.”

She shared half a clean biscuit with him, solemnly handing over a responsibility that weighed very little.

After lunch, Luna fitted a low-range calibration lamp to the empty frame. This was not another observation of Adrian's scar. The sealed records and medium from that examination had not been brought into the room. Only blank ceramic pieces occupied the holders, with everyone behind the shield in front of the bench.

When the lamp came on, a slender line of light appeared on the right-hand scale. They tested the shutter and independent release. The lamp went dark. The temperature marker beside the channel shifted slightly, then slowly settled.

Luna insisted on waiting to check for residual heat. An assistant withdrew the hand he had already extended toward the source box.

“I thought we could put it away once the needle returned to zero.”

“The needle hasn't told you whether the inside of that box is safe to touch.”

With a clamp, she lifted the outer indicator strip and showed them the heat-sensitive color still fading on its reverse. Waiting would not make the next set of numbers look more impressive. It might prevent a burn.

Viola tried the stop handle beside her with her right hand while still seated. It was slightly too far away; reaching it made her twist at the waist. She had the assistant adjust the chair and control together rather than describe the awkward reach as something she could manage.

Nobody was hurt during the low-power check. Nobody had to stage a heroic rescue. Two cartridges had lost part of their stored charge, and the heat sink needed to cool. That left only assembly work for the rest of the afternoon. There would be no extra lamp tests.

The administrator brought in a stamped notice.

Under its monthly schedule, the regional academy would inspect the tower's instrument register, premises, and storage arrangements on December 15. The letter had been sent before the visitors arrived, and the tower had already acknowledged it. Nobody had heard about that day's trouble and conjured inspectors onto the road.

Luna pinned the notice beside the door.

“Good. They can see the new escape route.”

“And the old maintenance records,” Cecilia said.

“Both. Personal information stays within the appropriate access limits.”

The administrator put the checklist in the public folder. Viola checked the date, then took out the reply about her follow-up examination. She still needed a medical review before her leave ended. Mailing arrangements and time at the workshop could not simply be piled on top of one another.

Luna noticed her crossing out an evening block beside her name.

“You aren't coming back tonight?”

“No. You said there was only assembly left, and the administrator will lock up. I've helped guard the site.”

“You make me sound like a child who'll sneak in and switch on the lamp.”

“Then give me a reason not to worry.”

Luna handed the source key to the administrator and pointed to the Cooling sign on the wall.

Satisfied, Viola stood. She stiffened slightly. Sitting for so long had still left her left shoulder tight. Adrian did not reach to massage it. He asked whether she wanted to rest at the guesthouse. She agreed, walking a little more slowly than she had that morning.

Cecilia was waiting on the porch with two ordinary teacups. Seeing Viola's expression, she asked whether they should go inside instead.

“No need,” Viola said. “I'd like to sit for a while. Please have your tea.”

The three of them settled in a sheltered spot, and Cecilia asked the kitchen for another cup. She had prepared several light subjects for conversation. Her first question nevertheless concerned whether Viola was following her rest schedule. Then she laughed at herself.

“I promised there wouldn't be a medical form.”

“You don't need a form to ask whether a friend is comfortable,” Adrian said.

It was ordinary black tea, slightly astringent. For once, Cecilia did not keep everyone's cup continually filled. She held her own and told them about Mira's letter. Someone had copied the new sheets' dimensions incorrectly, then sewn two together into one exceptionally broad sheet.

“She says it finally fits the big table in the courtyard.”

“Your flask strap still has plenty of possible futures,” Adrian reminded her.

Cecilia looked at him, her smile spreading slowly from the corners of her eyes. “Please don't abandon its dream of becoming a flask strap quite so soon.”

Viola laughed into her tea. She did not have to leave to prove she wasn't tired. Nor did protecting Adrian require her to turn this cup of tea into a patrol.

Toward evening, Adrian walked the short length of the porch with her and saw her to the guesthouse door.

“You had me doing a lot of unfamiliar things today,” she said.

“Sitting?”

“Letting other people work. Watching them finish. Leaving while nothing had gone wrong.”

“The last one sounds worth practicing.”

She looked at him, resting her right hand lightly on the wooden rail.

“I was worried you'd think I hadn't stopped anything.”

“The flour cart would disagree.”

She laughed, then grew serious.

“I'm not only trying to get you to praise me.”

“I know. But I do want to. Especially for not trading your arm for a door that ought to open properly in the first place.”

Viola lowered her head. Her ears were redder than the sunset in the courtyard.

“I could come for a little while tomorrow.”

“See how your shoulder feels first. The chair will wait. And I won't forget you because you rest.”

She looked up as though he had finally said the thing she had most needed to hear that day.

After dinner, Elena spread her corrected map beside the guesthouse window and asked Adrian whether she had made the kitchen too big.

“Judging by where the real power lies around here, I think it's still too small.”

She laughed, rolled up the drawing, and made room beside her. They read their book as usual. The captain finally asked a local fisherman for directions, at the cost of two silver coins and an entire evening's pride.

“He should have asked earlier,” she said.

“Some people have to walk a long way before admitting the road behind a door isn't the one they imagined.”

Elena glanced at him. Rather than make a lesson of it, she leaned lightly against his shoulder.

Downstairs, the west workshop was locked. No cart obstructed the outer door. No knight had been left inside to stand guard until midnight.

On the padded tray beside Bench Three, the two blank carrier plates for tomorrow lay quietly side by side. Luna had labeled them Plate A, P-1, and Plate B, P-2. She placed the public manual, open to the historical betrothal-carrier drawing, beside them.

After one last glance at the ceramic bridge that had yet to be fitted between the plates, she extinguished the final ordinary oil lamp.
