# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Three: The Knight Says She's Responsible for Me
### Chapter 80: Bowing Her Head Won't Bring the Books Back

On November 25, just before the military hearing, Viola discovered that she'd fastened her uniform collar through the wrong buttonhole.

She tried twice with her right hand. The more she hurried, the harder it became to undo. Adrian Reed stood beside her with the water he'd just poured, without immediately reaching to help.

“Should I get someone?”

“I can manage.”

The button slipped back into place.

She regarded the stubborn little object for a while, then quietly changed her answer.

“Please help.”

Adrian gave the cup to the clerk and waited for her nod before stepping closer. He straightened the fabric, eased the button out of the wrong hole, and fastened it properly. Viola held her breath, more rigid than she'd been passing through the outpost's narrow doorway.

“You can breathe.”

“I am breathing.”

“On yesterday's allowance?”

She laughed at last, releasing a breath as her ears reddened. Adrian stepped back and returned her water. He didn't smooth anything beyond her collar.

Elena came up the stairs carrying the authorized attendance list.

“At least your collar hasn't become a fourth disputed order.”

Viola bent over her water and nearly laughed again.

Inside the assembly building, the mood was less comfortable.

This was the first day of the full military hearing. Colonel Ward and the two officers from outside garrisons sat behind the long table, with the military examiner at a side desk. The local command's allegations and Viola's responses had been bound separately. Neither side had chosen how many pages the other could show.

The substituted annex and duplicated total excluded during the honor review remained subject to those earlier decisions. Ward did not have the clerk read them out again as fresh discoveries. She asked the examiner to identify what Viola still needed to answer.

He opened the Alder Watch report.

“Registers potentially containing vital receiving and dispatch records were destroyed during an operation in which she participated. Afterward, the only available witness to the site's long-term activities received financial assistance from House Rosenberg.”

Elena looked up.

The examiner did not claim collusion had been established. He asked the board to examine the failed recovery, contact with the witness, and the risks of the visitors' continued presence. He also requested that Viola be placed under guard at the garrison.

The wooden chairs suddenly felt very hard.

Viola's legal adviser rose and asked that the operation's authority be established before questioning who had made each decision and when. Ward agreed and called on Bellamy to produce the limited inspection authorization and temporary exception to Viola's town restriction.

“Three people entered, with professional escort and rescue personnel at the agreed support position,” Bellamy said. “Blackwood explained the terrain and potential hazards. She was not given patrol command. Reed identified civilian transport materials within the authorized scope. I was responsible for the scene record and evidence-recovery decisions.”

“Did she independently expand the search?”

“No. I invite the board to compare the authorized area with each place we actually entered.”

The map opened before the officers. Adrian could see only the edge of the wall he'd once used to steady himself.

Then came the fire.

Bellamy first relayed the guards' report of the escape, then described the first flames she had personally seen and the sequence in which the wooden structure became unstable. She did not present a relayed account as her own observation or supply a face she had never seen clearly. The rescue personnel then described their distance from the building, the rope, and the evacuation. Their estimate of one interval differed from hers. The clerk recorded both accounts rather than silently making them match.

When Adrian was called, the examiner placed a blank sheet on the table.

“Please draw how you could have recovered the registers.”

He drew the upper platform and the maintenance room below, then marked the guard holding the long hook in the open ground. His pen stopped beside the suspended bundle.

“There was a possible way here. The guard could hook that bundle down from outside. I wouldn't have needed to climb or enter the smoke. But we first needed the long rope I was holding to secure it so it wouldn't fall into the fire.”

“So recovery was possible.”

“Yes. We might have saved a bundle. The spare rope was still on the wagon. Doing this first would have delayed pulling Bell through the window. I chose not to recover the documents first.”

His answer quieted the room for a moment.

“You chose to use the rope to extract Bell.”

“Yes.”

“And abandoned records that might have established Blackwood's innocence?”

Adrian looked at the long hook he'd drawn, waiting in the open ground. The choices in a game never let you hear someone coughing. They never made you consider how long the person below would wait if you took away the rope.

“I didn't know whose innocence the books might establish,” he said. “I could see the man below was losing his grip on the windowsill, and the structure was moving. I chose to send the rope to him first so he could get out. Losing the documents was a possible cost I understood at the time.”

“Do you regret it now?”

The legal adviser began to rise, but Ward lifted a hand first.

“Ask about the options available then. Regret won't produce another coil of rope.”

The examiner paused and changed his questions. Was there another rope nearby? Could the building have been reentered once the rescue team arrived? Had there been an earlier opportunity to transcribe the records?

Adrian answered what he knew. Bellamy and the rescuers explained events outside his view. No one had guaranteed the entire outpost's safety. Nor had they preserved a complete copy of the registers.

Near the end, his chest tightened and he coughed twice.

Ward had him sit and suspended the questions. Someone in the back shifted impatiently. Viola gripped her cup but didn't answer in his place. She waited while he drank and his breathing settled, then turned to the examiner.

It was her turn.

“I'm willing to take responsibility for the entire—”

She stopped.

Adrian looked up. She didn't look back. Her thumb pressed gently against the wooden button in her pocket.

“I'm willing to explain the decisions I made there,” she began again. “I helped determine the extraction route, judged that remaining would increase the danger, and assisted in bringing Bell out. I did not recover the registers. Nor could I restore the upper platform's load-bearing strength.”

The examiner asked why, as the person most familiar with military installations, she hadn't identified the instability sooner.

“I reported the hazards visible from outside to Bellamy. I couldn't see the concealed structural members. If the investigation identifies something I should have noticed and missed, show me where and I'll answer.”

“You won't accept responsibility for the failure?”

“I'll accept responsibility for my mistakes. But my signature can't rewrite other people's decisions. And I can't claim I saw something I didn't.”

It wasn't an elegant speech. She even hesitated after “my mistakes.” But it was harder for her to say than a declaration assuming all responsibility would have been.

Ward told the clerk to record it exactly.

Next, the hearing examined Martin's condition.

Dr. Shaw provided only the necessary medical opinion: he still needed treatment and was unfit for a long transfer or an extended appearance that day. With his consent, an independent adviser had attended preliminary questioning, and Bellamy had recorded his words so the board could prepare further questions. That did not eliminate the opportunity to challenge his account.

Martin said he'd worked as the outpost keeper. He had seen wagons unload long poles, bundled metal components, and packages of cloth. He had helped count some external packages. He couldn't remember every date and had not personally seen how those goods were used in the village. After asking about unpaid wages and arrangements to leave, he had been locked downstairs.

At the end, the examiner asked, “All of that is his account?”

“Yes,” Bellamy said. “The lock's condition and where we opened it are separately recorded at the scene. His wages and the circumstances of his earlier confinement still require independent verification.”

Elena then submitted the funding agreement. The examiner paused at the visiting arrangements, which required her visits to be registered as well.

“If Bell's testimony harms your family, will you stop payment?”

“Confirmed treatment and accommodation expenses will still be paid against receipts.”

“Are you willing to stake your family's reputation on his reliability?”

“I can't guarantee his memory. I can confirm that the payment terms I've submitted are genuine and open to examination.”

Her uninjured hand rested on the table. The other remained lightly bandaged.

“If he believes someone in my family was involved, let him explain specifically. We didn't submit our commercial records on the condition that we would hear only good news.”

Ward checked the independent adviser's statement. No record presently showed the sponsor controlling Martin's testimony. His continued protection would still be administered by the responsible authorities, rather than relying on Elena's promise.

During the recess, Adrian found Viola in the corridor, looking at a small patch of frosted grass in the yard. She didn't reach toward her empty hip.

“I nearly said it again.”

“I heard you change it.”

“Saying I'll take only my own share sounds like retreating.”

Adrian sat beside her, leaving a little room between their cups.

“You found that harder to say than accepting everything.”

She turned toward him, weariness lingering in her gray-green eyes.

“I wasn't trying to make them think I was brave.”

“I know. You don't want the books we couldn't bring back to crush everyone else.”

She lowered her eyes and slowly exhaled.

Someone called the next witness. Viola didn't rise. When she confirmed it wasn't her name, she sat a little longer. That small pause was something Adrian suddenly wanted to preserve for her.

“Your collar looks good now,” he said.

She blinked and touched it.

“That isn't today's most important matter.”

“I'd like to know a few less important things today too.”

Her mouth finally curved. “Such as?”

“What you'd like for dinner.”

She considered carefully, as though she'd finally been given a question that wouldn't affect the fate of the whole town.

“Something hot. And not too difficult to eat with one hand.”

“I have a reasonable chance of managing that.”

That afternoon, Rusk presented the extent of West Slope Depot's checks. He didn't treat the arsenal annex's outstanding reply as an answer already received. He merely identified the differences between actual verified receipts and the transport plans used in the old summary.

The examiner asked that conclusions about the new commercial route wait until the independent counterparts arrived. Viola's adviser agreed to verification, while also asking that any necessary interim restrictions be justified separately. A reply still on the road did not automatically validate a request for guarded confinement.

Ward made no final determination that day. The existing town conditions continued. The temporary inspection exception was over. The hearing would resume the following morning with the supply records already requested. Any adjustment to personal restrictions would be based on specific risks and verified evidence, and issued in writing.

Afterward, an elderly patrolman waited at the foot of the steps. He saluted Viola, then asked whether she could add her signature to his training attendance sheet.

She looked at it and shook her head.

“Go to the officer currently responsible for you.”

He sighed. “It's only a missing name.”

“All the more reason to have someone who was there write it.”

He took the sheet back and answered seriously. Viola watched him leave without using that small act of obedience to recover an hour of command she no longer held.

Dinner really had been changed to a soft stew. Adrian placed a little dish of cut bread within Viola's reach. Elena watched him hand her an extra spoon by mistake.

“I have two now.”

“You could give one to whoever asked the most questions today.”

“I'm afraid he'd start asking where the soup came from.”

Viola laughed. Cecilia did too, then steadied her water cup.

Bellamy appeared in the doorway when they had finished their first bowls. She held a registered express letter and a stack of documents retained separately by the ferry office and the carrier.

“The arsenal annex's reply has arrived. And we have a wagon. We can begin comparing the transit reference on the sleeve, the ferry's vehicle number, and the carrier's counterpart records.”

Adrian set down his spoon and swallowed his bread first.

They hadn't recovered the burned books.

But that wagon had left written traces in more places than a single outpost.
