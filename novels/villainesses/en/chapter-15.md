# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume One: A Betrothal Written in Blood
### Chapter 15: Have We Met Before?

On the morning of September twenty-fifth, Adrian Reed spent an unusually long time staring into a mirror.

He wasn't adjusting his bow tie.

He wasn't checking his cuffs.

He was turning his head from side to side, studying his own face.

Martha came in with freshly laundered clothes and watched him for a full minute.

"Mr. Reed, is there something wrong with your face?"

"I'm trying to establish something."

"What?"

"Whether I look unremarkably ordinary."

Martha examined him.

"I wouldn't waste too much time on that question."

Adrian's self-esteem suffered a wound from which it might never recover.

Yesterday, they'd seen a signature in the academy's supplementary loan register.

*Adrian Reed.*

Written seven years ago.

The handwriting was disturbingly similar to his own, but similarity wasn't proof.

Today, Nora had secured permission for them to examine another piece of the puzzle.

A portrait dating from the same period.

As far as Adrian remembered, he'd spent the last seven years in his own world, playing games like *Crown of Fate* and leading a perfectly ordinary life.

He'd only woken up at the Rosenberg estate days ago.

How could someone have borrowed a book under his name here seven years earlier?

And what about the scar on his left hand?

"If there's really a portrait of someone who looks like me," he muttered, "I hope the fellow at least has better clothes."

Martha handed him his coat.

"You're becoming stranger by the day."

"Was I normal before?"

"Not particularly."

Adrian decided he was finished with the mirror.

At breakfast, Elena was already sitting by the window.

She placed a small silver case in front of him.

"Your hand."

Adrian instinctively hid his left one.

"What about it?"

"The other hand. The cut hasn't completely healed."

He glanced at the shallow wound from the broken glass on September nineteenth.

"It hardly hurts anymore."

"Let me see."

She didn't seize his wrist.

She waited.

Adrian placed his right hand on the table.

The cut had scabbed over without swelling or redness. Elena examined it, then pushed the case toward him.

"Medicine. Martha says you keep forgetting it."

"You sound like a physician's supervisor."

"Then remember to take care of it yourself."

At the far end of the table, Duke Edmund lowered his newspaper with a disapproving grunt.

"You're visiting an academy archive. Why does this sound as though someone's sending you off to war?"

"Because my administrative aide can't even remember to treat a small injury," Elena replied.

Adrian straightened.

"Your Grace, I promise I won't injure myself or touch anything I haven't been given permission to touch."

The duke looked at him.

"Last time you said that, the academy sent us two additional archival application fees."

"...I'll try to keep expenses down."

Elena laughed quietly.

The academy's old portrait room was quieter than the main archive.

Sheer curtains covered the tall windows, and portraits of former scholars and professors hung along the walls. Numbered labels, white gloves, and a shaded examination lamp lay on a gray-green tablecloth.

Nora Bell had prepared the reading register in advance.

"First the catalogue. Then the chief archivist will bring out the picture. You're not to move the frame yourselves."

Adrian nodded.

"Don't worry. I've developed a conditioned response to the words 'hands off.'"

"Elena's trained you well," Nora said with a laugh.

Elena glanced at her.

"This isn't training."

"Of course not."

Nora's smile widened.

Adrian focused on the catalogue before the conversation became even more dangerous.

The portrait wasn't a formal painting of a known individual.

It had originally accompanied identification documents in an old judicial case, then been moved to the academy's collection when the original file could no longer be assigned to a current office.

The entry read:

*Unknown male, approximately twenty-five years old. Likeness commissioned from an artist on the basis of witness descriptions.*

"So nobody sat for it?" Adrian asked.

"Not according to the record," Nora said. "That's why a resemblance doesn't establish identity. The artist might have misunderstood a description—or made the subject more handsome."

"Is the date certain?"

"The painting and its payment receipt were entered into the collection seven years ago. The dates are consistent."

Adrian stared at the sealed frame.

"Then at the very least, someone needed to identify a man who looked like this seven years ago."

"That's a fair conclusion."

Two archivists set the thin wooden frame flat on the table and removed the protective cloth.

The portrait showed a young man in a dark coat. His hair was slightly curled, his posture too casual for an official likeness, as though someone had dragged him away from his chair and told him to stand still.

Adrian stopped breathing normally.

The brow.

The nose.

The mouth.

Almost his own face.

Almost, but not quite a reflection.

The painted man's eyes looked exhausted. A faint, reluctant smile lingered at one corner of his mouth. Near the bottom of his left ear was a tiny pale mole.

Adrian touched his own ear.

There was a small raised mark in almost the same place.

"What is it?" Elena asked.

"That mole..."

He stopped himself.

A witness might have described such a mark. An artist might even have added it by chance.

None of that explained why his hands had gone cold.

Nora placed the catalogue entry alongside the painting.

"The portrait itself isn't named. The person who registered it left an identification code. The final two digits were later struck through."

Elena leaned closer.

"The same format as yesterday's loan record?"

"The first four digits match."

Adrian moved toward the lamp and stopped, waiting for the archivist to reposition it.

The man in the painting appeared to stare straight back at him.

He'd once believed this world was nothing more than a game he'd played.

Now he was looking at a painting created years before his arrival.

Of someone with his face.

"Mr. Reed?" Nora asked. "Are you all right?"

Adrian tried to keep his voice steady.

"I've just discovered I might not be as one of a kind as I'd hoped."

He meant it as a joke.

Nobody laughed.

Not even him.

Elena came to stand beside him without touching his arm.

"Let's get some water."

"We haven't finished."

"The picture isn't going anywhere."

"The register nearly got transferred yesterday."

"I've already asked Mr. Fenn to preserve this one formally. You can leave it for five minutes."

Adrian looked at her.

Then nodded.

Around her, he was slowly learning that he didn't have to pretend he was never frightened.

Outside the portrait room was a small stone courtyard.

Ivy grew along the walls. A few yellowing leaves floated on the pond. Somewhere beyond the arches, students were reciting lessons. It sounded like any ordinary school.

Adrian sat on a bench with a cup of water Nora had given him.

"My lady. If there really was someone here seven years ago who looked exactly like me, what would you think?"

"First I'd ask whether you had relatives."

"And if I didn't?"

"We'd speak to the artist and the witnesses."

"What if none of them could be found?"

Elena stood before him, composed.

"Then we'd admit we didn't know. Aren't you always reminding me that guesses aren't grounds for a conviction?"

Adrian smiled weakly.

"Using my own words against me hardly seems fair."

"Then stop making such good points."

He studied her.

"Have you considered the possibility that people knew me before I arrived here?"

She didn't answer at once.

The clock tower chimed in the distance, startling the fish in the pond.

Adrian expected her to ask again how he'd known the date of her execution.

Instead, she said quietly, "When I was a child, I used to have a recurring dream."

He sat straighter.

"What sort of dream?"

"There was a tall clock tower. Whenever the bells finished ringing, someone would walk away from me."

"Could you see who it was?"

"Not his face."

Elena rested one hand on the bench, searching her memory.

"I remember him reaching toward me. His left hand. There was a curved scar on the back."

Adrian went cold.

"Like a crescent?"

She looked up, genuinely startled.

"How did you know?"

He didn't hide his hand this time.

The crescent scar was pale in the afternoon light but unmistakably shaped.

Elena stared at it.

She didn't grab him or cry out.

Slowly, she held out her hand and waited.

"May I?"

Adrian nodded.

She took his left hand gently, careful not to touch the scar itself.

"The shape is familiar," she said. "But I can't possibly know it's the same mark. It was a dream I had years ago."

"Have you ever seen a scar like it in real life?"

"No."

Adrian looked at her.

"Why didn't you mention the dream before?"

Elena gave an uncomfortable smile.

"Because telling a servant I'd only just met that I dreamed about his hand would sound even worse than the stories in the newspapers."

Adrian finally laughed.

"I might have mistaken it for a proposal."

"Say that again and I'll send you back to the servants' dining hall."

"Too late. I'm earning triple wages now."

She released his hand.

The humor faded, leaving them both quiet.

"There's something I haven't told you," Adrian said.

Elena waited.

"Since September seventeenth, some of my memories have felt wrong. I remember things that were supposed to happen. Places I'd never actually visited."

"Like my execution?"

"Yes."

"And the man in the painting?"

"I don't know." He met her eyes. "I remember someone dying, but not his face. I don't know whether I saw the future, the past, or someone else's memories."

Elena looked down for a long moment.

"That explains why you kept trying to run."

"At first. I didn't want to die."

"And now?"

"I'm still afraid of dying. But I want to know why someone went to so much trouble to decide where you'd end up before you had a chance to choose."

She didn't demand the rest of his story.

She didn't call it a curse.

"At least this time you haven't simply said you can't explain."

"I still haven't explained much."

"Then we'll keep talking."

A leaf dropped onto the pond.

Adrian wondered whether the dead man with the crescent scar and the sound of the bells outside the courtroom belonged to the same mystery.

He wasn't going to turn that thought into fact.

"Do you think I'm suspicious?" he asked.

"Of course."

"...You could have hesitated."

"You are suspicious," Elena said. "But even when you were scared, you told me what you knew. I want to keep investigating with you."

Something in Adrian's chest eased.

When they returned to the portrait room, Nora and two archivists were checking the frame's preservation record.

"Good timing," Nora said. "I've found the original framing invoice."

It dated back seven years. The old Judicial Records Transfer Office had paid an academy-affiliated portrait artist to produce the likeness. An unidentified witness had certified that the image was *broadly accurate*.

"Broadly accurate," Adrian repeated.

"So it doesn't prove it's you," Nora said. "It doesn't even prove the artist copied the man's actual face faithfully."

Elena nodded.

"We only need to understand why this portrait was filed with the old case."

Under the archivist's supervision, Nora had the protective backing removed.

"Mr. Fenn authorized us to inspect the catalogue label, not to disturb the sealed backing paper."

A thin strip of paper emerged.

It bore a circular Judicial Office crest from a discontinued design. The numbered field appeared blank.

Adrian leaned closer.

Along the lower edge, he noticed faint embossed characters.

"That's not a framing number."

Nora fetched the old numbering guide.

"You're right. This format was used by the old court before the judicial reforms."

Mr. Fenn came over and examined it.

His expression darkened.

"This portrait wasn't originally intended to identify a missing person."

"Then what?" Elena asked.

The archivist pointed to the faint heading.

"A case file."

Adrian felt a mild warmth along the scar on his left hand.

It didn't glow. It didn't reveal a message or suddenly grant him knowledge.

It simply reminded him that he was here, looking at something he shouldn't recognize.

He bent toward the label.

It read:

**Old Capital Court — Discontinued Case No. R-074.**

Seven years ago, someone resembling Adrian had been painted, registered, and filed away as part of a case that no longer officially existed.

Adrian looked at Elena.

She was already looking at him.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we find out what that case was."

He nodded.

But there was one question he couldn't bring himself to ask.

If this portrait belonged to somebody's old trial records, why did the face inside the file have to be his?
