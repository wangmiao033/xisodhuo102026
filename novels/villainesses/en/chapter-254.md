# Chapter 254: What Still Counted After the Lamp Went Out

On April 17, Luna refused the flower Adrian brought to the academy.

“It sheds pollen. Am I supposed to identify that as another ancient conspiracy?”

He looked at the half-open white blossom and admitted he hadn't considered the laboratory. She told him to leave it with the attendant outside, not throw it away. Then she offered her uninjured hand for his other bag. Her assessment of ordinary breakfast was considerably kinder.

Yesterday's examination hadn't granted immediate eligibility. This morning medical staff checked her palm and vision again, shortened her reading period, and required another qualified reader to establish the baseline first. Her right palm must bear no force; her left couldn't sustain guidance either. She could make one brief reading before a mounted medium and leave at any discomfort.

She didn't bargain for twice as long. Instead she asked whether, if she stopped early, the partial findings would remain rather than disappear as an embarrassing failure.

Yes, the examiner said. She signed for her actual participation, leaving the space for a later, unapproved round empty.

There were no places for the five marked women in the isolation room. The custodian checked the seal of an old pattern-calibration plate brought from storage. Once used to inspect copied transfer patterns, it was neither a Crown of Destiny key nor powered by the core. The new node housings remained disconnected in another room.

Adrian mistook an ink line near its edge for a crack. An assistant changed his viewing angle: that was a later registration mark. The real missing corner was on the back.

“If you managed antiquities,” Luna said, “the kingdom's entire ink supply would become calamity evidence.”

“Which is why today I keep flowers outside.”

“A position requiring judgment.”

Her amusement faded at the assembled apparatus. The old bracket crushing their new housing had demonstrated that separation on paper wasn't physical isolation. The shutdown operator showed the independent disconnection point between charge unit and plate. The examiner checked for any link to the old system, then ordinary light exposed the sealed ends. This wasn't about trusting the right person. Someone who trusted nobody could inspect it too.

The other reader completed his initial examination. Repeated impressions existed beneath the surface, too faint to distinguish original manufacture from later overwriting. Both possibilities were recorded before Luna could supply her preferred answer. She put on narrow side-light shields and asked her assistant to lower the mount, without lifting her right palm to steady it.

The room arrangements gave every worker a place and an unobstructed way to leave it. Adrian could see the assistant checking that clearance rather than simply accepting the painted marks on the floor. The lesson from the relay had not been confined to a report nobody touched again. Adrian stayed in the marked observation space beside the window. He'd wanted to stand near enough to hear every breath, but that blocked the assistant's retreat. He moved back himself, resenting the distance and knowing resentment gave him no reason to cross it.

The charge unit supplied the approved small input. No golden radiance appeared. Fine lines emerged like writing through damp paper. Luna described directions while the recorder drew on a separate sheet. Her immediate speech wasn't treated as a confirmed instruction.

The first layer formed an intact turning line. The second broke beside an old reference number, its end perhaps worn away, perhaps pressed deeper. She requested the same intensity, not an increase. The other reader recorded another angle. An assistant slowly replaced a shield whose allotted interval had elapsed, so changed lighting wouldn't be mistaken for changed traces.

A small clink reached Adrian. Luna's left hand had caught her cup. Water circled the tray, untouched. Her eyes remained on the plate.

“What color is the white line now?” medical staff asked.

A pause. “Gray.”

It shouldn't have been gray.

“Stop,” Luna said.

The operator disconnected the charge unit and dealt with measurable residue. Luna closed her eyes without reaching for one final symbol. The assistant removed the small mirror so she could see the room directly rather than an afterimage. Adrian waited for permission to approach, then laid her shawl across her knees. He didn't need to embrace her to prove he'd arrived quickly.

Had they been almost finished? Nobody could answer. More of the second layer remained unread. Almost was a wish easily attached to an interrupted line.

“I want to swear at somebody.”

“You can choose me.”

“Stop claiming every position. You didn't manufacture my body.”

Her quiet voice stopped his offered apologies. He wanted her distress placed in his hands so he could feel useful; that still asked her to manage his anxiety. Water or quiet? Quiet first, she said, then changed her mind and requested a fresh cup.

Medical staff ended all magical work for her that day. Color distortion and fine tremors eased slowly with rest; no medicine returned what she'd spent. Follow-up was necessary, and she mustn't handle precision instruments alone tonight. Choosing to stop hadn't cured her injured palm. Irritated, she listened to every instruction.

The recorder asked how to describe the ending. Actual symptoms, Luna said, not brave persistence or emotional instability. She had made the decision to stop. Being helped didn't turn her into a nameless incident.

Outside the resting room, cloths swelled together on a washing line and settled separately. As a child, she'd imagined the greatest practitioners never tired first. Later she'd realized storytellers often stopped before they fell.

“Want to change the ending?” Adrian asked.

“I want an expensive lunch added.”

“That sounds easier.”

“I haven't said who's paying.”

He smiled and put the menu before her. Unable to read the smallest print, she requested it aloud instead of disguising help as a joke. He mispronounced a dish; she corrected him twice with enough energy to call it evidence she retained some academic ability.

Outside, her assistant argued with an academy administrator. Since the experiment ended early, he wanted the remaining room period surrendered and the shutdown and medical staff released. The assistant pointed at the plate's untransferred case: clearance, residue checks, and observation didn't complete themselves when casting stopped.

Hearing her name repeatedly, Luna braced to rise. Adrian asked whether she'd handle it or wanted him to hear the whole matter. He didn't close the door and choose for her. After a pause, she sent him, with instructions not to threaten anyone in her name.

The administrator had a genuine next-group booking. Adrian asked him to read the final page of the allotted period, which included clearance and observation. The next group had been called early by mistake. Luna leaving her chair wouldn't repair that message. They found its sender, clarified the error, and directed the waiting group to an available ordinary classroom rather than leaving two groups resenting one another at the door.

Through the partly open door, Luna heard it all. Was he enjoying finding something he did better than her? Tomorrow, he offered, he could demonstrate admitting he'd miscounted coins to a vendor insistent the change was right.

“That technique sounds dangerous. Independent witnesses required.”

Her assistant returned to a smile that no longer seemed entirely forced. The residue examiner stayed at the assigned post. When the custodian resealed the plate and read the number outside her door, Luna didn't have to prove again that she deserved their time.

Before noon, the other reader brought the organized results. Existing marks and the recorded drawings could be compared without further input. He didn't ask her back to the apparatus. The broken line did overlay an earlier directional symbol; its edges carried consistent deliberate pressure marks. One plate couldn't establish the entire system's operation. It did contradict a claim that nobody had ever modified it.

The adjacent number was more useful. Not a name or automatic proof of guilt, it matched an entry in the old catalog transferred with the plate. The entry recorded a pattern revision paid for by the royal household. The page reference was clear, but its underlying document belonged to another volume not yet retrieved.

Luna told them not to wait for her nod merely because she sat by a bed. The independent examiner should decide what the record supported; the custodian should confirm the number. Their judgments needn't fit inside her exhaustion. She added only where her own reading had been least certain, ensuring uncertainty appeared on the drawing too.

Watching that small blank, Adrian understood they hadn't exchanged her health for a complete answer. She'd paid a real cost and obtained perhaps only another door to knock on. Insisting the result must justify her suffering because she'd already suffered would resemble the old system too closely.

Lunch cost more than he'd expected. Luna chose a little jam pastry and declared it worth supplying beyond the end of the world. Tomorrow's shop could still sell it, he pointed out. Exactly, she said: somebody must survive to suggest improvements to the cook.

Then she quieted. If she could never research as before, would he transfer her to some safe, useless corner?

He didn't promise recovery.

“I'd ask what you wanted to do. Then admit I couldn't help with everything.”

“Am I allowed to hate that answer?”

“No application necessary.”

She tried to roll the menu with her left hand. A tremor let it slide into her lap. He waited until she asked, then rolled it into a small pointer. Indicating the laundry outside, she commanded him to fetch the flower. This wasn't a laboratory. They could tolerate something with no evidential value.

That afternoon, checked copies of the number and catalog went to Isabella. Each participant signed only their own work. Luna didn't guess a missing page into a royal secret order. The princess needed the corresponding account through ordinary archive procedures, then comparison with existing sacrifice records, before deciding what could be published. The old system's cost couldn't rest on one exhausted woman pointing at light.

Nothing in the letter made a damaged hand proof of a royal intention. They had numbers, retained material, and work still to do. Adrian left that work with the people actually responsible instead of asking Luna to certify the whole afternoon from her pillow. The flower had opened halfway when Adrian returned. In a borrowed bottle too wide at the neck, it leaned without dignity. Luna said it resembled him just dismissed from an aristocratic banquet.

“I thought you'd say a heroic researcher.”

“You'd have to blow on it until it couldn't stand.”

He reached to straighten it. She stopped him. Let it lean. Settling against her pillow, she closed her eyes without asking about the unread symbol. Her assistant set down an empty tray outside and walked away. Luna didn't reopen her eyes to ask which task came next.

The laboratory lamp was out. The preserved observations, the number still awaiting verification, and the stop she'd spoken herself hadn't ceased to count with the light.
