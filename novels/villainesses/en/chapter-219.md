# Chapter 219: No Convenient Villain

On March 18, the first thing Oswin brought them was not an idol but an old drawing of a courtyard wall.

Small houses stood beside it, ending at a well. Different diagonal strokes marked damage and later demolition. An ink-dark patch covered the house nearest the well. The Temple recorder explained that water had stained the sheet; the blot couldn't tell them whether that building survived.

Adrian had imagined a terrible voice or an illusion they couldn't escape by closing their eyes. Instead, they needed an ordinary lamp to read the drawing.

“These are your own records,” he said.

“Some of them,” Oswin replied.

They remained upstairs at the academy. Custodians brought the originals under agreed reading arrangements. Unavailable volumes appeared on a separate sheet; nobody disguised those gaps with the phrase complete archive. Luna's assistant arranged her reading stand. Her right palm stayed supported. No magical projection would be attempted today.

One historical calamity in the central tables concerned the old settlement on the southern canal. It wasn't the western relay-yard incident. Losses in different places couldn't be folded into Adrian's single mistake. Nor could the historical year yet be reliably converted into their present calendar. The Temple's continuous duty references initially established only the sequence within this group of records.

Oswin asked them to read a falling number.

Luna first asked which scale had produced it, and whether it matched the later series with the warning line changed to B-eight.

It didn't.

“Then two sevens don't belong beside each other simply because they're both sevens.”

Oswin had the relevant annotations displayed. The table included its old scale description and measuring positions. A missing page remained missing. Adrian noticed Luna hadn't relaxed after raising the objection. Incompatible scales proved neither that Oswin was right nor that the houses had never fallen.

A municipal archivist opened the independent material: bridgeworks supply and stoppage records, a rescue transfer register, and burial entries. They hadn't all come from one Temple cupboard or been recopied today from Oswin's tables.

At first, the bridgeworkers' plan didn't fit.

The well appeared across the street. Hope flared so sharply that Adrian almost seized on the difference as grounds to dismiss the whole display. Then he caught Elena watching him, and felt ashamed.

He wanted counterevidence because he didn't want her sent where any list directed. That couldn't entitle him to hope the dead were merely badly drawn houses.

The archivist produced a boundary sketch made before the bridge repair. The works plan looked inward from the old bridgehead; the Temple drawing looked outward from the well. Reversing the view alone still wasn't enough. An examiner compared independently recorded distances between bridge supports, unchanged stone steps, and the canal bend. Three fixed positions agreed. The misplaced well acquired an explanation that didn't depend on what either side wanted.

Adrian asked the clerk to retain their initial objection and its correction. He wouldn't pretend he'd understood immediately.

Names in the burial register were covered. Authorized comparison concerned historical references and date fields. Elena read line by line, then asked why one entry had no matching receipt.

“That item hasn't been found,” the archivist said. “The next entry cannot stand in for it.”

They left the gap. Other rescue transfers still independently matched burial entries, relief distributions, and evacuation lists. The loss wasn't merely a figure proclaimed by the Temple. Reading where someone had ceased to acknowledge receipt slowed the pages down.

Cecilia stopped at a small correction in a rescue register. A relative initially recorded as missing had been found at another shelter the next day. The entry below had no such ending. After a list of places searched, it recorded the return of clothing.

“Don't copy only the second one,” she told the clerk. “The person who survived was real too. Keep both.”

Oswin stood on the other side of the table and didn't hurry them. The clerk waited until Cecilia had finished before turning the page.

A tiny mark on an evacuation plan resembled a handprint. Adrian eventually learned it was staining from damp, not a child's testimony. The truth was difficult enough without requiring every blemish to perform another tragedy.

Before noon, the custodian displayed an associated works exhibit: part of a distorted window frame and broken glass in a shallow tray. An old deposit record identified its location; an independent repair account documented its removal. It supported abnormal damage at that building. The glass alone couldn't establish which order caused it, and touching it wouldn't reveal anyone's dying words.

Adrian kept his left hand in his lap.

Oswin didn't ask him to touch anything. He had the tray kept at a safe distance, then indicated records of local shielding brought into operation at two different times. Corresponding rescue entries showed periods in which people could still be transported.

“You can investigate these differences further,” he said. “But stop treating calamity as a word I invented to conceal wrongdoing.”

“Nobody here says the fallen houses are imaginary,” Viola replied.

“People outside are saying that human authorship of an oracle means the calamity was staged.”

Cecilia briefly closed her eyes. She hadn't said that. It couldn't become harmless merely because the words weren't hers.

“I will correct what I can. You mustn't count that correction as agreement with what you propose afterward.”

“Then keep reading,” Oswin said.

Luna's brief reading session ended here. Before leaving, she dictated a comparison request: keep terrain, population, and connection conditions separate; the presence or absence of shielding wasn't a perfect experiment. She also left a question: did effective local protection establish that designated sacrifice was the only method? The clerk retained it. Works staff would examine the afternoon material without waiting for her return or counting later estimates as her agreement.

The academy kitchen served lunch in a smaller room next door. Setting down the soup jug, the woman delivering it hesitated. If the prophecies were false, could her sister stop queuing for temporary shelter papers? The queue cost working time. She'd already asked twice.

Cecilia didn't immediately offer an answer that would save half a day. Which office issued the papers? Was there a current relocation notice?

The woman didn't know. People in the street said the Temple's books had been exposed, so there was nothing to fear now.

Adrian lowered his bread. Yesterday's assistant had wanted to announce good news. Today, good news had bypassed them and reached someone trying to earn a living.

“We've found that some explanations were invented,” Cecilia said. “I haven't established that your sister's street is safe. Don't cancel her existing arrangements because you heard I was investigating oracles.”

“Who should we believe, then?”

That was harder than the numbered questions next door. Cecilia didn't say Believe me. She asked the woman to bring the responsible office's name from the papers, so the current notice could be checked through the civic desk. If the office offered only a prayer with no practical scope, Cecilia would accompany her to ask again. She couldn't use her title to promise her sister would be safe tomorrow.

After thinking, the woman agreed to bring the papers. She also warned them that the soup would develop a layer of grease if they let it cool. Cecilia thanked her, then leaned back and slowly exhaled.

Elena asked whether she still wanted to return to the records. She did. She also wished, just now, for one answer that required no qualifications.

“I thought exposing what was false would make the truth easier to understand.”

“Perhaps it only means we can't hide behind the false part anymore,” Adrian said.

It wasn't comfort. They all knew that. Viola moved the jug to her right, making it easier to serve herself and sparing Cecilia a reach across her injured arm. For the remainder of lunch they talked about ordinary things. Even the kitchen's oversized pieces of carrot deserved a brief argument.

The afternoon's evidence was no easier.

Following activation of local shielding, some areas had suffered less damage and fewer interruptions to transport than places where it hadn't connected in time. Terrain, location, and original population differed too. Following Luna's written request from the morning, the works examiner marked those differences separately. Two neighboring tables weren't a perfect experiment.

Nor could the examination erase evidence that the installations might genuinely have saved lives. Independent rescue times, works repairs, and evacuation routes supported enough to rule out casually shutting every facility down simply because its manipulation was hateful.

The clerk read Luna's remaining question. “Does this establish that designated sacrifice is the only method?”

“Nor does it establish that you already possess another,” Oswin replied.

Adrian almost supplied a rebuttal for the absent Luna, then stopped. She had asked what the evidence established. She hadn't invited him to dress her judgment in courage incapable of retreat.

“We don't have one yet,” he answered for himself. “Please record that separately from her question.”

Works staff continued while Luna rested elsewhere. Later estimates weren't attributed to her. She would receive the written findings at the agreed time, not a demand to answer immediately that afternoon.

The Temple recorder produced a combined summary describing both parties as endorsing the tables. Isabella objected immediately. They had checked particular portions, not the entire compilation or calculations they had yet to see.

The clerk read back the limits. Oswin listened and agreed to retain the unverified portions as such. Establishing that a calamity was real hadn't magically supplied every missing page.

Adrian realized the hardest thing wasn't Oswin refusing every concession. It was that he could concede what he could afford to lose, leaving the difficult question standing.

Outside the reading room, Elena didn't immediately take his arm.

“You were pleased when the well appeared in the wrong place.”

He looked down. “Yes.”

“So was I.”

She wasn't comforting him by making their mistakes equal. She was admitting her own eagerness for one discrepancy that could overturn the frightening drawing.

“I want you alive,” he said. “So badly it makes me mean.”

“Wanting me alive isn't mean. What we do after seeing something we didn't want to see is where the choice begins.”

Only then did she ask whether he'd walk with her. The rain had stopped. Wet leaves scattered the academy paths. Viola and her assistant took the level section ahead; Isabella waited for her carriage beneath the eaves.

Cecilia wouldn't join them. She intended to visit the meal service and explain the part she could responsibly explain, without names or private historical papers for circulation.

Adrian accompanied her to the fork in the path and asked whether she wanted him along.

“Not today. Every difficult thing I have to say needn't become your assignment.”

“All right.”

She paused, then smiled. If she returned late, would the kitchen still save dinner for her?

“I'll ask. I won't give your explanation for you.”

“A useful division of labor.”

Elena heard and didn't treat ordinary kindness as something she needed to win back. She warned Adrian away from a puddle, then stepped into another shallow one herself. Mud speckled her shoe. When he bent to inspect it, she laughed and told him not to register a damp shoe as another calamity.

They laughed together, then continued slowly down the path.

No document had proved Oswin entitled to choose their deaths. No document had turned the dead outside into a lie that could conveniently be crossed out.

Until they found another road, they would have to remain awake to both.
