# Chapter 299: Each in Her Own Sunlight

The first argument at the new-system celebration on July 12 concerned a banner.

The organizer had beautifully lettered “Five Ladies Bind Their Fates Together.” The red fabric had arrived. Elena asked what, precisely, they were binding. If it meant betrothals, tomorrow's registration concerned only her and Adrian.

“It's symbolic. Everyone standing together forever.”

“I'm leaving at three,” Luna said.

Her right palm was supported beneath the canopy. She would not pull fabric with her left hand either. Her assistant showed the organizer her confirmed attendance window. She had come to discuss the institute's public, nonhazardous findings, not donate every future afternoon to a symbol.

Cecilia suggested the celebration's official name. Viola observed that the low banner obscured a side exit. Isabella asked who would pay for alterations. Temporary workers must not lose wages because five women had failed to arrange themselves as imagined.

Adrian reached for the organizer's scissors, caught himself solving everything without asking, and consulted the actual site supervisor. The decorating crew could replace the center panel. Their agreed wages remained intact, and the money would not come from meals.

Someone proposed another entrance beneath the corrected banner, so the audience could applaud. The women had already begun their separate work. The announcer explained the correction without calling them all back. Fixing one mistaken arrangement did not require a second grand performance, or another round of waiting while everyone was gathered into the right position beneath a ribbon.

The new banner mentioned neither betrothals nor forever. At least it no longer blocked the exit.

The inspected municipal courtyard offered morning explanations, neighborhood stalls, and short musical performances. Nobody had borrowed the palace to declare the world healed. No one had garlanded the old apparatus for visitors to touch. Maintenance continued, with replacement shifts, pay, and transport confirmed by the responsible staff.

The women had received individual invitations. Isabella explained public budget access. Cecilia brought ordinary-service information. Viola worked with the passage-assistance group. Luna offered a short research session. Elena joined her relief network's actual managers to explain what they could provide. None of the invitations required them to escort Adrian.

His own shift involved explaining old and corrected diagrams at the public information table. An informed replacement would take over afterward. He asked Elena if he looked too ordinary. She indicated his crooked tie in the mirror.

“You can redo it yourself, or let me help.”

“I request assistance, provided you don't tie my windpipe into the bow.”

Laughing, she loosened it. The nervous servant who had entered that September ball still couldn't manage his tie. At least he no longer had to guess whether she approached out of duty, command, or desire.

The first research question came from a tea vendor. Could the burden-sharing apparatus spare his stove from ever needing repairs?

“No,” Luna said.

“Half as many repairs?”

“Today's exhibit doesn't establish that either.”

Disappointed, he examined the empty model behind glass. Rather than offering a grander promise, she had her assistant hand him the public maintenance explanation. He found the small temperature-reading diagram useful. Could he put it in his back room?

“You may take the public version. It isn't new operating permission for your stove.”

“I just want my apprentice to stop checking by touch.”

She smiled and explained which reminders were general and which applied only to the particular trial. A research institute that promised no eternity had offered something useful to an ordinary person.

There were no human connections on the demonstration table. The independently reviewed, authorized short demonstration used a separate power source, two finite nonliving loads, and separate buffers within the established small configuration. Qualified technicians operated it. An independent monitor controlled stopping. Spectators outside the shield supplied no bodily burden.

Before the start, a passing cart loosened a barrier rope. The technician waited for it to be secured and the passage checked, then verified isolation and remaining capacity. The orchestra's schedule did not shorten those checks.

The two gauges did not rise identically. Within the approved window, at a moment the audience could not predict, the monitor stopped the run. Input ceased. Residuals entered their separate, established receivers. No light climbed into the sky. Nobody's injured arm healed.

“Is it broken?” a child asked. “It was so short.”

“We said we'd stop here,” the technician explained.

The child's mother laughed. So did Luna. She had the final readings displayed beside directions to the earlier failure record. A celebration would not erase April 23. What continued from the stable April 26 trial was a bounded method, not a promise of kingdom-wide safety.

The protective main bridge remained in service. Five core locks stayed sealed. The removed selector linkage had not been reinstalled for display. When a visitor asked whether the new law would lower maintenance costs, Isabella directed him to the actual figures. This pleasant day did not entitle her to promise smaller hazards or smaller bills.

Near the side door, Viola sat with her left arm supported. One assistance-group member nervously repeated the route instructions. She waited for him to stop, then offered water and another attempt.

“You won't say it for me?”

“You know what to say. You need a drink, not a knight who interrupts forever.”

Afterward, he guided an adult needing the level route to an available shaded seat. The visitor declined to write praise in the reception book and received the same assistance. A decorative flower tub obstructed the return turn. Staff moved it instead of asking the visitor to step over it for the celebration's sake.

At her scheduled break, Viola left her station after her replacement actually sat down. Public praise for bravery did not oblige her to accept another shift. At the refreshment canopy, she asked the vendor for cooler water.

“It'll take a minute.”

“That's fine.” She sat and waited.

At Cecilia's table appeared the visitor who had once asked her to leave while a complaint procedure was tested. Today the question concerned prayers. Must one sign support for reform to join them?

“No. You can pray or simply drink some water. There are other staff on the roster if you don't want to see me.”

“I don't mind today. Your cups are too small, though.”

Cecilia looked down and laughed. That criticism was easy to understand. The catering supervisor had no larger cups, so she brought a jug and arranged prompt refills. A wish could not enlarge the inventory. It could help a thirsty person drink enough.

A short voluntary prayer followed that afternoon. Some stayed; others listened to the violin at the corner. Nobody asked why particular hands remained unclasped. Cecilia spoke of caring for the living, her voice undiminished by the absence of compulsory responses.

Elena's relief-network information was ill suited to decorative borders: one bookable vehicle, limited storage, actual hours, and things they could not promise. A woman asked about moving a heavy chest tomorrow. The manager checked. Someone else had reserved the available time.

“She's getting married tomorrow,” someone whispered, as though that made difficulties impolite.

Elena asked for the next opening. The manager found one on the morning of July 14. The woman needed to ask her household whether they could wait. She received contact instructions, without a reservation being made for her. A wedding was no reason to leave a question unanswered.

After handing over, Adrian brought Elena two hot pastries. One lacked a corner.

“Calamity on the journey?” she asked. “Or an advance kitchen inspection?”

“I was hungry. You can have the other one.”

“A clear explanation and a practical remedy.”

She took the whole pastry, then broke off a piece for him. They ate beneath a tree without summoning four other women to complete the picture.

Passing with a sugared cake, Isabella declared budget scrutiny compatible with dessert. She did not join their private lunch. She did mention an inverted page number in the public material and asked Elena to pass it to the person responsible.

“The least romantic congratulations I've received today,” Elena said.

“It wasn't congratulations. It was work.” Isabella smiled. “The cake is personal.”

Later, an artist asked to sketch the five women together. He wanted a record of the day, not five brides. Each agreed to a short sitting. Luna required a chair. Viola would not hide her support beneath flowers. Cecilia declined a prop representing the old betrothal. Isabella stood on the same ground as everyone else.

“Do you need Adrian?” Elena asked.

The artist did not. Adrian fetched water for the staff without treating his absence as an injury.

The sketch contained five living people capable of changing their minds. They did not share a single future. They couldn't even agree on the worst refreshment. Luna wanted one hard biscuit submitted to structural research. Viola objected to wasting research funds. Cecilia had already soaked hers in tea.

At three, Luna left as arranged. She did not wait for Adrian to escort her; her assistant and attendant went with her. Viola left for her clinical review. Cecilia stayed for one song after handing over. Isabella had an ordinary budget record to finish. All had genuinely attended. All could leave.

At dusk, Adrian and Elena returned their borrowed plates. Tomorrow's registration time was confirmed, the documents shared a folder at home, and they had chosen their rings together. Tonight required no additional meeting on behalf of the kingdom.

The previous week, they had finally selected the smaller home with imperfect sunlight but separate work spaces. Both read the lease. Elena had not rented neighboring rooms under her family's name. Adrian had not confused independence with pretending she could afford no more than he could. They had discussed contributions, kept their own spending, and left room to reconsider. The mint tray would fit. The blue curtains needed shortening. Real inconveniences awaited them.

At the jeweler's, the assistant had offered Elena the most expensive pair. One edge pinched. Before Adrian could call it beautiful, she took it off.

“I intend to wear it every day, not prove every day that I could buy it.”

They chose two narrow, smooth bands. Trying his on, Adrian asked whether he looked aristocratic. She said he looked like a man who had finally learned his ring size. They paid their agreed portions, neither signing the other's purse over to love.

“Nervous?” she asked now.

“I'll probably get the tie wrong again.”

“That isn't a new problem.”

“There's a new one too.” He looked at her. “It's finally a day I want, and I'm afraid I'll do it badly.”

She squeezed his hand. “Then we'll take it slowly. There'll be plenty of ordinary days to practice after registration.”

The courtyard lamps went out one by one. The last cleaning workers received their agreed pay. The celebration ended. The world did not. Adrian and Elena turned toward home. Tomorrow was no longer a plot he had to predict correctly. It was an appointment they both knew about, and both wanted to keep.
