# Chapter 144: Lanterns for Those Crossing the Bridge

Isabella read the letter three times. Then she asked whether its bearer had eaten dinner.

The messenger blinked. He had.

“Then please wait a little. I'll provide a written answer.”

The fifth bell of January seventeenth had not finished ringing. The delivery register lay open beside the sitting-room door. The messenger remained voluntarily, neither dismissed nor detained. Isabella set the letter under the lamp and reread the conditions attached to support for restoring her coordinating powers.

Suspend public statements by the families. Transfer inquiries to the royal household's internal relief process. Acceptance of relief would constitute agreement to the arrangements that followed.

Each phrase sounded respectable by itself. And she knew how much faster certain applications would travel if she recovered her dispatch badge first.

“They could say they're still searching, only differently.”

Adrian sat opposite her. “Then ask whether people can keep asking the old questions under the new arrangement.”

She did not ask him to write her refusal. Instead, she sent two questions: Would receiving temporary assistance affect a family's right to petition or attend? Would those declining the internal arrangement still be sought under their existing case numbers?

“If both answers are no, there may be something to discuss.”

“The second answer should be yes.”

She rubbed her forehead, then smiled. “Thank you. I'm beginning to entangle myself.”

While the messenger carried the questions back, she went to the window. More lanterns were appearing in the street. The glass reflected hair bare of any royal ornament.

“Did you think I'd agree?”

“I didn't know.”

“How reassuring.”

“If you'd already decided against it, you wouldn't need to ask.”

She turned to dispute that, then failed to find the part she truly disputed. The offer recalled the dispatch badge she had surrendered. Before losing it, she had never noticed how many doors looked first at a small piece of metal.

Elena had not joined this conversation. After delivering the papers required for her father's seat, she was checking the family copies in another room. Cecilia was at the chapel. Luna and Viola were resting separately. Isabella could not make every letter a bell that summoned everyone.

The supplementary explanation arrived half an hour later.

Once an internal relief case closed, the recipient would no longer seek public examination on the same grounds. Nonrecipients could retain their search numbers, but would not be included in the current special assistance. The supporter offered neither unconditional kindness nor a promise already worthless. He would use his influence in exchange for containing the dispute over royal responsibility.

Isabella placed the two papers side by side. At last she could see what her recovered power would cost.

“He wants me to sign away their afterward.”

“And they're still waiting for an answer about before,” Adrian said.

Her refusal was brief. She appreciated the offer, but could not accept aid or priority assistance conditional on surrendering public inquiry. Unconditional support could be discussed separately. She did not accuse the man of holding families hostage or post his letter in the street, leaving someone who might reconsider no way back.

When the messenger took her answer, she asked for the receipt to note that both explanations had been retained. He agreed.

Only after the door closed did she sit, as if admitting the choice had really cost something.

“Tomorrow I still won't have my badge.”

“No.”

She reached for the teapot and found it cold. Adrian offered fresh water. She stopped him and drank a sip first.

“I'd like to know whether I'm allowed to dislike cold tea without proving how brave I am.”

“You are. It really is cold.”

This time she laughed longer, and let him call for hot water.

When fresh water arrived, Isabella studied the receipt instead of drinking. Would the families think she'd thrown away money they could really have received?

“Some might,” Adrian said.

“Can I say I did it for their good?”

“You can explain the conditions and why you wouldn't agree for them. You can't guarantee gratitude.”

She retrieved her explanatory note and deleted a phrase about protecting them. It wasn't exactly a lie, but it made anyone ungrateful sound ignorant of their own interests.

She replaced it: What I declined was an offer requiring my support as coordinator for a collective arrangement. It does not prevent anyone independently examining, accepting, or refusing lawful assistance.

Then she asked what happened if someone really chose internal handling.

Adrian wouldn't close that road for her. “That's their decision too. Warn them to read carefully. Don't erase them from the list for choosing differently.”

This time, writing that inquiry contact remained available, she didn't press hard enough to score the paper.

Her attendant asked whether dinner was still for two. Isabella checked Adrian's plans before answering the kitchen. Not a meeting for a dozen people or a banquet requiring extra dishes. Just hot soup and bread they could hold.

Her piece of bread arrived slightly smaller than his. After a serious inspection, she exchanged them.

“I made tonight's decision. I may eat a little more.”

“Of course.”

Adrian moved his soup farther away, lest the same argument requisition that too.

It wasn't another political letter that interrupted dinner.

The porter brought a message from the district patrol. People with permission to travel should temporarily avoid the side approach south of the old watercourse. The corrected handover was proceeding: replacements were on their way, and the old watch remained at its posts. But ice had turned a flour wagon sideways across the level approach. A clinic carriage was trapped behind it.

The plan had left no empty watch. The road had failed anyway.

Isabella rose, then glanced at the empty pocket in her sleeve.

She could no longer issue special dispatch orders.

Adrian was already reaching for his coat. “We can ask what the people there need. We can't command for them.”

“Do you think commanding is all I can do?”

“I think I could make matters worse too. Ask first.”

They arrived with her attendant and the guards on duty. The bridge approach was bitterly windy. The old watch really was still there, keeping pedestrians off the ice. The wagon leaned into a shallow roadside rut. A hard pull would tip its load toward the people waiting beside it.

The flushed driver kept insisting he had followed the posted route. He had not used the previously closed lane. Nor could he make his heavy wagon lighter by wishing.

An elderly patient's daughter peered through the clinic carriage curtains. She didn't notice the princess. She asked when they could pass.

The officer in charge could give no exact time. Road workers were bringing timber blocks. Another empty carriage might come around the northern approach, but festival traffic would make the detour slow.

Isabella asked whether the patient could be carried over the steps. Before she finished, the attending medic shook his head.

“Not with that much jolting. He's just had treatment.”

She closed her mouth. Her urgency did not outweigh his judgment.

Adrian went around the wagon and asked whether the flour could be unloaded onto waterproof cloth. The driver refused at first. He couldn't pay for sacks spoiled by damp. The district officer said they could use a nearby shop's covered porch; its owner was finding the key. Someone would need to record each sack handed over.

“I can count,” Adrian offered.

“Do you know the markings?”

“No. You read them out, I write them, and you check.”

Isabella offered to write too, but a guard stopped her from approaching the wheel's outer side. She didn't treat the interruption as an insult. Standing under a lamp, she took blank paper from the officer and recorded sacks already carried into the porch, rather than squeezing beside the unstable frame.

The workers did the lifting. Soldiers kept the crowd clear. Nobody shouted about serving the princess and sent injured people or bystanders rushing in to push.

When the sixth bell sounded, the replacements' leading lamps had not yet appeared. The old watch did not leave at the mistaken time specified in the earlier plan. Its sergeant tucked the corrected handover notice securely into his coat, then turned to warn a child away from the shafts.

Watching him, Adrian could finally connect Viola's advice to a person still standing at his post.

With half the flour unloaded, the workers blocked the wheel and slowly drew the wagon onto the road. The clinic carriage still couldn't pass. Flour scattered across the ice had made the surface slick as wax.

Some onlookers grew impatient. A man in a fur-collared coat shouted that royal people were here now, so why weren't they letting the carriage through?

Isabella did not answer by identifying herself.

“Because it's still slippery. Let them spread the grit.”

Her attendant held up a lantern, illuminating the workers' progress. The princess stood beside her, flour on her cuff, still clutching an undignified tally of sacks.

When the clinic horse finally stepped forward, the old man groaned. His daughter held him anxiously while the medic checked him as they moved. Only after the carriage crossed the approach smoothly did the officer release pedestrians in small groups.

There was no cheering. Everyone wanted to reach wherever they were going.

Isabella watched the carriage disappear and let out a quiet breath.

“She didn't know who I was.”

“She knew the road was open.”

“Is that enough?”

Adrian glanced at the tally. “For someone needing to cross tonight, perhaps.”

The shopkeeper emerged to check the sacks. One old ink marking was hidden under flour. He did not sign merely because a princess had written the total. The driver wiped the mark clean. Adrian corrected his transcription, and both parties checked it. The goods had not become a royal requisition or mysteriously changed owners.

Isabella returned the sheet to the officer. Her fingers were stiff with cold. As her attendant drew her coat close, she asked whether the woman was cold too.

“Yes, Your Highness.”

“Then let's go home.”

They passed the chapel on their way back. Cecilia stood outside distributing unlit lanterns. Seeing the flour, she first checked nobody was hurt, then brought two cloths for their hands.

“Which kitchen rebellion did you join?”

Isabella looked at her cuff and laughed. Adrian explained without making himself the man who had lifted an entire wagon. Hearing that the old watch had stayed, Cecilia nodded. She left official traffic notices to the proper office, simply telling lantern buyers that the approach was clear but still required care.

Isabella waited for the child ahead of her to choose, without sending her attendant to cut in.

Isabella chose an ordinary paper lantern and paid the same price as everyone else. She did not request her crest on it.

At parting, she asked Adrian to walk with her to the next corner, where their routes separated. It wasn't a detour.

He agreed, explaining that he would be reading with Elena afterward.

Her smile paused for half a heartbeat, then returned.

“I didn't ask to purchase your entire evening.”

“I know.”

They walked together a little way. Her lantern hand was steady; her other hand stayed warm inside her coat. At the corner she didn't ask for one more street, or produce the letter of withdrawn support as a reason to keep him.

Tomorrow's document examination would still be difficult. She had let go of the shortcut to restored authority herself.

Handing her lantern to her attendant so she could climb into the carriage, she turned back.

“If anyone asks what I did tonight, don't write that I rescued someone.”

Adrian nodded.

She considered. “You may write that I miscounted a sack.”

“That was my transcription error.”

“Then I still haven't learned to leave every responsibility with its proper owner.”

They were both laughing when the door closed. The lantern was not raised to the palace's highest window. It rested beside her knees, lighting the journey home.
