# Chapter 298: The Old Betrothal Becomes History

On July 3, Cecilia laid the envelope containing her historical betrothal notice on the table.

“Will I have to say I'm not married again?”

“No,” the archivist replied. “We're changing what people receive when they consult the record. You don't have to keep proving yourself.”

She sat and relaxed her hands. Adrian sat opposite her. They had received separate notices and chosen separately to attend. This was not a private invitation disguised as an attempt to mend things.

The old registration already had no present force. The trouble was that copies often left that qualification on a later page, while the first displayed two names like a debt waiting to be collected.

Reclassification would not scrape away the original words. The archivist showed the correspondence between the source and its certified copies. Registry staff brought the current index. Private envelopes were opened only to authorized readers; the public catalogue would not reproduce signatures or intimate historical passages.

The explanatory page retained an extract of the old agreement's purpose: recording cooperation, assistance, and risk-bearing through the partnership recognized at that time. It explained why people had put their names together, without establishing that every answer had been freely given. Cecilia pointed to a later reuse column. Would the two layers remain separately identified?

“Yes. A recording medium can carry old traces across a reset. It cannot carry someone's consent across every future occasion.” The archivist separated the purpose clause from the references documenting later use. The existence of an old relationship, the institution's use of it, and a person's wishes today were different questions.

Adrian had expected the satisfaction of closing an unpleasant cupboard. Instead, he realized he was afraid someone might ask whether he wanted its contents burned.

“I don't want it destroyed,” he said.

“Neither do I.” Cecilia looked up. “The past shouldn't govern me. That doesn't mean nobody lived earnestly in it.”

The archivist recorded their statements separately, without combining their feelings.

That afternoon, the historical review team presented the comparisons completed since June. The Public Contract Research Institute had independently reviewed the authorized technical portions; it had not taken custody of the originals. Luna attended a short session, her right palm supported and an assistant turning pages.

“We won't need your hand today,” she told Adrian.

“It's growing fond of that sentence.”

“Don't let it sign a research agreement for you.”

Their quiet amusement passed. Luna asked the examiner to explain why they could now interpret columns that had remained unresolved. The importance of this early institute finding did not excuse skipping its most troublesome foundation.

The May copies preserved legible traces without treating two similar, overlapping layers as continuous text. A month of ordinary conservation, line-by-line transcription, and catalogue checks had connected one documented layer to the *Reset Duty Review Log: Working Duplicate*, deposited in March. They had not recovered its missing leaves. They had identified return-check entries on surviving pages, previously outside the comparison's scope.

The examiner laid out June's retrieval acknowledgments. The initial request followed references on existing pages; Evelyn had not supplied a miraculous document from memory. The archive traced the accession catalogue to a separately held registry counterpart. That registry's own custodian retrieved it, checked its condition and borrowing history, and excluded a similar number belonging to routine repairs. After provenance checks in mid-June, two readers independently transcribed the legible portions before comparing differences. Today's text consisted of what both could verify.

“Did you know in March and simply not tell him?” Elena asked.

“No. March established the authorized operating note and warning-line reference. These columns can only now be interpreted with sufficient related material. We cannot rewrite an earlier uncertainty as an earlier finding.”

She added that answer to her notes. Even a convincing explanation could not quietly annex every preceding day.

The independent registry counterpart matched separate deposit references in the log and carrier. Two entries retained completed-receipt marks. The examiner covered the request column before showing the completed one. They would not turn an intention into an accomplished act again.

Both entries concerned the same outsider in different historical cycle batches. Their anchor designation matched the receipt trace already verified in April and the copying reference checked in May. The provenance supported identification as Adrian, rather than merely another man with his name.

“At least two completed returns, with reentry also recorded,” the examiner said. “The damaged portions cannot give us a total. Nor can we convert these old calendars directly into present dates.”

Adrian stared at the word *return*.

He had not arrived for the first time and had an unreasonable world borrow his identity. He had come before, tried to stop the sacrifices, failed, and been sent back to the reality he remembered. The copper handle, mistaken shutdown, and court were not scenery provided for a player.

Finding a route home did not acquit the person in those records.

“I still made that shutdown,” he said.

“Yes,” Elena answered. She remained in her authorized seat instead of taking his hand to soften the statement. “And it still wasn't the sole cause of everything.”

The return-processing entries distinguished memory stripping from anchor retention. Completion marks and receiving responses accompanied both. What had been stripped was the ability to recognize experience continuously, not every surviving trace. The crescent scar was a physical anchor retaining a connection across cycles. It contained neither a complete life available on demand nor a vessel intended for someone else's sacrifice.

“Then why did I think this place was a game?”

At last he asked the absurd-sounding question before the people authorized to hear this part of the account.

The examiner did not ask him to read the conclusion first. In early June, with his permission, they had sealed his limited description of *Crown of Fate* in his former reality. Private details had remained restricted. He had recorded discontinuities he remembered from its presentation: the same stretch of gray mist across different routes, a copper handle cut off by the frame, an interruption that did not fit the ending around it.

Those memories were not independent physical evidence. Their value was narrower: he had recorded them before seeing today's legible entries. He could not now shape an account around the text and claim he had always known.

The carrier's return note explicitly recorded historical residue projecting into the outer realm after memory stripping, preserved in rearranged fragments rather than continuous experience. The separately held counterpart linked that residue to the returned anchor. A previously unmatched log column described fragments from different cycles entering the same projected record. Their ordering matched the breaks in Adrian's remembered routes.

The comparison did not produce a screen from his old world or identify a game company. It established the origin of the supposed routes. They were erased real history, surviving reset as a projection, mistaken for a game by the man who had lost his continuous memories.

Luna asked her assistant to move the final page so she could read it without extending her session.

“*Crown of Fate* didn't write these lives in advance,” she said. “The story you recognized there was the shadow of history lived here. Your memory was stripped, but the anchor wasn't erased. You returned thinking you'd brought a walkthrough.”

Adrian did not suddenly remember every farewell. He remembered studying those endings and deciding which choice offered the better return. The people behind the words had never been disposable attempts.

A chair scraped outside. He was oddly grateful. This room would not freeze once he understood its ending. The archivist still had a shift to finish. Luna still needed rest.

“So no goddess selected me to achieve the best ending.”

“There is no omniscient goddess in this evidence,” Luna said. “There are people who built machinery, changed it, and assigned the consequences to others. And someone who returned before without getting everything right.”

He nodded. She left at her agreed time instead of waiting for everyone's feelings to settle. The institute certified the portions it had actually reviewed. Unreadable sections stayed unreadable.

On July 4, another restricted session placed the five marks beside the manufactured villainess accusations. This was less a new discovery than a map connecting established evidence without substituting a mechanism for a person.

The five-lock drawings checked in March, noble lists, fabricated oracle drafts, transfer orders, and royal countersignatures retained their distinct sources. The original-purpose documents and fixed-bearer revisions verified in April showed the progression from replaceable media to designated bodies. Family and financial records publicly examined in May showed institutions converting an engineered requirement into a reason someone must submit.

The women had not been selected because they were evil. Their marks corresponded to five crucial positions in the altered apparatus. Officials then used prophecy, trials, betrothals, and political crises to force them into those positions. A mark proved neither character nor consent. The records did not establish universal inheritance of marks or make their descendants reserve components.

Viola held the corner of her authorized transfer comparison with her right hand.

“They chose who was required, then wrote why that person deserved to disappear.”

“In many of the verified cases, yes,” Cecilia said. “We don't need to invent every missing page to reject that practice.”

Isabella did not turn away the page recording royal involvement. Evelyn's cooperation, Leon's leads, and Oswin's genuine hazard evidence remained separate from pardons. The danger existed. Compulsory designation was still a human decision, not an order signed by nature.

On July 5, the archivist completed the classification within each person's authorized review. Original betrothal records entered restricted historical-relationship files. Disputes and provenance questions received their own references. Existing copies were not silently replaced.

The first page of the current registry index now stated that the historical record created no present marriage, duty, or consent, and linked to its archival location. Any subsequent certified copy had to include the qualification. Nobody could cut away the inconvenient half.

A clerk read out “dissolution of the historical marriage.” Cecilia stopped him.

“We aren't dissolving a marriage that exists today. It wasn't a present marriage in the first place.”

He corrected the wording and retained the superseded draft with the correction record. Only when the new page was properly filed did she acknowledge her notice.

Adrian asked whether she wanted an additional reading copy.

“Not now. Knowing where I can find it is enough.”

She put away her envelope and left for an ordinary work appointment. She neither returned the past to him nor asked for another possibility.

That evening, Elena met him at the street corner. He told her what hurt most was not having returned before. It was mistaking a return for a first arrival, and trusting a walkthrough more readily than people.

“You'll still make mistakes,” she said.

“Your comfort always deserves preservation.”

“I don't intend to marry someone who'll never be wrong again. I want someone who will tell me, stop to listen, and change what he does.”

She did not look away from the word *marry*. He asked whether she had feared the woman in the old records might take her place.

“Yes. Then I realized even today's version of me can't spare tomorrow's version every choice.” She paused. “You'll still have to ask me yourself.”

He promised he would. Beneath his sleeve, the scar neither warmed nor supplied another scene. The past had finally found a place where it could be consulted. It could no longer reach out of every page to sign their future for them.
