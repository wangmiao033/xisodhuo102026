# Chapter 129: Your Highness, Please Answer the Question About the Accounts

On January 6, in a palace antechamber, Adrian crossed out the final sentence of his prepared statement.

He had practiced all yesterday afternoon, and that sentence still took half a minute. Now that the hourglass stood beside the door, he resented spending so much of his five minutes demonstrating that he knew how to begin.

Elena glanced at the deletion. “It sounded very impressive yesterday.”

“I can be clear without being impressive.”

“One less concern. Now I only have to wonder whether you'll remember to breathe when you stand up.”

She pushed a cup of warm water toward him. At almost the same moment, an attendant behind Isabella set down another.

The cups touched.

Isabella looked at Elena. “The palace is obliged to offer hospitality to invited witnesses.”

“And guests may look after their companions.”

“Then perhaps Mr. Reed can judge which cup best suits the occasion.”

Adrian studied the identical cups, then the women.

“I'll drink the nearer one first. The other can wait until I've finished speaking.”

Elena smiled. Isabella moved her cup an inch closer.

“Now they're equally near.”

Some disputes were plainly beyond the scope of his five minutes.

The people in the antechamber rose. A secretariat official confirmed the speaking order and led them inside. There was no crown on the long table, no proclamation awaiting a new monarch's signature. The agenda concerned oversight duties, funding powers, and preliminary succession business. Yet the neatly arranged nameplates made Adrian feel the presence of something harder than gold.

A gray-haired nobleman opposite Isabella inclined his head as she sat.

Lord Alistair Crowe wore an unadorned brown formal coat. A cane rested beside his chair; narrow slips marked numerous pages in his papers. He did not sneer at the princess. He asked the chair whether all three of his questions had entered the record.

Only after receiving an answer did he put on his spectacles.

Isabella did not begin with her bloodline. She explained why she wanted the old project examined. A royal archive summary claimed certain materials had been cleared for renewed use. Earlier documents located so far supported receipt, conservation, and transfer instead. The archivist had established changes in the composite volume; a conservator had identified damage beneath a later repair to an empty contract wrapper.

“These differences don't tell us who issued any original instruction,” she said. “They do make continued reliance on the summary a decision that requires explanation.”

Crowe spoke before she had read all her documents.

“Your Highness, who is included in this we?”

The chair asked him to wait for questions. He pressed a hand on his papers and did precisely that.

His patience gave the question more weight than interruption would have.

“Outside specialists, witnesses, and Lady Elena Rosenberg, who assisted with documentary comparisons,” Isabella answered afterward.

“Chosen by you.”

“Invited by me. They could refuse.”

“Freedom to refuse an invitation does not establish independence from the host. Who paid their expenses?”

A clerk supplied the statement. Ordinary access and certification costs came from the existing project provision. The Crown did not cover all the visitors' private expenses. Further research funding remained unapproved, and no exclusive consultancy had been signed.

Crowe read it without declaring it false.

“Clarifying expenses is sensible. But why should people selected by Your Highness judge whether the existing supervisors deserve trust? Temple expertise cannot become worthless merely because you dislike its conclusions.”

Several observers nodded. Adrian had been ready to place the man comfortably among their opponents. Now he had to admit the question was real.

Isabella did not evade it.

“I haven't asked to replace the entire supervisory body with my visitors. I'm asking that records from different sources be compared. The Temple can explain its routing seal. The Crown must also explain its summary.”

“What does the seal prove?”

“That the dispatch passed through the relevant office. Not that the person who stamped it knew everything inside, and not that the entire Temple approved what happened later.”

An accusation would have been easier to applaud. She gave the less useful answer anyway.

Crowe turned a page. “If this proves to be an archival error, will you still seek broader investigative powers?”

“The scope should follow the problem, not expand automatically because of my rank.”

“Then will you accept equivalent scrutiny of expenditure?”

She paused for the briefest moment.

“Yes. Including my own applications.”

The clerk's pen was very audible as he wrote that down.

When Adrian's turn came, he rose too quickly and struck his knee against the table. It seemed impossible that anyone had missed it.

Elena neither steadied the table nor made an elaborate reassuring gesture. She simply looked at him, calm and attentive. He exhaled and set down his shortened statement.

He described the versions and examinations he had personally seen, without presenting Luna's technical opinions as his testimony. West Wing Transfer Dossier W-24 now contained fourteen leaves. Its four-year-old receipt listed twelve and an empty wrapper. The two later additions had been checked against custody records. He had not pronounced the whole dossier forged because its papers differed in color.

“Did you see the original twelve-leaf arrangement?” Crowe asked.

“No. The custodial records and conservation examination support that reconstruction. Their custodians should explain the basis.”

“Then why connect it to the East Ridge accident?”

“Because it warrants comparison with the surviving East Ridge material. We cannot yet say royal wrapper K-9 and the tower's C-17 followed the same instruction. They aren't the same object.”

Someone shifted in disappointment at cannot.

Adrian did not have to resurrect D-17's lost image as a complete answer merely to keep that person interested.

The chair asked whether Isabella had privately commissioned him to reach a specified conclusion.

“I accepted limited access and agreed to describe what I witnessed. I didn't agree to provide a predetermined conclusion.”

“And if the conclusion harms Her Highness?”

“The basis should still be preserved, so she and anyone else concerned can answer it.”

He did not look toward Isabella. Only after sitting did he discover how damp his palms were.

The princess pushed the second cup toward him, without contesting Elena's priority.

Someone at the far end asked whether he would stay on as a royal adviser, sparing everyone repeated invitations. Isabella raised her head, plainly interested in the answer.

“I didn't come today to discuss employment.”

“It would improve efficiency.”

“I would need to know the duties, duration, and who could review my statements. I can't promise every day that follows simply to save a minute now.”

The questioner frowned. Isabella told the chair that the suggestion could be registered separately. It was not a condition of access or testimony.

She spoke quickly, as though another moment might make the opportunity too tempting to release.

Elena lowered the hand she had been about to raise. The women exchanged a look across Adrian. Neither competed to speak first.

When Elena received her three minutes, she did not waste them assessing Adrian's performance. She asked which office had compiled the summary, whether the later additions affected authorization for use, and why an unsupported expenditure statement had become a basis for further funding.

The last question made Crowe look up.

“Your family also operates transport businesses, Lady Elena.”

“Yes. I know a transport charge can be properly entered later, or improperly entered twice. A total won't tell us which.”

“Whom would you have examine it?”

“Authorized fiscal staff. We can identify discrepancies, but asking the question doesn't entitle us to carry home the national accounts.”

Crowe's fingers stopped on a paper marker. He asked the chair to record that alongside Isabella's agreement to financial review.

Elena acknowledged him. Agreement on this point did not make them allies in everything else.

The atmosphere changed as discussion reached expenditure. A dispute over records had become a question of who could spend money and who must produce receipts.

The fiscal summary showed a restricted-purpose tax allocation for November: twelve hundred silver coins received, three hundred sixty for storage, two hundred forty for transport and escort, one hundred eighty for conservation, and four hundred twenty remaining.

The addition was correct. Crowe opposed turning preliminary questions into unlimited examination of the books. Isabella pointed out that the summary described all expenses as verified but supplied no corresponding vouchers.

“You previously used this summary to request further funds,” he reminded her.

Her jaw tightened.

“Yes. Which is why I also need to know what verified means here. If I keep relying on it without answering today's questions, your objection is justified.”

This was not a clever line designed to embarrass him. She returned her own application to the table and requested that the unsupported increase be deferred. Existing conservation wages should continue. People who had completed their work should not pay for her political display of caution.

The chair rang the small bell.

The meeting granted no broad new power to Isabella. Nor did it suspend her eligibility for succession. The secretariat would request limited particulars and verification records for this allocation, for initial examination by authorized fiscal staff. Questions and answers would enter the supplementary papers. Later proceedings would be notified separately.

A pending-document slip went on top of Isabella's application for additional funds.

In the corridor afterward, she stopped and stared at the returned request.

“At least they didn't erase the questions,” Adrian said.

“They didn't give me what I wanted, either.”

“No.”

She looked up, perhaps expecting him to add something comforting. He offered to take the papers. She promptly gave him the heaviest stack.

He shifted under its weight, and Elena laughed.

Isabella gave a brief, tired laugh too. “Now you understand how much I'm asking.”

“Yes. Next time I'll recommend thinner paper.”

When the laughter passed, Isabella asked Ellery to assemble her expenditure records, without first removing anything unflattering.

Crowe emerged through a side door and told her he would attend the next meeting.

“I know,” she replied.

He offered neither a threat nor sudden friendship. He simply walked away with his cane.

The fiscal office sent its authorized document list that afternoon. Adrian did not open any originals. At the copy-receipt desk, he checked the attachments.

The two hundred forty silver coins for transport and escort cited the same receipt number twice.

One receipt could be apportioned or corrected. Repetition alone did not prove missing money. But the summary's assertion that its vouchers had been checked now presented a specific question.

The duty clerk said Hester Blythe would bring the limited particulars to the fiscal reading room the following morning.

Elena checked the allotted hours, then turned to Adrian.

“I'm coming too.”

“I didn't think you'd let that question go.”

“Or those cups of water.” Her tone was mild. “Your ruling clearly failed to settle the dispute.”
