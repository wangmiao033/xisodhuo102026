# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume One: A Betrothal Written in Blood
### Chapter 28: The Crescent Scar in the Old Courtroom

At eight on the morning of October eighth, Adrian Reed was doing his best to look like a calm, competent investigator.

He straightened his cuffs until the crescent scar on his left hand was hidden, then arranged three permits of different colors in his document case.

Finally, he asked Elena, "Do you think something will go wrong today?"

"That's the sixth time you've asked me."

"And you still haven't answered it a sixth time."

"If I say no, will that make you less afraid?"

Adrian considered.

"No."

"Then save your energy for walking."

She handed him a biscuit baked to a perfect golden brown.

He sniffed it suspiciously.

"Not burned this time?"

"Martha made it."

"...I was going to say how much your baking has improved."

"I heard that."

He put the biscuit in his mouth and devoted himself to chewing.

Today's destination was the sealed western courtroom of the Old Capital Court.

Yesterday afternoon, Hale had requested emergency preservation of evidence. With the review hearing advanced to October ninth, the judge had granted a limited inspection of the old transfer records, provided the Judicial Office, the Temple, and all parties received written notice.

The permit covered only the three-copy handover documents connected to R-074 and their custody references.

They were not authorized to open unrelated files.

Nor could they remove originals from the premises.

Adrian had memorized both restrictions.

"Who'll be there?"

"Two independent judicial clerks, a municipal custodian, and Hale," Elena replied. "The Temple received the notice but hasn't named a representative. We'll record its absence. We won't pretend silence means consent."

"And Leon?"

"His office was notified as well. He isn't required to attend personally."

Adrian nodded.

He wasn't particularly eager to see the prince.

He wanted to see the handover document Matthew Holt had been told to change from *pending verification* to *receipt complete* seven years ago.

The courthouse's west entrance was tall and narrow. Rain had left dark streaks down its iron gate, and years of use had polished the door ring.

After checking everyone's credentials, the independent clerks examined the old seals with the witnesses present. The wax was faded at one corner, but there were no obvious signs it had been torn away and replaced.

"An intact seal doesn't prove nobody interfered with the contents," the older clerk reminded them.

"Understood," Adrian said.

He rather liked these reminders now.

Anyone willing to say *we don't know yet* was more trustworthy than someone who declared the outcome inevitable.

The door opened.

No monster waited beyond it.

No glowing magic circle appeared beneath their feet.

There was only a deserted courtroom, rows of benches beneath dusty covers, and a brass evidence cabinet against the wall.

Boards had been fitted over the windows. Thin blades of morning sunlight cut through the gaps.

Adrian stepped onto the raised platform.

His left hand began to burn.

He stopped.

At first, it was a dull sting beneath the old scar.

Then the smell of damp wood seemed to vanish under something else.

Hot iron.

Candle wax.

Rain-soaked rope.

He heard a bell.

But the clock tower wasn't due to strike.

The old courtroom seemed to slide away from him.

A flight of wooden steps rose ahead.

At the top stood no judge's bench, but a temporary execution platform.

Dried blood stained the boards.

Adrian stood upon them.

A silver restraint circled his left wrist, its curved groove pressing directly into the crescent scar.

Someone called a name.

"Adrian Reed."

A second voice followed.

"Witness, do you consent to the Substitute procedure?"

Adrian struggled to look toward the sound.

Beyond a blur of white light sat a golden-haired woman in the witness box.

Her fingers were gripping the wooden rail so tightly they seemed to shake.

Elena?

The woman lifted her head.

Her lips moved.

But whatever he was remembering contained no sound.

A second bell rang.

Someone seized his arm.

"Adrian!"

He jerked back into the present.

The old courthouse floor was beneath his feet.

No blood.

No scaffold.

Only Elena holding his arm, with Hale and the two clerks staring at him in alarm.

"You nearly fell off the steps," Elena said.

Adrian looked down.

His left hand was resting against a brass handrail, the cuff between his skin and the metal. Near where he'd touched it, the rail bore a crescent-shaped patch of wear. An old court identification number was engraved along the outside.

"I'm fine," he managed.

Elena didn't accept that answer.

She asked the clerks to confirm that nothing at the site had been disturbed, then guided him to a wooden bench.

"What's wrong?"

"My hand. And... I think I remembered something."

"What?"

"A courtroom. Something like this one, but not quite. I was on an execution platform. Someone was asking a witness whether she agreed to a Substitute procedure."

"Who was the witness?"

He looked at her.

"I couldn't see clearly."

He didn't say the name he'd almost spoken.

Not to deceive her.

A flash of memory without a date or a voice wasn't enough to accuse anybody of betraying him.

Elena sat beside him.

"Then don't draw conclusions yet. Tell me more when you're ready."

She held out her hand.

"May I?"

Adrian gave a weak smile.

"Worried I'll fall down again?"

"Worried you'll insist you're not frightened when you obviously are."

He put his left hand in hers.

The burning gradually faded.

He couldn't tell whether that was because he'd moved away from the brass rail, or because he was no longer holding his fear entirely inside himself.

"I don't want to leave yet," he said.

"Nobody's asking you to. Just sit for five minutes."

When he'd recovered, Hale asked the clerks to document the worn railing and the time of Adrian's sudden dizziness.

The report didn't describe a vision.

It noted that he'd touched the railing and experienced a brief episode of disorientation.

Adrian was grateful for the distinction.

The examination resumed.

Following the approved inventory, the municipal custodian unlocked the brass cabinet. The clerks compared the seals drawer by drawer.

The upper drawers contained publicly accessible maintenance documents.

The third held a flat gray wooden case.

Its label read R-074.

Beneath it, in smaller letters:

*Temporary Handover Copy. Final Receipt Incomplete.*

Adrian instinctively reached toward it.

Then stepped back to let the clerk put on gloves and lift it out.

The paper was brittle, but far better preserved than he'd expected.

Sender: Old Judicial Archives Office.

Temporary recipient: Temple of Fate, Old Contract Records Office.

Destination: sealed western courtroom, pending further verification.

At the bottom was a space labeled *Final Receipt*.

It was empty.

No line struck through a signature.

No ink scraped away.

The space had simply never been signed.

"Matthew told us someone later ordered him to mark the transfer as complete," Adrian said quietly.

Hale nodded.

"Now we have a document that can be authenticated. First we determine whether it's the same version he saw."

The younger clerk compared the seal numbers against the academy's training copies. The first two handover references matched.

The older clerk documented the paper fibers, ink, and condition of the outer seals.

Neither pronounced the document genuine on sight.

"There's an attachment," the municipal custodian said.

A thin register lay beneath the transfer sheet.

It recorded the issuance and upkeep of old judicial seals from seven years ago.

But its most recent additional entry was dated September seventeenth of this year.

Purpose:

*Temporary loan of historical seal template for verification of Evidence Item 714.*

Adrian looked up sharply.

Seven-fourteen.

Not R-074.

Evidence Item 714 was the blue jewelry case delivered to the palace on the night of the ball.

"Why is that number in the old cabinet's register?" Elena asked.

"Someone may have signed out a historical template through this office," the custodian said. "Its presence here doesn't identify the person who ultimately handled the evidence."

Hale had the clerks record the date and the issuing stamp.

The borrower field contained only a reduced office mark.

The complete name should appear in the current year's tool issue book.

"Then we should compare both registers," Adrian said.

"We can provide a sealed extract within the limits of your permit," the older clerk replied. "The original stays here."

He took out a judicial tool catalogue and laid tracing paper over the September seventeenth entry. Next he compared the reduced stamp reference with the copy of the palace registration available to the examiners.

A small notch appeared in the same position.

"These marks belong to the same historical tool-numbering system," the clerk said carefully. "That does not prove who used the seal. It does confirm a traceable issue record."

"Who was authorized to borrow it?" Adrian asked.

"Normally only accredited archive verification officers. The year's issue book should contain the signer's full identity."

"Can we inspect that now?"

"Not under today's authorization. You can ask the court for an emergency supplementary production order. The custodian would then provide a certified copy."

Adrian glanced involuntarily toward the exit.

For one foolish moment, he'd been about to suggest running to the main building and finding the book himself.

Elena touched his sleeve.

"Remember why we're here?"

"To verify evidence, not manufacture a fresh dispute."

He took the application form and filled it out himself.

He requested the issue date, authorizing department, recorded borrower, and any return entry. He didn't accuse a particular person of committing a crime.

The younger clerk stamped the application.

"The judge can decide whether to admit the supplementary material before tomorrow's hearing."

Hale filed the receipt.

For the first time that morning, he looked almost relieved.

"We've done something important today. Not proved anyone guilty. Made sure two records that can still be checked against one another can't be dismissed as unrelated without examination."

Adrian looked toward the cabinet.

Maybe the most dangerous thing about the old courtroom wasn't a monster hiding in the shadows.

Maybe it was a pen capable of changing *temporarily held* to *fully received*.

They spent the rest of the morning checking seals, storage conditions, and transfer dates. Each observation was signed by more than one institution's representative.

Adrian didn't go near the brass rail again.

Yet on the way out he found himself looking toward the empty witness box.

In that fragment of another execution, a golden-haired woman had been sitting there.

What had she said?

Why had she looked almost as frightened as he felt?

Elena followed his gaze.

"Anything else coming back?"

"No."

"Then let's take what we found to the judge."

"Aren't you going to ask why I keep looking at the witness box?"

"I want to." She met his eyes. "But you don't know what you saw yet. When you do, we'll face it together."

On the way home, the carriage passed the academy.

Adrian still felt cold in his fingers.

"Why didn't you ask whether the woman was you?" he said at last.

Elena hesitated.

"Because you didn't say you'd seen her clearly. If I asked that first, you'd start wondering whether you'd forgotten something terrible about me."

"And if it really was you?"

"Then we'll investigate." She looked at him. "Even if some version of me did something wrong in the past, that wouldn't excuse me from answering for it. And it wouldn't mean you had to suffer now for something someone else recorded."

Adrian was quiet.

"I was afraid telling you would make it sound as though I didn't trust you."

"You're allowed to have doubts. Just give me a chance to answer them."

She took the document bag from his rigid hands.

"And you didn't run today. I intend to remember that."

"Only remember it?"

"What else would you like?"

He thought of the meal they'd already agreed to share when the case was over.

"I'll tell you later."

She smiled.

Adrian placed the authorized extracts back in his case.

The R-074 temporary handover was missing its final acknowledgment.

And on September seventeenth, someone had requested a historical seal template for work concerning Evidence Item 714.

Neither fact proved that Evelyn Vale or Edwin Clay had forged the royal letter.

But they gave tomorrow's judge a very precise question to ask.

Who signed out the old seal?

And where did it go?

Elena leaned against his shoulder.

"You've been trembling all day."

"I'm better."

"Then give me your hand."

He did.

No bells rang this time.

But beneath the wheels' steady noise, the unfinished question from his memory lingered.

"Witness, do you consent—"

Everything after that had been lost.

He wouldn't decide what Elena had done in another time based on a broken recollection.

If he ever got the chance, he'd ask her.
