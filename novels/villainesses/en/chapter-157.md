# Chapter 157: No One Signs for Him Tonight

The argument began while Adrian was peeling a chestnut.

On the night of January 26, the six chairs surrounded Martha's freshly wiped dinner table, not a council desk. She allowed documents at one end. The other was for people who actually wanted to eat. Adrian sat between them, a splinter of shell caught beside his nail. Tomorrow's discussion of personal choice had not inspired anyone to peel his supper for him.

Isabella described her afternoon. Three visitors would pursue separate questions; there was no joint pledge. She had returned her temporary coordination badge. Her extension request awaited an answer. Tomorrow she could attend only as herself.

Luna shifted her injured right palm toward the middle of its cushion.

“At least they didn't demand that your authority be restored before allowing you to ask questions.”

“One of them came close.”

The princess smiled without condemning the man's entire family.

Elena asked how they intended to address the shortcut offered through family guarantees. She wanted the special review's standards made explicit first, before the debate became a question of whether nobles could pay someone else's bill.

Cecilia objected. Discuss standards alone, and the interpretation using historical betrothals to assign candidates would remain underneath them, untouched.

“Someone must ask why that interpretation is valid.”

“You've submitted that question,” Elena said.

“Submission isn't an answer.”

“I didn't say it was. I said one hearing cannot decide everything.”

The chestnut cracked. Half its shell fell into Adrian's lap. By the time he retrieved it, their voices had hardened.

Luna turned her copy around. One technical citation needed clarification first, or the other side would keep treating it as a reason to end the discussion. Viola reminded them that the public session was limited. People could not wait indefinitely in the corridor; if someone required more time, those behind them needed to know why.

“So we leave the difficult questions until last, then discover there's no time?” Cecilia asked.

Viola flushed. “I meant the people outside. I wasn't telling you to be quiet.”

Silence followed. Martha looked out from the kitchen and set a hot dish at the empty end without declaring supper more important than their disagreement.

Isabella lifted her cup with a steady right hand.

“We may not need the same order. The questions the nobles will pursue can be handled separately.”

“What if they'll only ask for exceptions for us?” Elena said.

“Then their support must be described as exactly that.”

“And they'll say we object only because we weren't favored.”

The princess set her cup down softly. “I can't grow different motives for them.”

The words hurt Elena, and seemed to hurt their speaker too. Both were accustomed to making a situation presentable through a sufficiently careful explanation. Tonight nobody wished to be arranged into a presentable shape.

Adrian put the peeled chestnut on his plate.

“What do you need me to say tomorrow?”

All five looked at him.

Elena wanted his actual experience, not testimony on behalf of her family. Cecilia wanted him to explain that old records did not replace present consent. Luna asked him not to describe the model as absolutely safe. Viola wanted him to avoid suddenly entering a crowded side passage if he needed to leave. Isabella hoped he would clarify that rejecting the shortcut did not mean abandoning his application.

Each request sounded reasonable alone. Together, they made him feel like a bag into which five people had packed different tools.

“I'll consider all that. But I need to write it myself.”

Elena's brow moved; then she nodded. Luna began to explain that she meant only technical errors, stopped, and retrieved the paper edging toward his plate.

“I wasn't trying to prepare your testimony,” Cecilia said quietly.

“I know.”

“But it sounded like that.”

He did not deny it merely to make her comfortable. She looked at the cooling chestnut and moved the shell dish closer, adding no more papers at his end.

What restarted the argument was a private suggestion sent to Isabella by an afternoon visitor. It was not a new order. They might accept a limited priority assessment while explicitly retaining freedom over any final contract, then challenge the unreasonable elements afterward.

No immediate marriage. No risk contract signed that day.

That was precisely what made it tempting.

“If it could prevent us reaching the restriction's expiry without an answer,” Isabella said, “we should at least understand what accepting it means.”

Luna said that depended on the assessment's inputs. Existing information and a new experiment imposed different costs. Cecilia replied that naming a candidate first would give the historical arrangement a default standing it should never possess.

Elena was silent.

Adrian looked at her. Knowing they had noticed, she admitted that if the form merely named the two of them, expressly creating no betrothal, she would feel tempted to move faster.

Cecilia's expression changed. “Even if that priority comes from the interpretation we're opposing?”

“I said I'd be tempted. Not that I'd answer for him.”

“But they'll use your name to prove the route works.”

Elena tightened her grip on her napkin, looking away from the papers at last.

“I'm frightened too. You're not the only person afraid the past will take someone away.”

Cecilia's lips parted. Whatever sharp answer she had found remained unsaid.

A sudden pain caught Isabella's left wrist. She steadied the cloth beneath it with her right hand without speaking, but Viola noticed and asked whether they should pause. The princess began to refuse, then changed her answer to yes.

Nobody counted that as a victory.

They left the table for a while. Luna moved toward the window, away from the stove's warmth. Viola adjusted her support in a chair against the wall. Cecilia remained where she was and ate the food that had cooled during the argument. Elena went to fetch water. Adrian followed without closing the kitchen door.

“Do you think I've gone backward?” she asked.

“I think you said what you were really thinking.”

“After all the admirable things I said before.”

“Those were true too.”

She looked at the kettle without reaching for it. Martha set two cups before them, then returned to her own work.

Elena finally said she wanted him to choose her. Understanding a principle should not mean she must always appear free of jealousy, impatience, and fear.

Adrian nodded. “Liking you isn't a prize I award for the best self-control.”

Her eyes reddened; then she gave an almost irritated laugh.

“You chose the least suitable kitchen for that.”

“The place with the cold chestnuts might be worse.”

She took his hand briefly, then released it. She borrowed no signature for tomorrow from what he had just said.

Back at the table, Cecilia apologized to Viola first. She had turned a concern about passage into an attempt to silence her, without hearing the actual worry. Viola accepted and admitted she had sounded too much like someone urging a task toward completion.

They did not line up to confess in turn. Luna simply put her technical paper in its own folder. She would answer technical questions, not measure everyone's distressed expressions as votes of support. Isabella decided to submit her personal position first. The nobles' subsequent texts would remain separate, not become an authorization from all six.

Elena still wanted the reviewing body to state what it could actually decide. Cecilia would still question the foundation of the interpretation. They could arrive together without pretending to share one line of thought.

When Viola unfolded the route map, Isabella suggested a large shared carriage so they would at least reach the entrance together. Luna immediately said she might need to leave early. Cecilia had to return to the relief house that afternoon. Elena did not want waiting for one person to oblige everyone else to remain.

“I was trying to save a carriage,” Isabella said, then sighed. “Now it sounds as though I want to lock someone inside it.”

“It can just mean saving a carriage this time,” Adrian replied.

He asked her to finish. Having made a mistake before did not mean every ordinary suggestion must first be found guilty. Eventually they arranged to share only the stretch of road they needed in common, leaving return journeys separate. It decided nobody's future, but the princess finally ate a mouthful of the food waiting before her.

Luna asked Elena whether a request for more private material tomorrow would still make her agree too quickly.

“I don't know what I'll want in the first instant,” Elena said. “But I'll ask which material. You've already reminded me.”

“I'm not asking you to promise never to panic.”

“Then don't promise you'll never want one more measurement.”

Luna's smile stopped. Slowly, she nodded. She glanced at the corrected paper between them, no longer using technical caution to claim a more reasonable position for herself.

Cecilia cleared space for Luna's resting hand. If a new doctrinal interpretation appeared, she would bring back the actual text rather than leave them dependent on the anger in her account. Viola checked the clock and reminded Luna that their agreed rest time had arrived. Luna closed the folder. This time she truly put it away.

Adrian fetched a blank form from tomorrow's papers. It belonged to the optional priority route, not to every ordinary application. One box requested a proposed counterparty for compatibility assessment. Another stated that no betrothal arose automatically.

He pointed to the first box. “I don't want to fill this in now.”

“And if that makes it slower?” Isabella asked.

“I'll ask how the ordinary route continues. I don't want speed to depend on supplying a name before I'm ready.”

Elena did not tell him to write hers. Cecilia produced no ancient record. Luna did not claim she could calculate a cheaper answer.

Viola asked whether he needed someone beside him entering the waiting corridor.

“Ordinary directions. Not you standing guard.”

“Then tomorrow's staff will handle that.”

Some of the effort finally left the room.

They gathered their separate papers. There was no new protection agreement, no collective signature, and no declaration that an argument had destroyed their alliance. Sentences still needed changing. Questions still unresolved would not disappear merely because bedtime brought a gentler mood.

Martha removed the cold chestnuts and asked who wanted something hot. Only a small pot remained, she warned—not enough to fill every bowl.

All six spoke at once.

She raised the ladle. All six stopped.

“This time we can count bowls.”

Luna laughed. Isabella steadied her bowl with her right hand; Viola did not reach for a distant plate with her injured arm. Cecilia returned an extra empty dish. Elena left Adrian's place within his own reach.

Before returning to her room, Luna checked the medical instructions again and decided to rest at the house tomorrow. Her submitted written opinion would answer in her absence. The carriage need not wait for her, nor reserve an empty seat to prove she had tried hard enough.

Later, alone, Adrian crossed out his opening twice before leaving one short sentence: I will not name a counterparty in advance as the price of being seriously heard.

There were not five other hands on the page. A distant evening bell sounded. He set the ordinary review notice beside the unfilled form, checked the time, and put out the light.
