# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Two: The Saint Won't Let Me Get Married
### Chapter 46: The Fifth Evening Bell Isn't an Oracle

The estate's clock startled Adrian awake on the morning of October twenty-sixth.

He sat bolt upright, his left hand pressed to the sheets, waiting for the crescent scar to grow hot.

Nothing happened.

“Good,” he muttered. “At least fate hasn't sentenced me before breakfast.”

Martha knocked. “Mr. Reed, do you intend to attend the saint's hearing in your pajamas?”

“I'm considering it.”

“Then I'll charge the fine to the estate's reputation-repair account.”

He got out of bed.

At breakfast, Vera divided Cecilia's documents into two stacks. One could be submitted to the disciplinary committee; the other contained patient information that only authorized reviewers or physicians could examine.

Cecilia wore a plain white coat. Her investigator's badge was still in its box.

“If they ask whether I disclosed a restricted oracle, I'll ask them to identify the document first.”

“Good,” Vera said. “That's your answer. Not a line your lawyer made you memorize.”

Elena handed her some water. “Ask for a break if you need one. You don't have to endure everything in silence.”

Adrian produced last night's notes. “Before the hearing, I'd like to ask about something at the bell tower.”

All three women looked up.

“Not climb it! Just check the public bell register.”

Elena pointed to the time. “Eight seventeen? That's when you heard it?”

“Yes. A short extra chime. I don't know which bell, or who rang it.”

Vera checked the clock. “The hearing starts at nine. The tower office opens at seven thirty. Take Hale, and keep your guesses separate from the facts.”

The office was tiny, with repair tools hanging along one wall. Its bearded custodian, Rowan, was drinking black tea that looked strong enough to prescribe.

“The fifth evening bell?” He frowned. “The fifth evening time signal, or the five strokes at the end of evening prayers?”

“There's a difference?”

“A considerable one. Old archivists used ‘fifth evening bell’ as shorthand for that group of five. But without a date, how am I supposed to know which evening?”

“When did the bells actually sound last night?” Hale asked.

Rowan opened his register. “Main bell, five strokes at eight fifteen. Then a short additional chime at eight seventeen.”

“You heard it too?”

“So did my assistant. Don't start calling it an oracle. The workmen changed the hammer's leather buffer. It may have bounced.”

“May have?”

“We haven't taken it apart yet. To establish who pulled a rope, you'd need the operating record. And no bell can establish when somebody handed over a file.”

Adrian smiled. He'd finally found another witness to the strange sound, and the man had promptly dismantled the most exciting explanation.

Hale asked Rowan and his assistant for separate signed statements. The duty register stayed at the tower; they would take only a checked extract.

Adrian also asked about the arrangements on the twenty-fourth.

“There was a memorial service on the twenty-fourth,” Rowan said. “Different ringing sequence. That's in the special-service book.”

The archive's handwritten note had no date. It might not refer to the twenty-fifth at all.

Rowan also showed them the maintenance board. The underground warning bells and the main bell were separate mechanisms. Someone had tried to argue that taking the alarms out of service meant the entire tower couldn't ring. That was simply wrong.

“Who asked?” Hale said.

“A clerk in a gray coat. Signed the visitors' book, but I don't remember the name.”

Hale requested preservation of the entry rather than asking Rowan to identify someone he hadn't properly noticed.

“We even record split leather,” the custodian said as they left. “Otherwise the next shift hears an extra stroke and thinks the gods are knocking.”

Adrian understood now. The useful thing wasn't the mysterious sound. It was that someone had bothered to record it for the next person to check.

They reached the hearing hall with seven minutes to spare.

Over the entrance, a Temple maxim read: *The truth shall set you free.*

Adrian wondered who had been put in charge of locking it in Vault B.

Three disciplinary reviewers took their seats. Sister Anna, the elderly chair, was flanked by specialists in archive procedure and medical aid.

She began by defining the hearing's scope: whether Cecilia had violated restrictions on protected information. They would neither decide the authenticity of the R-074 betrothal nor pass judgment on every Temple prophecy.

Cecilia stood to acknowledge this.

Internal Oversight's representative alleged that she had given the restricted C-19 prognosis to aristocrats and municipal physicians, undermining public confidence in the Temple.

Vera asked him to identify the original text allegedly disclosed.

He listed three documents: an identifier-correction request, an anonymized medical verification summary, and a *Blessing Completion Bulletin* compiled by Internal Oversight itself.

“Who drafted the third document?” Vera asked.

“The Administrative Office compiled it.”

“Cecilia didn't issue it. Your own departmental summary cannot establish that she gave the original to an outsider.”

Anna required clarification. Eventually, the representative conceded that the bulletin alone could not prove disclosure.

Cecilia did not use that admission to claim she had never discussed the patients.

“I did tell physicians that C-19 and C-91 had conflicting identities, and asked them to check. If I exceeded the medical-reporting exception, please identify what fell outside it.”

The medical reviewer consulted the rules. There was an exception for correcting patient identities, but the necessity and extent of Cecilia's disclosure still required examination.

Vera submitted Dr. Winter's narrowly drawn statement and the receipt for the materials she had received. Patient names were redacted. The facts that mattered remained clear.

“Surely you recognize that this public controversy has shaken believers?” the Oversight representative demanded.

Cecilia paused. She remembered misreading a blessing to a patient as a child. The old woman had thanked her anyway, grateful for the company.

“Some people will be distressed,” she said. “But a patient's identity doesn't correct itself because everyone agrees not to mention the mistake.”

Anna stopped the discussion before it could become a dispute about faith.

“We are examining conduct and documents. Opinions cannot replace evidence.”

She requested the original annotated drafts of the prognosis cited by Oversight.

The representative said they were temporarily sealed away during fire-safety work and could not be produced immediately.

Vera submitted the previous day's application.

“Your office controls the originals, yet expects my client to answer for material she cannot examine. That gap cannot become a presumption of guilt.”

Anna authorized the admission of certified images of the three subsidy summaries held by the municipal office. Qualified reviewers would compare them without publicly disclosing personal medical information.

During the recess, Cecilia stood in the corridor, her palms damp.

“I'm afraid of saying something wrong every time I open my mouth.”

Adrian showed her the tower extract. “Last night I almost convinced myself that strange bell was a warning from fate. This morning I learned it might be a problem with the buffer.”

“You found a broken bell?”

“A possible fault. And a reason ‘after the fifth evening bell’ can't prove that a file reached the vault.”

Elena leaned over to read the times. “At least you didn't charge up the tower to arrest the bell-ringer.”

“I've matured.”

“You have. Should we mention it in your salary review?”

“I was about to ask.”

“This afternoon,” Vera interrupted, “we present it as a timing question requiring verification. Not a ready-made accusation.”

Over the lunch break, Vera took Cecilia through her morning answers in an empty consultation room.

“You didn't deny requesting an identity correction, and you didn't admit disclosing the restricted original. Good. Now tell me exactly what you know about the three revisions of the summary.”

Cecilia considered. “Before October fourteenth, I'd never seen the subsidy application myself. After Mira found the discrepancy on the sixteenth, I requested preservation of the relevant records. I didn't see who altered the third version.”

“Then if you're asked tomorrow, say that.”

“Won't the reviewers think I know too little?”

“Say what you know. Dressing a gap in your knowledge as a firm conclusion won't help you.”

Cecilia nodded, still twisting her hands together.

From the doorway, Adrian said, “I used to think the hero could get every answer right just by remembering the game's plot. Today, even a form has three different versions.”

“What do you do now?” Cecilia asked.

“Stop pretending I understand everything.”

She finally laughed.

Elena arrived with fruit pies and caught the sound. “Who deserves a reward? Our brave saint, or the officer who successfully established that a bell might be broken?”

“Both, surely.”

Hale happened to pass. “Then save the third for me. I've spent the morning chasing documents too.”

Martha wasn't there, but Adrian could hear her verdict: the estate's investigative team had finally found something they could divide without signatures.

When the hearing resumed, Oversight submitted an emergency maintenance completion certificate, asserting that all disputed materials had been safely filed.

Its time field contained only *after the fifth evening bell*.

Vera requested the actual date, any special ringing arrangements, the vault location, and both parties to the handover.

The archive reviewer examined the certificate. It established that somebody had reported receipt, he said. It did not yet confirm that every original had entered Vault B intact at a particular time.

While they waited for a reviewer to retrieve the catalogue, Adrian noticed a young filing novice sorting folders by color. The novice stopped at the certificate.

“Something wrong?” Adrian asked across the table.

“It's an old archive expression,” the novice said quietly. “But this year's rules require standard times. Anyone still using that phrase should attach a formal field that lets us convert it.”

“Where would it be?”

“Usually on the reverse of the handover register, stamped by the sending and receiving officers.”

Adrian didn't demand the novice's name or ask him to smuggle out the page. He passed the information to Vera. She immediately requested preservation of the whole register, rather than just the sheet mentioning the bell.

It was a small point, but it gave them something to test that didn't depend on what anybody had heard.

Oversight's representative pressed for an immediate ruling. Anna refused.

She deferred the disciplinary decision until October twenty-seventh and ordered Oversight and the archive to supply the missing handover entries and version catalogue. Necessary confidentiality restrictions remained in force, but missing material caused by the closure could not be used to presume Cecilia guilty.

She also made one qualification: the temporary suspension of Cecilia's access had not been lifted. An adjournment was not an exoneration.

Cecilia looked relieved anyway. At least a prewritten confession couldn't simply decide her future.

As they left, Hale handed Adrian a new receipt.

“Permission to compare the municipal third version with the Temple's third version, side by side. Tomorrow morning.”

Adrian put it away. He'd thought the bells might give them an answer. Instead, they had narrowed the question. Properly witnessed handover records would have to fill the remaining gaps.

Cecilia announced that she would not discuss betrothals tonight.

“Wholeheartedly approved,” Adrian said.

“Then we'll eat apple pie,” Elena suggested.

“Did you make it, my lady?”

“Martha did.”

“Thank heaven.”

“Was that a sigh of relief?”

“A normal post-hearing response.”

All three laughed.

Then an assistant reviewer hurried through the doors and called Cecilia back.

“Please be sure to attend tomorrow. There's additional text in the third version submitted by Oversight that doesn't appear in the municipal copy.”

“What text?”

“Someone added a sentence after ‘the patient's condition is stable.’ I can't disclose the wording before formal examination.”

He went back inside.

Adrian stood on the steps. They weren't merely investigating whether somebody had altered a prophecy. They needed to find out who had continued adding conclusions afterward.

Cecilia looked up at him. “Are you frightened?”

“Very,” he admitted. “But at least tomorrow we have a way to check.”

Elena tightened her hold on his hand.

The three of them walked to the carriage together. None tried to answer for either of the others.
