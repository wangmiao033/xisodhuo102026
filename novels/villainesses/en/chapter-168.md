# Chapter 168: Not Everyone Was Waiting at the Same Door

Martha set five parcels of different sizes on the table on the morning of January 31.

“Lunch.”

Adrian considered the smallest. “Is that mine?”

“That holds Luna's pencils. Yours is on the chair.”

Luna drew the little parcel closer with her left hand. Yesterday she had slipped a tea invitation into their working schedule, hoping he would come before realizing it wasn't business. Today she wrote separately: Would you have tea with me at four? Not to discuss testimony.

He agreed, promising to send word if delayed. He neither pretended yesterday had disappeared nor required every future invitation to begin with a confession.

She drew a cup beside the time. “No research fee.”

“Then I can afford it.”

The other papers concerned work. The registration archive remained sealed; the annex across the street accepted submissions, with review scheduled for February 2. A missing handover attachment and mismatched enclosure raised questions. Six people standing outside the same door wouldn't answer them.

Isabella wanted signatures showing how many people had suffered. Elena returned a blank statement.

“The seamstress doesn't want her employer knowing what she's applying for. We can't publish her name first and then ask her to justify being afraid.”

“It could remain private.”

“She still needs to know who will read it.”

Isabella wanted something substantial. Actual people were making substantial look untidy. Eventually she left the public petition behind. She would ask willing councillors about their objections and speak to an ordinary registration agent, not revive her expired royal coordination commission.

Cecilia would visit her patient-record assistance office, finish promised work, then ask a document collector whether he wished to describe his experience. Viola would remain at the house, speaking with a sergeant already coming to submit his duty report. Luna, likewise staying home, would receive a copying assistant. No sealed apparatus would be touched.

Adrian went with Elena. They weren't dividing the capital by five impressive titles. They each knew someone willing to answer part of a question.

The room behind the cloth shop had a low ceiling. Elena's hat caught a sleeve hanging to dry. The seamstress laughed, then looked worried that she'd been too informal.

“It arrived first,” Elena said, removing her hat. “I can yield.”

Beth Rowan hadn't come to prove the closure unreasonable. After her husband's death, cancellation of an old household agreement had remained unentered, obstructing a new rental guarantee. She admitted arriving yesterday without a required page. She was first told to supply it; only afterward did the closure prevent her asking how.

Adrian stopped writing refusal to accept.

“The doors weren't sealed when you arrived?”

“I was there first. Don't make it sound as though I brought everything.”

He corrected the sequence. She couldn't give the exact time, but remembered the hot-bean seller adding charcoal again. Elena didn't ask her to invent a convenient minute.

An apprentice collecting thread announced he'd seen a guard snatch papers. Beth asked which papers. He didn't know; he'd seen a hand reach.

“From outside?”

“Below the stairs.”

Adrian asked him to draw his position. The drawing was poor. Elena asked whether one black patch was a doorway or a person.

“A pillar,” he said, embarrassed.

“Good. It needn't agree with our conclusions.”

Once the drawing was turned correctly, they could see his position didn't overlook the counter. Disappointed, he changed snatched to reached. He wasn't dismissed. He had also seen a document box carried back inside.

Beth would submit to the reviewing officer, not publish her name. Elena separated the permissions and left a copy for her to consider. Beth read slowly, tracing the lines. A needle passed through thick cloth nearby. Nobody announced she'd finished for her.

On the second page, she found her husband described as still living.

Adrian recopied it and apologized. She waved away the apology but watched the correction.

“The records keep him around. Apparently the paper won't let him go either.”

Elena set down her hat.

Before they left, Beth offered two cloth-covered buttons made from scraps, not payment for testimony. Elena initially refused. Beth pointed at her cuff.

“That one's coming loose. I'm rescuing a button.”

Elena looked down and accepted one with a smile. Outside, she asked whether she'd seemed ready to reject every kindness.

“I think she knows more about buttons than we do.”

“Not particularly comforting.”

“She wouldn't alter her statement because we needed comfort, either.”

Elena studied the button. She didn't convert it into a promise to finance the whole shop.

While they ate outside, Isabella's messenger found them. One councillor would supply his complete objection to the rejected proposal, but not have it described as support. Isabella had agreed. A registration agent retained a payment acknowledgment and would explain it somewhere suitable.

Adrian retied his parcel around half a pastry. He wanted to visit the agent immediately. Elena reminded him that the man had arranged to see Isabella, not anyone who learned his name.

“I want everything filled in today.”

“So do I.”

She didn't present caution as a personal virtue. They waited for the reply already requested. Wind caught the bag; their fingers met as both reached. She left hers there, then said every touch needn't become evidence.

They were running fifteen minutes late. Adrian sent Luna word through a familiar messenger before joining the annex queue to confirm submission times. Sealing one building hadn't supplied the other with more counters. An elderly man held a dripping umbrella halfway around the room.

At last, a clerk confirmed that supplementary material could arrive tomorrow afternoon; the February 2 review stood. Statements requesting confidentiality could be enclosed separately. A request didn't guarantee its approval.

Adrian noted this without promising Beth's name to the public.

Back at the house, the accounts didn't all agree. Viola's sergeant confirmed instructions to preserve the premises and clear people out, not detain all visitors. He admitted the first route had left a man with a stick waiting too long. A side passage was later used; nobody drew swords. Viola hadn't been there and claimed none of this as her own command.

Cecilia brought the collector's authorized scope and retained receipt. He would discuss one delivery, not patients' family affairs. She deleted the latter questions.

Luna's copying assistant hadn't witnessed the enclosure opened. He had copied a number from a supplementary inventory and later been told to make a clean version, without receiving the original attachment. His working draft remained with its authorized custodian. Luna couldn't simply order it fetched.

“At least we know which document to ask about.”

Isabella returned last, muddy shoes beneath her skirt, unfinished pastry in her right hand. Seeing no unanimous statement, she sighed and sat.

“The agent thinks sealing the archive was necessary.”

Adrian looked up.

“If originals are compromised, he says continuing is dangerous. But he objects to withholding his copies. He wants to prove what he submitted.”

“Both can be true,” Luna said.

“I know. I wanted someone more helpful.”

Cecilia placed hot water within reach of her uninjured hand. “He has helped.”

Sorting the papers produced a smaller crisis. Viola held a map with her right hand while Cecilia reached for a receipt beneath it. Two folders caught on one cord. Isabella thought she'd tangled them; Luna blamed her left-handed knot. Four people said wait at once.

Adrian raised both empty hands. “I haven't joined in yet.”

Elena laughed first, then asked everyone to lay the pages flat. They freed them from the top downward. Martha's lunch string had wandered onto the table. Nobody was responsible for the entire disaster.

Viola asked whether it could still bind documents.

“If we don't mistake the grease for a new seal,” Luna said.

Cecilia checked that no writing was stained, then discovered everyone waiting for her to laugh.

“I genuinely wanted to know.”

“We know,” Adrian said.

She smiled. Isabella finished eating and cleaned her right hand before taking more papers. Important work hadn't abolished lunch or taught anyone to untie every knot one-handed.

They separated direct observation, hearsay, and inference. Five routes returning home didn't authorize a shared imaginary ending to the missing attachment. The two people willing to compare copies weren't among the eight missing adults from the old project. That investigation hadn't suddenly lost two unresolved names.

There were reasons for preserving an archive, failures in how people had been received, and limits on what each witness could see. None canceled the others. Adrian found himself wanting to stack the useful pages first and leave the awkward ones underneath. Instead, he placed Beth's admission of her missing page beside the account of the later closure. The order mattered.

Isabella's last sheet was a copy of the office contact register, lawfully obtained by the agent. Under execution liaison stood a familiar surname: E. Vale.

Elena's finger stopped.

“We've seen that abbreviation.”

“We must ask whether it's the same person,” Adrian said.

Luna nodded. The assistant had also mentioned the execution liaison office. That didn't establish he'd met Evelyn herself. They would first verify the register and role, not put a surname into the hole left by the missing page.

The distinction disappointed Adrian more than he liked. A recognizable name promised something solid after a day of partial answers. He remembered Beth watching him correct her husband's status and left the question open. Being tired of uncertainty didn't make someone else responsible for ending it on demand.

It was past four when they finished. Luna put the testimony away and asked whether he still wanted the delayed tea.

“Yes. I haven't washed my hands yet.”

“A very reasonable delay.”

He returned to two cups and fresh ordinary biscuits on a plate. She asked what had been hardest today.

“Accepting that someone who disagrees with us might be telling the truth.”

Luna turned her cup with her left hand.

“Yesterday, mine was asking directly whether you would come.”

“I'm here now.”

“I know.” She smiled. “This time I actually know why.”

She pushed the plate closer, leaving him to choose. The smallest biscuit had broken in two; he took both halves rather than making her choose which piece counted as a whole. Luna watched, then selected one that looked much more impressive and turned out to be nearly hollow.

“I've been deceived.”

“You saw all the available evidence.”

“Not the interior.”

He laughed. She did too, carefully keeping her injured hand on its cushion. Nobody proposed cutting every remaining biscuit apart to protect the afternoon from disappointment. She ate the hollow one anyway. It tasted good, and being wrong about its size didn't require finding a culprit.

She asked no further questions about testimony. They tried identifying the dried fruit in the biscuits and guessed wrong twice. Another name still waited to be checked outside this quiet moment. It didn't need to occupy all of it.
