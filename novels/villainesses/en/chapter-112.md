# Chapter 112: The Saint Read the Missing Half-Page

On December 21, the carriage slowed at the first bend down the mountain.

Luna immediately opened her eyes.

“I thought the court would be the first thing to make me feel ill.”

“Look farther away,” Viola suggested.

“Only trees out there.”

“Steadier than the words on your lap.”

Adrian moved Luna's document bag to the other side of the carriage. She stopped demonstrating her ability to read testimony while travel-sick, accepted water left-handed, and looked toward the distant trees.

Elena sat beside Adrian. Last night she'd checked his cloak and clean gloves. Today she left everything alone except to steady his elbow when a wheel crossed an icy rut.

“You look as though you want to tell me not to be nervous,” he said.

“I considered it.”

“And?”

“I'm nervous myself. It didn't seem convincing.”

He smiled and touched her hand. Opposite them, Cecilia held the ritual text in plain cloth. No saint's emblem invited people to kneel before listening.

She did not recite passages throughout the journey. Once the ride steadied, she asked whether Viola had saved another biscuit and thanked her seriously when she had.

They reached East Ridge before noon. Bakeries were bringing out their second batches. At a small guesthouse near the tribunal, they ate the planned hot lunch. Luna lacked appetite but managed half her soup and a slice of bread. Hensley's statement went to the registry. Luna could testify seated and request a pause if needed; the accommodation did not eliminate verification.

The courtroom was brighter than Adrian expected.

Long tables stood beneath high windows. No sacred fire. The reviewing officer wore dark gray; a technical recorder sat to one side, with places opposite for the church's advocate and an academy representative. Few observers attended. Material concerning bodies and health had restricted handling. Nobody outside was authorized to turn their names into advertisements for a spectacle.

Adrian confirmed the scope of his account and sat.

Luna's old report, S-48, was laid on the table. Its signature was indeed L. Ashcroft; its date matched the employment history she'd disclosed. The applicant noted that three years earlier she had described comparable layer shadows as an ordinary coating defect. Now she directed research possibly involving the reappearance of old vows. That presented a technical contradiction and an insufficiently explained historical connection.

“They're persecuting a witch” would not answer the question.

Luna acknowledged that the old conclusion had been premature. The original appendix retained the observation of two separately fading layers. She had pushed an unexplained anomaly into a convenient label that allowed the work to end. Leaving her post had not removed the mistake. Whether the present material belonged to the same class required a sourced comparison. Two shadows alone did not establish one mechanism.

“Which report do you ask us to believe?” the reviewing officer asked.

“The observations you can verify first. Then judge my explanations.”

Her right hand rested on a pad. Her left did not fidget toward a pen behind her ear.

“My signature cannot preserve an old conclusion. My injury cannot validate a new hypothesis.”

She spoke haltingly, stopping once for water. Adrian watched her face. She was no longer sharpening embarrassment into a joke.

The formal accident report had reached the tribunal the previous afternoon. The registry verified receipt and entered it as a new safety matter. Continued control-side return flow after disconnection showed that the protection was insufficient. Successful emergency discard had limited the injury; it did not establish a safe design. The laboratory remained suspended, with damaged components awaiting examination under appropriate conditions.

Luna accepted that. Rescue did not exempt her from review.

Then the church advocate introduced a different argument.

A reappearing record of an old vow might indicate continuing obligation. If an observer experienced repetition or a distinctive association with the trace, it could be necessary to register him as a potential oath-bearer and restrict further contractual commitments and departure from the kingdom pending investigation.

Adrian's fingers tightened.

A moment ago, they had been discussing whether a room could safely light its equipment again. Now an unnamed fragment of a date was being used to decide whether he could walk through other doors as himself.

The officer did not grant the request. He asked for its evidentiary and doctrinal basis.

The advocate cited the ritual text on old vows calling forth witnesses. He was not unaware of the commentary. He argued that unusual manifestations required special caution, and ordinary interpretations concerning lost documents might not cover unknown restricted magic.

Cecilia requested permission to respond. She first explained that she offered verifiable text and established commentary, not a saint's seal of approval for technical claims.

“You accept that this may be more than an ordinary lost document?” the advocate asked.

“I accept uncertainty. Uncertainty does not remove the need for grounds before restricting a person.”

She opened the cloth wrapping at yesterday's marker. The recorder fetched the court's copy of the same edition and checked volume, passage, and commentary before allowing her to read.

She did not read only the line most favorable to her.

An old vow's record might call forth witnesses, but could not replace the voice of those now present. Obligations involving the dead required separate determination under existing legal relationships. For the living, remnants could not supply an intention never expressed. Where text, persons, or formation were uncertain, one must seek witnesses rather than enforce the agreement in the name of investigating it.

Pens moved in the quiet room.

“This does not say magical remnants can never matter,” Cecilia said. “It places people between a record and its enforcement. You can investigate a record. You cannot first put him inside it and then demand that he prove he doesn't belong there.”

“What if waiting creates danger?”

“Identify its source. This equipment, or this person? The equipment is sealed and the materials secured. If you have specific new evidence concerning him, present it separately. The same unknown cannot simultaneously prove that the technology requires investigation and that he must be registered as an oath-bearer.”

She did not raise her voice. Her knuckles were pale against the book's edge. She kept them there instead of reaching for an ornament of authority.

The academy representative reminded the room that C-17 was an unsigned, blank contractual casing. No name, complete vow, or witness to its formation had been recovered. D-17 had shown only part of a date and was no longer available for replication after emergency disposal. Surviving drawings established what observers reported; they could not supply what nobody had read.

The advocate did not withdraw every concern. He asked to continue investigating provenance and relevant earlier archives. The officer allowed lawful inquiry while making clear that it would not be recorded as anyone's current marriage contract.

When Adrian's turn came, he described the unfamiliar corridor.

A footstep seemed to repeat. Less than two breaths. No clear face, no complete name. He could not establish whether it was a memory, an impression caused by reflected light, or another unexplained experience.

“What would you like it to be?” the advocate asked.

“I'd like to know the truth.”

“That wasn't my question.”

Adrian looked up.

“Perhaps I'd like it to explain why I'm here. Wanting that doesn't put another name into what I saw.”

The answer entered the record. It was not rewritten as a denial of everything, or an admission that he possessed a marriage contract from another life.

The preliminary directions were read that afternoon.

Equipment-safety and historical investigations would continue. The laboratory seal remained. C-17 would stay at the tower under its current no-pressure-contact storage method, with no unauthorized transfer. There were insufficient grounds for the proposed oath-bearer registration or contractual restrictions on Adrian. Substantive new evidence would require a separate explanation and an opportunity for him to respond. The registry would notify the parties of the next technical and custody witness session.

Luna's research had not been declared faultless. Nobody's future had become simple on one sheet of paper.

But today, at least, the remaining light had not become a rope around a person.

Outside the courtroom, Cecilia stopped.

She shifted the book and discovered that her palm was wet with sweat. Adrian offered to carry the bundle and waited for her nod before taking it.

“I kept worrying I'd read a line incorrectly.”

“You didn't.”

“I know. With you sitting there, I wanted every word to be reliable.”

He looked at her.

“You didn't use caring about me as proof that the passage was right.”

She raised her eyes and slowly blushed.

“Caring about you is true too. It couldn't testify today.”

Elena coughed softly outside the porch. Luna hid amusement in her scarf. Viola studied the steps for ice with abrupt scholarly intensity.

Adrian's ears warmed, but he thanked Cecilia seriously. She accepted calmly.

“Then could you buy me tea when we get back?”

“Yes.”

“Ordinary cups,” Luna added.

Cecilia turned to her. “Yours can be empty. The doctor said no weight in your right hand.”

They all laughed, more freely once they were away from the court entrance.

On the way to the guesthouse they passed an open bookshop. Adrian recognized the next volume of the travelogue in the window and asked Elena to wait a moment.

She went inside with him.

The new book had crisp corners. Its back cover predictably exaggerated matters: the captain would make the bravest choice of his life. Elena whispered that if the choice involved correctly addressing a letter, she would endorse the claim.

Adrian paid and handed her the book.

“The first passage tonight.”

“You won't check whether he reaches home?”

“No. I'd rather find out with you.”

She looked down at the cover and stopped teasing the captain. Outside, she took Adrian's right hand gently and held it for half a street before letting go.

At dinner, Luna raised her spoon left-handed and announced that her signature finally resembled writing. Elena credited her decision not to write in the moving carriage. Viola distributed the last little packets of biscuits, including her own, which she had saved until now.

Tessa arrived that evening. The administrator had invited her to explain the technical work voluntarily. After confirming that her ordinary pay did not depend on testimony, she had taken authorized copies of maintenance records on the afternoon delivery cart. Tomorrow morning she would register with the clerk's office. She did not need to rush into anyone's private room tonight.

Hanging her coat, she showed Luna an ordinary file mark on her left fingertip.

“I repaired that old spring today. Much more reasonable than your control ring.”

“Don't praise it too soon,” Luna said. “It'll ask for higher wages.”

Near the end of dinner, the porter delivered a folded note. Someone requested Miss Quinn at an office down the street to check her statement first, lest mistakes tomorrow affect her employment at the tower.

Tessa's smile disappeared.

There was no tribunal registration number. The signature said only Custody Liaison. Yet the note referred to the maintenance copies she intended to submit tomorrow morning.

Viola set down her cup. She did not head straight for the door. She turned the paper toward the lamp and studied the words about employment.

Adrian looked at the porter.

“Is the messenger still here?”

“Waiting for an answer in the front hall.”

Outside, the first stroke of East Ridge's night bell sounded.
