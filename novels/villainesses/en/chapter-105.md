# Chapter 105: The Lady Wants to See How a Witch Gets Things Wrong

That afternoon, Elena opened the reading-room curtains.

Luna shielded her eyes. “Is the first step in open research blinding the researcher?”

“The first step is seeing which paper you've put underneath.”

The old report, explanations of the separate observations, and the equipment application that had never been sent lay on the table. Adrian's restricted personal pages did not. He sat by the window while Elena placed a blank sheet on top.

“I want the process open. That doesn't mean posting his body, other people's wages, and your private letters on the dining-room door.”

“Open to whom?” Luna asked.

“Authorized reviewers. An independent technician. The people operating and witnessing the work. Public explanations can describe methods, limits, and findings; personal information requires separate permission. No polished summaries outside and nothing but 'I've checked' inside.”

Luna leaned back. “I'd hoped a pretty face might still earn something.”

“Your face doesn't need review. Your conclusions do.”

Adrian laughed. Luna gave him a look that requested sympathy.

“I agree with her,” he said. “But how would you avoid spending the whole day turning pages for people?”

That brought her upright.

She stacked three repetitive forms, keeping one original record and verifiable copies. Future ideas went separately from observations. Unknowns needed clear statements, not identical declarations of caution on every page.

“If someone asks which second we stopped, I can show them. If they ask whether it's a betrothal, I can ask them to point to a name.”

“People who disagree with you must see the same page,” Cecilia said.

Luna sighed. “So I can't limit the invitations to admirers.”

“How many do you have?” Elena asked.

“Mostly myself at present. A difficult position.”

They laughed.

The deputy inspector arrived with an academy instrument restorer. Both belonged to the existing inspection party; the restorer had been reviewing storage that morning, not conjured from the distant academy to solve their problem.

She read C-17's technical attachment and examined its support through the cabinet. The foil was unsuitable, and yesterday's dispute had left no pressure damage. The silver-gray interface must remain under its original glass cover. A limited external oblique-light observation, without stripping or heating, could be proposed as a separate preservation examination.

“So W-3 isn't reopening?” Adrian asked.

“No. That pulse bench still needs an appropriate gauge. We won't use it.”

Luna offered a small diagram: lamp, fixed filter, external receiving frame, sealed specimen. No branch led to a person. The two-handle frame could provide an outer shutter that closed when either operator released it. It remained an uninstalled demonstration piece.

The restorer set limits: one sealed miniature power cell, one exposure of no more than four seconds, followed by at most six seconds observing the external receiving plate without power. C-17 would remain level inside the cabinet. Further analysis required another decision. The administrator and designated inspection witness must supervise access; the inner cover would stay closed.

“I can't promise nothing unusual will appear,” Luna said.

“And you can't add light to make it more impressive.”

“I'm becoming familiar with that requirement.”

The deputy inspector looked at her. “Familiarity isn't practice. Let's practice.”

The demonstration frame went onto an empty table. There was only white card inside and no cell in its power slot. Window light crossed the copper leaves.

Luna prepared her most professional voice and asked who would call the stop.

Viola sat against the wall, holding a biscuit in her right hand.

“Stop.”

Luna released immediately. “Stopping. Light closed.”

The leaves fell shut.

“I hadn't said begin,” she added.

“If I wait until you're ready, it won't be unexpected.”

The restorer made a note. Luna regarded the biscuit with an injured expression.

“Even your refreshments have become tactical.”

Viola bit off a corner. “This isn't.”

They rehearsed twice more, changing positions and callers. Adrian tried the outer mechanical blackout handle, reachable without leaning across the lamp. The administrator had the independent power disconnect; Cecilia counted, Elena recorded. Three successful dry runs did not prove all future operations safe.

Still, the restorer nodded. Under pressure, Luna should acknowledge the stop first, not explain why she wanted more time.

Before Luna could answer, Adrian offered water.

“Stop. Drink.”

“That wasn't in the exercise.”

“You can refuse.”

She drank more seriously than she protested.

The restorer tested the closure herself before returning the frame. One handle alone could not keep the light path open. Luna watched without helping her through the motion. A device intended to stop in someone else’s hands had to work in someone else’s hands.

After verification, the preservation permission became an attachment to that day's inspection record. The administrator read the object identifier, limits, and witnesses aloud. Neither the earlier human observation, its sealed plates, nor W-3 acquired new authority by association. Adrian chose only to assist at the external stop position; his body would provide no medium or power.

Luna's fingers paused at the edge of her copy.

“This is much smaller than what I wanted.”

“And much larger than what would remain if that clamp had come down yesterday,” Elena said.

Luna smiled reluctantly and put the permission away.

After the visitors left, Elena released the pen she had been gripping. The new skin on her palm felt tight. She lowered her hand beneath the table, but Adrian noticed.

“Could you dictate the last two lines? I'll write.”

“Speaking at length doesn't mean I need looking after.”

“I'd like holding the book not to hurt tonight.”

After a moment, she handed over the pen.

The lines specified that the pulse bench remained closed and tomorrow would begin with teaching controls. He wrote carefully. Elena checked his work and pointed out a missing letter.

“You recently said my handwriting was better than a map.”

“You didn't leave anything out.”

Smiling, she added the letter. Their hands did not touch. Their contributions simply made one complete word.

Cecilia collected the cups and announced that her letter to the clinic required attention; she would not witness the ensuing private compliments.

“Who said there would be compliments?” Elena asked.

“You checked his handwriting without calling it ugly. I expect a continuation.”

She departed serenely, leaving Luna laughing into her elbow.

Elena decided to rescue the book before it too became research material. Adrian followed her to the doorway.

“Was that sentence for yourself as well?”

“Which sentence?”

“People who disagree with you get the same page.”

She considered.

“Yes. I keep wanting to block the risk before it reaches you. But if I only let people tell me there is no risk, I become another person who loves summaries.”

“You didn't today.”

“And you didn't hand Luna over to my suspicions the moment you heard her past.”

She glanced back toward the woman gathering her diagrams.

“I still get jealous. I still want you to come to me first. Neither should mean I get to choose whom you trust.”

Adrian took her unhurt hand.

“I'll come tonight. We'll read the part where they finally get a hot meal, not the part where they're lost.”

“The captain will lose half his biography.”

“He can complain.”

She laughed and let her fingers linger in his palm before leaving.

Luna reset the two-handle frame for alignment in ordinary daylight. No power cell was attached. Both operators needed a clear view of the indicators; her handle was half an inch too high.

“Help me check this. No testing your heart this time.”

“What happens to yesterday's failure?” Adrian asked.

“Filed under deliberate interference.”

He adjusted the white alignment card opposite her. Luna looked around the frame with a practiced smile.

“I could simply say, look at me.”

“I am.”

The smile wavered.

“You're learning quickly.”

“You teach frequently.”

“I didn't teach you that answer.”

“Then it must be one I wanted to give.”

A screw rolled from her fingers. She caught it in her palm. No equipment was powered, nobody was hurt, and only the witch turned aside to study a screw that did not deserve so much attention.

Adrian waited. Once it was fitted, they tested the shutters twice. On the second attempt she kept the count, and the line across the card stayed complete and steady.

“Good,” he said.

“The mechanism or me?”

“Both doing well.”

“Devious.”

Nevertheless, she put the card carefully into the teaching box. When the administrator collected the work keys, she did not attempt to trade good spirits for one extra exposure tonight.

Before dinner, she washed the handkerchief she had borrowed. The ink resisted more stubbornly than most arguments. Holding it toward the light, she resembled a researcher confronted with another uncooperative specimen.

Adrian arrived with empty cups.

“I've washed it once already,” she said. “I don't want to return an allegedly clean handkerchief.”

“We can wait for it to dry. If the mark won't come out, tell me.”

“That makes me bad even at borrowing things.”

“You started returning it when you brought it here to wash.”

She set it beside the basin.

“Do you think Elena won today?”

“She got the explanation she wanted. You got a small project you can continue. If anyone lost, perhaps it was the beautiful report that only permits success.”

Luna laughed once.

“I sometimes hate how quickly she spots the page I want to hide.”

“You could tell her.”

“She would enjoy that.”

“Probably.”

She brushed her forehead, leaving a damp streak. He offered a dry cloth, which she accepted steadily this time.

“I want her to notice what I do well too.”

“She didn't dismiss your design.”

“Does that count as praise?”

“You can work toward the next sentence tomorrow.”

Elena, arriving for water, paused at the doorway.

“If you faithfully write down nothing happened, I'll acknowledge good work too.”

Luna's first expression was dismay. It slowly settled.

“Understood. Walk more loudly next time.”

“I'll consider charging.”

She took the kettle. Adrian laughed by the door while Luna hung the handkerchief up rather than hide behind it.

After supper, Dr. Hensley reviewed tomorrow's arrangements. She would provide ordinary medical support at the tower, not authorize a new examination of Adrian. He explained the external stop position. After asking about his recovery, she reminded him that new symptoms meant leaving, however minor his assigned task seemed.

He agreed. Viola would observe briefly and keep her existing convalescence and follow-up arrangements.

That night, Elena placed the book between them. Cecilia's loose bookmark thread protruded from its pages. Elena straightened it instead of removing it.

“Nobody put the important page underneath today,” she said.

Adrian moved closer. Their captain was paying for an expensive but hot dinner.

“Let's hope tomorrow is the same.”

At the upstairs cabinet, the new permission and temporary preservation record shared one transparent sleeve. The lamp was still dark. Everyone already knew precisely how small a part of the unknown they would be allowed to illuminate.
