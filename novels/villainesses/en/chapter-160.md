# Chapter 160: Outside Her Family Name

The bookshop courtyard’s front-row chairs were lower than Elena expected.

On the morning of January 28, she lifted her skirt from the damp bricks and shifted sideways. Nobody immediately fetched a taller chair. The owner, who would moderate the discussion, was securing the canvas overhead. One gust threatened to empty last night’s accumulated water over an entire row.

Adrian held a corner until an assistant tied the rope, then took a seat in the third row.

Elena glanced back once. He raised a hand. He did not stand behind her.

Yesterday’s correction hung beside the gate. Some read it; others passed. The owner explained that this was a discussion of registration measures. People could question the applicants, but not shove anyone or demand private letters and medical records as the price of speaking.

“And don’t call my courtyard a tribunal. I have books to sell this afternoon.”

That rule was particularly memorable.

The first speaker wore twice-mended cuffs, not noble finery. Holding a queue ticket, she described four trips to change the name on her shop lease after her husband died. Every explanation at the counter sounded reasonable. Every visit cost half a day’s work.

“Your family has lawyers. Is waiting unfair now because you’re the one doing it?”

Elena had prepared to distinguish betrothals from leases. Seeing the softened folds in the ticket, she let that answer go.

“Finding a lawyer is easier for me. And I may have been slower than many people to notice what waiting costs.”

“Will you pay for all of us, then?”

“I can’t promise that today. What I’m challenging does not depend on House Rosenberg offering a guarantee. If they open a door only for my family, I won’t call everyone’s problem solved.”

The woman sat without nodding. She hadn’t come to provide a noblewoman with an approving expression.

A young copyist raised a crumpled broadsheet. He no longer insisted on its current-marriage claim. Instead, he asked whether Elena was using her position as Adrian’s employer to court him.

“You pay his legal fees. Arrange his carriage. What happens to his job if he says he doesn’t like you?”

The courtyard quieted. Adrian’s cup felt suddenly heavy.

Elena did not look to him for rescue.

“You’ve identified a real imbalance. My belief that I haven’t forced him doesn’t entitle me to ask others to ignore it.”

Wind lifted the canvas. The new knot held.

“Our working relationship requires independent assessment. Courtship can’t replace that. I won’t pretend a new arrangement has already been completed when it hasn’t. Refusing a private invitation should never justify docked wages, punishment, or extra work. You can ask about specific things I’ve actually done.”

“What if he’s afraid to say so?”

Sincerity didn’t answer that question. Adrian saw her mouth tighten. She wanted to say he wasn’t that sort of man. But making his courage the answer to a power imbalance would place the burden back on him.

“Then my assurances alone cannot establish his position.”

She moved her water aside without directing the question toward him.

Adrian put down his cup and raised his hand. The owner called on someone already waiting before him. Adrian listened as the next speaker asked whether family assistance required accepting a relationship. Elena said no. She could explain this assistance’s scope, not promise family support for every future dispute.

When called, Adrian stood where he was.

“I submitted an application I read and signed myself. She didn’t sign for me or require a date before helping.”

The copyist turned. “Will you say you love her?”

“I’ll decide what I’m willing to say. Your question concerned exchanging work for affection. I don’t need to repeat every private conversation to answer it.”

Someone sighed in disappointment. Adrian stayed standing.

“I’ve accepted invitations from her. I’ve also refused arrangements. Yesterday, I didn’t trade her name for faster technical review. Today, I won’t trade my private life for a certificate declaring her trustworthy. Concern about unequal power is reasonable. Assuming every word I say is hers can also make it impossible for me to speak.”

Elena looked back. Briefly, he felt closer than he would have standing behind her.

A cloth seller asked why they couldn’t obey for seven days, then argue. Elena answered that even limited restrictions needed an explanation, particularly when priority required choosing an assessment partner first. She sought neither to erase genuine danger nor to portray ordinary company as a contract automatically transmitting fate-related risk.

Someone asked for details of the princess’s injury. The owner recalled the rules. Elena refused to use another person’s body to demonstrate her sincerity. Adrian added nothing. The releasable, limited conclusions were in the materials. More was not this discussion’s admission price.

The copyist raised his hand again.

“But you still have money. Ordinary people could agree with your principles and still be unable to take your route.”

“Yes.”

She allowed that word to stand before attempting anything more elegant. Adrian watched her unclench her hands.

“These demands won’t pay your carriage fare or immediately add another clerk. I want refusing a proposed partner to remain compatible with ordinary review. I want clearer information requirements. Even then, not everyone will find things as easy as I do. That should remain in the account.”

The owner wrote it down. The woman unfolded her ticket and asked to describe the documents her counter kept demanding. He could record her individual concern, he said, not insert it into Elena’s case and pretend it had been accepted.

Her cold fingers blotted the opening words. Adrian put his cup on the empty seat for her to warm her hands. He did not rewrite her account to sound more aristocratic.

A child began crying at the back. His father picked him up to leave, catching a stool with his bag. Elena stopped speaking while he cleared the way. The owner sent for a dry cloth. The child’s gloves were wet; his hands were miserably cold.

During the pause, the copyist asked whether these discussion notes would go to the registration office. The owner said they contained only what each person permitted to be public. Any submission needed separate confirmation. Entering his courtyard did not make someone a signatory to a joint letter.

The woman withdrew part of her lease account, leaving the number of wasted journeys. She still had a business to run. The whole street needn’t know how much rent she had once owed. Elena didn’t argue; she asked that the shop address be covered before the page was displayed.

“Didn’t you want specific facts?” the copyist asked.

“Specific doesn’t mean everything. She doesn’t owe this courtyard an entire ledger.”

The woman looked up and studied Elena properly for the first time. It wasn’t agreement with every position. At least she no longer seemed to see only a family name waiting to be praised.

Before the end, a man by the gate shouted that, if Lady Rosenberg believed in fairness, she should surrender her wealth.

Anger finally showed on Elena’s face.

“I haven’t claimed a sentence can erase differences of birth. If you want to discuss particular rights, name them. Watching me perform the loss of everything won’t save that woman another journey.”

He tried to continue. The owner announced the end of the allotted time without declaring him a conspirator. Some left; others stayed to read the correction. No applause concluded the difficult morning.

Elena stood and discovered mud still clinging to her hem.

She looked down, suddenly exhausted.

Adrian approached without asking whether she had won. He carried two empty chairs under the eaves; she gathered cups. After one trip they realized others were already clearing the remainder. They didn’t have to finish the entire courtyard before leaving.

The owner told her the copyist still distrusted her, but was willing to read the next official response.

“Is that bad news?”

“Better than reading only the broadsheet. I can’t believe you for him.”

She thanked him, without requesting a heading announcing public support for a noblewoman.

Before leaving, she went back to the third row for Adrian’s forgotten cup. Busy moving chairs, he had forgotten his own drink. Returning it to the owner, she emerged to find Adrian holding the gloves she had left on the table.

Neither had much right to laugh first.

“This needn’t enter the discussion notes.”

“I’ll submit my independent opinion.”

She finally laughed and slipped her fingers slowly into the gloves.

A hot-pie stall at the corner had just lit its fire. Elena’s nose reddened in the cold as she waited. Adrian offered to find a warm shop. She wanted the thing she could smell right now.

They bought one each. She bit too soon and winced at the hot filling.

“Don’t say it.”

“I haven’t said anything.”

“You’re thinking it.”

“I’m thinking mine is hot too.”

Watching him carefully blow on it, she laughed again. In the shelter of a wall, she admitted how angry she had been.

Not just with the man demanding her fortune. She had worked seriously on the application and still wanted everyone to recognize her sincerity immediately. Hearing employer, she had wanted to display every happy walk and joined hand as evidence she had done nothing wrong.

“Those things weren’t meant to win an argument.”

Adrian folded his bag lower, keeping grease from his cuff.

“Not being evidence doesn’t make them things that never happened.”

She looked up. Her determined smile softened into something easier.

Her pie had cooled enough to eat, but she realized she had been holding it without taking another bite. He didn’t hurry her. They moved a little closer to the wall, out of the wind sweeping around the corner. She bit into it and finally tasted the pepper.

“Worth waiting for?” he asked.

She looked at him and said yes, without specifying quite what she meant.

He asked what she wanted to do that afternoon. Her own work first, then the travelogue. They could read together later if he wanted. The invitation wasn’t a reward she had earned for enduring a difficult morning.

“I’ll ask you later,” he said. “Unless another inaccurate broadsheet catches me.”

“Then I’ll turn two pages ahead for the captain.”

“Don’t let him sink the ship without me.”

“He’s getting quite close on his own.”

She finished her pie and offered her free hand. Adrian took it. Nobody asked which application it belonged to. A woman selling onions merely requested that they stop blocking her scales.

They moved together, laughing again.

At home, a letter for Cecilia waited in the entrance hall. Coming downstairs, she read the enclosed form. Her smile faded.

If she confirmed that the historical betrothal represented a continuing relationship, the letter proposed, the material could receive earlier examination under an existing-relationship category.

Adrian saw the line and supplied no answer for her.

Cecilia turned the form over. Two spaces waited for their signatures. After a moment, she turned it back.

“This afternoon, I’m going to ask exactly what they want me to acknowledge.”
