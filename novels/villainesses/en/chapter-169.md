# Chapter 169: She Didn't Just Carry Other People's Letters

Evelyn Vale's name reached the table before she did.

On February 1, an annex clerk checked the contact register against its certification and current corrections. E. Vale wasn't an unrelated copying clerk. Her full name, former office number, and current appointment matched.

Adrian stared at execution liaison and remembered papers forever stopping in other people's hands. The evidence meant to send Elena to an execution platform. The emptied Old Contract Records Office. A maintenance warning forwarded four years ago. One name connected them without explaining every action.

“Will she come today?” Elena asked.

To deliver copies of the present preservation notice, the clerk said. She had internal business afterward. Whether she would answer additional questions remained hers to decide.

“This isn't a summons?”

“No.”

Elena wrote that down heavily. Adrian didn't say they'd finally caught her. Elena hadn't said caught either.

A woman in a dark gray coat entered, handed her umbrella to the porter, and brushed melting snow from her cuff. No veil hid her; no armed entourage required silence. A young assistant carrying a narrow wooden case stopped outside the receiving line.

The clerk verified her identification and requested her signature.

“Ms. Vale.”

Her gaze paused on Elena, then moved to Adrian. She didn't smile like a prophet greeting someone expected for centuries.

“Mr. Reed. Lady Elena.”

Knowing them didn't make her known to them.

Elena rose, her chair scraping softly. “Why are you still allowed to execute this office's decisions?”

Evelyn first had the case received, its numbers and page count checked. Her unhurried movements tightened Adrian's chest. Someone could be doing careful work—or using work to make others wait.

After four copies were accounted for, she answered. “Because my appointment hasn't been revoked. You may ask that it be examined. Holding it does not mean every previous question has been resolved.”

“You know I didn't come to hear that you haven't been convicted.”

“I know.”

Adrian couldn't hear an apology. The clerk reminded them that others needed the shared counter. If both parties agreed, a short discussion could take place at the side table.

Evelyn agreed to fifteen minutes about her public appointment and today's delivery. Older inquiries belonged with the investigating authority. She would neither disclose protected originals here nor ask them to abandon their questions.

Elena looked at Adrian. He would stay, but asked for a record they could check. Evelyn agreed: actual answers, not assumptions. The clerk remained while business continued behind a low screen.

Someone was asking for the wrong window. Oddly, that steadied Adrian. Evelyn wasn't the sole answer emerging from behind a stage curtain. Other lives still had things to accomplish.

The appointment was fuller than anything they'd previously verified. Besides being an old-contract records officer and verification contact, Evelyn was registered as an execution liaison officer for historical contracts. Under specific written decisions, she could issue instructions for designated records to be held, transferred, and followed up for acknowledgments. Each action required its relevant authority. One title didn't make every decision hers.

“Sometimes you do more than carry a letter,” Adrian said.

“Yes.”

“You decide who receives it?”

“I can designate an implementing recipient within the authorized scope. I cannot invent an additional obligation.”

“What if the list is wrong?”

She looked at him. “It should be returned for correction or referred for confirmation.”

“Should isn't the same as always was,” Elena said.

“No.”

The quick acknowledgment arrested Elena's prepared reply. Adrian saw her tighten her grip on the pen. He didn't take the silence away.

Eventually she asked whether the verification mark in her former case fell within that role. Evelyn said the matter remained under investigation. An oral answer here couldn't replace internal acknowledgments still requiring verification.

“Can't answer, or won't?”

“I cannot provide the complete material you're requesting today. I won't ask you to interpret that as nothing having happened.”

“I won't.”

Evelyn inclined her head.

Adrian turned to the present. Had she ordered the building sealed? The clerk checked the delivery. The issuing authority wasn't Evelyn; the execution-forwarding page did bear her signature. She was responsible for recipients and delivery timing. Assigned duty staff enforced the closure. Whether the attachment was already absent before her involvement required the original inventories and counterpart records.

“Your signature doesn't answer for someone else's.”

“Correct.”

“But someone else signing the decision doesn't make your page unanswerable.”

Her hand paused beside the table. “Also correct.”

Beyond the screen, a child spilled a bag of old buttons. Adrian retrieved the nearest two without touching the document case. Evelyn moved her shoe to reveal another. Her assistant crouched to collect it.

The child thanked Elena as though she were an office worker. She answered without correcting the title.

Their fifteen minutes were nearly over.

Adrian asked about the four-year-old maintenance warning, specifying that he'd seen only an authorized copy. Evelyn recognized the reference format but wouldn't guarantee the text's authorship from memory. She would verify handling records through a formal inquiry. She also warned that instructions for one device weren't permission for every modern experiment.

“Luna already said that,” Elena replied.

“Then we agree on that point.”

Elena didn't like the we. She recorded the words without turning them into Luna siding with Evelyn.

When time expired, Evelyn didn't stay to discuss her life. She checked the record, correcting issued the decision to forwarded for execution, retaining both wording and correction. Adrian restored his request to examine the authority for each act.

Before leaving, she said they could apply to inspect the public execution scope without her personal consent. Custodians must explain what could be disclosed from any restricted annexes.

“You want us to look?” Elena asked.

“I said you can.”

She retrieved her wet umbrella and left. Adrian read neither a plea for rescue nor a challenge into that answer. At least a specific role could now be questioned.

The young assistant caught them at the door with a receipt they hadn't collected. He struggled visibly before saying Ms. Vale, at least, never made assistants imitate her signature.

Elena's face didn't soften. “That doesn't answer my question.”

“I know. I only—”

He hugged the empty case. Adrian asked whether he feared everyone who handled papers would be treated as an accomplice. The young man nodded, then hurriedly denied asking them to stop investigating.

“Say what you know. You don't have to prove her entire character.”

His grip loosened. He had received the case that morning from its present custodian. He had witnessed only its delivery, not the creation of the contents. With the clerk still available, they had this recorded rather than turning hallway kindness into an unverified defense.

Elena waited, then took the receipt. At the stairs, she admitted she'd almost asked whether he'd praise signature practices so readily with his own name on an execution notice.

“Why didn't you?”

“He didn't say what happened to me was unimportant.”

She watched ice forming in the gutter outside. That didn't make her like him better. Adrian said disliking someone didn't prevent recording his words accurately. She smoothed the receipt she'd nearly crushed.

“I'll manage that much today.”

They applied to inspect the relevant authorization index and received a slot that afternoon. Originals remained with their custodians. Public appointments could be cited; private material from the old case could not.

In the carriage, Elena kept holding her pen.

“She wasn't what I expected.”

“What did you expect?”

“Some fear, at least.”

Ink had dried along her thumb. She hadn't noticed.

“I nearly decided that if she wasn't frightened, we must still not understand how secure she is.”

“She might not let you see it.”

“Yes. So her face gives me nothing I can take away.”

She sounded angry, then put the pen aside. Adrian neither defended Evelyn nor called Elena irrational. He asked whether she'd like to stop for food.

Somewhere she chose, she said. She didn't want the last thing today dictated by that woman too.

She selected a shop selling hot apples. Her first bite left her briefly speechless. Adrian passed water. She accepted it and glared.

“You can't laugh.”

“I'm trying.”

“Unsuccessfully.”

The shopkeeper offered cinnamon. Elena asked Adrian whether he liked it, then took some for her own apple when he declined. She didn't change her order to match his. They stood beneath the awning, waiting for the heat to become bearable, while a boy argued that the smallest apple ought to cost less.

The shopkeeper weighed it and agreed.

Elena watched the little transaction with an expression Adrian couldn't immediately name. Finally she said she'd been ready to spend the whole afternoon thinking of another question she should have asked Evelyn.

“You still can think of one.”

“I know. But I want to taste this first.”

He waited while she took a careful bite. This time it didn't burn her. She liked the cinnamon, disliked the soft patch near the bottom, and finished it without turning either judgment into a statement about the morning.

Before leaving, she checked her glove for the cloth button Beth had given her. It was still there. She closed her fingers around it, then put the glove on. Adrian offered his hand. She took it for the short walk to the waiting carriage.

Back at the house, Luna listened beside a bright window. Her right palm had fresh dressings; no apparatus stood nearby. She asked that the certification and conversation record stay separate. A person's acknowledgment of a role didn't authenticate every historical signature.

Then she asked whether Adrian had wanted something more satisfying.

“Yes.”

“I had a line ready about finally catching her tail.”

“And now?”

“She isn't a cat. Also, it isn't accurate.”

He smiled. Luna slid him a small drawing of yesterday's teacup with a comma apparently escaping beside it. She hadn't labeled it an experimental result or used his agreement to tea as its title.

“May I give you this?”

“Yes. Why is the comma running?”

“It was meant to be the handle.”

He studied it, then laughed. Her ears reddened. An injured right hand didn't oblige the left to have artistic talent, she informed him.

He would keep it. She added that it needn't be displayed where everyone could see.

“You want me to see it, but not everyone?”

“Those aren't contradictory wishes.”

She indicated a damp patch on his sleeve and asked whether the apples had been terrible. Only hot, he said. Next time she could choose something cold, she offered, without hiding peel he disliked just to make an invitation seem special.

“Disliking peel wouldn't mean I didn't want to eat with you.”

She aligned a pencil on the table and quietly said she understood.

Adrian placed the drawing in his travel book, not among the afternoon's applications. They still didn't know everything Evelyn could do or had done. He did know who had willingly given him this unnumbered page.
