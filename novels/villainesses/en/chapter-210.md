# Chapter 210: The Door Finally Had an Address

On March 9, the first things Adrian saw were two pairs of mortar-stained boots.

The engineers backed out of the turning passage and set a final wooden pad beside their frame. Yesterday's uncertain floor had been inspected from below, its loose sections supported. A temporary walkway kept them clear of the old wall channel. Another maintenance route to an existing stairway had been cleared, leading to the same attended ground-floor exit.

“You can reach the antechamber,” the lead engineer said. “You still can't go through the door.”

Not a magnificent announcement. Adrian listened more closely than he would to any prophecy.

Their viewing order had been extended to the antechamber's outer side. His protective cap retained yesterday's adjustments; its straps didn't defeat him again. Luna stayed in the temporary ground-floor reading room and sent her questions with an assistant. Viola compared the two verified routes from the broad landing below. Isabella added padding beneath her left wrist before picking up the directory with her right hand.

“You look ready to negotiate with a door,” Adrian said.

“Doors are generally more honest than certain nobles. When they won't open, they don't claim to have forgotten your invitation.”

He called that a welcome advantage. She gave him a small, pointed look. Joking about the withdrawn dinner invitation hadn't made its loss insignificant.

The walkway crossed yesterday's gap and turned left. New boards changed the hard sound of boots on stone to a muffled thud. Adrian knew the alteration came from today's timber, not an awakening old world. He still looked from side to side.

Faded yellow paint clung to the narrower walls. Square patches showed where shelving had stood. A worn line marked the passage of small carts. People had pushed things here, stopped, turned around, year after year, before there were seals and a name difficult to breathe around.

They stopped at the first recess.

The custodian's lamp revealed an external directory rack beneath a sloping dust cover. Seepage dripped from a wall joint onto the backing paper. Nothing crumbled instantly, but the edges had curled; ink on the foremost sheets was beginning to spread.

Elena asked the custodian to look there first.

The document conservator accompanying them could remain below only for this period. If they measured the inner doorway first, his shift would end before they returned. Wet paper would wait through another handover. Their permission covered emergency preservation of this outer rack, not unrestricted examination of everything it held.

Adrian looked toward the passage's end. Half a dark doorframe had emerged in the lamplight. Last night he'd repeatedly imagined what he would say on finding the entrance. Now it might stand a few steps away, and they were stopping to save papers whose contents they didn't even know.

“Could we identify the doorplate first?”

Elena shook her head. “He needs to assess the wet layers. Lift the wrong sheet and others might come with it.”

“I didn't mean grab them carelessly.”

“I didn't say you wanted them destroyed.”

Their voices tightened. She'd noticed a faint noble-archive color stripe along one edge. That made her less willing to postpone these papers for the door they wanted. They might implicate her family or concern somebody else's property and livelihood. Their value couldn't depend on being favorable.

Adrian understood. He was still disappointed. He wanted her beside him when he reached that name, as though at last they might accomplish something important together.

Instead, she chose to stay.

“Go check the frame. I'll witness preservation here. Afterward I'll decide whether to join you.”

“You don't need my help?”

“I need you not to mistake my staying for not wanting answers.”

After a silence, he nodded. Asking for companionship and asking her to abandon her judgment could be only a sentence apart. She neither apologized nor promised to arrive for his moment of discovery.

The conservator approached with support sheets. Elena moved where she wouldn't block the light. Her task was to record each layer's condition as it came away, not touch wet paper or open sealed packets for names. Adrian followed the engineer around the corner, looking back just once.

Cecilia waited beside the inner wall. She'd had an old cross-shaped sign checked. It marked a stretcher waiting point, not a place to kneel. That small correction gave a bare wall another purpose.

“You think she should stay too?” Adrian asked.

“I think that's what she wants to do.”

“Do I sound petty?”

“A little. And like someone hoping an important person will be beside him.”

“You could keep only the second part.”

“Then you might mistake me for someone unusually good at comforting people.”

She accompanied him to the marked position, then returned to her medical-route signs. A little shared walking didn't purchase an answer about a different kind of relationship.

The frame was wider than it had appeared from a distance. Dark rough stone carried no game-like emblem bright enough to illuminate the room. A wooden shield concealed the inscription above. It kept off dust, wasn't connected to the inner structure, and appeared in the old maintenance drawing.

The engineers checked the authorized removal scope. Two men supported opposite ends. When the first fastener loosened, fine dust fell onto their catching cloth. Adrian didn't rub at the emerging letters. He moved an ordinary lamp to the angle requested.

CROWN OF DESTINY.

Old lettering cut into stone, no more luminous than any other sign.

For a long while he said nothing.

The fear he'd brought from a game had met an actual door. But a name alone couldn't establish what stood behind it. Wanting to find this place didn't entitle him to declare every missing fragment restored.

“Check that the inscription wasn't moved here later.”

Isabella opened the engineering directory she was permitted to carry. The Crown of Destiny's antechamber had its own entry, listing frame dimensions and the inscription's position relative to a side inspection recess. A separate outer-frame acceptance copy came from works custody, independently of Evelyn's log. Its locating marks corresponded to yesterday's corrected foundation positions.

Their first width measurement was an inch too long.

Isabella didn't remove the inch from the page. She asked the clerk to draw both ends of the measuring rod while she examined small print caught beneath a fold. Closer lamplight revealed a thin stone edging added later. The directory measured the original inner opening; today's rod rested across the new edging's outer faces.

They couldn't simply choose whichever measurement agreed. The engineers also checked the separately listed spacing between the posts and the side recess's height, taking reference points from original stonework. The new edging received its own annotation. A sketch in the independent acceptance copy identified the old opening's endpoints. They hadn't invented suitable edges today to accommodate the figures.

Holding the lamp for the clerk, Adrian felt his arm begin to ache. The ache was reassuring. He was doing something others could understand and another person could take over. Isabella noticed the wavering light and called an assistant. Recovering courage didn't require holding a lamp until his arm locked.

“Only an inch in the way this time,” he said, rubbing his arm.

“Enough to make a wrong plan appear right.”

He nodded and left the inch on the paper.

The engineer checked the frame and inspected the inscription obliquely. Its lettering belonged to the original stone lintel joined to the posts. No seam suggested an inserted plaque. Local repairs couldn't prove the entire building unchanged, but dimensions, surviving structure, and two independent records pointed to this place.

“We can identify it as the Crown of Destiny's outer entrance,” the custodian concluded. “We haven't examined the core.”

Adrian accepted that.

The brass handle in the western relay court retained its own accident chain. The laundry yard's low arch, its narrow slope, and the entering haze couldn't all be swallowed into this doorway. He circled the two locations separately on the plan. Underground no longer had to substitute for every unmeasured distance.

Along the side wall, inspection fittings remained beneath individual round covers. Dust lay unevenly across five discernible outlines. Today's permission didn't expose what lay below. The engineer wouldn't infer operation from their number.

Five faces came to Adrian's mind. He kept the thought to himself instead of declaring that the door had been waiting for them.

Lower down, a narrow shallow slot held a thin plate whose face couldn't be read. The custodian recorded its distance from the frame for later external inspection. Nobody inserted a finger or suggested asking Luna to make it glow.

Isabella passed Adrian the site annotation.

“The entrance is here. What next?”

He wanted to say open it.

Two words. A breath. Every unanswered question waited behind them. His earlier self had once believed doing this one thing first was simple enough, until the handle crossed its stop and the remaining danger fell upon people unable to leave.

“Take out what we've actually seen. Apply to inspect the covers. Leave the door shut.”

His curiosity hadn't vanished into fear. It hurt beneath his ribs. His fingers still wanted to discover how cold the stone felt. He tucked the recording board under his arm and gave himself a task he could finish: reading back the dimensions. One transposed number emerged. He asked the clerk to correct it there.

Elena missed the inscription's unveiling.

She arrived as verification ended. The damp outer directory sheets had been separated onto supports. Inner packets remained unopened; destinations and handover were recorded. Wet gray dust marked her shoe. Her sleeves hadn't been rolled up. The conservator had handled the physical work.

Adrian didn't accuse her of missing something. He shifted the light and pointed out the lettering.

“We found it.”

She read it, then asked about confirmation. He showed her measurements and both records. Only after examining them did she nod. Trust in him didn't eliminate her need to see evidence.

“I wanted you here earlier,” he said.

“I know. I'd still stay to preserve those papers.”

Not a soft answer. He didn't have to wonder whether she'd swallowed another choice to protect his feelings.

“You can tell me that directly next time.”

“I did this time.”

After considering, he admitted she had. Her mouth curved without erasing his small defeat for him.

Upstairs, Luna demanded scale drawings before anyone supplied stories for the five covers. Viola checked the exit record. Discovering a door hadn't extended anyone's authorized time underground. The wet-paper condition report and doorway record entered separate custody. Preserving documents didn't mean their contents had been read.

Adrian finally spread out the plan.

Maintenance room, steps, broad landing, turning passage, old reference point, antechamber. Every section had measurements left by ordinary people. Not one line came from his scar.

Isabella placed a paper marker over the entrance so a cup wouldn't cover the fresh lettering. It was too large. Luna had an assistant trim a corner. A brief disagreement over its direction followed; Elena straightened the orientation mark, Cecilia moved the teacup, and the whole plan could be seen again.

The door remained closed.

But it no longer existed solely inside one man's nightmare. The Crown of Destiny had an entrance they could identify together, and a route they had walked that day and could use to return.
