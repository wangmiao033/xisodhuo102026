# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Two: The Saint Won't Let Me Get Married
### Chapter 56: Why Does a Miracle Need the Door Shut?

On November 5, Adrian Reed encountered a scarf dispute before he had even left the house.

Elena had brought a navy scarf. Cecilia had brought two gray ones. Adrian, despite everyone's apparent expectations, still had only one neck.

“The gray ones belong to the clinic volunteers,” Cecilia explained. “One is for me.”

“The blue one is a gift,” Elena said. “There isn't a second.”

Adrian had intended to ask whether the weather justified a scarf. He glanced between them and reconsidered. “An important distinction.”

Elena handed him hers rather than putting it on him.

“Wear it if you like. But remember, you promised to come back for chestnut cake.”

“I've saved you half an hour.”

“An hour.”

“It was half an hour yesterday.”

“You said you disliked very sweet cakes. I had the kitchen try another recipe. The extra time is for tasting.”

Cecilia put the spare gray scarf away. “May an observer attend?”

“You may observe the cake.”

“I was hoping to observe how you have a date.”

Adrian's knot went crooked.

“If that would be inconvenient, I'll go to the clinic,” Cecilia added. “Mira says other people's courtships aren't Temple demonstrations.”

“Mira is remarkably wise.”

Elena touched the crooked knot. “May I?”

He lowered his head. She straightened it, then left her palm against his chest for half a heartbeat. The warmth arrived before the scarf could possibly have helped. He caught her hand and kissed her gloved knuckles.

Cecilia fell silent.

“I see,” she said at last.

“No notes,” Adrian warned.

“I haven't brought a pen.”

“Why isn't that reassuring?”

Martha met them at the door with three labeled lunches. Adrian's bag had a little fork drawn beside his name.

“A reminder to eat,” she explained. “You brought yesterday's lunch home untouched. The bread has more patience than you do.”

“I'll supervise,” Elena said, taking his bag.

Cecilia smiled. “So caring about someone can simply mean telling him he forgot to eat.”

“Preferably not in front of twelve guests.”

“Eleven or fewer. Understood.”

He suspected she was doing that on purpose now.

On the steps, Elena fell into stride beside him. She wore a plain silver leaf instead of her mother's brooch.

“Thinking about the donation?” he asked.

“I'm glad we know what Mother intended. I can't pretend that settles where the money went later.” She looked toward the waiting carriage. “People at that hall need winter clothes. If the money was diverted, they have more reason to be angry than I do.”

He reclaimed his lunch and took her newly freed hand.

“I like that about you.”

“What?”

“You're upset, and you still remember other people are cold.”

Her fingers tightened. Then she gave his hand a small squeeze.

“You can say things like that in front of twelve guests.”

“I'll choose an event with paid admission.”

“I'll take the front row.”

In the carriage, they returned to the North Cloister relief hall permit. Its approval required the main entrance and side exit to remain open. Dorian Mercer's addendum ordered the right-hand door shut during the luminous sign. They had raised the discrepancy through Hale the previous day, and Ward had checked it against the original permit.

They were going to stop that arrangement, not see what happened when it went wrong.

Bess Ward arrived first. In a sturdy short coat, her tool bag over one shoulder, she was shifting an enormous flower box away from the side entrance. Two hall attendants hurried to help.

“Devout flowers,” Adrian observed. “Refusing to let anyone leave.”

“The flowers are innocent. Whoever put them here needs a lesson.” Ward offered him a wooden wedge. “Want to help? Hold that door open.”

Elena took the wedge for the main entrance and gathered her skirt to kneel.

“A duke's daughter shouldn't be doing that,” an older attendant protested.

“Don't tell my father. He'll complain that I never asked him to donate better wedges.”

She caught Adrian smiling.

“What?”

“Deciding how many forks we need for the cake.”

“Two. You've earned yours.”

“Does the observer get one?” Cecilia asked without turning from her count of the exits.

Only the ground floor was open. The upper gallery had been roped off. Sixty-odd residents waited outside: tradespeople collecting winter clothes, older neighbors happy to hear a blessing. Admission would wait until the inspection was complete. At the front, Mercer wore spotless silver-gray vestments.

The consecrated optical lamp stood beneath a milky glass shade. Its registration described colored illumination to accompany the prayers. It had nothing to do with the old transfer ring, which remained sealed in independent custody.

After checking the equipment number, Sabine Rowe asked to see behind it.

“Ritual objects shouldn't be casually handled,” Mercer said.

“Then have the custodian open the curtain. I don't need to touch them.”

Behind it stood a frame, an oil reservoir, and a wooden board lined with reflective foil. The young operator, Rafe Cole, waited with his hand on a lever.

Ward checked the reservoir's clearance and followed the exhaust pipe toward the wall. Its outlet was clear. She ordered gauze ribbons removed from the hot housing. No extra fuel; no curtains over the ventilation.

“And the doors stay open.”

Mercer glanced at his addendum. “Only to make the light easier to see.”

“Then let it be harder to see.”

The display remained suspended while the doors were secured and the ribbons removed. Rafe acknowledged the restrictions. Ward stationed a colleague at the main entrance and stayed in the side aisle herself. Sabine remained beside the platform.

Rafe showed Ward the approved flame setting. A small flame shivered behind the glass, unimpressive in daylight. She watched it settle and checked the airflow again.

“Eight minutes. Stop at any smell of scorching. Anything that needs total darkness can wait until sunset.”

Rafe nodded and showed two assistants the fuel cutoff. He plainly knew his work. Only when Mercer approached did he swallow the rest of his sentence.

Cecilia noticed. “Do you need someone to relieve you?”

“No, thank you. I only look after the lamp.”

“Then look after its safety. Whoever hurries you, don't turn up the flame.”

Adrian remembered the real medicine bottles at the clinic. Her growing interest in him had not displaced the things she had cared about before. If anything, she was learning to name them more clearly.

Only after Ward confirmed that the corrections were complete did she authorize admission. Volunteers brought people with limited mobility along the cleared aisle last.

Cecilia took an aisle seat in the front row, close to the platform steps. An older woman recognized her and moved her basket.

“Have you come to see the holy light?”

“I'd rather see whether you received boots that fit.”

Laughing, the woman lifted her hem. Cecilia crouched to loosen the over-tight laces while Adrian held the basket.

“You're better at knots,” she murmured.

“I've dealt with far less cooperative ones for Lady Elena.”

Elena turned from across the aisle. “I heard that.”

“I meant ribbons.”

“I know. You look ready to apologize anyway.”

The woman studied them, then distributed three pieces of gingerbread from her basket.

“Something sweet to keep those sweet tongues busy.”

Cecilia received the largest piece. She looked surprised.

Adrian considered an appeal, realized it might constitute an admission, and ate his gingerbread in silence.

At the bell, Mercer raised his hands. He offered a blessing for the relief workers, then spoke of recent disputes that had shaken people's confidence in the Temple's care for the sick.

Cecilia looked up.

“Records can be twisted,” he declared. “The divine answer is beyond private argument.”

He nodded toward the operator.

The lamp brightened. A golden hand opened across the white screen, wheat unfolding between its fingers. Soft amazement moved through the room. An old man bowed over the wooden token at his chest.

At first, Adrian saw only a handsome image.

Then daylight from the side entrance crossed the screen. The wheat did not vanish. It showed a gray-green outline.

He looked toward Sabine. She was studying it too.

She stepped beside the screen and asked the custodian to raise a work lamp.

“Pause the display. Explain these lines painted on the fabric.”

Mercer addressed the audience instead. “Those who doubt are welcome to witness.”

At his side, his right hand made a sharp downward motion.

An attendant at the rear kicked out the wedge and closed the right-hand door. Ward immediately went to stop him, circling the winter-clothing baskets. Metal clanged on the other side before she reached it.

“Open this door!” She struck the panel. “This violates your permit!”

The room darkened. The golden palm blazed. Behind it, Rafe tugged at his lever twice.

“Inspector! The curtain's over the vent! We have to lower the auxiliary burner!”

Mercer told him to continue.

Adrian was already on his feet. A gray thread rose behind the lamp. That was not part of the picture.

Sabine ordered the ordinary wall lamps lit and called for the display to stop. An attendant hurried backstage and stumbled over a hanging length of cloth. The exhaust coupling behind the curtain had darkened. A fresh cord near its mouth held a thick blackout drape against the hood.

That drape had been lowered after the inspection.

Cecilia was closest to the platform. Rafe touched the control and recoiled, burned. She snatched up the thick pad used for handling hot vessels and pushed down the external cutoff he pointed to.

The fire did not go out at once. Oil already inside the housing kept burning. Heat curled up the wooden frame. A blackened scrap of curtain dropped at her feet.

“Leave your seats! Use the main entrance!” Elena called. “Don't crowd the right-hand door. It isn't open yet!”

She dragged two empty chairs out of the aisle. Adrian shifted the clothing baskets to leave turning space for the slower residents.

“Saintclair!” Mercer thundered. “You dare interrupt consecrated illumination?”

Cecilia had reached the steps with the injured operator.

“He needs cool water and medical attention.”

“You have desecrated a holy object!”

“The holy object is on fire!” Adrian shouted.

His throat gave out before his anger did. Smoke caught him. He lowered his head and steadied the older woman's elbow, sending her toward the entrance with her neighbor.

Sabine handed the ritual's running-order folder to the municipal officer at the door, then turned back to help move the crowd. Sparks had reached the timber above. Winning an argument would save no one.

Mercer followed into the porch and ordered his clerk to record a heresy referral, demanding the capital penalty under an old precedent. He drove his seal into the paper as if sufficient force could make everyone sit down again.

A municipal duty officer accepted the referral and barred his way when he reached for Cecilia.

“Your complaint has been received. You have no order authorizing her detention.”

Ward was still at the right-hand exit. Its bar had been fastened from the outer passage, where a scenery cart blocked access. Through the gap, she called for her colleague to bring tools, then turned two panicking residents back toward the main hall.

Elena stood at the main threshold, receiving people from the last rows.

“Adrian!”

He raised his head so she could see him, then pointed toward a narrow service passage. The attendant had just remembered someone going into the changing room to fetch coats for the older visitors. Its outer route led to the barred door; there was no second exit directly into the hall.

Smoke was drifting from behind the curtain into the top of that passage.

Frantic pounding came from inside.

Cecilia heard it and turned toward Ward.

“There are still people in there.”

Her voice was quiet. There was no light around her.

This time, it was the only call in the hall that needed answering.
