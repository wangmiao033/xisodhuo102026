# Chapter 291: Preserve the Past, Not the Waiting

On May 25, Adrian entered the preservation room and found a basin beside his chair.

“Your confidence in me is very specific.”

The medical attendant moved it clear of his feet. “You needn't use it. But don't hide nausea to prove us overcautious.”

He agreed and removed his coat. Taking it, Elena discovered half a roll in the pocket. Which historical figure was expecting breakfast? Someone living, he'd intended. Then he'd forgotten to eat. She returned it without signing his consent form for him.

Yesterday's recovered recording housing remained on its cradle. Its damp edge had darkened. The conservator compared it with the May 15 inspection drawing: this section had already faded further. No curse would destroy it tonight. Waiting nevertheless risked losing more.

One group of traces shared an index section with the old receipt carrier located on April 21. Existing catalogs identified its bounds, but later renumbering had crowded two overlapping faint tracks into one entry. The conservator proposed copying identifiable sections while preserving uncertain ones unchanged. Comparing the established anchor response might distinguish their copying order without guessing which came first.

Adrian had received the explanation last night. He wasn't the only option. Low-disturbance preservation could continue, though further fading remained possible. He'd read through the explanation rather than merely finding the signature line. What would happen if the comparison failed? Would a disappointing result become a reason to ask for another attempt? He'd asked both questions last night, when there was still time to consider the answers without everybody waiting beside a prepared chair. Today's permission covered one brief observation. Staff and equipment would perform the actual transfer. He could stop whenever he chose. Only after confirming that the answers matched last night’s explanation did he sign for himself.

Luna sat away from the bench, her right palm supported. She neither led the reading nor supplied magic. Her presence let her answer questions about the earlier work; it did not turn her injured hand into an available source of energy. Another qualified reader checked recording direction against the verified old calibration sheet and comparison medium. Technicians checked the certified transfer media's issue numbers, blank condition, and separate academy supply.

The old receiving plate wasn't connected to the Crown of Destiny. Main-bridge duty reports went to another desk; his hand wasn't needed to verify the five locks' seals. Outside, someone repaired a window with an unevenly struck wooden mallet. The sound reassuringly belonged to today.

Could his chair turn slightly so he needn't face the old housing? The attendant agreed. A technician rechecked his position, maintaining the required distance.

Elena stood where he could see her. Did he want her to stay?

“Yes. But if I stop, don't ask for another moment.”

“You'd better not ask yourself.”

Smiling, he held his left hand steady at the separated receiving position. Timing began. The crescent scar felt briefly immersed in cool water. No door opened before him; nobody narrated an entire life. Familiar displacement and a small tightening recalled the direction of the previous observation's pull.

He described sensation and direction before seeing the concealed calibration details. The reader recorded the plate's limited corresponding trace. At six seconds, the mallet sounded suddenly farther away.

“Stop.”

The shutter closed. Supply ended; technicians checked residuals. The attendant seated him and asked whether he could hear clearly. Yes, though his head felt heavy. She recorded both discomfort and stopping time without letting praise turn the cost into nothing.

Luna leaned forward. Her assistant asked what she needed.

“Take my drawing over. Not me.”

Further comparison didn't require Adrian's hand. The reader secured the original observation before comparing the calibration sheet, April 21 record, and housing marks. The limited offset supported matching one track to the established receipt trace. The other remained unidentified. The conservator numbered the first separately and left the second unresolved. Adrian's discomfort supplied no missing names.

Before copying, a technician stopped over the first blank medium. Its sleeve carried today's issue number, but the enclosed piece's edge mark belonged to the previous batch. An anxious assistant explained that the specifications were identical. Someone had simply packed it incorrectly.

The conservator neither scolded him nor accepted “probably” as verification. Another worker compared the unused pieces with the issue register. The misplaced piece was blank, but its provenance needed correction. Its replacement required a fresh blank check too. The delay reduced today's copying window and therefore the scope. Nobody borrowed extra seconds from Adrian's finished observation.

Before leaving, Luna reviewed the revised scope. Prioritize material they could independently compare, she advised. Don't merge uncertain double traces into the text merely to label the last line complete. The conservator recorded her recommendation but retained responsibility for the final order. She handed over to her assistant and left on time instead of watching until everyone obeyed.

Adrian heard about the delay later. Once, enduring discomfort might have made him demand a handsome result to justify it. Now he asked how the remaining original would be preserved and who would check tomorrow. He listened to the uncertainties too. Nobody owed his scar a success.

During copying, he rested next door. Through the door came a technician's completed section, another person's verification, then the next medium. Requests and receipts retained their separate columns, with indistinct timing marks left indistinct. Making a page easier to read didn't justify turning two events into one. They were preserving readable history in these carriers, not everything inside his head. The past could survive without keeping his body beside the table.

Elena put his half roll on a small plate. Noticing her check the basin's rim, he asked whether she was disappointed.

“I'm checking that you haven't used it for pastries.”

He laughed, felt heavier-headed, and closed his eyes. She drew the curtain and settled with her family's reply. She didn't announce that everything was over or require him to stay awake reassuring her.

By evening, the attendant confirmed his symptoms had eased. No further receiving observations today. He went to Luna's room for his travel book. Her assistant was resting, a caregiver waited outside, and two cups of warm water stood within.

“Finished for today?” she asked.

“With that part. There's something else I don't want to keep postponing until afterward.”

She watched him without asking which research report he meant.

With permission, he sat. He valued her: their arguments, reading together, hearing her criticize lunch like faulty equipment. Those days hadn't merely served their work. He didn't want to make the answer easier for himself by pretending that their time together had been nothing but a useful collaboration.

“But I love Elena. I want us to keep choosing a life together. I can't be your romantic partner. I don't want my care to keep sounding like a possibility I haven't answered.”

Luna's left hand stopped beside her cup. Eventually she said she'd imagined this answer. Hearing him say it remained awful.

“I know.”

“You don't know how awful.”

He lowered his eyes. “No. Not that part.”

A faint smile briefly touched hers, then vanished. “Don't tell me I'll find someone better. You'd sound as though you'd planned my next research project too.”

“I won't.”

She pushed the travel book toward him. “Take it. For a few days, at least, I don't want you reading to me. Or to wait for a date that will never be one.”

He accepted it without asking her immediately to reserve a friend's place. If she rejected his next proposal at review, would he assume she was angry?

“I'll consider your reasons. And you can be angry with me.”

“I am.” She sniffed. “Also, your archival headings are too long. That isn't retaliation.”

He seriously promised shorter headings. Neither laughed much. At the door, he said his care hadn't been a lie. But he shouldn't have used it to avoid clearer words.

She nodded without telling him it was all right.

After he left, Luna requested her preferred tea instead of water. She removed the extra private reading time from tomorrow's schedule while retaining the copy verification her assistant would attend. Grief neither decided she must stop working nor ordered her to work harder.

Before noon on May 26, corresponding checks of two independently preserved copies were complete. Before witnesses, the conservator identified the unresolved sections and resealed the original with its condition drawings. Copying hadn't destroyed it or filled its blanks with new evidence of guilt.

An index page concerning historical betrothals went into a restricted sleeve. The public catalog noted private historical records awaiting separate handling by the people concerned and their custodians. It displayed no names and treated no old registration as a present relationship. Cecilia read the explanation she was entitled to see, then closed it.

“At last, it can be something preserved,” she said. “Not something people keep producing to tell me where to stand.”

She asked Adrian to join her on the courtyard bench that afternoon. She wanted to discuss her feelings. He needn't bring old pages. He agreed and came on time, rather than using the completed verification to postpone things another day.

She brought two plain rolls and offered one. Only after accepting did he notice that she wasn't eating hers.

“I thought sincerely loving someone and sincerely respecting his choices would make this hurt less. Apparently I imagined respect as a way to earn a reward.”

He listened. She loved the present man who made foolish jokes and admitted mistakes, she said. Her feelings hadn't come from the historical betrothal. She wouldn't have them brushed aside with a reminder that it wasn't binding.

“I believe you. You don't need to prove they're real. I treasure you too, but I can't offer the romance you want. I choose Elena. I won't leave you waiting for a place as my partner.”

Cecilia looked down. Her fingers pressed a shallow crease into her roll.

“Then today I don't want to give you my blessing.”

“All right.”

“Perhaps someday I'll want to. That's my decision. It isn't a promise I'm making now.”

He nodded. She finally took a bite and chewed slowly. Wind swept her skirt against the bench. She held it down herself rather than letting him manage every small inconvenience.

“I'll keep discussing the reception shifts. I also want to meet believers who disagree with me. But that isn't a substitute I've found after heartbreak. Before I knew you, I wanted people to pray without being frightened.”

“I know. You don't need to do better so I can feel comfortable refusing you.”

She turned, her eyes reddened. “You've said two very good things today. And one terrible thing.”

He didn't ask whether two could cancel one.

She asked him to leave first. She wanted to stay until she'd finished her bread. Adrian rose, collected his empty wrapper, and went as she'd requested. He didn't hide nearby to keep watch or leave another invitation she must repay.

In the preservation room, technicians moved the checked copies into separate secure storage. Sections of the original remained unresolved. The old history would take time to understand.

Nobody in the courtyard had simultaneously finished grieving either.

Some things had finally been carefully preserved. Some waiting would no longer be gently prolonged.
