# Chapter 193: Nobody Could Choose His Road

Elena turned the bookmark over and remained silent for three full seconds.

“You've sewn a centipede.”

On the morning of February 20, Adrian sat beside the conservatory window, drawing yesterday's thread through his needle. He had meant to mend the bookmark before asking about his correction request. Instead, crooked stitches sprouted from both edges of the fabric like the legs of a startled insect.

“It qualifies as a bookmark.”

“I wasn't disputing its identity.”

She brought scissors without taking over. He trimmed the longest loose end. The centipede lost a leg but maintained its grip on the old cloth. Elena laughed until he hid it in his palm.

“I could show you how to finish the seam.”

“Without unpicking it.”

“Has your trust in me fallen that far?”

Her smile faltered. Both heard the other question inside the joke. Adrian held the needle near her fingers, letting her demonstrate the knot.

“In this particular matter, I trust you to find it hideous.”

“Quite correctly.”

She didn't use the opening to ask anything larger. He tightened the little knot. It held. At least one thing had been finished this morning.

Wheels sounded beyond the door. Isabella arrived with rain on her cloak. Cecilia followed, planting a muddy shoe on the mat and announcing that she hadn't stepped into a puddle. The puddle had appeared after she'd put her foot down.

“The capital must investigate this premeditated water,” Isabella said.

Viola came last, her caregiver arranging a cushion. She glanced seriously outside before Adrian explained that only a shoe was wet. The question she'd been preparing about road conditions went unasked.

Luna already occupied the window couch. Her right palm was raised on its pad, her left hand under a light blanket. She had one short visiting period.

“At least one of today's guests admits exactly what she's put her foot in.”

The laughter didn't last. Martha delivered a letter and placed it beside the bookmark.

The coach station requested Adrian's attendance tomorrow to clarify concerns about unusual devices. Participation was described as voluntary. At the bottom came a recommendation: until the concern was resolved, he should refrain from using that station.

It identified no prohibited object.

Adrian remembered the ordinary clasp, now back at the bookshop. Both driver and ticket seller had seen it. He turned the letter over. Blank.

“I can go.”

“Not alone,” Elena said immediately.

Isabella laid down her gloves with her right hand. “Going alone makes this look like a private appeal. I have a public audience on the twenty-fourth. Before then I can put the question to representatives handling city affairs. Let them explain what the notice actually authorizes.”

“That's four days,” Cecilia said. “A volunteer at the meal house asked yesterday whether people with marks should enter the prayer room. We can't wait until they've written a list.”

“Take him there now, and every child becomes an illustration for the newspapers,” Elena said.

“I wasn't proposing to use children as witnesses.”

“I know. Other people may not care what you intended.”

Luna looked up. “How many people do you want believing an unexplained fragment? Let me establish the sampling limits before you choose somewhere that can withstand questions.”

“What does he do meanwhile?” Isabella asked. “Hand your technical explanation over at every door?”

Rain suddenly seemed loud against the glass. Adrian still held his needle. As he secured it in the needle case, Elena mentioned that she'd asked about the road to the country house. If he wanted, he could spend a few days there until the excitement diminished.

“You've asked?”

“Whether it's available. I haven't accepted for you.”

She clarified quickly, stiff with being discovered preparing something.

“I could come too. Work could be forwarded. You could walk without—”

“Seeing people afraid of me every time.”

She didn't deny it. He wanted to accept. Imagining a road on which he needn't first inspect people's expressions eased something in his chest.

That made him angry. Every proposal made sense. Five women could each draw him a road, while he sat among them like a sixth document awaiting consideration.

Viola pushed the station letter toward the center with her right hand.

“They call it voluntary, but recommend he stay away until he answers. Who decides when the concern has been resolved?”

“Exactly why we need public pressure,” Isabella said.

“And the manager's authority. If the station added this itself, it should explain. If there's a dangerous object, the ordinary inspection should apply. Knowing Adrian doesn't entitle us to demand that nobody ever question him.”

Elena stood. “Who demanded that? They've already inspected the clasp.”

“Then that inspection belongs in the record.”

“Must he keep everybody else's memory for them too?”

Viola's jaw tightened. Her supported left arm stayed still, but her body leaned forward as though she might rise. Her caregiver quietly spoke her name. She settled against the cushion.

“I once wanted a superior who believed me to say one word and end everything. Later I understood how easily that word could destroy somebody else.”

Elena had no immediate reply.

A gardener entered to move a pot. His trolley caught on the threshold. Seeing their expressions, he tried to withdraw, blocking the maid bringing Luna's medicine. Adrian rose and opened the other door so both could pass.

The gardener apologized repeatedly. Adrian steadied the handle and said the doorway was narrow. He had been sitting in an increasingly narrow passage himself, waiting for others to decide which direction he should be squeezed.

He didn't sit down again.

“I'll go tomorrow. I'll ask about this letter and answer what I know. Viola, would you come for as long as your medical instructions allow? I need somebody to ask the questions I'll forget when I want to argue.”

“Yes. With my caregiver, until the agreed time.”

“Elena, I'm not going to the country house today.”

She nodded, fingers tight on the chair back.

“Keep the address. Don't order the carriage. If I want a rest, I'll say so. That won't make today's decision untrue.”

“All right.”

Isabella asked whether she could name him at the public audience. He wanted to see the scope first. She could support his rights; she couldn't promise that no danger could ever exist.

She put away a draft she hadn't unfolded.

“I'll have to delete the finest sentence.”

“How fine?” Luna asked.

“Fine enough to make you write ten pages of corrections tomorrow.”

Cecilia left the meal-house invitation with him. Ordinary visitor arrangements, his decision. He offered to distribute empty bowls on the morning of the twenty-second. If the person in charge objected, she should tell him beforehand, rather than let everybody discover it at the door.

“Empty bowls?” Luna asked.

“My technique with full ones is unreliable.”

“A risk worth publishing.”

Her caregiver announced the end of the visit. Adrian picked up the travel book, remembering yesterday's promise to ask whether she wanted him to read. Luna regarded it with genuine regret.

“Your argument used up today's allowance. Find out whether the captain keeps his hat this time. Don't tell me the ending.”

Cecilia held the door on her way out. The disagreement moved with the chairs. A few jokes hadn't removed it.

Isabella lingered over her cloak. An attendant handed her a narrow envelope. She read it and folded it neatly. Her earlier smile was gone.

“Bad news?” Adrian asked.

“Baron Fenwick is willing to co-sponsor the proposal for public answers. He can get it onto the next agenda.”

“That sounds useful.”

“He wants it kept general. No mention of you on the twenty-fourth, and no impression that I support any particular individual.”

It wasn't a sudden threat. They'd discussed the draft last week. Fenwick favored letting ordinary applicants know who was responsible for answering them. He didn't want responsibility for a controversy over old history. His letter was courteous. He even asked after her wrist.

“You could do the general work first,” Adrian said.

“Certainly. While making sure everyone understands that ordinary people don't include you.”

She pocketed the letter and instructed the attendant to promise a complete answer by tomorrow. She didn't tear it up or order the baron to be brave. The sponsorship mattered. Losing it would hurt.

“I don't want you burning every bridge for me.”

“Then don't stand beside the bridge directing the fire.”

He stared. She smiled first.

“Sorry. That line belongs in an assembly debate. I've heard your concern. I'll make the decision.”

He opened the door. Two steps later she stopped and sent her attendant back for her gloves. A dignified departure still had to accommodate one forgetful right hand.

While waiting for her carriage, Viola asked whether he'd prefer her in uniform tomorrow. Whatever was comfortable, he said, but he didn't want a party that looked prepared to occupy the station.

“I can't even open my own medicine bottle one-handed.”

“A reassuring level of military strength.”

She studied him, then smiled. “You handle the bottle. I'll handle the questions.”

He agreed. Her caregiver, having overheard, firmly retained responsibility for the medicine. Half their division of duties vanished before it had begun. Viola's ears turned pink. Adrian refrained from teasing her all the way to the carriage.

That afternoon he wrote his own reply to the station. He agreed to attend but left acceptance of restrictions unwritten, asking for the basis of the travel recommendation. He had just sealed it when Elena brought an arrival slip.

The supplementary questions concerning the old judicial file were still being pursued. She'd already read the unpromising answer. She hadn't waited until dinner to bring it.

He placed it where he could see it.

“I almost offered Father's carriage for tomorrow,” she said.

“It's a very large carriage.”

“I know what you're mocking.”

“I'm worried the driver will have to inspect an entire vehicle.”

She laughed, then ran a finger along her cuff.

“I hate that you have to keep explaining. I thought if they saw you get out beside me, perhaps they'd say less.”

“Perhaps more.”

“Yes.”

He slipped the repaired bookmark into the travel book. Its ugly stitches protruded. She noticed and refrained from commenting.

“You have your own reading appointment tomorrow,” he said. “Don't cancel. I'd like to tell you about my day in the evening, even if I tell it badly.”

“Is that an invitation?”

“After dinner. Don't bring a correcting pen.”

“I'll try.”

“Not a reassuring answer.”

She sat beside him. Together they checked the station's address without making a single word in his letter sound braver.

After it was sent, the porter brought a message. The station would receive him at the original time. Its manager would explain the recommendation about travel in person.

Adrian put tomorrow's ordinary ticket stub in his coat pocket. Only then did he realize he'd spent the morning waiting for five women to choose a method that couldn't be wrong.

They couldn't. Neither could he.

But the door he would cross tomorrow had finally become his own decision.
