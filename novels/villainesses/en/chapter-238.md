# Chapter 238: Let Them Read the Words

On the morning of April 6, a printer turned the princess's first declaration over.

“Nothing on the back. No room to hold it on the front.”

Adrian thought she meant the argument. She pointed to the margin. The copy ran so close to the edge that the press clamps would cover the last line. That line happened to say silence must not be treated as consent.

Isabella moved the sheet with her right hand. Her brief smile vanished as she read it.

“Then give it another page.”

“We don't have the paper.”

The shop also owed price lists to the spring market. Those sheets already belonged to stallholders under their contracts. The printer wouldn't leave ordinary customers without notices because a princess had entered the room. After saying it, she glanced at the manager, apparently expecting a delayed reprimand.

“How much has my deposit actually secured?” Isabella asked.

The manager gave the quantity on their order. Isabella struck out the decorative signature treatment. They would use a plain heading, with a full text and a separate short explanation. Her name mustn't consume the space needed for the statement. Capital copies would be limited to the paper available. Complete specimens could go to willing provincial printers and registries, which would decide whether to reproduce them.

Adrian moved nationwide from the completed-distribution column to intended destinations. People at the border wouldn't hear by lunch merely because they were anxious here. A horse still had to travel the road.

The ordinary public-notice registry had accepted the declaration. Its receipt permitted publication as a statement; it neither restored Isabella's special coordinating authority nor made the text a royal decree. Her title accompanied her signature. The registry and printers retained their own responsibilities.

“By royal command might make inspection easier,” the manager suggested quietly.

“I have no such command.”

He said it was customary. She had the phrase removed, including the type already set. Adrian watched a small metal word fall back into its compartment. It was quieter than dramatically tearing a document in half.

They had argued about the opening before coming here. Isabella had first written, We have found a way to spare everyone from sacrifice. During her short review session, Luna had asked her assistant to circle everyone. Did that include untested scale and the unestablished limits of human contracts?

“If we start by saying we're not ready, nobody will read the next sentence,” Isabella had objected.

“Then make the next sentence worth reading. Don't ask the first to finish the engineering.”

Elena supported removing the guarantee, but refused to turn the declaration into something only researchers could understand. Cecilia said those facing conscription needed to know what was happening before being taught an entire vocabulary.

Adrian eventually put three separate questions on paper: What had happened? How much did they know? What could an ordinary person do now? At last they worked on one page instead of competing over whose voice sounded most like victory.

The second proof began: An institution operated by people is preparing to concentrate a shared danger upon a few designated individuals. We reject designation as a substitute for consent.

It acknowledged real evidence of calamity. Exposing lies wouldn't make disasters disappear. Limited simulation had shown small-scale distribution was possible, but didn't establish national protection or demonstrate any result through a person's body.

The difference between the original design and later targeted sacrifice appeared with conclusions cleared for publication, document categories, and places where verification could be requested. Restricted pages, originals, and private identities weren't emptied into the street beside stirring prose.

Isabella stopped at the passage acknowledging royal participation in the institution. It didn't convict every person still under investigation. Nor did she remove her family name and leave the history for others to bear.

“This goes in too?” the printer asked.

“Yes.”

“You can't collect it again afterward.”

“I know.”

Her right fingers weren't quite steady on the sheet. Her left wrist remained supported. Knowing the consequences didn't mean she wasn't afraid of them.

The shorter explanation told recipients of summonses, equipment demands, or unusual orders to retain the documents and delivery times and seek advice through appropriate registries, medical services, or legal channels. It didn't instruct them to refuse ordinary protection work or assemble somewhere to demonstrate loyalty to a princess.

Cecilia had insisted that food relief and medical help mustn't depend on a supporter list. Elena had replaced offer voluntary support with ask questions or raise objections. A kindly phrase shouldn't suggest that only allies would be heard.

“You're making this recruitment letter remarkably bad at recruiting,” said the typesetter.

“It isn't a recruitment letter,” Adrian replied.

The man finally nodded and took the proof away.

While the ink dried, Isabella invited Adrian to lunch. She heard her own meeting-chair tone and added that he could decline if he'd made plans.

“I promised Elena.”

“Then I asked too late.”

She answered quickly, as though disappointment might show if she took longer. Adrian didn't compensate with a promise to spend every other afternoon with her. He could check the final dispatch list before lunch, then return here for work. She accepted the invitation's failure without recasting it as official business requiring his presence.

“At least give me your opinion of this writing.”

“Very neat.”

“The declaration.”

“Then I need to read it again. I really was looking at the lettering.”

She laughed, pointing to an overlong sentence with her right hand. “Start there. Seriously this time. My courage can't breathe for the reader.”

They divided the sentence without losing its meaning. When she accepted responsibility for signing publicly, he didn't transform that into accepting every risk for the kingdom. The people between those two meanings were precisely the people they wanted to protect.

Elena waited at a small shop on the corner. Their table barely held two bowls. She draped her shawl over her chair to leave his papers somewhere free of soup.

“She invited you?”

He nodded and told her his answer.

Elena stirred her soup. Hearing he'd declined pleased her, she admitted, but she didn't want that pleasure to become proof he owed her after every invitation. He didn't want lunch to become an inspection either.

“Then why is the dispatch list between our bowls?”

He looked down and put it away. She smiled and shared her toast.

“That's better.”

Back at the shop, the first sheets were drying. The printer found a missing word in the short version: not had vanished before verified. She immediately separated the stack and stopped the press.

The manager stared at the wasted paper. Isabella had the correct sentence read aloud before asking how much could be reprinted. Waste belonged to this printing job, with charges handled under the actual contract, not privately deducted from the worker who noticed. The samples already placed in mailbags were taken out and checked. None had left the shop.

Adrian held the sheet with its single missing word and felt cold. This wasn't Oswin's conspiracy. Haste, fatigue, and misplaced type were enough to promise something they had no right to promise.

During the afternoon, the full text and short version received their version date. Dispatch staff recorded destinations, channels, and actual departure arrangements. A capital notice office received an archive copy. Its receipt was retained with the dispatch record, so that anyone checking later could distinguish a statement accepted for filing from a packet merely prepared for departure. One did not silently stand in for the other. Provincial packets used existing couriers and commercial mail. Costs came from the specified declaration budget. Calling an unresolved household shortfall patriotic generosity wouldn't erase it.

A mail carrier refused one bag because his order covered only two towns. He would quote another route and price, not promise additional delivery today. Isabella left the bag among the unsent items while her assistant sought an appropriate service. Delivery wasn't an attitude created by her signature.

By evening, people were stopping at the designated capital noticeboard. An ordinary clerk displayed the registry copy and read the printed words. Those unable to read could listen; others could compare the text line by line. Isabella stood beside the gathering instead of occupying a platform or answering every question immediately.

A woman selling thread asked whether ordinary people would be conscripted tomorrow. The clerk explained that this declaration didn't establish an order covering everyone. An actual summons needed examination of its scope. Oswin's deadline for the five marked women had been stated; that wasn't evidence that every household would lose someone.

A man asked whether the princess wanted people to believe her rather than the High Priest.

Isabella stepped forward. “I'm asking you to examine these words and their basis. My title doesn't make an error true.”

He wasn't satisfied. Who had time to investigate every archive? Adrian didn't accuse him of laziness. The office retained the short explanation and an index to material already public. Willing local representatives could collect further questions. Nobody expected each resident to enter a records vault himself. The man took a sheet without declaring support.

A young copyist hurried after Isabella with a personal keepsake book. His mother wouldn't believe he'd stood on the same street as a princess. She reached for the pen, then noticed the heading: Names of Those Supporting the Declaration.

She returned the book and asked which sort of record it was.

Both, the embarrassed young man admitted. He'd thought putting names together might encourage people to pass it around. Isabella said a keepsake could have a fresh page. Support required asking each person, not constructing a list from chance meetings. She wrote the date and her own name on a new page, signing for nobody else.

Adrian steadied the cover against the wind.

“Won't it look as though there aren't many of us?” the copyist asked.

“We don't know how many. That's a reason not to fill the page first.”

The young man folded the original page and went to explain again to the people whose names he'd collected.

At dusk, the first acknowledgment arrived from a neighboring notice office. Just one. Only packets that had actually departed were marked sent; others remained blank. Isabella considered the small receipt.

“Does it look like very little?”

“It is very little,” Adrian said. “But it arrived.”

She handed it to the registrar without expanding it into the whole country knows.

As they left the board, a Temple attendant delivered papers to a duty office at the far end of the street. He neither tore down the declaration nor challenged Isabella publicly. He simply asked which porter would receive delivery acknowledgments for the designated individuals after noon tomorrow.

Adrian paused at noon. Isabella heard it too.

Inside the print shop, the last sheet still wasn't dry. Outside, other people were already preparing to deliver a different kind of document.
