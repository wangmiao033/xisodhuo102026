# Chapter 287: No Pardon on the Back of a Receipt

On May 19, Evelyn entered court without another document capable of rescuing them.

She brought only her attendance notice and the receipt from handing over the old log in March. Its corners had softened. She smoothed it on the table, and Adrian remembered the narrow wooden box beside her in the receiving room.

The box was no longer hers. Its contents no longer depended on her explanation alone.

Today's independent judicial proceeding concerned Evelyn's specific acts. It wasn't simply the public inquiry turning another page. Her representative sat beside her; the prosecution material had been supplied beforehand. The court established what she admitted and what remained contested. Custody gaps and uncertain old chronology stayed in the record. Being a records officer didn't make her a witness to every historical cycle.

When the clerk read the allegation of retaining the old volume without permission, Evelyn raised her head.

“I admit that fact.”

She'd borrowed it during her current appointment. Her loan slip had a counterpart at the original office. She hadn't returned it when due or answered the reminder. Her explanation remained admissible; it couldn't become the extension permission she'd never obtained.

Her representative requested consideration of her later surrender of everything she then possessed, including explanations of her annotations. The prosecution accepted that as subsequent cooperation, not proof that earlier retention had created no risk. Responsibility for two already-missing leaves remained unresolved. This admission didn't prove she'd torn them out.

Adrian wanted clarity, yet disliked wanting her answers to sound a little better. He remembered the route she'd eventually provided because it had helped the people beside him survive.

The next exhibit was the maintenance diagram's suppression authorization. Receipt, amendment, suppression, and external custody records were compared. Evelyn's recollection didn't authenticate them all. She admitted signing the stated rationale of preventing unqualified entry. She also admitted knowing the diagram could enable local custodians to request independent checks of the outer console.

“Were you acting under orders?” the prosecutor asked.

She distinguished written instructions from oral directions and identified their actual scope. Then she separated her own act from somebody else's instruction.

“My signature is on it. Nobody held the pen for me.”

Her representative immediately noted that admitting the signature didn't concede every alleged consequence. The judge agreed. Questions continued about what she could know and later requests or objections she'd received. A sweeping confession couldn't supply evidence for every missing page.

A representative authorized by affected people asked why, fearing the returned volume would become selected quotations, she had still decided who could see it herself.

Evelyn was silent for a long time.

“I thought I knew who would make things worse.”

“Which kind were we?”

She looked at him and couldn't answer.

The judge retained the question without requiring an immediately satisfying performance of remorse. The representative asked only that eventually surrendering material not be rewritten as having allowed access earlier.

Adrian discovered he'd clenched his hands. Releasing his right, he saw the crescent scar resting quietly on his left. It shone for nobody and supplied no judicial answer.

Before the break, the prosecution asked him to testify about the March handover and April maintenance-route explanation he had witnessed. His representative and the court had already confirmed the scope. He agreed and described only what he'd actually seen and heard.

Had that diagram helped restore joint attendance and achieve controlled parking, Evelyn's representative asked?

“It was one useful source. The present key, route inspection, ruling, and qualified workers were necessary too. She didn't open the door alone.”

“Would you call it assistance?”

Adrian glanced at Evelyn. Sitting very straight, she seemed to await a sufficiently kind word.

“She supplied material that helped a safe operation. I can't forgive her for people who aren't here.”

The favorable evidence remained recorded. The prosecution then asked whether he knew all her original reasons for suppressing the diagram. He didn't. Questions from either side could make him uncomfortable. Neither side could turn that discomfort into broader testimony.

At lunch, Elena offered warm water outside. Before drinking, he asked whether he'd sounded too cold.

“You said what you could.”

“I know she helped us.”

“So do I.” Elena paused. “I still don't want her approaching me alone. You needn't make that look nicer for me.”

He nodded and drank. She hadn't demanded permanent allegiance or submitted her unwillingness to forgive as a proposed verdict. Sitting together, they stopped searching for a result that balanced every kindness against every injury.

Evelyn emerged through another door with her representative. Seeing them, she slowed, then continued toward the designated waiting area without requesting conversation. Adrian didn't follow with reassurance, as though court were merely bad weather he could help her endure.

That afternoon, the court established the uncontested borrowing, unanswered reminder, and suppression signature. Duties, causation, and consequences received further examination. Allegations concerning other documents required separate evidence. Cooperation neither ended the case nor produced a comprehensive judgment on unresolved liability.

The day's actual order kept originals in independent custody and barred Evelyn from resuming the relevant custodial or issuing duties. Further explanations would follow recorded procedures. Existing attendance and contact restrictions were set out within a new written scope, subject to review requested through her representative. Another evidence hearing would address remaining criminal responsibility and penalty. One afternoon couldn't settle years of conduct.

She didn't say she hadn't wanted the job back anyway. Losing that key meant others no longer had to pass through her. The restriction genuinely applied to her life.

Before she signed acknowledgment, her representative told her to finish reading. Evelyn put aside the old receipt and read every line. She could challenge the order. Surrendering procedural rights wasn't the price of sincerity.

Before leaving, she requested one addition. No claim of redemption. No fresh secret.

“March's receipt proves what I handed over. Please don't let it prove that I withheld nothing before.”

The clerk recorded it for evaluation with her other statements. Her representative queried one notice date; the court checked it and confirmed its accuracy. Someone facing responsibility could still ask others to write her dates correctly.

Nobody applauded. Duty staff directed departures as arranged. Evelyn's new notice was thicker than her old receipt. It listed when she had to return and answer further questions. The useful diagram had not bought her freedom, and the old receipt had not become a pardon. Its value remained exactly what it had been: a record of material transferred to someone else's care.

That evening, Isabella waited for Adrian in the side courtyard.

She'd chosen an ordinary bench without royal banners, not to pretend she'd never been a princess. Her left wrist was supported. One place remained beside her, deliberately offered rather than commanded. She was still the woman who had addressed the committee, with the same family and the same work waiting tomorrow. Tonight she wanted an answer that none of those things could purchase. First she asked whether he still wanted to talk. If today had exhausted him, they could rearrange.

“I do. And there's no other meeting I need to reach.”

She nodded, folded her prepared paper, and put it in her pocket.

“I wrote an opening.”

“Should I pretend I didn't see?”

“No. It was dreadful. Like inviting you to sponsor a national enterprise.”

They laughed. Then she looked directly at him.

“I love you. Not because you can save the kingdom or because marrying you might change someone's place in a succession. I love that you won't pretend not to hear when I'm wrong. And that you remember which side my pillow belongs on.”

He didn't push the admission back with a modest smile. He listened while she explained that she wanted a life as his romantic partner, without trading a throne, money, or anything he might feel obliged to repay.

“You may refuse,” she said. “But don't leave me with maybe forever because you're afraid I'll be hurt.”

A cart passed outside, wheels rattling over joints between stones. He waited until it was gone.

“I enjoy being with you, and I respect the choices you're making. Both are true. But Elena is the woman I love and want to build my life with. I won't enter a romantic relationship or marriage with you.”

Isabella watched him. The small prepared smile finally failed.

“Is she better suited than I am?”

“I'm not choosing the most qualified candidate. You don't need to complete another reform to improve your score.”

She looked down. After a while, she said that didn't sound much nicer either. He knew. Difficulty couldn't justify leaving her waiting for an answer he already possessed.

Her right hand found the paper in her pocket. She crumpled one corner, then released it. There was no useful amendment to write in that margin, no alternative condition that would make this a successful negotiation.

“I thought I'd prepared.”

“You don't have to look unhurt immediately.”

“An irritating thing to say.” Her voice roughened. “I do rather want to look beautiful right now.”

He didn't reach to embrace her or offer friendship as compensation she must accept at once. After some silence, she asked whether he'd still give her ordinary work handovers. She didn't want supposed kindness to mean he spoke only to her assistants.

“I will. Unless you'd prefer a temporary arrangement.”

“Keep work the same. Let me pause private invitations.”

“All right.”

She looked up. He hadn't added that he'd always be there. With a bitter little smile, she said she'd once have mistaken such silence for discourtesy. At least now she knew he hadn't comforted her with another promise he couldn't fulfill.

Eventually she asked him to fetch her caregiver. They wouldn't dine together tonight. Adrian stood and said goodbye. Isabella nodded without escorting him to the gate simply because she'd initiated the conversation.

When his familiar footsteps faded, she unfolded her paper. Its first sentence read: I have a proposition that would benefit us both.

She stared at it for a long time, then laughed once. Tears fell onto the words.

She didn't burn it. Turning it over, she wrote tomorrow's place on the empty side: public gallery. At Leon's proceeding, she was neither defense counsel nor judge. Her own hurt couldn't subtract a fact from her brother's case or become anger directed at him.

Outside, Adrian waited until the caregiver entered before returning to the front room. Elena was putting away a half-read book. He explained that he'd answered Isabella clearly. The princess wanted private distance, and he would respect it.

Elena closed the book without asking whether Isabella had cried.

“Do you need to sit awhile too?”

“Yes.”

She cleared the neighboring chair. They didn't celebrate another person's disappointment or hide their own affection because of it. Adrian took the hand she offered. Honesty didn't always lighten an evening. Sometimes it simply kept tomorrow from resting on an unfinished sentence.
