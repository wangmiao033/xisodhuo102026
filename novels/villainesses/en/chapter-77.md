# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Three: The Knight Says She's Responsible for Me
### Chapter 77: She Wanted to Sign for Them All

Early on November 22, when Viola handed Edith her completed statement, the stove hadn't yet been lit again.

She had written that, despite knowing the post's terrain, she had failed to identify the altered passage in time, resulting in a person's escape and the destruction of evidence. She had also written that the others had entered at her suggestion. She hoped to bear all responsibility without involving the civilian assistant who had accompanied them.

Edith read to the end, then looked up at her for a while.

“You want me to put this in the case file?”

“Yes.”

“Including the sentence saying you directed the operation?”

Viola's eyes stopped on the page.

Around the room, the guards who had risen early were rolling up blankets. That sheet of paper had neatly packed all the orders they had received yesterday, all the judgments they had made, and the times they had reached the window behind a single person's name.

“I know you were acting under the permit—”

“I signed the field plan,” Edith said. “The captain chose which exits to guard. You provided old information about the terrain and identified where that information was uncertain. Any inspection you omitted can be examined. But this wasn't a night when you took everyone in on your own.”

Viola's left arm rested on a folded coat. The wound had been cleaned, and no more blood had seeped through the dressing, yet she still hadn't settled her hand in a comfortable position.

“If not for me, Adrian wouldn't have had to face that choice.”

“If not for the report about clearing the paper cabinets, none of us would have gone.” Edith pushed the statement back. “Many people were involved before it happened. You can't be the only one left afterward.”

Sitting beside the door, Adrian put down his warm water when he heard his name.

“Sending the rope to the window was my decision. You can't make the record say I never made it because you want to protect me.”

She looked at him.

“It will affect how people see you.”

“I know.”

He didn't say he wasn't afraid. The cabinet full of papers was a real loss. Whenever anyone asked why they hadn't brought it back, he would have to answer for what he had seen at the time.

“Only the day before yesterday, you refused to let other people rewrite your statement,” he said. “Don't make today your turn to rewrite ours.”

Viola lowered her head. After a long time, she finally crossed out the last paragraph.

She didn't crumple the whole sheet. Her failure to confirm the old footpath promptly and her urgency over the cabinet's importance remained to be explained again. But she no longer used “I accept all responsibility” to settle the cause of the fire, the escape route, and everyone's judgments before they were investigated.

Edith asked her to rest first and submit a formal supplement once her own legal adviser was present. The draft could be retained. Exhaustion wasn't a reason to force herself into signing the harshest possible verdict.

Outside, the sky slowly brightened.

The craftsman returned from another inspection of the post. He confirmed that the records room's roof beams had collapsed through it. The original dispatch and receipt ledgers and accompanying vouchers had been badly burned and mixed with wet ash. They couldn't be recovered in their original state. Any fragments safe to collect would be numbered and sealed. Whether legible writing remained would be determined by later examination. They wouldn't describe a bag of black ash as recovered accounts.

The answer quieted everyone around the table again.

Edith didn't put down her pen. She packaged separate requests for independent records and gave them to a messenger bound for the nearby relay station.

Records of RW-6's entry into and departure from the arsenal, its transfers, and any possible disposal would be obtained separately from the authority responsible for ordnance. Matching Northroad Freight settlement counterparts would be checked with the office already preserving the originals. Local archives would be asked to retain ferry and toll records for the relevant period along the routes to Alder Watch.

Martin remembered only approximately that the deliveries had come early in the month. An uncertain date couldn't be made the sole search date. Nor was the code on the paper sleeve necessarily a unique identifier for a batch of weapons. Each office needed to explain what it represented in its own system.

The messenger checked the seals one by one and rode away.

Along another road, guards remained at the post, watching its smoke-blackened door. Both kinds of work had to continue. Nobody could use replies that hadn't arrived to cancel out the originals they had lost before their eyes.

The return journey had to wait until sand had been spread on the icy slope. The attendant examined Martin's foot and had him eat before boarding the padded carriage, then checked Viola's left arm again.

“A small wound. Don't keep pulling at it over the next few days. You can walk and you can eat. You can't treat it as though it never happened.”

“I wasn't planning to—”

The attendant looked at the wet armor Viola was about to pick up.

Viola withdrew her hand.

Adrian sat waiting for the slight tightness he'd felt in his chest on waking to pass. He didn't stand and take everything upon himself. He only pointed to the table.

“Put it here. I can wipe the outside. Ask someone else to undo the straps.”

A guard helped set her already removed light armor on the table, checked that nothing had stuck to the wound and no plates were loose, then went to prepare the carriage equipment. It was her own protective gear, with a small scrap of old cloth worn soft tucked inside. It had nothing to do with the sword still unreturned in the armory.

Adrian first asked which places needed oil. Then, with a dry cloth, he slowly wiped the mud from the exterior.

Sitting opposite him, Viola raised her right hand several times, then put it down again.

“You can tell me if I'm doing something wrong,” he said.

“You aren't.”

“Then are you staring at the cloth because you're worried it'll get hurt?”

Her ears immediately reddened.

“That shoulder plate. Wipe along the edge.”

He did. The metal gradually regained its original dull sheen. It didn't shine like some grand reward. It was simply an ordinary piece of armor to be worn again tomorrow and cleaned today.

“I've always done it myself,” she said quietly.

“Which is why you nearly snatched it back with your left hand just now.”

“It isn't that I think you—”

“I know.”

He turned the cleaned shoulder plate toward her and waited for her approval before beginning the next section. Gradually, she stopped staring at the cloth. Her gaze moved to his fingers instead and lingered a little longer than an inspection of his work required.

Adrian noticed, but didn't deliberately move his hand in front of her. He only set the oiled cloth farther away so it wouldn't stain her sleeve.

“Is it very boring?” she asked.

“Easier than being hit by an old ledger.”

She didn't laugh. He paused, then added seriously, “I'm willing to do this one small thing for you. I'm not asking you to be responsible for shielding me from every piece of wood from now on.”

She looked at the half-cleaned armor.

“Last night, I nearly wrote that I would compensate you.”

“Payment for cleaning the armor?”

“No.”

“Then don't decide what I've lost on my behalf just yet.”

After a moment's silence, she finally asked, “What do you want?”

“Right now, I'd like you to pass me that cup of tea. And next time you invite someone to the training ground, don't feel obliged because of this.”

Viola pushed the tea toward him with her right hand.

“I didn't feel obliged that time.”

She spoke very softly, then looked up at him, as though finally willing to let a sentence stand without armor.

Adrian nodded. “I understand.”

Neither expanded it into a vow. Outside the shelter door, the driver began calling people to board. The cleaned light armor was wrapped and packed with her luggage. Their next invitation to the bakery remained only a question that could be asked another day.

The convoy didn't leave the roadwork shelter until after noon.

The road was worse than on the journey out. Martin's carriage moved slowly, stopping at rough patches to make adjustments. Viola looked ahead several times without hurrying the driver. During their second rest, Adrian closed his eyes and slept briefly. He woke to find his cushion had been moved to a less drafty spot.

He asked who had done it. Viola was looking out of the window. After a while, she admitted that she had made the suggestion, though a guard had actually moved it.

“I didn't overstep my authority,” she said.

“I was only going to say thank you.”

At last she laughed. The laugh didn't pull at her injured arm. It was enough to add another sound to this stretch of road, so slow that it frayed their patience.

On the evening of November 22, Greymarch's gate appeared in the carriage window.

Elena stood outside the infirmary. When she saw Adrian get out on his own, she first came closer to study his color, then gently took his hand. She said nothing about their overdue promise to read. The chill of her fingers told him she'd been waiting outside for a while.

“I received the message,” she said. “I still wanted to see for myself.”

“I wanted to see you too.”

His answer was quiet. She squeezed his hand a little tighter, then let go to make room for the stretcher.

Cecilia was checking bed availability for Dr. Shaw. On hearing that a new injured patient had arrived, she first asked the attendant how the journey had gone. Then she helped bring Martin into a room where he could receive separate care. She didn't demand an account of their adventure at the door, nor did she use any title of hers to decide in the doctor's place who could be discharged.

Dr. Shaw examined Viola's wound, said it had been treated promptly, and instructed her to keep changing the dressing and avoid straining her left arm for the time being. She also examined Adrian, ruled out any lengthy questioning for him that evening, and told him to go back and rest.

“I only need to report my return,” Viola said.

“Report it sitting down.”

Once Dr. Shaw had written a brief note, Elena accompanied Adrian to the end of the corridor and sat with him for a few minutes. He told her he hadn't pushed himself during the fire. He also said he was still thinking about the ledgers they hadn't recovered.

After listening, she didn't tell him that every choice came with a better reward.

“Then don't force yourself to feel at ease yet.” She set the travel book on his knees. “We don't have to read today. I only brought it because the bookmark's still there.”

Looking down at the bookmark with its shell illustration, he rested his hand over her good one.

“If I have the energy tomorrow evening, I'll read to you.”

“All right. If you don't, we'll sit.”

Her shoulder brushed his. These few moments didn't replace the formalities waiting outside, but at last they let him stop staring at his own two empty hands.

Soon, the clerk Ward had sent checked their return time in a small room beside the infirmary and received Edith's account of the proceedings. Viola's exception allowing her to leave town ended once she reported in. The original requirement to remain in town continued, and her command was not restored.

Ward herself arrived later and heard only the necessary safety account. She confirmed that the exit arrangements, circumstances of the fire, and loss of evidence would be reviewed separately. Each person could submit additions with an adviser's assistance. Rescuing one person wouldn't erase every question today, nor would Viola's draft claiming all responsibility be used on its own as grounds for disciplinary action.

“You can acknowledge a specific mistake,” the colonel told her. “You don't need to take every unknown cause on your shoulders first.”

Viola gave the original draft to her own legal adviser, explaining that it still needed revision. Then she signed beneath her actual return time. That field neither cleared her of the charges nor made anyone else's name disappear.

When Martin woke, his clothes brush stood beside the bed.

Edith told him that neutral guards from the review team were currently on duty. Anyone taking over at the door would identify themselves, and Dr. Shaw was responsible for his medical care. He was willing to stay temporarily for treatment and could ask to speak with a legal adviser. What he had signed for would still need to be examined truthfully later. Protection didn't mean exemption from responsibility.

The man listened to the end and nodded, though his eyes stayed on the door.

“Will it still be them tomorrow?”

Edith checked the temporary arrangements without offering a guarantee that hadn't been approved. The existing emergency protection would continue until the preliminary handover the following afternoon. Under the usual arrangements, responsibility would then pass to the local garrison. Continued protection by personnel unconnected to the receipt-and-dispatch chain under investigation would require a separate review and authorization.

Standing outside the door, Elena heard this.

She didn't have her own guards take him away. She asked Edith to give the available application procedure to the legal adviser and asked how, tomorrow morning, they could set out the continuing threats and risks of access, and who had authority to approve an extension of the neutral guard detail.

“I can cover reasonable accommodation and transport costs,” she said. “But he needs to know where he'll be staying and who can question him. Someone needs to be accountable for those arrangements.”

Martin didn't understand every provision. He only rested his hand on the brush beside the bed.

In the corridor, Viola was reminded to return her left arm to its soft support. Watching Elena and the adviser check tomorrow's application, she finally refrained from rushing to say she would stand guard all night herself.

Adrian pulled out a chair and asked whether she would like to sit.

She sat down.

This time, the continuing task of protecting a person had finally escaped being written as a debt one knight must repay alone.
