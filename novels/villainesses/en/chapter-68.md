# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Three: The Knight Says She's Responsible for Me
### Chapter 68: The Page Lady Elena Chose to Hand Over

On the morning of November 15, Adrian Reed found three ledgers, a roll of blue cloth, and a baked pear on the breakfast table.

He reached for the pear first.

Elena didn't object. She merely moved the nearest ledger away from the juice.

“I thought you'd ask about the cloth.”

“I'm applying the lessons I've learned about skipping breakfast.”

“Good. Once you've applied them, leave half for me.”

She sounded more rested than she had the previous evening. Her dressing had been changed, too. Dr. Shaw had examined the burn and warned her against gripping anything hard. The landlady had taken away the small paring knife. Adrian divided the soft pear between two plates, pushing the sugared half toward Elena.

The cloth was an ordinary sample purchased from a shop that morning. Yesterday's banner remained in Ada's storeroom, kept entirely separate. A municipal clerk had copied the measurements and weave details they were permitted to compare, with a note that the dye was common.

Their task today was to establish who had bought such materials.

An adviser from the family's Greymarch trading office arrived before their carriage. His hair was immaculate, though mud from helping accommodate the wounded still clung to his shoes. He brought the civilian transfer-station ledgers and a carefully worded recommendation.

They'd already checked the transfer mark. The old crate had passed through as commercial packaging before being released for reuse. There was no need to volunteer additional family accounts. Some loss entries in particular would require lengthy explanations, while other people needed only to copy down an ugly figure.

“People in town are already saying the attackers used things supplied by our family,” he told Elena. “Your goodwill won't stop them talking, my lady.”

She folded the letter and placed it beside her plate.

“Then I'd like to know exactly which things.”

They went to the smithy first.

The smith was repairing a wheel rim split by a blade. Firelight shone on rows of ironwork along the wall: horseshoes, door bolts, plow points. Being capable of hurting someone didn't automatically turn every piece into military equipment. When they asked about spearheads, he wiped his hands and told his apprentice to bring over a wooden box.

Inside lay short, narrow civilian spearheads, sold to caravan escorts and rural watchmen. They bore neither military inventory numbers nor royal markings.

“I've sold these to more than one customer,” he said. “I can't see over a mountain to know what they do with them afterward.”

Elena didn't push him to identify the attackers' weapons as his. She asked whether he'd voluntarily show them the relevant recent sales, so the accompanying town clerk could copy the entries.

He produced a book dusted with coal ash. On November 6, Northroad Freight had bought twenty-four spearheads.

“You didn't supply the shafts?” Adrian asked.

“That's a woodworker's job. I fitted two to make sure the sockets were the right size.”

He demonstrated with a length of scrap timber. Looking at its smooth grain, Adrian thought of the young man on yesterday's cart who had been in too much pain to speak. He found he didn't want to touch it.

The smith put it away. The purchase had been described as caravan self-defense. He'd bought the same kind for his own watchman. They needed to establish where the particular shipment had gone. Writing the buyer's and seller's names in large letters wouldn't fill in the road between them.

The clerk recorded the original page number, left a formal acknowledgment, and arranged for Ada to send someone to witness preservation of the relevant originals. The smith refused to surrender the entire book of other customers' unpaid bills. Nobody called that suspicious.

Next came the timber merchant who supplied poles to carriers.

At the mention of Northroad Freight, the owner glanced toward the street. Someone had torn down a blue curtain the previous night, declaring that it resembled an enemy flag, and nearly smashed the cloth seller's counter. Elena explained that they were checking two specific purchase documents. They wouldn't post an unverified list of buyers at his door.

Only then did he retrieve his retained copy from a drawer.

The order listed twenty-four ash poles, along with two bolts of blue twill he was to collect from the neighboring cloth merchant, who billed for them separately. Northroad had put both items on one collection order. Delivery was to a civilian freight shed near the lower river road junction.

A stretch of trade route had finally joined up on the table.

Adrian studied the address without imagining every rider from yesterday inside that shed. The order showed that Northroad had bought these things. Who collected them, which wagon carried them, and whether they changed hands again still had to be established.

The cloth merchant brought his account page. The dates and quantities agreed. He spread out some remaining material, its diagonal weave catching narrow bands of light by the window. The clerk wrapped the sample and documents separately, identifying the material as a comparison sample obtained from the shop that day.

As the merchant put it away, a neighbor hesitated in the doorway. He'd ordered waterproof canvas, but now wondered whether he should choose a different color.

“Will red stop a sword?” the merchant snapped. He realized how loud he'd spoken and bent behind the counter to find another roll.

Elena didn't tell the neighbor to be brave or demand that he buy blue to prove his innocence. She asked whether there was any uncut gray material left. A draft came through the shelter windows; the attendants had mentioned it that morning. When the merchant returned with a roll, his hands were steadier around the measuring rule.

She paid the normal price and had his assistant deliver it to the rooms assigned by the town. Curtains wouldn't testify in the investigation. They could keep out some of tonight's wind.

Then her gaze settled on the bottom of the collection order.

Packaging supplied from the Rosenberg transfer station's used-material stock.

She read it twice.

The adviser stepped closer at once.

“That is the loss account I mentioned this morning. We should discuss it at the office.”

They didn't argue in the shop. The town clerk took charge of the merchants' relevant documents. Elena and Adrian returned to the trading office, where the adviser opened a narrow cupboard and produced a thin ledger he hadn't placed on the breakfast table.

Early that month, the station had written off wrapping cloth, packing straps, and old crates as water-damaged. The usable portion had been sold cheaply to Northroad Freight. The write-off and recovered payment didn't reconcile. The difference wouldn't buy an estate, but it was enough to discourage whoever checked the stock from looking closely.

This wasn't the entry returning R-417 to the shared crate pool. It was a separate disposal of the station's own used supplies. The family initials hadn't been fully removed.

Elena held down the page with a fingertip, careful of her injured hand.

“Who approved skipping the count?”

The adviser named a position at the station, then reminded her that the person involved had not yet arrived and the signature required verification. He hadn't destroyed the ledger. He'd simply hoped to keep it in the cupboard until things outside grew calmer.

“If we hand it over now, the papers will say the duke's station supplied the attackers.”

“And if we don't? When Northroad says they collected the packaging from us tomorrow, do we intend to claim we knew nothing?”

The room fell quiet.

The adviser looked at Adrian, apparently hoping he'd persuade Elena to consider her reputation. Adrian moved her water closer without taking over the conversation.

It wasn't for him to be generous about what her family should pay.

Elena finished reading the payments and asked the bookkeeper to explain how the losses had been entered. At the phrase “to avoid disrupting the monthly close,” she looked up.

“Does it look bad because it was recorded badly, or because it was handled badly?”

The bookkeeper flushed. He admitted that one payment had first been offset against freight charges. The remainder hadn't promptly received a separate entry. Whether that was carelessness or something else required checking the recipient. The account already established a failure in the office's own management.

Elena closed the book.

“Give the town the Northroad write-off, sale, and payment entries for comparison, with the surrounding pages for verification. Don't mix in other customers' private accounts. Preserve the original under witness. I'll ask our own auditors to investigate the difference.”

The adviser sighed, then nodded.

“The station workers will worry,” he said. “Some are already asking whether the whole place will be closed.”

“Tell them what we're examining today. Have those involved explain their actions. Pay everyone's wages as usual. Don't invent faults we haven't established.”

Outside the door, two men waited for their loading wages, wood shavings still clinging to their shoulders. One noticed her looking and immediately removed his cap.

Elena nodded to him and asked the bookkeeper to deal with the money owed first. She remained at the desk while the relevant pages were copied. She couldn't ask the people beneath her to bear the consequences of being honest, then return to the inn to await good news.

She didn't suggest that handing over this page would clear her family. The used packaging had genuinely left their station. Who later packed iron weights, who used the poles in the attack: unexamined people and places still lay between those events.

At least she was no longer putting her own hand across that road.

The clerk left with the necessary documents that afternoon. Elena remained seated for a long time. Only when Adrian drew back half the curtain did she notice people lighting lamps in the street.

“You hardly tried to persuade me today,” she said.

“I was afraid I'd sound like someone who doesn't have to pay explaining how you ought to spend your money.”

She looked at him and gave a small laugh.

“Do you think money is all I care about?”

“Your family's trust. The name your mother left behind. Your own judgment.” He paused. “I can't pretend those things don't matter on your behalf.”

Elena looked down at the impressions the papers had left on the desk. A faint gray smear marked the edge of one copy, left by the smith's hand.

“For a little while, I truly wanted to close that book.”

“I saw.”

She looked up. He met her eyes without hastening to call her hesitation something else.

“Would you still like to go out with me?” she asked.

“Where?”

“Next door. The pastries don't look as though they have an opinion to offer.”

Only a little window table remained at the pastry shop. Elena ordered two ordinary apple pastries. Recognizing the woman who'd paid for the shelter the day before, the owner tried to return her money. Elena shook her head and told her to keep it for flour for the next batch.

Adrian moved his chair beside her rather than sitting across the table. Her gaze traveled from the chair to his face. She said nothing, simply spreading the other end of her shawl a little farther toward him.

“Am I pressing on your hand?” he asked.

“No. You wouldn't be if you came closer, either.”

So he moved closer.

The shop was warm. Someone pushed an empty cart past the window. Elena took a careful first bite. Her second scattered flakes of pastry across her lap. She looked down at her skirt with the most unguarded dismay he'd seen from her all day.

“Don't laugh.”

“I haven't.”

“You're preparing to.”

He tried to suppress the smile and failed. She touched the back of his hand with her good one and began laughing, too. Afterward, she didn't withdraw. Her fingers remained between his.

Adrian held her hand beneath the shawl covering their laps.

“The things that made me like you today,” he said softly, “include those two bites of pastry.”

The tips of her ears reddened. She turned to face him anyway.

“That's rather specific.”

“I'm building up practical experience.”

She put her last small piece on his plate, apparently approving this promising line of research.

A message from the southern posting station awaited them at the inn. Cecilia Saintclair's relief wagon, traveling with licensed staff, was expected the following morning. Dr. Shaw was waiting for the extra nursing hands and supplies of cloth. Elena asked the landlady to reserve a quiet room where Cecilia could sleep.

Another notice reached Viola at the same time. Major Voss required a review of the patrol's decisions on the fourteenth, particularly its delayed return after receiving his instruction.

Her existing provisional patrol authority remained in place. Tomorrow's shift had not been canceled. But she was to bring the receipt and duty officer's record, and the notice was considerably colder than an ordinary handover.

Adrian looked at the two papers side by side on the counter.

Tomorrow, help would arrive.

And the person who had brought the villagers back yesterday would be required to explain why she hadn't left them sooner.
