# Chapter 173: Don't Touch That Wire

Luna's first recommendation was not to move the old reader into her bedroom.

The apprentice delivering its condition report on February 4 looked startled. He'd thought it considerate. She could examine the machine without leaving home.

“I would watch it all night,” Luna said. “That's one reason not to bring it.”

Her right palm had only just been dressed again. She placed it carefully where it would not drag against the papers and told him to leave the device in its custodian's repair room. The drawings omitted the backplate. An undated handwritten amendment interrupted the safety instructions. Treating those as complete could make every subsequent precaution useless.

Adrian set water beside her, clear of the drawings.

“What can I fetch?”

“The gray book. Not your hand.”

He paused on his way to the shelf. She wasn't waiting for him to blush.

“I want him to understand.” She indicated the apprentice. “We aren't touching your scar or using anyone's mark as a test specimen.”

The young man wrote it down, red-faced. Yesterday he'd only heard that experienced help was wanted. He hadn't understood how much unrequested work could hide inside help.

The backplate drawing and maintenance record arrived later. The instrument technician's signed note confirmed that the old stored-energy cartridge was disconnected. Proposed repairs concerned the external support and ordinary optical path. Sealed originals would remain in custody. Any impression comparison required an authorized copy preserving surface texture, not something capable of replaying old memories.

Luna agreed to a short visit. Her work would be diagnosis and advice from a chair. Any dismantling would be performed by the technician, whose hands were trained for it and who was responsible for the repair. Hartwell would compare instructions and custody records, not guarantee infallibility. Her permitted journey and rest limits remained unchanged.

Before leaving, the six brought their mutual-aid arrangement to breakfast. February 4 was its review date, not the day every offer expired. Viola admitted nearly waiting too long to request rest yesterday. She wanted clearer wording allowing accepted assistance to be shortened.

“Who takes the remaining work?” Isabella asked.

“First ask who's willing,” Adrian said. “Not automatically whoever stands nearest the door.”

Passing with bread, Martha gave him an approving look.

They checked two small amendments on their own copies, not a grander alliance. Isabella suggested marking uncertain return estimates rather than turning guesses into disappearance deadlines. Cecilia reminded them that yesterday's applicants hadn't authorized wider publication merely because six people were discussing their papers.

Elena returned a copy without waiting to be asked. Her fingertips lingered on the enclosure. Adrian thanked her as someone keeping a practical promise, not passing a loyalty test.

The repair room smelled of warm oil and old timber. The technician arranged three screws in a chipped saucer. Luna requested a box with sides.

“Doubt my hands?” he asked.

“The floor. It refuses to return things.”

He inspected its gaps and changed containers.

Adrian sat clear of the doorway, gloves on. The reader resembled a narrow wooden box laid sideways, with a small reflector between old glass plates. Its frightening component wasn't present. Another custodian held the sealed energy cartridge separately, its reference listed on the maintenance sheet.

Luna didn't touch the box. At her request, the technician inserted a blank calibration plate and illuminated it with the room's shielded lamp. Lines on the white board tilted sideways.

“Loose mirror mount,” murmured the apprentice.

“Or a crooked plate,” Luna said.

They changed the plate, leveled the base, then loosened the external mount. The apprentice recorded each change. Adrian had imagined repair as clever people recognizing an answer immediately. Instead they spent considerable time earning permission to discard their first guess.

An old washer had compressed unevenly. Folded paper propped up the other side, bearing half a purchase calculation rather than mysterious symbols.

“It was already supported like this,” the technician said.

“Since when?” Hartwell asked.

Before the technician's employment; he couldn't say. The maintenance book said leveled without specifying materials. They marked the entry and retained washer and paper rather than discarding them to make the new repair look timelessly tidy.

After replacing the support, the calibration lines separated. Relieved, the apprentice reached toward an impressed comparison plate.

“Stop,” Luna said.

The technician blocked his reach without hurting him. An old wire hung over the edge, pulled free while the mount moved. Disconnecting the cartridge didn't make every remaining wire safe to handle.

The technician extinguished the lamp, checked the diagram, and established that the wire led to a disused side mount. He secured and isolated its end. Nobody tested whether it might light before checking.

Luna gripped her cup with her left hand. Her right palm twitched against its cushion, then settled. Adrian saw that she wasn't less frightened. This time, eagerness wouldn't decide on fear's behalf.

“I only watched those three lines,” the apprentice admitted.

“So did I,” she said. “We needed someone watching the table's edge.”

The repaired part was the mirror mount, not everything that might fail. The technician recorded that scope with the remaining restrictions. He did not apply a stamp that could be mistaken for a declaration that every historical attachment was now safe to use.

Before eleven, the archive review's progress statement arrived. The custodian had reported within the deadline, but the transfer attachment was still missing. Yesterday's completion discrepancy was registered; complete receipt remained unconfirmed. Ordinary supplements continued. Specified unsupervised original transfers remained restricted. A further review would be separately notified; this report didn't indefinitely extend other measures.

Adrian read it from beginning to end before placing it in his own folder. A report delivered punctually was not the answer they had wanted. At least it had not filled the unresolved space with a claim that someone had already established the truth.

The custodian opened the authorized comparison enclosure and removed only the listed plate. Overlapping impressions resembled two messages written across the same window.

With the lamp restored, Luna requested gradual changes to ordinary illumination, not additional energy to force out a result. Two edges separated on the board. The lower line didn't end in the completion character the apprentice had guessed; only part of a similar stroke survived. Received was clear above, but a corresponding page count remained worn below.

“Can we establish an alteration?” Adrian asked.

“More than one impression here. When each formed, and whose writing, needs the source records.” Luna glanced at him. “The mount doesn't recognize liars.”

He replaced already altered with two layers visible. Hartwell checked orientation and reference, requesting an unchanged-angle comparison too. They shouldn't retain only the image that most resembled an answer.

Beside the record, the apprentice placed his sketch upside down. Adrian noticed, then hesitated. He knew little about instruments. Perhaps unfamiliarity was masquerading as criticism.

“That chipped corner was at the lower left,” he finally said.

The technician checked the alignment marks. Adrian was right. Reversal made the partial stroke resemble another character, a more complete sentence than the correct orientation offered. Hartwell recorded the placement error rather than merely turning the sketch. Someone comparing the earlier drawing later deserved an explanation.

“If it had been the word I wanted, I might not have asked,” Adrian said.

Luna didn't reassure him otherwise.

“Draw the chipped corner too. The next reader shouldn't need to guess your wishes.”

Someone outside demanded the instrument back for an afternoon arrangement. The technician explained that packing and inventory remained. Grumbling that half a day had produced only a replacement washer, the messenger went to change his delivery order.

Luna didn't hurry the apprentice to save face. Her left hand ached too much to write steadily. She stopped taking notes herself and let others finish recording what had actually happened.

Adrian counted the parts and found a screw missing. They searched until they discovered it between the technician's fingers. He'd intended to fit it before recording the last step, then forgotten he held it. Relief became embarrassed laughter. No enemy lurked beside the table. They still needed to put the screw in its proper place.

Their result was small but located the disagreement at a particular worn patch rather than whoever sounded most certain. The technician closed the instrument inside its outer case. With it he left a description of the work actually completed, the parts removed, and the conditions that would have to be met before another use. Repair hadn't authorized unrestricted access to sealed originals.

In the carriage, Luna leaned into the padding, tired.

“Bored?”

“I've never seen three people argue about a washer for so long.”

“It was carrying weight it shouldn't.”

“Are we discussing the washer?”

She considered, then smiled. “Actually, yes.”

He offered water within reach of her left hand. Rather than taking it, she asked him to support the cup's base against the carriage's motion. He checked whether that was enough. It was. She drank slowly, without adding further tasks to the closeness.

That afternoon Adrian kept his courtyard appointment.

Viola had a blanket between their places on the bench. It wasn't a boundary. Unable to spread it with her right hand, she'd finally waited for him.

“May I help?”

“Yes. Don't package me for casualty transport.”

He smoothed it. She pronounced it barely adequate. They sat, beginning ten minutes that felt like a very small adventure.

She kept picking broken pieces from a bag of dried fruit. Asked where they were going, she admitted she planned to eat them herself. Adrian took one and bit it before saying shape didn't affect flavor.

“Must you always steal the largest ugly thing?”

“This one isn't large.”

“Your excuse has changed.”

Laughing moved her left shoulder; she adjusted immediately. He didn't cancel the afternoon on her behalf, only asked whether she'd prefer to return early. She could sit a little longer. She told him how, in training, she'd mistaken a commander's cough for the starting signal and run halfway around the field alone.

Adrian nearly choked laughing. Nobody had caught her to explain because everyone wanted to see how long she'd continue.

At ten minutes she rose herself. No enemy waited in the courtyard, and nobody needed her guarding the door. Leaving was a little easier than yesterday.

“Next time you needn't bring a job for me.”

“I didn't today.”

“I know. I'm getting used to it.”

Inside, Isabella had read the repair record. She wanted its layered image for tomorrow's council agenda request. Adrian reminded her what it showed: a recorded discrepancy, not a decision about anyone's relationship.

“That's why I want a public vote on something they can vote on,” she said. “Not your afternoon.”

She changed the heading to a request for an open vote on intake and objection procedures. Plenty of blanks remained. This time she would carry them into the chamber and ask those willing to say no to leave their reasons too.
