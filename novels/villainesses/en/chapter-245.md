# Chapter 245: Where a Stretcher Couldn't Turn

When Viola found the shortcut on the map on the afternoon of April 11, she didn't draw a handsome arrow.

She asked who had last walked it.

Last month, said the person who'd supplied the map. Yesterday, said a riverside mover, there was a trench. The answers could both be true without producing a route usable today. Viola settled her supported left arm and asked her assistant to mark the corners needing inspection.

She was joining a brief route survey approved by the local duty office, not commanding through a restored military rank. The street officer brought the actual closures. A relief coordinator brought the temporary reception site's capacity. A road needed to lead to people willing and able to receive arrivals, not merely point away from danger.

Was the hall at the shortcut's end open? Adrian asked. Only until early evening. Its ordinary users had arrangements afterward. An extension could be requested, not counted as overnight space already obtained. A more distant works rest hall had approved limited overnight stays while retaining its own workers' rest area.

“Let's check the longer route first,” Adrian said.

A delivery worker objected. Extra streets could cost people time in a genuine emergency. Adrian didn't close the argument with the word safe. He would walk it with the street officer, then report the time and obstacles.

“Your being able to walk it doesn't mean everybody can,” Viola reminded him.

He asked experienced movers to bring an empty stretcher. No patient was recruited as proof; practice wasn't called an actual evacuation. The movers would bring experience of carrying people, but this trip required only an empty frame. Nobody waiting for care needed to be disturbed for the sake of making the map more convincing. Viola traveled only the medically allowed distance to the first broad junction. Thereafter her assistant relayed questions while she remained at a duty point where she could sit without obstructing pedestrians.

The shortcut did have a trench. Contractors had left narrow boards for two workers moving tools, not a stream of people. A merchant said he crossed twice daily without trouble. A mover turned the empty stretcher at the corner. Its rear immediately struck a stone pillar.

Adrian wanted the boards to be wide enough. If he said he'd walked them, one blank could disappear from the map. Instead, he asked the contractor when an inspected passage could be supplied. Not today; materials hadn't all arrived.

They removed the shortcut from today's usable routes without declaring it permanently closed. The contractor's explanation and conditions for another check remained with the street officer. Authorized staff placed ordinary exclusion signs. Adrian didn't blockade a street under the Rosenberg crest.

The longer route presented another difficulty. A warehouse cart occupied part of the street under that day's loading permission. Its owner saw the stretcher and asked whether they wanted him to abandon a whole load. Yesterday's diversion had already cost wages. He didn't want another acknowledgment of how hard everyone was working.

Adrian asked him not to move yet. Measurements showed room to pass straight, not to turn. They could use a different junction or arrange pauses in unloading for necessary passage. The owner could manage the latter, but his recipient must accept later completion. He couldn't absorb a contractual penalty alone.

While someone consulted the recipient, nobody told him to yield first and discuss it afterward. Viola's reply recommended retaining a second route avoiding the loading point and identifying which ordinary duty officer could request a pause. It couldn't depend on her standing here personally.

The recipient eventually accepted an adjusted unloading window. The owner designated someone actually remaining on site. Upon the established emergency-passage notification, unloading would stop and the cart move to a checked waiting position. Otherwise, permitted work continued. One exercise didn't confiscate his livelihood for the day.

During the wait, an older man came by pushing a wheelchair to visit a relative. Did their stretcher mean another incident? Adrian explained that this was a survey, not a new evacuation notice. He asked whether the man wished to wait while they finished the empty turn. The wheelchair wasn't conscripted as free testing equipment.

The man said he'd scraped a wheel beside that pillar before. With permission, the officer recorded the obstacle, not his relative's address. Hearing someone's experience didn't require taking the rest of his life along with it.

The empty stretcher reached the works hall by the longer route. Its entrance ramp was usable. Inside, a registration table prevented turning. Staff needed it near the lamp to read. Adrian didn't send them into darkness. They moved an ordinary lamp to a checked safe position and turned the table toward a side wall. The exit stayed clear too; one passage wasn't purchased by blocking another.

Only after checking actual seats, water, and overnight attendants did the reception coordinator confirm capacity. They could take people under specific conditions, not an entire riverside neighborhood told simply to go there. Overflow required the local coordination channel, not automatic occupation of every duty worker's bed.

Should every sign say evacuation? an attendant asked as they left. Adrian looked at people still buying bread. No such general order existed. Signs should identify the actual reception site and applicable passage. A preparation sign mustn't accidentally tell residents to flee now.

A mover objected that too many words were hard to read in a hurry. The coordinator chose a short name that couldn't be confused with ordinary freight markings. Arrows pointed only along approved directions; explanations stayed at staffed points. When the first sign stood, Adrian approached again and found half of it hidden by an open shop door.

The shopkeeper wouldn't keep his door closed all day. With the street officer's agreement, they moved the sign to an unobstructed position and checked visibility from both approaches. It wasn't larger, but readers no longer needed to know the answer before understanding where it pointed. The patrol attendant would include the open-door sightline in his handover.

Adrian returned dusty to the first junction. Viola asked for the differences from the original map before asking about speed. He named the trench, loading corner, indoor table, and actual receiving hours. Her assistant marked the main and warehouse-avoiding alternatives separately. Sections awaiting another check retained that qualification, not one uniform color of certainty.

“You didn't use your legs as a guarantee for a stretcher,” she said.

“Because you reminded me.”

“You could still have decided I was being inconvenient.”

Her praise was quiet. Adrian straightened a little anyway. A young duty worker asked whether another round could improve their time. Viola said correcting the first round's problems came first. Running faster wouldn't widen a corner. The movers' shift was ending; agreement to help once wasn't agreement to repeat it until everyone was satisfied.

Before evening, continuing inspection kept one low riverside storeroom closed. Local staff directed several residents waiting for belongings to an arranged temporary waiting point. Calamity hadn't suddenly engulfed the street. Adrian's group wasn't launching a district evacuation. Actual passage nevertheless used the signs corrected that afternoon.

At the junction, he watched the warehouse contact receive notice, pause unloading, and resume after the small group passed. A child looked for his mother at a fork. The receiving attendant clarified the direction and returned him to family in the same group, rather than sending him alone to another destination.

Viola stayed at the duty point without carrying anything in her injured arm. A young guard tried to bar every onlooker with his body. She had him give clear directions and preserve the passage boundary instead. Protecting a road didn't require treating everyone curious as an enemy.

Once unloading resumed, the owner lifted a hand toward them. The arrangement had worked. That didn't make him a member of their reform alliance. Adrian reported the actual passage to Viola with its limited numbers and conditions, not a declaration that every future evacuation was safe.

She handed over to the next responsible person. Rising, she winced at the edge of her arm support. Did she need to wait for a carriage? Adrian asked. It was arranged. Concern wasn't permission to cancel tomorrow's inquiry for her.

“What can I do?”

“Give the checked map to my assistant, not my left hand.”

He looked down. He had indeed offered it on the wrong side. All afternoon he had remembered her limitation when talking about roads, yet a routine gesture had almost forgotten it. He shifted the paper toward the assistant without asking Viola to demonstrate that she could manage. Her brief smile softened the sharp edge of exhaustion.

“You can remember. More useful than apologizing.”

At dinner time, Cecilia was explaining bedding collection at the temporary waiting point. Her replacement had arrived, but an anxious resident kept confusing two slips with different purposes. She helped distinguish them instead of passing the unresolved difficulty along merely because someone stood behind her.

Adrian brought two simple portions of barley porridge and boiled eggs. He opened them only after her actual handover.

“An offering for a saint,” Cecilia asked, “or dinner for someone finishing an ordinary shift?”

“The second. The first probably shouldn't include a chipped spoon.”

She chose the intact one. If it wasn't a ritual, she could have a preference. He laughed and accepted the other. They sat at the staff eating table, leaving visitor rest places free.

She asked what had happened on the road. He described the stretcher meeting the pillar without turning every corner into a life he'd saved. She listened, then admitted that the morning listener's words remained with her: at least the High Priest knew what to do.

“I'm afraid I'm only getting better at saying no.”

Adrian nudged the egg closer.

“Those people used a road they could actually pass along. You told them coming here didn't mean selling themselves to a trial. Neither is only saying no.”

She cracked the shell with the back of her spoon. His comfort was beginning to sound like a report of actual results. He admitted he sometimes didn't know how to make things sound beautiful.

“This time is fine. Don't analyze all the porridge. It'll be cold.”

After dinner, the academy sent confirmation of space for tomorrow's external-node supports. Permission covered unpowered installation and mechanical checks inside the isolated test area. The distinction was written on the confirmation, not left to anyone's enthusiasm about finally having a place to work. A support could be fitted without making a person its load. Tomorrow's installation would have to keep the difference visible to those doing the work as well as those reading about it. It allowed neither human connection nor use of ancient energy. Placement needed the confirmed entry and exit route, with actual room to operate a stop.

Adrian passed the map to the correct recipient, attaching the correction about the indoor table. He suspected Luna might consider ordinary furniture uninteresting. When someone really needed to leave, however, the thing in the way might not glow.
