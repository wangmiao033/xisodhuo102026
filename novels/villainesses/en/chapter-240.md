# Chapter 240: Twelve Days Wasn't a Promise

The academy entrance clock was seven minutes slow on the morning of April 8.

Usually, few people cared. Today someone pinned a reset preparation countdown notice beneath it, and seven minutes became an argument. One student wanted to advance the hands immediately. Another insisted yesterday's clock couldn't establish today's starting time.

When Adrian arrived, an attendant was fetching a ladder. He asked them to have the clockmaker check it by the ordinary calibration procedure and retain the discrepancy in the record. Moving the hands where everyone wanted them wouldn't give them seven extra minutes.

The notice came through a verified issuing channel. Reset preparation would open this morning, with stages for records, assembly, and equipment checks. The earliest point for final activation review was noon on April 20, subject to listed conditions and confirmation by authorized people. It wasn't a prophecy of automatic destruction at that hour. Nor did it promise that every place would remain safe beforehand.

Adrian read that passage three times. Twelve days looked generous until each contained specific things they hadn't yet managed to do.

Yesterday's objections to compulsory assignment were being examined elsewhere. Cecilia and Elena explained their personal refusal of designated sacrifice. Isabella brought her attachment. Luna participated through an approved written statement from her resting room; Viola answered within her medical allowance. A handsome picture of everyone together wasn't a reason to ignore injuries.

Did they claim calamity didn't exist? the examiner asked.

“No,” Cecilia said.

Did they have a complete apparatus capable of replacing current protection?

“No,” Elena answered.

Adrian's stomach tightened. Both answers were true. They could easily be joined into therefore, accept. He placed the simulation record on the table and asked that its limited results and unproved scope both remain.

The Temple representative brought a maintenance-station summary showing shrinking reserves. Independent examiners had checked originals at two locations; a third reply remained outstanding. Oswin believed established procedure could no longer be postponed indefinitely. They didn't need to endorse sacrifice to acknowledge worrying facts.

Why could a decision imposing grave risk assign a person before obtaining consent? Elena asked. The representative cited an old emergency clause. Cecilia requested the whole clause, including medical limitations, specific necessity, and the right to submit objections—not only the half easiest to enforce.

The examiner didn't abolish the entire old law. Current movement and physical restrictions required individual assessment. External registration could use identity material already supplied; a demand that everyone stand together couldn't bypass medical advice. Compulsory movement would remain stayed pending a further written determination of that objection. Other lawful preparations might still proceed.

They had gained usable time, not a final answer. The distinction mattered when they carried the result back outside. Nobody had withdrawn the original demand, opened the sealed locks, or accepted their incomplete alternative as a national replacement. The decision concerned a particular act of compulsion and the objections still awaiting determination.

Outside, Adrian noticed Cecilia gripping her cup. Her voice had never shaken in the room. Would she like to sit? She first offered one chair to an elderly person waiting for questions, then took another herself. She needed a seat too. Kindness didn't require giving away every place.

“I wanted to say that if I'm willing to save people, I should decide how,” she admitted.

“Why didn't you?”

“Others live with the consequences. My refusal is real without a story in which only I can save them.”

Elena offered her untouched water, asking whether Cecilia preferred something warm.

“This has no pepper,” Cecilia said with a smile.

“Clearly I haven't mastered the highest form of saintly comfort.”

Adrian laughed and discovered how tense he'd been. He didn't interpret their understanding for them. He sat quietly and let them finish.

During the afternoon, academy observers were permitted to inspect the starting state of external preparation records. The liaison hall was aboveground, not beyond the five locks. Adrian accompanied an independent observer past ordinary racks of changeable cards. Two people checked a completed stage before replacing its card with one showing a time and signatures.

No giant crystal clock was draining their lives.

An operator inserted the first record. Newly made warning lamps used their own finite supply, isolated from ancient energy stores and the core. Their colors represented external procedure. A change would have to be connected to a recorded action by the people responsible for that part of the work, rather than interpreted as an unanswerable message from a hidden power. A lamp couldn't prove the core was operating. The danger wasn't smaller, but it now had hands and pens that could be questioned.

During the initial check, the observer found a time copied from the slow entrance clock. They neither declared the whole record false nor quietly corrected it to preserve a prompt official start. The operator brought the local calibration book, identified the clock and reference actually used, and retained the mistaken copy with the correction.

A worker waiting to hand over his shift asked impatiently whom these minutes would save. Adrian noticed the tool strap biting his shoulder. He'd been standing a long time.

Minutes alone wouldn't save anyone, Adrian said. But if they later needed to establish whether an action preceded an order, leaving no basis today would let everyone choose the story they preferred.

The supervisor arranged relief to take the tools, allowing the worker his scheduled rest while those still on duty continued. Adrian didn't make staying to appreciate his argument another obligation. The man merely nodded before leaving. The first card went up later than planned. Its record wasn't backdated for a tidier appearance.

Could the operator choose not to change the card? Adrian asked.

The man glanced at his supervisor. This was assigned work. Stopping required grounds; an inaccurate record should be challenged.

“What if the procedure itself is wrong?”

“I can't rule on the entire procedure by myself.”

Adrian almost replied that apparently nobody was responsible. The independent observer asked instead who could review it, where objections went, and what could continue pending a decision. The supervisor answered separately, with two points requiring written confirmation. Adrian retained those unanswered questions instead of making one worker the institution's sole face.

As they left, he heard a light click from the rack. Just a wooden card. Someone nevertheless entered that sound's accompanying action in today's starting record.

The countdown had begun.

Isabella met him in the courtyard. He kept touching his coat pocket. Was there a clock in it more accurate than the entrance clock?

“I'm calculating what we can do in twelve days.”

“You're already trying to lend tonight to tomorrow.”

“Aren't you?”

She admitted it. Then she said her assistant had retained daily rest periods rather than filling every blank with liaison meetings. Her left wrist remained supported. A real person had no convenient hand that could be moved forward on a dial.

Would he walk with her to the corner? No business, she said; she wanted a little company before returning. He could walk that far, then needed to see Luna's written observations. Isabella accepted the distance. Passing an umbrella repair shop, she said the tangled umbrella was finally repaired. The invoice didn't celebrate Her Highness's heroic removal of thread.

“That achievement was mine.”

“Which is why your name isn't on it either.”

His expression of injured pride made her laugh. At the corner she let him keep the arrangement instead of using the deadline to stretch a short walk into a longer one.

Luna had read only the authorized external-process copy. Her assistant turned the pages. Had anyone drawn April 20 as a boundary between perfect safety and certain destruction?

“They're saying that in the entrance hall.”

“Correct it. Earliest activation review doesn't mean no disasters before it, or no intervention afterward. We need accurate fear.”

He separated preparation stages, earliest time, and outstanding conditions. She corrected one point, distinguishing reserve power from available time. Then she stopped at the end of her allowance. A countdown didn't justify an extra session.

The repaired chess piece lay in its box. The woodworker had smoothed the sharp edge, leaving a paler patch. Adrian asked whether they could play tonight. Luna considered his face. Her assistant could set the board, but they would each make one move, not play to a result.

“What use is one move?”

“At least you can't arrange a heroic death for every piece on the first.”

He laughed and chose an ordinary advance. She dictated her reply without moving her injured hand. They left the board in a corner. Neither claimed that reality would therefore yield a victory under orderly rules.

At dusk, the notice officer brought a correction to the rumor that reset was inevitable in twelve days. Adrian required the verified original wording. A correction mustn't become a newly discovered safety guarantee. It would use established capital notice channels. What distant readers had received still depended on acknowledgments.

A night-shift works employee stopped him in the courtyard. An east-side lamp's stabilizing plate was flickering unusually. Replacement generally resolved such faults; today the new plate showed the same behavior. He didn't call it calamity. Should he send the observation time to the duty technician?

Adrian first asked about injuries and whether the suspect lamp had been disconnected. Ordinary precautions had been followed. He asked the man to report the actual sequence. Their constant talk of fate shouldn't turn every broken lamp into proof. Fear of alarming people shouldn't erase the repetition after replacement either.

At home, Elena had placed the overnight contact address beside the table. Should they move their clock into the bedroom? Adrian considered the ordinary clock and said no. Someone was on duty outside; a real notification would come.

“I'm afraid I'll sleep through something.”

“So am I. I'm also afraid we'll stay awake until exhaustion is the only thing left to answer when a choice comes.”

He sat beside her. It wasn't only running out of time that frightened him. Twelve days might pass and he might still have no answer that satisfied everyone.

She took the hand he offered. Tonight she didn't need someone capable of satisfying everyone. She needed someone who could tell her honestly what frightened him.

They ate and prepared the papers for morning. Adrian didn't write certain success or surround every unfinished task with one vast red circle. He left the night staff his actual address and a means of waking him. Their responsibility was to bring real news, not to spare him every moment of uncertainty.

The entrance clock had been corrected. It moved at exactly the same speed as it had before breakfast.

Not long after nightfall, three knocks sounded. The messenger brought a second report of abnormal lamps—and a request for help just sent from the riverside duty station.

Adrian reached for his coat.

This time, he didn't look at the clock first.
