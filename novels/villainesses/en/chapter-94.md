# Chapter 94: The Witch Signs Her Name Too

On December 10, Luna brought two copies of the paperwork to breakfast. One went beneath the breadbasket. Elena immediately rescued it.

“Is this what you meant by storing the copies separately?”

“The bread provides wind protection. Not confidentiality.”

Adrian took his copy. The open-ended permission to add procedures was gone. In its place was an earnest drawing of a hand, helpfully labeled *left*, although all five fingers appeared eager to flee the page.

“Can your subject ask for a different artist?”

“Yes. The artist will be hurt, but that won't increase the exposure time.”

Cecilia compared the drawn fingers with her teaspoon. “At least there are the right number.”

Luna pressed a hand to her chest. “Thank you. Your compliments are always so specific.”

As they laughed, the administrator showed in a middle-aged woman carrying a leather medical bag. She introduced herself as Dr. Ruth Hensley of the East Ridge clinic, explained that the tower received no portion of her fee, and asked Adrian whom he wanted to remain in the room.

He chose to discuss his medical history alone. Elena and the others waited outside. Luna went too; directing the research didn't entitle her to keep a chair.

The physician asked about coughing, sleep, and how he felt after walking uphill. Adrian distinguished his recovery from the Greymarch fire and the rest taken on the journey from the uncertain sensations around his old scar. Hensley didn't assume that speaking in complete sentences made listening to his chest unnecessary. She explained each ordinary examination and waited for permission before touching him.

Outside, somebody set down a coal bucket with a dull clunk. Adrian realized that answering questions about his body in private didn't make him less courageous. The physician didn't ask which of the beautiful women worried about him most.

“Do you feel that refusing would disappoint someone?” she asked.

“Yes. I'm afraid of disappointing myself too.”

Hensley nodded and put down her pen. “You can take that answer into the examination room. You don't have to stop being afraid before you're allowed to decide.”

She read Luna's proposal, then invited her in to explain what would happen if his hand grew warm. Luna described what the isolation material could block and what it couldn't guarantee. When Hensley asked about unknown contract effects, Luna didn't pretend to know.

“I haven't found an obvious urgent condition that needs treatment first,” the physician told them. “That does not establish that this research is safe, and it isn't a recommendation that he take part. If he chooses to proceed, I'm willing to observe this limited step. Any discomfort gets attention before anyone finishes reading a result.”

Luna wrote down her conditions. Adrian noticed she didn't reduce them to an attractive stamp reading *fit for experimentation*.

They moved to the wide table upstairs. The lamp and receiving frame remained in the cabinet. On the table lay diagrams, costs, and an equipment inspection record. They could check the inspection date, the clamp number, and the energy cell's seal. The sliding demonstration box had acquired its mounting clamp.

“We're discussing one procedure,” Luna said, turning the other sketches facedown. “After ordinary observation, and only if you still wish to proceed, one examination of a nearby medium without touching your skin. This does not read memories or find your old betrothal. Your hand will not be used to power an artifact.”

She drew the lamp's branches. One led to a manufactured reference plate sealed inside its frame. Another passed through a filter limiting the light and onto a receiving plate outside a transparent barrier. Adrian's hand would rest on the opposite side. A rigid support maintained the distance, and the beam couldn't fall directly on his skin. Whether something unusual nearby could affect the receiver was precisely what they didn't know.

“If nothing happens, I won't turn the lamp toward your hand for another look.”

“And if something does?”

“We still finish. Another procedure would need another proposal.”

The exposure would last no more than twelve seconds, powered by a single small cell inspected for that setting. It couldn't be replaced midway. A blank branch, containing no old material, would record alongside the manufactured reference, helping distinguish the apparatus's own afterglow from other responses. Luna also bounded the reading period: no more than ten seconds after the shutter closed, with the power disconnected. His hand wouldn't be kept in place to fill gaps in the data.

Elena pointed to a circle. “Why are there two stop handles?”

“Yesterday's handle on my side wasn't enough.”

The handle beside Adrian's right hand would drop a mechanical shutter and break the power contacts. If he said stop, anybody could tell Luna to operate hers. The physician would call a halt if she noticed a problem. *Bear with it* and *one last second* weren't permitted to turn withdrawal into a negotiation.

Viola brought a chair nearer and tested where she would stand.

“I'm blocking the other way out.”

She shifted the chair aside. Luna looked ready to ask whether everything needed to be treated like defending a gate. Then she noticed the now-clear passage and asked the administrator to tidy the trailing cord instead.

To explain afterglow, she produced two prepared teaching specimens. One bore ordinary fine lines; the other had a known light-retaining coating. Both were nonliving materials, not scraps torn from a secret betrothal. She didn't energize them. She showed the manufacturing dates and registered formulas on the backs.

“This one stays bright briefly after the light stops. So if a line takes longer to disappear, the first explanation may simply be the material.”

“Then why do you want to do this?” Elena asked.

“Some old instruments preserve layers in an order that this coating doesn't produce. I want to know whether the sensations he describes accompany an external trace we can examine. But this observation could provide no more than a lead.”

As she said *I want to know*, her fingers tightened on the specimen box. Adrian saw the impatience. He also heard the limit she added afterward.

On the second page, the costs had moved out of a corner and stood as prominently as the risks. Adrian confirmed the quoted materials and medical consultation himself. Elena didn't simply order everything charged to House Rosenberg. He chose to pay for the consultation he had decided to receive. If he withdrew before the experimental procedure began, its unused materials wouldn't be charged.

“Does the tower need money?” he asked.

“Frequently.”

“Will refusing make that worse?”

“Not as much as supplying a bearing in the wrong size. That supplier remains in excellent health. You can refuse without fear.”

Hensley looked up. “That sentence needn't go in the agreement.”

“Doctor, you've deprived it of its only literary merit.”

When the laughter subsided, Luna spread out the matching copies. Adrian's name appeared only on the covered personal-information sheet; observation plates would carry a local identifier. The original readings would go into a locked tower cabinet, and he would receive a complete copy. He and Luna would each hold a seal for the records; access to the personal information would be logged. Safety incidents and procedures already performed couldn't be made not to have happened, and those records had to remain for the period in the retention rules she showed them. He could withdraw from work not yet begun. Further analysis or external use required separate permission.

If regional supervisors lawfully requested access, the tower couldn't promise to defy them in secret. It would notify him of the request's scope, record any handover, and raise available objections. Routine academy equipment returns would report use of the apparatus, without his name, scar drawings, or speculation. Nobody had permission to publish his experiences.

Hensley gave Adrian a small sheet listing the clinic's duty arrangements and how to summon help at night. If he developed chest tightness, dizziness, or discomfort in his hand afterward, he could contact her directly. He didn't first need to prove that the research had caused it.

“What if I'm just nervous?”

“You would still be uncomfortable, and that deserves attention. It wouldn't automatically mean the magic had malfunctioned. It wouldn't automatically mean you should endure it either.”

Luna turned back to the risk section and changed her original reference to local reactions to *any new discomfort*.

“I was looking too narrowly.”

Watching her write, Adrian realized that the changes weren't making the paper more frightening. The frightening things had been there already. Now somebody was willing to put them under a light.

Elena read the access provision twice.

“You haven't promised there will never be an outside inspection.”

“There might be,” Luna replied. “I don't want another pleasant sentence standing in for something I can't guarantee.”

Adrian asked about retention periods and where to submit a withdrawal. He filled in his contact information on the slip he could deliver independently to the administrator. Cecilia kept the relevant rulebook open beside them instead of accepting a section number spoken from memory.

They paused before lunch. Having spent so long reading didn't make refusing a signature impolite.

Elena and Adrian sat in the sheltered part of the guesthouse porch. She selected a piece of bread that wasn't too hard, then seemed to remember how often she'd already chosen things for him and pushed the basket over.

“That one's fine,” he said, smiling as he took the piece she'd picked.

“I know.” She sounded perfectly composed. “There are no dangerous additional clauses.”

He took a bite and watched a patch of winter sunlight move slowly across the courtyard.

“I want to take this small step,” he said. “Not because she's answered everything. Because I can stop while questions remain.”

Elena looked down, breaking her bread. “I still wish nothing would happen.”

“Not even a result?”

“A result would be acceptable. Preferably one that writes a polite letter and arranges to visit next week.”

He laughed. She did too, without letting the smile completely hide her worry.

“Do you need me there?”

“I'd like you there. You can go outside if it's too hard.”

“I know how long I can sit.” She raised her eyes. “You can manage your own stop handle. Don't pull mine for me as well.”

That landed gently but squarely. He promised, then asked whether he could sit close for a while. She made room, and their shoulders touched. Neither mistook the contact for proof that fear had disappeared.

When they read the agreement again that afternoon, Adrian explained the purpose, risks, and ways to withdraw in his own words. Luna deliberately asked whether an unusual response at the twelfth second would justify one more second of light.

“No.”

“What if the observer is exceptionally beautiful?”

“Still no. But she could be invited to continue being beautiful at supper.”

Luna's pen stopped in midair. Elena carefully aligned the two copies to conceal her smile.

“That should go somewhere prominent.”

“It isn't part of the research record,” the witch said, having finally recovered her voice.

“You asked.”

Hensley cleared her throat and reminded them that the ordinary visual examination hadn't happened yet. Adrian agreed to that step separately. He removed his left glove and rested his hand on a cushion with no magical apparatus nearby. Luna asked before moving closer to measure. She held an ordinary wooden ruler beside the cushion, without touching the scar. The physician checked for fresh broken skin and recorded his description. The crescent remained an old scar. Being observed carefully didn't make it shine.

Afterward, he put his glove back on. Nobody took that movement as permission for the next step to begin.

Adrian read the first page once more, then signed and dated it. Across from his signature, Luna wrote her own name, accepting the duty to stop, the limits on materials, and responsibility for the records. The witnesses confirmed how the decision had been made; they weren't guaranteeing its outcome.

They exchanged and checked both copies. One went into Adrian's folder and the other stayed with the tower. There was no ring beside them, no historical betrothal, and no new claim on him hidden in the page.

Luna offered him the spare pen.

“What now, research partner?”

“Now you drink some water,” Adrian said. “You haven't had any since lunch.”

She looked at her cold tea and, for once, couldn't decide which joke to make.

Only after she picked up the cup did Hensley go to inspect the room for that afternoon's observation. The cabinet remained closed. The written agreement was complete. The examination light still awaited a separate, explicit decision to begin.
