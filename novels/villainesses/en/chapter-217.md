# Chapter 217: The Crown on the Second Line

On March 16, Isabella lost a button in the palace's outer court.

Not for long. It rolled beneath a changing-room bench. As Adrian retrieved it, the seamstress asked whether the princess needed a spare wrap.

“Yes. An ordinary one, please.”

The woman selected gray, then glanced uncertainly at another embroidered with a crown.

“That one won't sew the button on for my left wrist either.”

Isabella accepted the wrap with her right hand, her left still supported. Adrian placed the button in a saucer. The seamstress didn't work faster because important secrets awaited her royal customer. An attendant's sleeve was ahead in the queue. Isabella told her to keep that order.

“No emblem today?” Adrian asked.

“Today, especially, I can't pretend I don't have one.”

She glanced at the embroidered garment. This wasn't a demonstration of royal humility. She needed clothing that didn't press against her injured wrist. The identity accompanying her to the document room couldn't be removed so easily.

Permission to examine the royal retained copy had arrived through the ordinary process. Yesterday's engineering index supplied its storage reference without restoring Isabella's lost coordinating powers. A royal official, an independent examiner, and authorized companions would attend. She couldn't take the original away.

Before they entered, a secretary offered a prepared statement: historical supplementary use of the seal did not represent the Crown's present intentions.

“To prevent misinterpretation, Your Highness. You could adopt this wording first.”

“First, the original.”

“If we let others explain it for us—”

“I'll argue with them. I won't defend something I haven't read.”

He didn't withdraw the sheet as though its existence had become shameful. He put the draft in the pending folder. It remained something they could discuss after reading, rather than a conclusion everyone must carry into the room. She didn't have him dismissed. His concern was real; some of it was hers. Acknowledge one line, and someone might try to fit the entire palace inside it.

Adrian waited until she sat before taking a place opposite.

First came the previously verified military quotation: transfer those designated in the name register without postponement for an unfinished review. Today they would establish whether the cited joint royal order existed and what it had actually authorized.

The custodian opened the sealed box. The crown appeared on the second line. The first carried an old filing reference.

Isabella's heart sank at the emblem. She didn't cover it. A lifetime of seeing similar crowns couldn't authenticate this one.

The examiner compared the custody history with the old accession directory, then requested ordinary signatures from the same period. The Royal Chancery deputy's signature appeared on two unrelated transactions: road repairs and winter-grain allocation. Scale and pen habits could be compared. Resemblance alone wouldn't establish that he'd signed this order.

A retained dispatch register supplied the movement: who collected the document, where it went, and where its acknowledgment was filed. Another custodian brought the Temple's received counterpart. Matching cut edges, copy references, and the final sentence linked them independently of the crown seal.

The secretary pointed to the bottom line. It clearly said review permitted.

Isabella leaned too quickly. Her shoulder pulled her supported wrist; she straightened and requested the stand be turned closer. Pain wasn't proof of diligence.

“Read the preceding sentence.”

The passage ordered transfer by the register, then permitted review after transfer. A further sentence prohibited postponement merely because review remained incomplete. It allowed examination after people had been taken away. It didn't require examination before deciding whether to take them.

The secretary hesitated.

“This could still have been temporary placement for safety.”

“There may have been a safety argument,” Isabella said. “That doesn't reverse the sequence.”

They checked the attachment directory. Its noble-register reference matched the volume recently retrieved. The military acknowledgment's destination connected to this order. The text called the marked people burden-bearers and provided for their detention by the designated receiving institution. It contained no record of their individual agreement and made outstanding objections no reason to suspend transfer.

This wasn't a newly issued arrest order. The historical date remained in its original calendar; nobody forced it onto a day in the present timeline. Nor could one document establish that every instruction had been carried out or what ultimately happened to everyone taken away.

But royal participation in this particular arrangement was no longer merely a military office's assertion.

Independent retained copies, dispatch history, and the receipt supported an authorized royal officer's co-signature. A concrete approval stood before them: even knowing that review might remain unfinished, the office permitted transfer of registered people to the specified recipient.

Isabella read it again. The first reading had made the emblem heavy; the second made the verbs harder to escape. Authorize. Transfer. Review afterward. None became gentler because the paper had been preserved carefully or because she knew the building in which its counterpart had waited.

She wasn't that officer. Leon's name wasn't there either. Courage didn't entitle her to confess for someone the evidence hadn't identified. Equally, the absence of the king's handwriting couldn't make an institutional authorization into ownerless ink.

“Keep the whole paragraph,” she told the examiner. “Not just review permitted. And don't reduce joint authorization to notification.”

Would she agree to family consultation first, the secretary asked, allowing the palace to settle the language before circulation?

A familiar route. Polite enough to be described as consideration for her. Adrian watched her fingers tighten and remembered waiting in another room for someone to decide when he could read material about himself.

He didn't answer for her.

Isabella asked whether the ordinary verified extract could go to the affected parties' representatives. Yes. Then it should follow that route, without an additional condition requiring the Crown's explanation first. Private pages not authorized for disclosure would still be masked. Refusing family precedence didn't mean handing strangers' addresses to spectators.

“People will understand this as an accusation against your family.”

“Then specify who authorized what, in which document. Preventing others from exaggerating isn't a reason for us to diminish it.”

She signed today's limited-extract application with her right hand, slowly. She hadn't confessed for the dead or announced nationwide acceptance of new rules. She'd prevented an established fact from taking a narrower route simply because her name was Aurelian.

As the secretary gathered the papers, he asked whether genuine danger to people beyond the city would make the historical order unavoidable.

Isabella didn't answer with the fabricated oracle. Outside, a coal cart was turning. Its driver called for someone to watch the opposite wheel. Discovering a dishonest document upstairs wouldn't remove his need for a safe street.

“The danger doesn't vanish because they lied. This page establishes their authorization. It doesn't establish that no alternative existed. Don't hide an unanswered engineering question beneath a crown.”

“Can you offer an alternative?”

“Not today.”

That admission cost more than identifying the signature. Adrian didn't add that one certainly existed. Easy reassurance would leave its cost with people who didn't know they were being discussed. Isabella kept the unanswered question separate from the authenticated co-signature.

Afterward, he accompanied her to the outer court.

The seamstress hadn't finished. She cleared one end of the bench and continued with the attendant's sleeve. Studying the spools, Isabella said she'd once believed that putting the right person in the right position could turn bad orders into good ones.

“And now?”

“I still want good people empowered to act. Today reminded me that someone in the right position may first ask how to make the paper look better.” She smiled without much amusement. “Including me.”

“You didn't do that just now.”

“Don't make me someone who never would.”

“I said just now.”

She looked at him. A small misunderstanding: she'd expected praise to turn her into a princess required to be permanently correct. He was acknowledging something he'd witnessed today.

“Then I accept.”

The seamstress asked whether to fasten the button closely or leave a little room. Isabella looked at her wrist support and chose room. Rank couldn't make tight clothing fit.

That afternoon's bakery offered no royal credit. Isabella opened her purse with her right hand, miscounted a coin, and took it back when Adrian pointed it out. She ordered two hot drinks but asked how long he wanted to stay rather than announcing that her carriage would take him home.

Only a short while. He was meeting Elena afterward, as agreed yesterday. She would bring her application acknowledgment; he would bring the unfinished book.

Isabella fell quiet, then nodded.

“When I asked, I did hope you'd stay longer.”

“I know.”

“Knowing doesn't require you to rearrange things.” She moved her cup within easier reach. “But don't pretend I was merely discussing journey times.”

He thanked her without promising an equivalent date tomorrow. Her own afternoon wasn't over. Once the extract was delivered, she wanted to confirm the representatives had received every permitted page, not a sentence stating that the Crown had been informed.

Before leaving, he asked whether she'd like his company tomorrow.

“Let's see what tomorrow requires. You may ask about a particular occasion. Don't make every day of mine something you need to compensate.”

He agreed. There was nothing clever to add that would make both of them pleased with the answer. He could respect her candor without rewarding it with a promise he hadn't chosen. She lifted her drink and blew across its surface, allowing herself a little disappointment without writing it into someone else's obligations.

That evening, an invitation arrived through the independent liaison office at the ground-floor workroom.

High Priest Oswin offered to answer the submitted questions about the Crown of Destiny and the transfer arrangements tomorrow, in a neutral meeting room provided by the Academy. The letter matched its dispatch entry. An invitation wasn't agreement with his explanation.

His voice didn't emerge from the paper. He didn't suddenly appear outside the door.

Isabella placed the limited finding on the joint order beside it. Cecilia requested independent witnesses. Viola asked about entrances and arrangements for leaving. Luna wouldn't extend her session limit for the meeting. Elena wanted attendance confirmed individually. Adrian asked the liaison to convey their conditions: a neutral location, unlocked exits, and separate records of the questions, answers, and sources of any materials.

This wasn't a new calamity deadline. Receiving a letter hadn't made tomorrow a day on which someone must be surrendered.

Adrian looked at Elena. She met his eyes without demanding that he pretend not to fear or choosing his answer for him. Their book lay by the window, marked at the agreed page.

“Finish these two pages first?”

“First.”

They didn't know what the man would produce tomorrow. Today, at least, they hadn't let a crown conceal the words beneath its second line. Nor had they allowed it to sign consent for anyone sitting at their table.
