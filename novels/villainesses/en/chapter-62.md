# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Three: The Knight Says She's Responsible for Me
### Chapter 62: The Wagon That Refused to Admit It Was Overweight

On the morning of November 11, Adrian Reed discovered that his safety route came with a timetable.

Seven forty-five: meet outside the inn.

Seven fifty: pass the bakery.

Eight: arrive at the south checkpoint.

He pointed at the middle entry. A glimmer of civilization.

“We can have breakfast?”

“We can purchase portable food,” Captain Viola Blackwood said.

“What about sitting down to eat it?”

She studied her paper as though someone had added a mountain to the route.

Elena came downstairs with a crumpled bookmark caught at the collar of her shawl.

“We came down ten minutes early. That should let him leave in human form.”

Viola nodded and crossed out an entry.

“I allowed time for changing horses, but not breakfast. An oversight.”

“Don't worry,” Adrian said. “I used to forget, too.”

“What changed?”

“Someone started getting cross when I went hungry.”

Elena sat beside him. “She still does.”

Viola spread jam in an even square, its edges apparently measured. Adrian considered his own bread. The jam had already escaped onto the plate.

“Shall I request reinforcements?” Elena asked.

“No need. I'm abandoning that territory.”

It took Viola a moment. Her mouth twitched, and she didn't straighten the jam again.

Before they left, she placed thick leather gloves in front of him.

“The checkpoint is windy. Owen said yours got wet yesterday.”

At the same moment, Elena produced a clean woolen pair.

Adrian regarded the two offerings. Breakfast had acquired a question he hadn't studied for.

“Leather is better for rough surfaces,” Viola explained.

“The wool ones leave your fingers free for writing,” Elena said.

Both looked at him.

“Could I use each pair for its intended purpose?”

“Of course.” Viola seemed puzzled. “That's why you bring spare equipment.”

Elena looked at her guileless expression. Whatever competitive impulse had stirred suddenly had no opponent.

She put the woolen gloves into Adrian's hands.

“You'd better fill several pages today.”

“To do justice to the gloves?”

“So you'll have something to tell me when we get back.”

Her voice was quiet. Adrian stopped joking and briefly closed his hand around her cold fingers.

Viola had turned away to check the steps outside, leaving them room to speak. Whatever had shifted between them, she didn't try to explain it with her map.

At the street corner, Elena asked where the leather gloves had come from. Learning that a local tailor repaired old equipment for the patrol, she noted the shop's location.

“I'll look in. Our driver could use a pair.”

They reached the gate on time.

The town weighhouse was open. Ruth Fen, a middle-aged woman with a formidable voice, was checking the scale against standard weights. She showed the duty sergeant the balance before signing the day's record.

Viola stationed Elena and Adrian behind a white line. They could observe, take notes, and ask questions.

“No climbing on wagons. Don't handle the seals.”

Adrian raised his pen. “My most dangerous equipment today.”

She glanced at the whistle around his neck. “Please don't test that one without cause, either.”

Owen coughed into his hand.

The supply wagon arrived fifteen minutes late.

Two horses pulled a four-wheeled vehicle with dark oilcloth covering its upper sacks. A military transit plaque hung from the side. The driver explained that frost in the pass had slowed him down. Viola let the horses rest before examining his papers.

The seal was genuine. Its number matched the day's register.

Adrian began to relax.

Then the wagon crossed a joint between paving stones, and the right rear wheel gave a dry groan.

Ruth looked up.

The wagon sagged sideways before slowly recovering. A woman carrying vegetables stepped back, scattering leaves from her basket.

Viola raised her hand.

“Stop. Pedestrians to the inner lane. Owen, close the downhill approach.”

Short, clear orders. Nobody drew a sword or lunged for the driver.

Chocks went beneath the wheels. The horses were led aside. The town wheelwright crouched to inspect the axle bracket and found a fresh crack. The wagon couldn't safely continue downhill.

“I won't say it'll overturn,” he said. “But I won't stand below it.”

The driver paled. “It wasn't like that when we left.”

Viola told the clerk to record his statement, too. A dangerous wagon did not automatically make its driver a criminal.

Lieutenant Rusk, the acting supply officer, arrived to hurry them along. He examined the wheel, then pointed to the red wording on the permit.

“Urgent provisions. To reach the western-slope military depot under the original seal. You're already under investigation over one shipment. Now you're holding another?”

“First we check the load and the damage. That slope also leads to the market.”

“Replace the bracket.”

“First,” Ruth said sharply, “we find out what it weighs. Unless the lieutenant would like to test it with his shoulders?”

Adrian quietly wrote that in the margin of his private notes.

“Keep it out of the formal statement,” Elena murmured.

“It's for personal comfort later.”

The wheelwright helped move the wagon slowly onto the weighhouse lane. Soldiers controlled it with ropes and a winch, keeping clear of the leaning side.

Ruth weighed it twice. The readings agreed.

The wagon was more than two hundred pounds heavier than the accompanying declaration, well beyond the scale's permitted margin of error. Its chassis number matched the recorded vehicle. Calling it the wrong wagon would not explain the difference.

“It rained,” the driver said. “Sacks soak up water.”

“They do,” Ruth agreed. “But saying rain doesn't turn every extra pound into water.”

Rusk still objected to breaking the seal. A delay could force the soldiers at the depot onto reduced rations that very day.

Viola didn't dismiss that concern.

“We'll move usable provisions onto a suitable vehicle promptly. Damp sacks will go aside for the supply inspector. We'll preserve samples and records without letting the whole load rot here.”

“You cannot redistribute army grain.”

“Then supervise its transfer and delivery. Under the checkpoint's hazardous-transport provision, I'm stopping this vehicle from proceeding.”

She laid out her provisional authority beside the checkpoint regulations.

An actual vehicle hazard and a substantial weight discrepancy permitted a documented, limited inspection. Military seals required two witnesses and the duty supply officer to attend. They did not make inspection impossible.

Rusk read the provisions for a long time. Finally, he stayed beside the table.

“Record my objection.”

“Record it,” Viola told the clerk.

Adrian thought those two words carried more weight than an argument.

Before the seal was cut, Elena asked the clerk to note her family's connection to the transfer stations on the route. She and Adrian could record what they saw, but Ruth and the duty sergeant would serve as the independent witnesses.

Viola acknowledged her with a nod.

The old seal was sketched and numbered. A supply official cut it and preserved its pieces separately. Under the oilcloth, the top flour sacks felt dry. Several lower ones were visibly wet.

“There. Rain,” the driver said.

Adrian stared at one corner.

“Wait a moment, please.”

He stayed behind the line, pointing toward the inside of the rear board.

Dry flour still clung to a nail head. Beneath it, a water stain ran outward from inside the wagon. The bottoms of the sacks were wet, but the underside of the oilcloth had no corresponding patch.

“If it leaked from above, why is that part dry?”

The wheelwright reached behind the board and retrieved a scrap of split water-skin leather.

Ruth set it in an empty dish.

“Something leaked inside. We still need to establish how much, and when. We can't assign the entire difference to the weather.”

Viola ordered the damp sacks lifted away first. Wooden crates underneath had shifted toward the damaged wheel, their boards strained under the uneven load.

“Clear the right side,” she said sharply.

A sack slid down and struck a crate. The corner sank. A restraining rope snapped taut with a sound that made Adrian's teeth ache.

He instinctively reached out.

“Back! Hold the warning post outside the barrier.”

He obeyed at once, steadying the post his coat had caught so the onlookers behind him could see the cordon. Owen's men tightened the side ropes. The wheelwright set a timber brace. The wagon stopped leaning.

Nobody had needed to hold up an entire load of grain with their body.

Only then did Adrian discover how badly his palms were sweating.

Elena retrieved his pen from the ground.

“You dropped your equipment.”

“I nearly became the flattened edition of myself.”

“That's why we listen to people who know what they're doing.”

Once the wagon was secure, Viola returned to the line.

“Any injuries?”

He shook his head.

“You moved back quickly when I told you. Well done.”

She sounded as though she were praising an excellent tactical retreat.

Adrian had been embarrassed. Somehow, he felt less foolish now.

“At last. A knightly skill that suits me.”

A hint of a smile reached her eyes.

“Staying alive isn't a dishonorable skill.”

She returned to the wagon.

The dry provisions were weighed, marked, and transferred sack by sack to lighter vehicles supplied by the quartermaster's staff. Rusk personally checked their destination and assigned an escort. He wasn't going to let the inspection become an excuse for losing supplies.

A young soldier complained that he'd missed breakfast. Ruth gave him half her cold bread and told him to eat while the clerk finished, without getting greasy fingers on the paper.

Adrian recorded that the grain had indeed continued toward the depot.

Their dispute, at least, hadn't become a reason to starve people who had nothing to do with it.

While the workers adjusted the restraining ropes, Elena took the bread left from breakfast out of its paper bag and gave Adrian half.

“Still hungry?”

“Being frightened takes energy.”

“Then finish that mouthful before you start talking again.”

He obeyed, unaware of the flour on his nose until she pointed it out, trying not to smile, and offered her handkerchief. Seeing that neither of them was hurt, Viola finally took a drink from her water flask. Some of the tension left her face.

Having unfinished work did not require any of them to pretend they were never hungry, thirsty, or afraid.


At last, the crates were exposed.

The transit copy listed them as empty return containers. One required two men to lift. When the limited inspection opened its lid, they found iron ballast wrapped in sacking, not flour.

Nobody could establish its purpose or ownership on the spot.

But the description “empty” was no longer tenable.

Viola requested a separate evidence number, preserving the relationship between the crate, wrapping, and weights. She did not use one discovery to pronounce judgment on the entire border grain account.

Another crate lay underneath. They would have to move it onto level ground to examine it safely.

As the workers lifted it, torn sacking slipped from its side.

Beneath was a dark red transfer seal.

A rose. Crossed stems. The family initials Adrian knew very well.

Rosenberg.

Elena's expression changed. She did not approach or touch it.

“Our transfer stations use that design. I need to check the number. The image alone cannot establish who used it or when.”

Rusk looked at her, then at their outside-visitor badges.

For the first time, his gaze held unmistakable suspicion.

“You arrive to inspect military supplies. A supply wagon turns out to bear your family's seal.”

Wind passed through the gateway, stirring the cordon.

“Is House Rosenberg here to witness an inquiry,” the lieutenant asked, “or to use the invitation to examine our border transport?”

Adrian set his pen back against the page.

He had thought the day's greatest difficulty would be a wagon that refused to admit it was overweight.

Now the people beside it had been placed on another set of scales.
