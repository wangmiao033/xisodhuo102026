# Chapter 102: The Thing They Must Not Touch

Breakfast on December 15 was fifteen minutes earlier than usual.

When Luna sat down, her cuff button was still firmly where she had sewn it. Adrian noticed her deliberately resting that hand beside her plate and obligingly praised it first.

“No plans to explore the soup today?”

“It has secured a permanent position.”

“Congratulations.”

Elena set down her cup. “If you're going to compliment everyone's sleeves, breakfast may last until noon.”

“Then I'll start with you.”

“I heard you last night.”

Luna looked over. Cecilia raised her head too. Realizing she had left an ordinary sentence open to interpretation, Elena's ears reddened slightly. She did not withdraw it.

“He knocked, as promised, and read to me.”

“Did the captain finally buy a clock?” Viola asked.

“No. He bought an inaccurate map.”

The knight shook her head in disappointment, as though discussing an incorrigible colleague.

Adrian laughed and passed her the bread. She accepted with her right hand; her left arm remained on its cushion. Her convalescent leave had begun on the fourth, with dates set for follow-up care and contact about returning to duty. Today's visitors would neither lengthen those fourteen days nor heal her injury early.

“I can come to the reading room,” she said. “Find someone else for the boxes.”

“I wouldn't let you carry them,” Luna said. “I only learned to sew on a button yesterday. I'm not ready to mend a knight.”

Wheels crunched over gravel outside.

Two ordinary carriages stopped in the courtyard, their ruts wet with mud from the mountain road. A coachman jumped down and steadied a sliding step. The visitors had not emerged from a magical circle or arrived in instant response to yesterday's discovery. They had followed the schedule posted in advance, carrying academy inspection cases and an itinerary stamped at last night's inn.

The administrator checked their credentials and commission. Luna stood beside him. Disliking inspections did not entitle her to send the person who opened the door into battle first.

The lead inspector produced the regional academy's written authority: inspect registered workrooms, controlled equipment, and preservation records; verify safety measures; and review limited personal material relevant to declared projects under confidentiality restrictions. The commission did not permit a general search of the guesthouse, reading private letters, or compelling another physical examination.

Adrian checked the passages concerning him against the copy provisions in his consent agreement. Calling something private did not make lawful oversight disappear. Nor could a badge make every drawer the inspector's property.

Luna handed over the register.

“West workshop, bench W-3, is out of service. The aborted-trial record is in the second folder. Maintenance findings are in the third.”

“The worksite first,” the inspector said.

W-3's power cell had been removed. The ceramic bridge, shutter, and discharge tray remained as they had been left after the shutdown inspection. Tessa explained that the replacement gauge had not completed its suitability checks. Passing a static calibration did not justify restarting the old one. The inspector examined the western passage and required another slightly raised threshold to be leveled. He did not close the entire floor's kitchen or ordinary corridors.

With her right hand, Viola indicated the delivery route they had previously checked. She described what she had seen without appointing herself commander of the site again.

The threshold went onto the correction record. Adrian felt some relief. An inspection could genuinely discover things they had missed. The visitors' potential to cause trouble did not make their work worthless from the beginning.

What made Luna uncomfortable was the review that followed.

Reading that she had requested another look after the stop call, the inspector asked why she had not immediately repeated and acknowledged it.

Her fingertips touched her cuff button.

“I wanted a result. I almost let that desire override the standard we'd agreed.”

“You wrote this account yourself?”

“Yes. Adrian stopped the procedure without waiting for my permission.”

The inspector checked the witness record and required all operators to rehearse verbal stop acknowledgments before work could resume. This was a correction she owed, not a judgment about who was attractive or trusted.

Outside the room, Luna leaned beside the window for a moment.

“I was hoping he'd say that admitting my mistake showed I was reliable enough already.”

“He didn't,” Adrian said.

“No. He told me to practice stopping.”

Adrian did not call the requirement unfair on her behalf. He moved a cold cup off the sill, making somewhere for her to rest her hand.

Luna looked at his right hand.

“Could I borrow that? No instruments involved.”

“Yes.”

She held it for two breaths. Her fingers were a little cold. Then she let go herself.

“There. I can now endure having a button witness an embarrassing moment in my career.”

“It's sewn on securely. It should keep your confidence.”

She laughed, straightened, and went back to answer the next question.

Returning to the corridor, Adrian found Elena at one end of the bench, drawing her slipping shawl back around her shoulders. She didn't ask how long Luna had held his hand. She asked whether he wanted company during the next review.

“I'd like to check it myself first.”

“All right. I'll be here.”

Instead of leaving at once, he indicated the space beside her. She moved her book satchel, and he sat. The bench was harder than the guesthouse sofa, but neither hurried to speak. Downstairs, someone made two unsuccessful journeys in search of a key. On the third, they finally returned with the right one.

“The administrator is the person with real power today,” Adrian said.

“He also knows when lunch will be served.”

“A remarkably comprehensive authority.”

Elena smiled. Then she turned over one of her letters to show him its back. She had written a fragment there the previous evening: Remember hot soup for him tomorrow. Beneath it, she had added: Myself.

“You were going to send someone?”

“I'm used to asking other people to arrange things. Then I thought I could be here too.”

Adrian studied the words and lightly touched her fingers.

“Your writing is easier to follow than the captain's map.”

“Is that your highest praise?”

“No. But I'd like to give you the better version alone tonight.”

The amusement settled in her eyes. She didn't insist on hearing it immediately. When his name was called from the inner room, she took the coat he had left on the bench so he could go through his own records with his hands free.

It did not remove a single step from the inspection. It did help him open the door with something on his mind besides defending himself.

The limited review took place in the inner room before noon. Only the inspector responsible for that part examined the necessary pages. Personal copies stayed off the public table. Adrian checked the list of what had actually been consulted. The inspection did not authorize anyone to reuse his sealed observation medium.

Cecilia left her medical-aid correspondence at the guesthouse. She carried only the material relevant to the tower's observations. When someone mistook her newly knitted bookmark for something attached to a document clip, she immediately retrieved it.

“That isn't a specimen.”

Adrian was about to explain when the saint added, earnestly, “It's a present I made for him.”

Luna turned her face away, shoulders moving. Elena steadied an empty cup endangered by the suppressed laughter.

The inspector confirmed that the bookmark was irrelevant and looked elsewhere. He had no particular interest in their private dispute.

“I wasn't laughing at you,” the witch whispered.

“I know,” Cecilia replied. “You envy me for having one record that doesn't need amending.”

Lunch was shorter than usual. It was not canceled. The administrator arranged a separate table for the visitors, and everyone's soup came from the same pot. Elena moved her stewed carrots onto Adrian's plate with such familiarity that she only paused when she noticed Luna watching.

“What would you like to say?”

“I'm trying to decide whether that's courtship or picky eating.”

“It can be both.”

Adrian ate a piece and accepted evidence of both. Even the administrator relaxed enough to laugh. He reminded them that the upstairs equipment cabinet came first after lunch.

None of them yet knew that the most difficult argument would begin with a classification form.

C-17 had long appeared in the monthly collection declaration. The inspectors found it through the inventory, without needing to overhear yesterday's conversation. The administrator brought out the original custody register and set the technical care sheet beside the box.

After reading the prohibition on pressure contact, the lead inspector initially agreed to have the exterior sketched through the glass and place a temporary inspection seal on the cabinet.

Then the accompanying custody officer took a supplementary list from his transfer pouch.

Issued on the twelfth, it identified several classes of old contractual carriers for centralized examination at the regional store. C-17 had been classified as a metal specimen according to an earlier inventory. Its transport instruction specified standard adhesive foil sealing, in conflict with the care sheet before them.

“It doesn't require us to destroy it,” the lead inspector said.

“But that method will press against the lining.” Luna turned the diagram on the side of the box toward him. “It isn't solid copper. This boundary is thinner than a layer of dust.”

Elena asked him to check which inventory edition the supplement cited. The page reference matched the old catalog, but the description lacked the later condition note. That discrepancy alone did not prove deliberate alteration. Nor could they pretend it wasn't there.

The lead inspector laid the two papers side by side and frowned.

“I can record the objection on the transfer form. But the scope of this requisition does bear the inspection office's seal.”

“Authority and method are separate,” Adrian said. “Being allowed to take an object doesn't mean you can first wipe away the only part worth taking.”

The inspector looked at him, then at the foil the custody officer was preparing to unwrap.

He told the officer to wait. He sent a clerk to the document chest in the courtyard for the accompanying preservation guidance, then went downstairs to verify the supplement's source with the traveling inspection communications staff, who were still unloading. A message could not travel to the regional academy and back instantly. But they could first check the originals, accompanying guidance, and dispatch records already with them.

Before leaving, he explicitly ordered that the specimen remain as it was. The inner box was not to be opened.

Luna pulled a chair beside the cabinet and quietly cursed the outdated catalog. She did not announce that every inspector wanted to destroy research or insist that her own judgment required no checking.

Adrian sat beside her.

“Was that enough hand-holding earlier?”

She glanced at him. At last, her tight mouth moved a little.

“Are you charging by the minute for that too?”

“I'm practicing offering help clearly.”

“Then sitting here is enough. Right now, I need someone to remind me that taking a person's head off is harder to repair than opening a box.”

“That reminder doesn't require a specialist.”

Viola settled by the door and confirmed that the corridor remained passable. Elena took a copy of the objection to find the lead inspector. Cecilia stayed to witness the proceedings. No one drew a sword. No one slipped the box into a sleeve.

The person who returned was the custody officer, carrying a shallow metal case.

He now held another page of general instructions. Under the old classification, loose metal specimens required individual adhesive foil labels. If a lining obstructed the seal, it could first be detached and separately bagged.

Luna stopped smiling.

“He said to leave it as it was.”

“He suspended dismantling the inner box. I can prepare the individual items for transfer.”

“The lining is part of this specimen. Your preparation already involves dismantling it.”

The officer did not answer that. He signaled two porters at the door to set the case on the table. A narrow strip of foil lay in their wooden clamp. Once pressed down and lifted again, it would take the gray-silver surface with it.

Cecilia closed the record and stated clearly that the lead inspector's stop instruction had not been withdrawn. Viola reached for the administrator's bell cord, pulling steadily with her right hand rather than straining her injured left arm against the door.

Adrian stood beside the table, leaving everyone a clear route past him.

“Ask him to come back first.”

The custody officer said the carriage could not wait indefinitely and reached for the tools beside the box.

Below the second-floor window, a driver had begun putting away the other carriage's step. From the stairwell came Elena's voice urging the clerk onward. One turn still separated them from the doorway.

Luna pressed C-17's technical care sheet flat on the table. The glass lid remained closed. The specimen was still in its original support. They had not moved it away.

But Adrian finally understood that preserving it would take more than persuading someone to read a line of small print.

The clamp was moving toward the lid.
