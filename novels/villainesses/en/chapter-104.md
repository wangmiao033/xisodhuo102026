# Chapter 104: The Witch Once Sat Behind a Temple Desk

On December 16, Luna arrived before breakfast.

A faded blue cloth envelope lay beside her unbuttered bread. When Adrian entered, she was turning an old copper fastener between her fingers.

“If I told you this was an unsuccessful fashion choice, would you believe me?”

He noticed the Temple Instrument Office emblem on its back.

“I'd ask whether the failure was the outfit or the thing you don't want to say.”

“The latter. The clothes wore remarkably well.”

Elena sat beside him and asked the kitchen to serve breakfast as usual. Cecilia arrived last, with a letter for Mira. She handed it to the administrator before sitting; the aid station would not wait because a secret was being disclosed.

Luna opened the envelope. Inside were her departure certificate and two certified copies bearing a seal across their joined edges.

“From five years ago until three years ago, I worked in the Temple Instrument Office. A paid recording and restoration assistant, not a priest. I was already an adult. The work I took and the things I signed were my decisions.”

The signature was short: L. Ashcroft.

Adrian recognized the long final stroke. The older handwriting pressed harder, as though determined to carve a place for her name into the paper.

“Why didn't you mention it?” Elena asked.

“At first, I thought my employment history had nothing to do with your drawings. When you began discussing the Temple, I knew it mattered. By then I wanted to explain even less.”

Luna held her gaze.

“I dislike people seeing that emblem and deciding what sort of person I am. But I let you decide whether to trust me with incomplete information. I can't blame the emblem for that.”

Cecilia did not immediately reach for the documents.

“When you heard my name, did you know about the old betrothal?”

“Not its contents. I'd heard of the controversy over the saint and historical registration. I didn't work in personal contract archives or have access to those volumes.”

“Did you ever receive instructions concerning Adrian?”

“No.”

“Any of us?”

“No. You're asking whether our meeting was arranged. It wasn't. You don't have to accept that solely because I say so.”

Only then did Cecilia examine the certificate. Employment, departure, final wages, scope of duties: it established some things. It could not automatically establish everything that had never happened.

“Why did you leave?” Viola asked.

Luna looked at her bread, suddenly very interested in finding butter.

“Because of this report.”

The copy numbered S-48 concerned an old blank sleeve bearing no names. Luna had helped clean its outer casing, document its condition, and observe its fading light. The summary called the effect an ordinary coating defect.

An attachment said something different: the second layer faded after the surface layer; cause undetermined.

Both statements carried her signature.

“I proposed another examination. My supervisor said the budget covered an inventory, not chasing every stain. I left the anomaly in the attachment and put the easier answer in the summary.”

“Did he force you to sign?” Adrian asked.

She shook her head.

“He rushed me. He said assistants shouldn't obstruct completion with speculation. But I chose those words. Afterward, everyone was pleased. They said I'd finally learned to write a useful report.”

The stove door closed with a dull sound.

“It took me time to realize they used the summary to decide how to preserve the object. Keeping the attachment didn't mean anyone read it. I submitted a correction. It was added to the file, not substituted for the original. Later I left and kept the work copies I had permission to take.”

She showed them the copying authorization. The original remained at the Temple office, beyond her ability to quietly revise. C-17 was not S-48; their provenance records differed. Similar layering allowed a technical comparison, not a declaration that both belonged to the same conspiracy.

“That's why you knew yesterday's fold,” Elena said.

“I made hundreds. Once I packed my wage receipt into one and spent an afternoon looking for it.”

Nobody laughed.

Luna broke her bread. Crumbs fell into the plate.

“Yes. That joke won't save the morning.”

“It can wait,” Adrian said. “We haven't finished breakfast.”

She looked surprised. He had not announced forgiveness or assigned her report to somebody else's conscience.

“You think my observation plate is the same?” he asked.

“Resemblance made me impatient. It isn't identity. Yours showed two traces, no date, no established cause. I should have explained why I particularly wanted to catch this one.”

“You wanted to correct the past.”

“And prove the person who wanted to keep asking questions had been right.”

That admission seemed harder for her than admitting the signature.

Elena placed summary and attachment side by side.

“What if the answer is only a coating?”

Luna took a long time to reply.

“I'll be disappointed. Then I'll write that it is a coating.”

“You might have answered that way then.”

“Yes. Which means my promise isn't enough now.”

Sunlight showed the old folds in the paper. Adrian pushed his untouched butter toward her.

“Eat first. That doesn't withdraw the question.”

“I never knew butter could arrive with such explicit conditions.”

Cecilia smiled faintly, then asked whether the correction had a receipt. Luna produced a small slip from the envelope. Cecilia noted the original file number and the location of the correction without promising immediate access to everything.

After breakfast, Elena stayed to handle business correspondence. Cecilia consulted the deputy inspector about obtaining the old report properly. Viola, due for her permitted gentle exercise, asked Luna to walk the corridor with her.

“I'm rather better suited to being disliked alone.”

“I need someone who won't turn a walk into a training ground.”

Luna looked at Viola's injured arm and picked up her shawl.

Adrian finished the instrument index he had promised the administrator. When he emerged, the women were sitting at the corridor's end, debating the worst features of old uniforms.

“The collar,” Viola said.

“Cuffs. They always find the ink.”

“Did you keep pens in your hair then?”

“A supervisor called it undignified. I asked whether putting one behind my ear insulted the faculty of hearing.”

Viola laughed, her right hand on her knee, without straining her left arm.

Luna shifted when Adrian approached.

“She won't tell me whether knight training covers getting people through bad mornings.”

“It doesn't,” Viola said. “I'm learning for myself.”

She returned to the guesthouse to rest rather than turn kindness into an indefinite duty. Luna watched her leave.

“She didn't ask why I was so stupid.”

“You've explained. Everyone doesn't need to ask again.”

“What about you?”

“One more question.”

She straightened.

“Did you keep those papers to show someone someday, or so you would never have to?”

Luna looked at her empty hands. Without a pen to turn, her fingers had to remain still.

“At first, to show someone. Every time I moved, I told myself I'd wait until I had a real result. Then they'd have to listen before deciding I was merely the assistant who signed the wrong summary.”

“You don't have a result today.”

“No.”

“You brought them anyway.”

She searched his face, perhaps looking for an easy absolution. He did not offer one.

“It lets us decide how to work now. I dislike being kept in the dark. But sitting behind a Temple desk once doesn't make everything you say today a lie.”

Wind passed between the pillars. She tightened her shawl.

“That makes it harder to invent a pretty answer.”

“An unpretty one is fine.”

“I don't want to eat lunch alone today.”

“That isn't unpretty. I'll come.”

A trace of her familiar smile returned.

“Only lunch? I'd hoped my status as a sorrowful former Temple employee might earn more generous treatment.”

“Such as?”

“Permission to sit closer.”

Adrian moved, leaving a more comfortable place beside him. She did not immediately take it.

“Why aren't you nervous?”

“I thought you actually wanted to sit closer. If you only wanted me nervous, I'll reconsider how to help.”

Her ears reddened.

“Reconsider at lunch.”

He laughed and left her that small retreat.

Back in the reading room, Elena had marked three page references. Luna could choose which private employment details to provide for verification. Relevant interests and the old report could not remain omitted merely for convenience.

“My wages too?” Luna asked.

“No. I don't need the whole academy to establish that you were underpaid.”

Some tension eased from Luna's expression.

“But how you work, what failed, and why you chose an explanation can't stay hidden behind whichever page you feel like opening.”

“You want an audience to watch me be wrong again?”

“I want other people to be able to see whether you are wrong.”

Luna fell quiet. Looking at the contradictory old pages, Adrian understood that the next light required more than an accurate gauge.

When Cecilia returned with the archive address, Luna stopped her.

“Are you angrier than the others?”

Cecilia capped her pen and considered.

“Yes. I once believed that if the people behind that emblem were conscientious, important things couldn't be missed. Your pages remind me that I've read only summaries too.”

“That isn't your responsibility.”

“I'm not taking yours away. I'm explaining my anger. Part of it is that you knew the Temple mattered to me and decided what I was better off not knowing.”

Luna's hand withdrew from the envelope.

“I thought you'd defend it first.”

“That should still have been mine to say.”

After a moment, Luna nodded.

“I'm sorry.”

Cecilia accepted the apology without declaring everything settled. She indicated an ambiguous technical term and asked Luna to draw what she had actually observed that afternoon, rather than repeat a name already responsible for trouble.

“I can. My drawings of hands are terrible.”

“We don't need hands today.”

“At last, a chance to look competent.”

Adrian held the door for Cecilia. Passing him, she murmured that she would like a very ordinary piece of good news.

“Your bookmark hasn't been mistaken for public property today.”

She saw its loose thread showing between his book's pages. Her eyes brightened.

“That really is good.”

Luna did not record that exchange. She began clearing plates, preparing to keep her lunch invitation.

When the bell rang, she left the authorized copies with the administrator rather than sweeping them away. Then she followed the others and sat beside Adrian, a little closer than usual. Her cuff stayed safely clear of the soup.

“Today's carrots,” she whispered. “Will you eat with me?”

“Yes. One each.”

Elena shielded her plate.

“Don't look over here.”

Cecilia laughed and saved Viola a seat near the stove. The report had not become harmless. Luna had not become a different person. But at this meal, she did not need to prove herself trustworthy before she was allowed a chair.
