# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Three: The Knight Says She's Responsible for Me
### Chapter 76: He Sent the Rope to the Window

When the first bucket of water struck the stairs, the fire hadn't yet reached out through the window.

The guard captain had the door into the warehouse closed off to keep a draft from drawing the flames toward the outer passage. The craftsman broke open the repair room's old lock, only to discover that the newly added frame above the door had warped under strain. The door opened barely two fingers' width.

Martin coughed inside.

Viola and the craftsman tried together to pry the frame apart. Edith confirmed from the window that he could still respond, then divided the party: two would keep working on the door while the others prepared to bring him out through the window. The attendant waited on a clear patch of ground with a blanket already spread.

Adrian stood upwind, holding the long rope originally intended as a safety line for the paper cabinet.

Only moments earlier, the craftsman had been explaining that they should tie the cabinet doors shut first. Once the platform was secure, they could lower the documents a bundle at a time instead of having people carry them back and forth upstairs.

Now the gray above was turning black.

Adrian stepped back, away from the falling flakes. His chest didn't hurt, but his throat tightened in anticipation, remembering the smoke from before. He couldn't take a drink. Still gripping the rope's end, he heard the captain order everyone beside the stairs to withdraw immediately.

A dull crack sounded within the wall.

The later-added wooden platform tilted outward a little. The paper cabinet slid to the edge, its half-open door hitting the railing and exposing rows of bundled ledgers. The outermost volume fell and caught on broken timber, its paper corners fluttering in the wind.

He could see “Dispatch and Receipt” on its spine label. The cabinet's edge hid the month that followed.

This was what they lacked.

Northroad's purchase orders could establish that goods had been bought. The paper sleeve could point to a transfer. The ordnance mark could get a cabinet opened in another archive. But only the actual records of receipt and dispatch might tell them who had brought those once-scattered things together in a single place.

They might also tell them that those names had nothing to do with Viola.

Adrian looked down at the rope in his hands.

Two paces away, a guard had already picked up a long hook. From clear ground, he could pull down the hanging bundle. If they secured the rope to it first, the falling papers wouldn't go straight into the fire. Adrian wouldn't even have to approach the smoke or do anything Dr. Shaw had forbidden.

“Bring the long rope to the window!” Edith's voice came from the other side.

The repair-room window was above Martin's shoulders. The floor inside lay lower, his injured foot couldn't bear weight, and the short straps could only help him sit securely. They wouldn't let those outside haul him up while staying clear of the direction of the collapse. A second coil of rope was still at the carriage. A guard had gone to fetch it, but the time that took couldn't simply be wished away.

The craftsman struck the door again. The frame split, and the man inside cried out in pain. His boot had become trapped. They had to stop first.

Viola looked up at the paper cabinet.

She didn't reach for Adrian or tell him to choose. That glance was enough for him to know that she too had seen the opportunity hanging in the air.

He remembered her silence before the recommendation letter yesterday. He remembered the sword left on its felt in the armory.

Then Martin cried out again.

Not a deposition. Not a name that could win the case. Only a trapped man calling that he couldn't hold on to the window ledge much longer.

Adrian carried the coil to the nearest guard's feet.

“Take it over. Get him out first.”

The guard took it immediately. Adrian followed along the upwind side, straightening the loose loops so they wouldn't knot as they dragged across broken timber. Two guards bore the actual weight. The craftsman secured the anchor point, and Edith and Viola guided from the window. Adrian only paid out the remaining rope as instructed. He didn't brace a person's weight against his chest.

The long hook stayed where it was.

The bundle of ledgers still hung there.

The attendant told Martin to brace his uninjured foot against the wall first and avoid grabbing the sharp iron edge outside. He was too frightened. After two failed attempts, Viola crouched lower and told him to look at her hand.

“Do as she says. When we stop, you stop too.”

This time, he understood.

The craftsman moved the piece of wood trapping his boot. At last, Martin's upper body cleared the ledge. The guards slowly drew in the rope while Viola supported one shoulder to keep him from twisting toward his injured foot.

Another noise came from upstairs.

She turned instinctively, shielding him from a small falling piece of wood. Her left forearm scraped across an upturned edge of metal sheeting. A tear opened in her coat, and blood immediately seeped through.

She didn't let go. Nor did she use being injured as a reason to step farther into danger. Edith caught Martin's other side, the guards moved him onto the blanket, and the captain ordered everyone beyond the area the upper wall could strike if it collapsed.

Adrian released the rope's tail and retreated with them.

The tightness in his chest became a bout of coughing. He didn't stifle it and keep coiling the rope. He told the attendant at once and sat on the sheltered tree stump she indicated.

“Did you breathe in thick smoke?”

“I didn't go inside. I could smell it. My throat's a little scratchy.”

“Sit for now. No more whole speeches.”

He nodded and took a small sip of water.

At last, the paper cabinet toppled from the platform.

It didn't sound like the burial of their evidence. Just one great crash of wood hitting the ground, followed by cabinet doors splitting, paper bands snapping, and the contents scattering among burning beams and wet ash.

Someone ran into the yard's entrance carrying the second coil of rope, then stopped.

The guard captain didn't let him charge forward. The people and the sealed evidence bags they could carry had already been moved to safety. What remained was to keep the fire from spreading through the warehouse and wait for the water cart and more tools. No cry of “Faster!” before the wreckage could grow supports back beneath an already broken floor.

Adrian saw the ledger labeled “Dispatch and Receipt.”

Its cover curled. The paper inside first turned white, then quickly shrank into brittle black edges. Wind carried a few torn pages into the yard. By the time they settled on the wet ground, only fragments too broken to reassemble remained.

He gripped his water flask hard.

In the game he knew, a choice often produced a reward once clicked. Save someone and receive crucial testimony. Give up a treasure chest and find something better on the next road.

There was no such sound here.

Martin lay trembling on the blanket, his foot badly swollen, unable even to answer where he had been born without faltering. He had survived. That didn't mean he owed them a complete ledger.

Viola came to stand in front of Adrian. A clean cloth was pressed against the blood on her left sleeve. There was a little ash on her face, but no sign of reproach.

He spoke first anyway. “I was the one who sent the rope over.”

“I saw.”

“I could have had them hook that bundle down first.”

“And that might have slowed things at the window.”

“Yes.”

She crouched opposite him, only for the attendant to call out that she mustn't brace herself on her injured arm. She had to sit on another stump instead. With a wet stone between them, they looked more awkward than ever.

“Right now, I badly want to know what was written inside that cabinet,” Viola said.

Adrian looked at her.

She wasn't smiling. She didn't pretend not to care merely to make his choice weigh less.

“So do I.”

“But you don't have to tell me you could have left that man in there longer.”

From the yard came the attendant's voice, asking Martin to breathe slowly. Viola looked over, her right hand resting lightly against her left sleeve until another guard came to secure her dressing.

When Edith counted the sealed evidence bags, they both turned to look.

The iron band and paper sleeve were there. So were the original position sketches, discovery times, and preliminary notes. The dispatch and receipt ledgers and accompanying vouchers in the gray cabinet hadn't been recovered. No one had read them in full. There was no copy anyone could reconstruct from memory.

She recorded these facts separately and asked everyone to describe only what they had actually seen.

Adrian described the spine label on the volume he had seen, specifying that the month was unknown and he didn't know what the other ledgers contained. He didn't expand “Dispatch and Receipt” into an established supply chain.

Viola reported when she had discovered the drainage footpath. The old map hadn't confirmed that altered exit, she said, and whether the person who had escaped had used it needed investigation. The captain added the basis on which he had assigned guards to the exits. Edith wrote each of their decisions in the same account of the proceedings.

Nobody assigned all the fault to one person on the spot.

Hearing them say the ledgers were gone, Martin suddenly pushed himself upright.

“I can't remember that much.”

His voice trembled with urgency. “I really can't. They filled in the dates. I only opened the gate. Sometimes they told me to put my mark on something, and I did. I might not be able to tell you what you want.”

Edith closed her notebook.

“When you're asked later, tell us what you know. If you don't remember, you can say so. What you did will need investigating too, but treatment won't wait for you to come up with a good answer.”

Martin looked at the blood on Viola's sleeve, then at Adrian, as though still waiting to hear an opening price.

Adrian handed his empty cup to the attendant and said nothing more. His throat hurt. He also didn't want an injured man to be responsible for believing an eloquent declaration.

Before dark, reinforcements arrived with water and firebreak tools. The fire was contained to the records room and adjoining platform, and guards secured the rest of the warehouse. The craftsman confirmed that the broken beams remained unstable. No one was to go back in to sift through the ash. A formal examination of the scene would wait until daylight.

Outside the collapse zone, the captain found the heat-cracked base of an oil lamp. Edith only recorded its position. It might help explain what had fueled the fire, but couldn't establish on its own who had lit it or whether it was deliberate. The fugitive's route and the cause of the fire would be investigated separately. Their need for a culprit couldn't turn a lamp base into a person's name.

The nearby roadwork shelter had a room out of the wind. A frozen-mud slope on the return route had iced over in the night, and the driver refused to risk taking injured passengers downhill in darkness. The captain sent a messenger back to town along a safer path to report that they were safe. An extension allowing them to stay overnight was granted, and their scheduled return report was changed to immediately upon arrival the following day.

After hearing this, Viola confirmed with the clerk that it gave her no right to leave the party independently.

“The only right you have tonight is the right to stay away from broken beams after your dressing's changed,” the attendant said.

Adrian laughed, coughing. Viola looked at him at once, and he raised his water cup to show that he was already sitting down.

The shelter had only one long table, and it wasn't very level. The guards left the drier benches to the injured and sat on overturned wooden crates to eat cold meat. Viola started to help distribute water. When she was called back, she stayed at the table and counted cups with her right hand instead.

Adrian set a cup in front of her without finding some new purpose for her disappointment.

“Where's your bread?” she asked.

“Still here.”

He opened the paper wrapper. They looked at the flattened half piece of bread, and neither made another joke about its morale. Viola took out her own portion. The meat inside had gone cold, but she still carefully cut it into manageable pieces.

They found nothing suitable to say. Someone in the room was washing bloody cloths. Someone else held wet boots upside down to drain. Martin opened his eyes whenever the door made a sound. A guard had spread the ordinary length of rope to dry well away from the stove. It was muddy and would need checking for wear tomorrow.

Adrian took his first bite. After a while, Viola began to eat too. What they hadn't recovered didn't come back because of it. Their bodies still needed hot water and sleep, though. They also needed permission to continue a supper that felt very little like victory.

That night, people took turns keeping watch outside. Martin slept in a berth farther from the stove. His rescued clothes brush lay within reach.

Under the lamp, Viola spread out a blank statement sheet. She wrote the first line, then paused for a long time.

From half a room away, Adrian saw how heavily she had written the words “failure to preserve evidence.”

Her own name followed on the line.
