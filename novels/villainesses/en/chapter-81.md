# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Three: The Knight Says She's Responsible for Me
### Chapter 81: The Saint First Asks Whether It's a Proposal

Before breakfast was over on November 26, Cecilia noticed the paper beneath Viola's plate.

The exposed line read: I will share your burdens and will not forsake you in hardship.

The saint's teacup stopped halfway to her mouth.

“That sounds like a marriage vow. Are you discussing an engagement?”

“No,” Adrian Reed and Viola answered together.

Viola hastily pulled out the whole page. It contained several versions of traditional knightly oaths, with her thoughts about future duties written beside them. At the top, previously hidden by the plate, was a clear heading: “Working Draft.”

“I was considering what I shouldn't leave vague if I ever take on protective duties again,” she explained. “Adrian said this sentence had serious problems.”

“It doesn't say which burdens, or how either person gets to decide.” He pointed to a note in the margin. “I'm currently trying to persuade her to shorten the beautiful part.”

Cecilia read the context and set down her cup.

“I hadn't seen the whole thing. But that line really does sound like a vow. I'd change it now.”

Elena picked up the bread basket with particularly deliberate composure.

“Especially if the alternative is making a work draft more solemn than our actual dates.”

“Dates can be written up solemnly too,” Adrian said.

She looked at him.

“Preferably not on military stationery,” he added.

Viola laughed hard enough to lose her grip on the pen, then crossed out the sentence. Her replacement was short: Explain the risks. Ask what the other person wants. Answer for one's own decisions.

“I'll leave it there for now. I'm not swearing an oath to anyone.”

Cecilia nodded and brushed a crumb off the page. The misunderstanding ended there. When the hearing bell rang, no one left breakfast carrying an inexplicably binding betrothal.

At the assembly building, Adrian placed three long sheets side by side.

The top sheet wasn't an elegant diagram of his theory. It was a source index. Every document from a shop, the ferry office, or the military store listed its original custodian and the person who had checked it.

The outpost's destroyed general registers had their own entry at the bottom: not recovered.

The examiner looked at it. “And you still call this a complete supply chain?”

“The purchase, carriage, temporary storage, and delivery of this particular consignment can be checked stage by stage,” Adrian said. “There are gaps for other dates and other goods at the outpost. I haven't filled those in.”

He asked the clerk to call the smith first.

The man removed his hat, revealing a forehead reddened by furnace heat. His original sales entry for twenty-four civilian spearheads on November 6 had been preserved under witness since the fifteenth. The timber merchant's separate collection counterpart for twenty-four shafts and two bolts of blue cloth had spent those days in another cabinet.

“Previously, this established only that Northroad had purchased them,” the examiner said.

“Yes,” the smith answered. “I'm still testifying only to what I sold.”

Next came Northroad Freight's retained carriage record. The unobtrusive commission reference N26 on the merchants' copies matched the carrier's collection list: four bundles of six shafts each, spearheads in a small separate crate, and two bolts of blue cloth. The goods had been assembled at the civilian shed by the lower-river junction on the afternoon of the sixth.

When the driver responsible for the journey appeared, he began by correcting one word.

“Not evening. It was still light. My son had asked me for lamp oil, and I told him I'd buy it after that run.”

The clerk recorded his correction and asked him to identify his signature.

He could describe loading and delivery. He could not verify the manufacture of the ironwork on the smith's behalf. Once he'd made that clear, he indicated his wagon, number seventeen.

On November 7, that wagon had crossed the county ferry. The ferry's retained toll register recorded the vehicle, cargo owner, and number of external packages. The accompanying statement verified the pages with the staff who had been on duty. It did not establish what was inside the crate. It did anchor the route between the merchants and Alder Watch at a point independent of both.

“Who received it at the outpost?”

“Bell,” the driver said. “He complained that the long shafts blocked the doorway and told us not to stack them beside the stairs.”

The carrier's retained delivery copy bore Martin's thumbprint. At the infirmary, his legal adviser had already shown him the pages separately. The record acknowledged that he did not independently remember the date as the seventh; that needed checking against the delivery paper. Martin confirmed that he had pressed his thumb on this particular form and remembered the four bundles, small crate, and two cloth rolls. The print itself was not treated as a completed forensic identification.

The paper had remained in the carrier's accounts office. It was not a copy of the general register salvaged from the fire.

The driver rubbed his hat brim and suddenly asked whether he had to stay too.

He'd intended to collect a load of earthenware that afternoon. His employer had already been waiting two days. The board confirmed that its present questions were complete and asked him to leave an address for future notices. He still had a duty to answer truthfully. Appearing as a witness did not require surrendering the rest of his life to that room.

“I'll come back,” he said. “But those pots can't be left in the frost. Please don't let them leave the wagon outside.”

The clerk took his contact details and returned his hat. Watching the driver repeatedly check whether he was free to go, Adrian understood something: they could reconstruct the road because people had used it to earn money, eat, and buy lamp oil for their families.

Not every surviving piece of paper had been waiting for them to solve a mystery.

He pushed the first sheet a little farther forward.

The small part Martin had personally seen no longer had to support the entire building alone.

Then came the royal stamp.

The arsenal annex's clerk opened the reply. RW-6 referred to a group of old weapons racks registered for repair at the end of October. The transfer list contained no weapons. The regional store had received the racks on November 2. On the fifth, one had been lent to Northroad under packaging-circulation procedures. The store still held the loan counterpart. The return field was blank.

The driver recognized the rack used with the cargo and the place where the transit sleeve had been attached. Bellamy separately presented the recovered fitting's dimensions and old damage, alongside the store's registration drawing, for qualified examination.

The arsenal stamp was genuine. It did not, by itself, explain an entire force of attackers.

“Why was the rack loaned to this company?” the examiner asked.

“The issuing and approval decisions require separate questioning,” the store clerk said. “This counterpart records how it was entered. It cannot supply the approving person's motives.”

Ward added the original loan paperwork to the records to be obtained and preserved. Nobody could overwrite the old entries with “repairs completed.”

Adrian moved to the second long sheet.

Another Northroad carriage settlement, dated November 13, still cited N26. The driver had collected four wrapped bundles of long pole-shaped items and two packages of rolled cloth from Alder Watch and taken the county road to a rented yard. There was the carriage office's dispatch copy, a toll slip from another road barrier along the way, and a delivery acknowledgment retained by the carrier.

Martin confirmed seeing the outward packages loaded, without inspecting each item inside. The driver did not claim to have tested every spear either. What they could answer for was how goods under the same reference, four long bundles and two cloth packages, had been loaded and where they were unloaded.

“What connects the yard to the attack?”

Bellamy submitted two separately prepared records.

Before seeing any diagram identifying the premises, Ellis had further described the place where he'd received the long weapons and blue flags: a white wall, a broken gate, and half a millstone beside the courtyard well. He still didn't know the owner or the formal street name. An authorized town inspection had recorded corresponding features. The lessor's retained rental record and the charge for using the loading yard on the thirteenth also matched the carrier's delivery address.

Taken together, those details supported identifying the distribution site. They had not chosen an answer simply because it had an ordinary white wall.

Ellis's supplementary statement remained open to questioning. He said he'd received a pole weapon and blue flag there. He could not establish that every object in the four bundles had been used in the attack, nor could he testify to everyone else's nationality.

The examiner stood for a long time, comparing the accounts and the address records again.

Then he stopped at the carriage settlement for the thirteenth.

Its stated purpose was: return transport of empty racks.

“Empty racks?” he asked the driver.

“There were packages. They took up plenty of space.”

“Why did you sign that description?”

The driver's face reddened. “The settlement office filled that field later. I signed for the mileage, wagon number, and receipt of the fare.”

That answer did not settle the matter. The clerk immediately recorded the alleged order of signing and the entry process still requiring investigation. Ward directed that the original, unaltered counterpart be preserved.

But the four bundles could not become one empty rack merely because someone had written it down.

Civilian purchasing, outpost storage, delivery to the yard, and borrowed military packaging had finally formed a complete route that could be examined link by link. A form charging the journey as military empty-rack carriage had concealed a different load actually transported by the same wagon.

Who arranged the charge, who knew the real cargo, and who ordered the attack remained distinct questions.

“Did Viola sign any of these deliveries or settlements?” one officer asked.

“Her signature appears on none of the originals verified here,” Adrian answered. “That doesn't establish that she never had contact with anyone involved. But the original report attributed this supply route to her. It needs additional evidence linking her to it.”

He stepped aside while the officers compared duty records and the material originally cited. His route chart couldn't make every judgment for them.

Rusk was questioned about the grain next.

The old report had combined this irregular carriage with Willowford's shortages, creating an apparently larger loss. The lieutenant unfolded the corrected quantity sheet, his voice a little hoarse.

“WF-18 was twelve sacks at fifty pounds each, six hundred pounds in total. The two earlier summary columns referred to the same consignment. Today's shaft-and-cloth transport papers haven't doubled those twelve sacks.”

“Do we know the final destination of every grain sack?”

“No. That remains under investigation. The excess weight and iron ballast on the damaged wagon of the eleventh are not another description of these six hundred pounds either.”

He produced the old version himself and laid it beside the new one. The figures that had made the allegations appear more serious couldn't disappear from view simply because they'd been corrected.

In the observers' seats, Elena slowly relaxed her hand. She hadn't received a certificate clearing her family's reused packaging. The difference between the trading station's write-offs and recovered sale proceeds still required an audit. Today's examination had, if anything, made every handover more specific.

She could see the ugly pages she'd surrendered helping others follow the actual route and ask more precise questions.

During the recess, Adrian noticed that Viola had brought her oath draft. It rested beneath her water cup, with only the revised short sentence showing.

“I wasn't planning to read it aloud,” she said at once.

“I know. Your cup needs a little honor underneath it.”

Cecilia happened to pass and smiled at the remark.

“If you ever really do have something important to say, please let me hear the whole sentence.”

Elena joined them. “Tell the person concerned first.”

“Of course,” the saint replied seriously. “And especially don't make me discover it under a plate.”

Viola put the paper back in her pocket. She smiled, then quietly told Adrian, “Thank you for not filling in the parts we couldn't find.”

He looked toward the three long sheets still being checked on the table.

“I wanted to.”

She regarded him, her expression slowly softening.

“So did I.”

Before the afternoon session ended, Ward directed that the verified supply chain be preserved. She requested settlement instructions, signature registers, and the original rack-loan approval from the relevant military supply offices. The new evidence weakened the basis for directly attributing the identified transport to Viola. It did not close the case for everyone involved.

Ward would issue the written decision on Viola's personal restrictions the following morning.

Hearing the appointed time, Viola briefly clenched her right hand, then relaxed it.

The wind had risen outside. Adrian accompanied her down the steps, with Elena drawing her shawl closer on his other side and Cecilia carrying clean cloth back to the infirmary.

None of them held a flawless truth.

But the next demand for records could no longer be addressed only to one suspended knight.
