# Chapter 275: The Prayer Did Not Answer for Her

On May 4, the first thing Cecilia brought to the reading room was a blue-stitched cloth.

Adrian recognized it. The kitchen woman had returned it through the porter on the day they'd examined the oracle in March. Today it wrapped two borrowed cups against breakage.

“The evidence has its own delivery,” Cecilia said. “Cups needn't wait for an oracle's approval.”

Adrian took one and found kitchen written underneath. Would using it for tea violate its purpose? After serious consideration, she said their existing faith could sustain that interpretation, provided it was washed and returned.

They entered laughing. She wasn't wearing her formal reading robes, but she still wore her Temple insignia. She still prayed. Today's facts wouldn't decide that for her.

The old proclamation, drafting papers, and printer's settlement counterfoil were already familiar. Earlier permission to provide extracts to affected parties hadn't permitted street publication. Now the independent adjudicating body and custodians had approved the necessary texts and sources for public examination. Protected individual pages stayed separate. Original volumes remained on supports, not in circulation through the audience.

Oswin watched from the applicant's seat as she opened the amended draft. He didn't demand she kneel. A liturgical assistant rose, asking to explain first that religious wording was often revised. Editing human language didn't necessarily fabricate divine intent.

“Yes,” Cecilia said.

He seemed surprised by her ready agreement.

“So today we'll examine how the revision changed the order of events.”

She didn't begin with the most shocking sentence. The custodian aligned two dates in the same old liturgical calendar. The verifier explained their relative order without forcing them into present dates. The draft's submission note said it followed a received register; the public text claimed revelation preceded designation. Two dates alone weren't sufficient. A later copy might postdate composition. Others were entitled to ask.

Then the printer's independently preserved settlement bundle opened.

Its counterfoil recorded receiving a marked-up manuscript, matching page count and final line. The compositor's question asked why a revelation was to be printed as earlier when confirmation of names came later. The response required typesetting by the proclamation date, omitting how it had been composed. This paper had entered another archive with ordinary paper costs and wages. Its accession history mattered; different seals alone didn't prove independent sources.

Gripping his chair, the liturgical assistant asked whether the writer might have organized an earlier oral revelation.

Cecilia requested the neighboring instructions. Proposed designated persons was to become persons revealed. The names would be filled after register confirmation. A sentence permitting family objections was to be removed because it weakened the proclamation's certainty.

“The material doesn't establish that no oral traditions ever existed,” she said. “It establishes how this text followed selected people, then rewrote that selection as a preceding command. If you have an actual earlier source, submit it. We needn't believe you first.”

The assistant noted the retrieval office. He no longer offered custom in place of the missing document.

A chair scraped at the back. A man rose, clutching a rolled cloth cap. At his father's deathbed, he'd recited that prayer: the appointed bore repayment, and the rest should have peace. Now they said someone had composed it to move names on a list.

“Were all the things I said to him false too?”

Cecilia stepped half a pace from her table, then stopped. His loss wasn't an opportunity to demonstrate a graceful explanation before a crowd.

“Would you like an answer here, or at the break?”

“Here.” His voice tightened. “Everyone's heard.”

She nodded. “The text's claim about who owed repayment was fabricated. I can't pronounce your day beside your father true or false for you. Your choice not to leave him alone was something you did. The writer doesn't own it.”

He didn't relax. What could he believe now?

“I can't give you another sentence guaranteed never to fail. You may keep asking, or not decide yet. Remaining here doesn't require a declaration of faith in me.”

Still gripping his cap, he sat. Adrian looked into his cup. The difficult thing was leaving an empty place unoccupied. Cecilia could see it; she dearly wanted to fill it for him.

Before noon, Oswin requested the clause specifying separate verification of disaster observations. Cecilia read it herself without making him ask twice.

Danger hadn't disappeared with the false oracle. Several facilities still operated under heavy loads. Outer preparation was parked, but the protective main bridge remained active. Closed roads required site inspection before reopening. She wouldn't use fabrication to cancel maintenance obligations, or genuine danger to rename fabrication necessary comfort.

What could she offer people who knew the truth and still feared? the high priest asked.

“Questions they can ask. The care actually continuing. And refusal to use their fear as someone else's consent. That won't solve the disaster. Saying where it falls short is also something I can do.”

Her voice grew hoarse. She drank without inviting applause to fill the silence. Some listeners agreed, others looked cold, and some merely searched their copies for the cited line. She wouldn't combine those responses into believers finally awakened.

At midday, they visited the food kitchen. The regular shift had already served lunch; Cecilia's testimony hadn't kept anyone waiting. The older woman accepted the cloth and checked the cups, first asking whether one's shallow crack had worsened.

“No,” Cecilia said. “Though I gave it to someone talented at disrupting solemn occasions.”

Adrian admitted that, but denied breaking cups.

After a brief laugh, the woman asked whether tomorrow's kitchen workers would hear what had been said that morning. Cecilia would explain during the approved ordinary question period. Public copies were available. Nobody must abandon work to listen.

The manager approached with arrangements they'd discussed two days earlier. He'd agreed that receiving a meal here wouldn't depend on joining prayers or expressing support. Questions about corrections to old liturgical texts could be raised. Worried about unhappy donors, he asked whether Cecilia could guarantee the shortfall.

She couldn't. She would seek people willing to support ordinary care, speaking honestly. Existing food would be served according to stock. Undelivered money couldn't promise another meal. Sighing, he left the uncertain column unfinished, then separated the previously linked meal and prayer times at the entrance.

A helper asked whether the Temple had changed.

“One practice here has changed. I can't answer for every place.”

Cecilia wrote clearly that anyone wishing to listen could stay. People carried meals out without providing reassuring looks of continued faith. Two remained with questions. Another left halfway through for work; she handed him a copy rather than making him wait for a conclusion.

Adrian helped move an empty bowl rack. Once again, the third bowl wouldn't fit. By the time Cecilia looked, he'd spaced the first two apart himself.

“Progress,” she said.

“At least the bowls haven't reset.”

Her shoulders shook with laughter before she turned aside. In midday light, wearing an ordinary apron, she remained beautiful without resembling a saint able to answer the world.

“I'd like to invite you,” she said.

“To move bowls?”

“You could. But I'm inviting you, not an extra pair of unpaid hands.”

He put down the rack and listened properly.

On the afternoon of May 6, she'd do ordinary work here, followed by reading and questions for those choosing to remain. She wanted him to see the life she was rearranging. No historical marriage would reserve a seat; he wouldn't be required to prove that life worthwhile.

“You can be busy,” she added. “You can also come and discover you dislike the prayer.”

“I'd like to come. If the inquiry runs late, I'll tell you ahead of time.”

She nodded, unable to suppress her smile. “Then please don't enter it as an inspection of care arrangements.”

“I'm coming to see you. That can be true without adding a future we haven't agreed on.”

Something shifted in her eyes. She answered seriously that it could. Affection hadn't ended, nor had acceptance become an equal promise to everyone. She knew his relationship with Elena. She could still invite someone she cared for to see her without making the invitation a debt.

The kitchen woman leaned out to ask how long they intended to stand there. Their rack blocked returning bowls. Cecilia stepped aside, ears reddening. Adrian lifted it and received a thoroughly practical instruction: left, please, and stop making the passage a room for heartfelt conversation.

Back at the reading room, the liturgical assistant waited by the door. Hearing she'd still lead ordinary readings, he wondered why she wore Temple insignia if she no longer believed that revelation.

Cecilia looked down. The silver was worn, with a trace of washing water caught at its edge.

“It reminds me how I learned to care for people. Also how I thought I could explain their destinies for them. I still need to remember both.”

“People will think you represent the Temple.”

“Then I'll state how far I can speak today. An ornament isn't authorization. I won't discard my whole past and pretend I never spoke wrongly either.”

After consideration, he asked for the printer's complete reference. People at his small chapel were already asking. If he merely said they awaited an answer from above, the same passage would be read tomorrow.

She asked the archivist to supply approved explanatory material, not private copies of restricted cases. Arrangements at his chapel required its responsible person's decision. If he sent actual questions from believers, she would answer within what she knew and leave the rest for examination.

He accepted the papers without agreeing with her. But for the first time he left carrying a reason to ask further, rather than an instruction to reassure everyone.

The chair received Cecilia's objection to use of the text in this case, recording the supported finding of fabrication and the disputed matters. Withdrawal of other editions and individual accountability required further decisions under their respective authority. She didn't rise to announce reform of the whole Temple.

Near closing, the man with the cap asked whether he could return to read copies over the coming days. He didn't thank her or offer to hear new prayers.

She gave the actual opening times and had the archivist confirm them. He nodded and took a cup of water. It belonged to the archive; it needn't become a symbol between them.

Finally, the custodian displayed the proclamation's military distribution references. That oracle had accompanied transfer instructions. Religious records could establish how words were composed, but couldn't independently establish what soldiers had done.

Viola had received notice to attend the following day. Her assistant brought a reply: the original order, receiving counterpart, and execution material would be submitted separately. Missing attachments must remain identified.

Cecilia read it and put the cloth in her basket. Tonight she would pray again. She wouldn't ask a prayer to answer for tomorrow's military order.
