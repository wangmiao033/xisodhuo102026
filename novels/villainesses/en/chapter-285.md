# Chapter 285: The Witch Allows Someone to Call Her Wrong

On May 14, a red line appeared on Luna's drawing. She disliked it immediately.

An outside engineering reviewer had drawn it across two interfaces she'd admired, adding: Insufficient evidence for this inference. After studying it, she asked whether he intended to eat lunch before continuing to ruin her mood.

“I brought sandwiches.”

Adrian laughed. Luna informed him that visitors weren't entitled to enjoy this. He unsuccessfully concealed his amusement and moved the drawings she no longer needed to another rack.

The independent hearing authority had checked the reviewers' qualifications. They hadn't designed or funded the trials. A Temple engineer familiar with the old facility, but not a signatory to the sacrifice orders, attended too. Luna could answer them; she couldn't dismiss whoever asked unpleasant questions. Full records containing private information or dangerous access instructions remained in a controlled room. The public record explained restrictions. Disagreement had to remain in the findings.

She'd expected exposing failure to be hardest. Instead, people could seriously examine success and still refuse to understand it as she wished.

Her assistant reached for a fresh sheet. Luna almost let him replace the marked drawing, then asked him to leave it where it was. The reviewer waited instead of repeating his objection more loudly. With the paper still between them, she had to look at exactly what he had questioned.

The red line concerned April 26. Separate buffers had remained stable under bounded inputs. But her future extension drawing assumed a common return section could distribute residuals in the same way. The reviewer requested measurements directly supporting that claim.

“That's a hypothesis for the next stage.”

“Then don't draw it like an established result.”

She nearly said colleagues who'd read the full explanation would understand. He had read it. She'd wanted familiar notation to spare her an unpleasant admission.

She dictated instructions to her assistant: make the extension dotted and mark it unassessed. The red line stayed. So did the original drawing and revision date.

Another reviewer compared April 23's independent near-branch readings with the bench's total gauge. Why did the instrument section explain its lag without a reference on the operator's page? Equipment-based filing had placed information vital to practical judgment far away.

“Nothing's missing,” the assistant murmured.

“No. But people shouldn't have to learn our drawers before finding our failure.”

Luna requested a cross-reference without erasing the old index. The missed review was her responsibility. Someone who'd filed as instructed wouldn't become today's cheapest mistake.

The civilian access group had also sent questions. Which warnings could be posted publicly? Which details needed qualified readers? The engineers didn't provide a blacked-out page. On the current site plan they marked restricted maintenance areas, external signs, duty contacts, and directions to leave if something went wrong, without exposing internal activation interfaces.

Viola wasn't attending. The safety coordinator came on her behalf. If signs disagreed with actual barriers, which should helpers trust? Report it immediately, the reviewer said. Until resolved, don't guide residents into doubtful areas or move barriers themselves.

“What if the duty officer says it's fine?”

The independent engineer supplied another review contact. Questions couldn't go only to the person whose answer was being questioned.

In the waiting area, someone asked whether finding errors in the explanation invalidated April 26's success. The records reviewer didn't simply defend Luna. He distinguished the actual two-load trial's bounded stability from the claim that it extended to a shared return section.

Still doubtful, the visitor requested the independent timing comparison. He received approved references and verification results, not a personal guarantee from the witch. A reviewer helped him locate the stop signal on the recorded trace. Evidence supported that section; the untested extension remained blank.

Luna had thought this delayed more important work. Watching the man take his marked copy, she realized he was doing what they'd asked the public to do: examining more than a speaker's confidence.

She requested a reference to this answer in the public explanation. Must they comply with every criticism, her assistant asked? No, she said. Every response required a reason. Direct people to existing answers, correct errors, and admit unknowns. A questioner's patience wasn't evidence that she knew.

The assistant turned to the relevant page, copied the reference, and asked the visitor to try finding it without help. The man opened the wrong section first. Luna started to point, stopped herself, and watched them discover which heading needed to be clearer.

At the agreed lunch break, she left the technical room. Adrian accompanied her next door. Her caregiver adjusted the right-palm support and advised resting her eyes from fine lines. Eyes closed, Luna complained that she'd handed over an entire road and the reviewers questioned every stone.

“Would you rather they stopped?”

“I'd rather they complimented me, then continued.”

That sounded like her, he said, laughing. She smiled without requesting a testimonial.

Next door, a reviewer struggled with his lunchbox lid until someone else lifted it with one fingertip. Luna opened her eyes. At least one field still needed her guidance.

“Eat your own lunch first.” Adrian moved her spoon closer.

Eating left-handed, she recalled her first invitation to work for the Temple. They'd praised her understanding of what others couldn't comprehend and given her a room only she could unlock. She'd mistaken being beyond questions for magnificent freedom.

“What did you discover?”

“They could tell me half the purpose and make me proud of understanding that half.”

She didn't attribute everything she'd done to deception. She'd accepted ambiguities she shouldn't have accepted, and used intelligence as an excuse for impatience. Today's red line couldn't wash that away.

Adrian admitted he'd first imagined her tower as a place in a game that supplied answers. Reach it, receive the item that solved everything.

“At last, a confession.”

“Now I know the person inside gets tired and sometimes thinks my questions are stupid.”

“Some are.” She paused. “I don't dislike you continuing to ask.”

He valued her willingness to let others see uncertainty. Looking down, she quietly asked whether he valued only a useful researcher.

“No. If you stopped researching today, you wouldn't become someone who'd lost her worth.”

Her grip tightened around the spoon, then eased. She didn't demand that the sentence promise romance too. He didn't let it imply every future she might want.

“Then don't come in this afternoon. I don't want to look at you every time someone marks my work, wondering whether you feel sorry for me.”

“All right. I'll help locate the verified indexes.”

She watched him close the door before lifting her cooling soup. A meal without an audience could be another way of being treated seriously.

On May 15, the review turned to boundaries for decommissioning the old facility.

This wasn't a search for human-trial permission. Post-parking diagrams, actual custody records, and current unpowered inspections were compared to distinguish the idle preparation branch from parts connected to the operating protective main bridge. All five core locks stayed sealed. Nobody proposed opening them merely to find out.

Luna attended a limited question period. Qualified engineers compared the structures. An old drawing showed two assemblies sharing a frame. Removing that entire frame, as an early draft proposed, would disturb a main-bridge monitoring mount even without opening the core.

“It can't come out whole,” the Temple engineer said first.

The room paused. Supporters had imagined parking left only bolts to undo. Opponents might use this discovery to prohibit touching anything.

The independent reviewer requested later maintenance changes and actual measurements. Inspectors brought dimensions from authorized outer-area checks, identifying where the old drawing still matched and where it didn't. Their resulting boundary wasn't imaginary: independent preparation-selector rods and the recording housing could be removed first. The shared frame and main-bridge mount must remain. Isolation, residual energy, and adjacent stability required rechecking before actual work.

Decommissioning now had a real first scope, with real limits.

Luna asked that disagreement be recorded. One reviewer still wanted additional load-bearing analysis; another considered the limited outer removal ready for detailed planning. Nobody erased the slower person's name.

A custodian noted deformation in the old recording housing beside the selector. Continued damp could further impair its recording medium. Removing and preserving it separately might be wiser than leaving it for some later grand demolition. Conservators and engineers would determine timing together. Rumors wouldn't create an overnight deadline.

Outside, Adrian stopped at the mention of his scar. Staff meant the index previously identified through his anchor response, which might help distinguish historical tracks. They weren't proposing his body as a dismantling tool. The distinction mattered to him enough that he listened through the remainder of the exchange instead of retreating with the first alarming phrase. Nobody had established a new ability or requested immediate use of the old one. Existing catalogs and other media had to be compared first. Any new observation required its own application.

He entered and asked to receive the actual question if they later needed him. Don't write him into a plan as permanently willing and available.

Luna nodded and told her assistant to preserve his exact request.

By evening, the independent findings had two access versions. The public document described the limited trials' actual results, indexing and inference problems, unfinished verification, and conditions for first-stage dismantling. Dangerous interfaces and personal data remained controlled. Confidentiality didn't swallow dissent.

The review approved no new connection or nationwide substitute. Its real change was that Luna couldn't personally declare the next stage ready. Others now had the material and standing to say it wasn't.

Before the packets left, an assistant noticed that two drawings shared a heading. He checked the actual sheets rather than relying on their covers, then placed the restricted drawing back in its controlled folder. The public packet kept the findings and the reason for withholding that drawing. Luna waited until he finished.

Before leaving, she studied the first red line again.

“An ugly color.”

The reviewer capped his pen. “Blue next time, if you prefer. Same opinion.”

She finally laughed aloud.

When Elena collected Adrian, he carried his own materials rather than all of Luna's work. Had dinner been detained by another set of problems? No. He'd handed over his duties. They could walk their promised short street tonight.

Passing the workroom, they found Isabella reading the review. Her left wrist rested on its small pillow. Her right hand paused above the old preparation apparatus's authorization provisions. Where engineers could begin was clearer than yesterday. Who could still order people toward it wasn't a question a removed bolt could answer.

The princess looked up.

“I'll take this to the people empowered to change that law. They can't stay behind a door while you repair a machine that keeps them from ever answering for it.”

From inside, Luna nodded. She'd given up exclusive possession of her drawings, not possession of herself.

Tomorrow, authority would face the same unwelcome examination.
