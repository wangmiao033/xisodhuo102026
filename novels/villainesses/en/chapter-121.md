# Chapter 121: Does Dinner Count as Official Business?

On the morning of December 29, Luna conceded that the three-hundredth pine outside the carriage looked much like the first.

“At least they don't question your old report,” Adrian said.

“They don't offer advice on the new one either.”

“Already excellent trees.”

Laughing, she settled against the cushions without opening her document bag. Wheels rattled over frozen earth. A shallow rut occasionally made all three sway before settling again. Elena turned a page of the travelogue and waited for smoother road before continuing.

The captain had finally reached home. Unfortunately, the incorrect address he'd once written now belonged to somebody else.

Luna recommended the post office. Adrian wondered whether the gatekeeper would let him see the person he sought. Elena didn't skip to the ending. She placed their marker where they'd stopped together.

They stopped at the second relay station for lunch, then climbed back into their carriages to continue the journey through the afternoon. On the thirtieth, an icy bridge delayed them almost an hour. Viola inspected the railing and wheel tracks, listened to the driver, and returned to her seat. She didn't offer her injured left arm as proof that the road was safe for two horses.

Once the workers had spread coarse sand, they followed the carriage ahead slowly across. Watching the people at work, Luna swallowed her planned praise of bridge design and asked whether they could stop somewhere out of the way after crossing, so she could buy cups of hot soup from the stall at the bridgehead.

Everyone drank a cup of hot soup standing in the wind. Nobody felt that all the time they had lost that day had been wasted.

On the afternoon of December 31, the capital's walls finally appeared.

Adrian saw the high flags first, then the queue at the gates. Vendors selling winter fruit crowded along the roadside. Inspectors walked the waiting line of carriages, checking where each had come from. Travelers hurrying to arrive before the new year made the city considerably livelier than it ever sounded in noblemen's stories.

The palace invitation didn't take them past the whole queue. Elena asked the guards to check the necessary entry papers. Before their wheels moved again, Luna had identified every hot-food stall outside.

“My first criticism of the capital is its unfair distribution of appetizing smells.”

“Lodgings first,” Elena said.

“You invite prisoners that way too.”

“Prisoners rarely request extra cheese.”

Brief letters sent from the road had reached the Rosenberg residence ahead of them. Rooms were ready. They ate a hot meal, with time reserved for the doctor's dressing change. The healing of Luna's right palm remained slow, although there was no new deterioration. She still had to rest rather than treating arrival in the capital as the end of her recovery. Once seated, Viola didn't keep rising to carry everyone's luggage.

Cecilia would organize the public ritual texts and previous statements for accurate reference tomorrow. Missing one meeting caused her no mysterious alarm. She merely asked Adrian to tell them what the princess actually proposed afterward.

“I will.”

Luna raised her left hand. “Including dinner.”

“If there's dinner.”

“Trust me. Someone who sends four pages of invitation won't offer only plain water.”

Before evening, Adrian and Elena took a carriage to the palace's west wing.

Seeing the gates again, he glanced at the old scar on his left hand. The crescent lay quietly against his skin, neither warm nor forecasting the conversation beyond. He put on his glove and concentrated on the steps.

An attendant checked their reply and the guest list. The courier had arrived ahead of them along the main road. Isabella knew the expected party and their conditions: companions could attend; discussion formed no contract; absent further arrangements, they would return to their own lodgings tonight.

Nobody stopped them outside the door to demand a signature on a sheet whose other side they had not even been allowed to see.

While they waited, an attendant automatically reached for Adrian's document bag, intending to pass it inside. Adrian kept hold.

“These are copies for my reference. If Her Highness needs one, I'll discuss it during our meeting.”

For a moment, the attendant looked taken aback. He glanced toward Elena, apparently waiting for her to add some explanation of the identity of this man who had accompanied her.

She didn't call him a Rosenberg retainer.

“The invitation names Mr. Reed. He has answered you.”

The attendant withdrew his hand and apologized. After his footsteps faded, Adrian glanced at Elena. She was studying a hunting painting as though she'd said something entirely ordinary.

“Thank you.”

“Don't thank me yet. If I start arguing with her, you may remind me too.”

“Gently.”

“That doesn't mean squeezing my hand.”

He said he hadn't thought that far ahead. Her ears reddened. Before the doors opened, both could finally hear more than their heartbeats.

Inside, Adrian still felt the pressure of careful arrangements. Curtains blocked precisely the slanting light. The fire was warm. Only two chairs stood visible at the table: one at its head, one opposite.

Elena walked beside him and looked quietly at the empty carpet.

The woman by the window noticed.

“Another chair,” she told the attendant. “The reply was clear.”

The attendant promptly brought another chair. Nobody offered an explanation of whether the original arrangement was a matter of custom, a misunderstanding, or some deliberate test of their response.

Isabella Aurelian turned, her skirts brushing polished floorboards. Her dark court dress suited a meeting. It carried little decoration, but its gold fastenings and collar looked as though haste had never touched them. Her gaze was steady. She didn't hurry to soften her rank with a smile.

Adrian bowed and introduced himself. She returned a smaller inclination without keeping his head lowered.

“Mr. Reed. Miss Rosenberg. Were the roads worse than my courier expected?”

“One icy bridge,” Adrian said. “Otherwise, well enough.”

“Then you're more punctual than I anticipated.”

“We didn't trade a knight's injured arm for punctuality,” Elena said.

“Wise. I appreciate people who don't squander valuable resources.”

The words sounded approving. Adrian nevertheless noticed resources. Elena heard it too and sat without smiling.

Tea arrived. The attendants withdrew beyond the door. With only three people remaining, Isabella laid down a blank sheet, not a document awaiting signatures.

“East Ridge's dispute won't remain there. The academy, church, and Crown all funded earlier work. Once ownership of old apparatus arises, people at court start looking for the most convenient conclusion.”

“Which do you want?” Adrian asked.

“One that survives the next question.”

“What would you gain?”

Her first genuine smile appeared.

“More effective than asking whether my intentions are good.”

She claimed no exclusive devotion to justice. She wanted to participate in the royal review of those earlier projects. To do that, she needed people capable of independently describing both the technical work and what they had personally witnessed. Leaving everything with those already controlling the material would reduce her choices to their selected phrases.

“I dislike having only two accounts chosen by somebody else.”

Adrian remembered her equally attractive sentences in the letter. Now they had a speaker, with undisguised ambitions.

“Our records contain many things we don't know.”

“I read your preliminary testimony.”

“It doesn't prove I possess another lifetime.”

“I know. September twentieth hasn't become a complete name. Nor has an old record supplied you with a current fiancée.”

Her precision demonstrated preparation. Elena's fingers moved against her knees; she'd noticed the particular detail Isabella had selected.

The princess described possible support: formal access routes, appropriate royal records, and applications for necessary funding and escorts. In return, she wanted truthful accounts, their attendance at arranged inquiries, and important discoveries before court gossip carried them to her.

Adrian didn't let the list of conveniences draw an easy nod.

“We can discuss truthful accounts. I can't agree to report exclusively to you, or remain silent about findings unfavorable to the Crown.”

Isabella raised her cup without drinking.

“You think I'd request that?”

“I don't know. That's why I'm asking.”

After studying him, she set it down.

“Restricted originals have limits on access. Personal materials can't be freely copied and removed. Those restrictions apply. I haven't said you must pretend not to see something because it harms me.”

“What if someone says so later?” Elena asked.

“Ask for their name and authority. Don't accept a paraphrase of the princess's wishes.”

The answer amounted to no oath. At least, however, it gave them a position they could question further. Adrian made a note of it. He didn't turn the words spoken at a single meeting into a permanent guarantee.

When discussing arrangements, Isabella naturally suggested that Mr. Reed first describe his observations to her alone tomorrow. Elena looked up. Adrian answered before she could.

“I'll describe my subjective experience. Technical questions require Luna; ritual commentary requires Cecilia. We can speak separately, but whichever person you find convenient can't answer for everyone.”

Isabella's gaze lingered between him and Elena.

“Do you habitually bring others into your decisions?”

“I'm learning.”

“It takes longer.”

“Sometimes going slowly tells you whose commitments you're accepting.”

A faint crack came from the fire. Isabella pressed the pad of her finger against one corner of the note paper, as though recalculating an arrangement she had originally thought a particularly good bargain.

Then she looked up, composed again.

“Let's move somewhere that requires no immediate technical decisions. Stay for dinner. I've ordered three places. Miss Rosenberg is included.”

Elena raised an eyebrow. “Better prepared than the chairs.”

“I'll remind them to check the dining table.” Isabella's smile deepened.

Adrian didn't immediately accept.

“Your Highness, does dinner count as official business, or a personal invitation?”

She looked as though nobody had asked to see the price list before the food arrived.

“Does that make a difference?”

“Yes. For business, I'll keep my pen. For a personal invitation, I need to know what you're inviting me to.”

Elena glanced at him, the tension in her eyes quietly easing.

“You fear a meal might alter your status?” Isabella asked.

“I fear everyone might think it alters something different.”

After a pause, she pushed the blank sheet toward him.

“Business, then, for tonight. We haven't finished discussing our terms.”

“Agreed. Eating doesn't mean accepting conditions I haven't heard.”

“You're unfair to the chef, Mr. Reed. He might have persuaded you for me.”

“I can praise him sincerely.”

At last, Isabella laughed aloud. The sound made her seem a little less like a figure in a portrait hung high above them. It didn't make her forget which chair she occupied.

When an attendant entered to guide them, she quietly requested another document case. Adrian heard without imagining it already held the answers.

He and Elena crossed the doorway together. Softly enough for only him to hear, she said he'd better finish his bread tonight.

“Why?”

“Because the princess seems to have prepared a rather demanding main course.”
