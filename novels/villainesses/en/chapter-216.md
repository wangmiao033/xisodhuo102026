# Chapter 216: Not Made by Heaven

On March 15, the clockmaker's shop contained eleven different versions of now.

The tall clock ran ahead of the brass one on the counter. A smaller clock had stopped last night. After standing among them, Adrian felt he'd missed three appointments and arrived early for two funerals. Luna, seated by the door, congratulated him on entering the expensive stage of temporal research.

They weren't here to repair the world. Her new reading stand was ready. An assistant checked whether it could hold drawings without Luna's hands. The clockmaker occasionally accepted small woodworking jobs; springs salvaged from old clocks gave its retaining strips a gentler grip.

“That has human-made gears too,” Adrian said, indicating an open clock case.

“Which proves someone made that clock. Not the moon next door.”

He remembered that. A few exposed gears could be reassuring precisely because they were easy to understand. He wanted that reassurance to extend much further than the open case on the counter. Luna had noticed before he had admitted it to himself.

Last night, the military order's engineering handover reference had been added to their existing application. Today's requested material wasn't another command. It was the facility's manufacturing and assembly volume. Luna wanted to know what the Crown of Destiny was. At the word manufacturing, Adrian had started wondering how to stop it.

The assistant tested the stand. Luna's right palm remained supported; her left hand stayed out of the contest. She requested a lower angle, then approved it. Waiting for an ordinary purchase didn't make her hands' limitations easier to endure.

“That look is dangerous,” she told Adrian.

“Looking at the moon?”

“You're thinking a machine must have a shutdown procedure some villain refuses to disclose.”

He almost denied it. “Couldn't there be one?”

“Yes. It might also stop whatever keeps people alive. You have better reason than I do to know that.”

A pendulum crossed the space between them. His smile faded, but he stayed.

“Then today we establish how it was made.”

“Only that. Don't finish my first sentence with the answer you need.”

Her voice was sharp. The assistant paid with money Luna had provided. Adrian didn't seize the bill as though buying her a stand could cancel their disagreement. He waited until she rose, then accompanied her to the waiting carriage.

Three sets of material occupied separate parts of the upstairs reading table.

The Temple's historical engineering volume described the Crown's central assembly. An independent works repository had supplied an acceptance copy traced through the engineering directory. The old foundry guild's deposited register recorded dispatches, returns, and payment, without passing through Temple custody.

One damaged page was available only as a facsimile while its original underwent conservation. Luna wouldn't let the assistant strengthen a faint line. She needed to know where it actually went.

The assembly plan didn't depict a doorframe.

It ran from a central distribution ring through five receiving mounts and returning bundles of conducting branches. Manufacturing entries listed casting, grooving, fitting, and assembly. Rejection marks interrupted the sequence. One dimension had been crossed out and reduced by half a division. A first assembly hadn't closed properly; reworking and another inspection followed.

Adrian studied the correction. He'd imagined the Crown as something innate and terrible, to be accepted or opposed—not something someone had measured incorrectly.

“Could people simply have built a shell around an older object?”

Instead of immediately praising the question, Luna had the assistant expose the component directory.

“If these were covers and frames, that's all they could establish. These are internal distribution components. But a manufacturing claim on paper isn't enough either.”

She asked the engineer to read the independent acceptance specifications before consulting the corrected plan. Another clerk read the foundry dispatch. The rejected piece had a seven-tooth junction; the final tooth was too wide. Its replacement retained seven teeth and an offset notch beside the sixth to establish orientation.

Terminology had changed between documents, but delivery acknowledgments linked their references. The foundry register also listed ordinary pump parts. Their different quantities weren't added to the Crown simply because they occupied the next page.

The difficult part was identifying the equipment below.

Luna wouldn't descend today. Authorized engineers examined an existing exterior service face. Its diagram specified which exposed junction could be seen. No mark lock turned, no door opened, and no historical power was connected. Ordinary lamps supplied illumination. The workers didn't dismantle hidden sections to complete a drawing.

They returned with two independently prepared sketches. The first showed six teeth; shadow concealed the last position. A clerk asked whether a dotted seventh could represent the written specification.

“Represent what isn't visible,” Luna said. “Not a seventh tooth you've established exists.”

The second sketch used another permitted lighting angle. It showed the final edge and the offset notch beside the sixth tooth. Measurements came from fixed service-face reference points, not the later stone edging outside the door. The engineer indicated what he'd actually seen. Much of the deeper structure remained blank.

A younger works examiner still objected. Something untouched by human hands might remain inside. This junction could be a later addition. He pointed to the join, worried that signing a larger conclusion would discourage anyone from investigating the blank spaces.

Luna let him finish.

“Keep that qualification. We also need to establish whether acceptance covered separate pieces or their connected assembly.”

An acceptance line crossed the distribution-ring and branch-assembly entries. The custodian displayed the matching handover page. Individual deliveries preceded acceptance of the completed assembly; one receipt hadn't simply been copied twice. Replacement specifications followed, addressed to maintainers rather than worshippers.

The young examiner agreed that this supported manufacture and assembly of the central system, without claiming they'd personally seen everything. Luna retained his qualification and expanded her own abbreviated conclusion. Adrian watched him loosen his grip on his cap. Disagreement hadn't cost him his place. It had made the approaching statement stronger.

Luna studied everything again, separating the documents and rechecking the rejection reference. The assistant moved the new stand rather than asking her to steady it. Its salvaged spring held the page without covering the altered measurement. Nobody hurried the quiet into a declaration. Adrian kept his eyes on the evidence, resisting the temptation to make her next words solve a different problem.

“They didn't merely house an object fallen from heaven. People designed the central distribution components, manufactured them separately, corrected them, and assembled them. The Crown of Destiny is a human-built machine.”

No bell accompanied the words.

Outside, road workers dragged a plank aside. Someone warned the lunch-cart driver about fresh mortar. The statement arriving on this ordinary day was harder to dismiss than a sudden underground light would have been.

Cecilia asked whether distribution in the plans established how an actual person was harmed.

“No. We've established manufacture and component relationships, not the complete operation. These drawings don't explain why calamity begins or where every input ultimately reaches a body.”

“Nor that dismantling it would be safe,” Isabella said, steadying a curling sheet with her right hand.

“No.”

Adrian nearly said that at least they now had an enemy. But foundry laborers might never have known what their pieces became. Pump-making wages couldn't become criminal because their entry shared a page with another.

“At least we can investigate who made the decisions.”

“And what they knew when they made them,” Elena said.

She wasn't reserving that question for other people. Since the old judicial handover, she knew how difficult it was when applied to herself.

The assistant reminded Luna that her allotted session was nearly over. Luna stared at a folded margin, lips pressed tight. Adrian thought she might ask for more time.

“List that for next time,” she said instead.

As the assistant gathered the drawings, Adrian asked whether they could read just the heading concerning power interruption.

Luna looked up.

“You agreed we were examining manufacture today.”

“I haven't asked you to test anything.”

“I didn't say you had. You're asking me to spend my remaining strength on another question that requires preparation.”

He stopped. She didn't owe him a smaller, quicker answer because he was frightened. Nobody intervened to smooth the exchange. Finally he moved the directory back within the assistant's reach.

“You're right. I wanted to reach a way of saving people so badly I stopped listening.”

“I want to save people too.” Her voice softened. “Don't mistake refusing an immediate answer for being less anxious than you.”

He nodded. Then he entered his question for a future application instead of slipping it between her and today's rest. Writing it down felt slower than asking. That was the point: someone would have to prepare the relevant material, decide what could actually be learned, and choose a time they could afford. Nobody had to pay for its first appearance with their body.

They ate beneath a roofed passage outside the maintenance yard. Wind missed the table. A street clock sounded in the distance, at least unopposed by ten neighbors. Luna's assistant had cut her bread. Her right palm rested on a pad; her left hand only occasionally touched her cup.

“Will you still speak to me today?” Adrian asked.

“Depends. No power interruption.”

“The travel book. We didn't finish last time.”

“You remember that you owe me reading, not an opportunity to ask my advice?”

“Yes. The explorer currently needs to escape a goose.”

She finally laughed. “All right. Don't make the goose a metaphor for the distribution ring.”

He produced the book and asked whether she wanted it now. Two pages after lunch, she decided. He didn't read a third or turn a passing description of a bridge toward the old relay court. A highly dignified scholar was pursued into a pond. Luna laughed until she had to pause for water.

Elena peeled an orange beside them and put half on Adrian's empty plate. She neither mediated nor asked what kind of date two pages constituted. She had a separate family application to deliver that afternoon. He wanted to accompany her to the gate. She agreed, provided he went home to rest afterward.

The fear he'd voiced yesterday hadn't disappeared. It didn't have to become a hand stopping her every time she left.

Before they departed, the custodian brought an accompanying handover index from the assembly volume. Engineering acceptance had cited a jointly signed order. The royal retained copy's location matched the reference in the military instructions. It wasn't a signature by every royal, nor did a crown emblem establish which present-day person knew what.

Isabella read the portion authorized for transfer and asked that it be added to her pending limited-access application.

“Tomorrow, I'll see what the royal office retained.”

She settled her left wrist on its support and took the acknowledgment in her right hand. Not one mark lock had turned below. Upstairs, they could finally say the apparatus had passed through human hands. They still had to ask who had turned the work of those hands into a destiny nobody was permitted to refuse.
