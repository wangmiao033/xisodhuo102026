# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Two: The Saint Won't Let Me Get Married
### Chapter 33: Why Is My Signature Older Than I Am?

On the morning of October thirteenth, Adrian received an appointment slip so unexciting that he almost felt insulted.

There was no mention of a strange power crossing the boundaries of time.

No declaration that he'd definitely died seven years ago.

Just:

*Handwriting comparison. Ten a.m. Document Restoration Room, Saint Rose Academy.*

"This sounds like I'm going to be examined on penmanship," he complained.

Elena was reviewing yesterday's meeting minutes.

"Perhaps you should practice. Your signature always looks as though it's trying to escape."

"I can write it beautifully."

"When?"

"...I'm sure it happened once."

Cecilia pushed a blank card across the desk.

"If you're willing, write your name three times now. We're not grading handwriting. We want to establish how much variation occurs when you sign naturally."

Adrian complied.

The first name was much too large.

The second devolved into an impatient line of connected letters.

The third ended with that familiar little inward hook.

He stared at it.

"I can't even stop doing that."

"Which makes it useful," Cecilia said. "Natural handwriting varies. A traced pattern often reproduces mistakes far more precisely."

Elena looked at the paper.

"The ending is rather attractive, actually."

Adrian looked up.

"Are you trying to make me feel better?"

"I'm making an observation."

Cecilia carefully wrote, *Lady Rosenberg considers final stroke aesthetically pleasing.*

"Don't record that!" Adrian and Elena said together.

The saint looked startled.

Then crossed it out.

The document restoration room occupied the second floor of the academy's west wing.

Curtains protected the old materials from direct sunlight. The shelves held quills, tracing lamps, magnifiers, and samples of letter forms from several centuries.

Nora had invited an independent examiner, Ms. Maeve Carter.

She was in her fifties, with permanent ink stains on her fingertips.

Her first words made Adrian like her immediately.

"I won't be deciding whom anyone ought to marry. I'm here to look at ink."

Hale laid out the written authorizations.

Carter could compare certified images of the Temple's historical record, the academy's seven-year-old loan entry, and Adrian's newly written samples. Originals remained with their custodians. Cecilia's personal records would only be shown to the extent she voluntarily authorized.

"That means anything I say today is preliminary," Carter warned. "Without the Temple original in front of me, I'll be irresponsible if I declare a signature genuine or forged."

Nobody objected.

She began with Adrian's three modern signatures.

Under transmitted light, she marked changes in the direction of his strokes, the pressure on the paper, and the places where the pen lifted.

Then she turned to the old academy loan record.

Finally came the Temple betrothal facsimile.

Three sets of names lay side by side.

Adrian's first impression was the same as yesterday.

"The little hook looks identical."

"Not exactly." Carter slid the magnified images toward him. "Your three modern signatures end in slightly different places. These two historical copies contain the same tiny break where the pen seems to pause."

"So the same person wrote them?" Elena asked.

"That would be a premature conclusion. A person signing twice doesn't normally reproduce every accidental hesitation. This looks more like two reproductions derived from a common written model."

Nora produced the academy's old reproduction register.

"The loan page was photographed for a training plate so clerks could learn the pre-reform stamp formats. There's a record of its preparation."

"Where's that plate now?" Adrian asked.

"We have the manufacturing entry. The storage room was closed years ago."

Cecilia rested her hand against her document case.

"Could somebody have transferred the same signature image into the Temple betrothal?"

"Possibly," Carter said. "But we only have a certified image of the betrothal, not the physical original. It's also possible the two modern reproductions used the same restoration template. We need paper impressions and ink-layer examinations to distinguish those possibilities."

Adrian nodded.

He'd hoped for a definite answer.

Instead, he'd learned that the alleged signatures from seven years ago couldn't safely be treated as two independent handwritten acts.

Carter asked them not to focus solely on the little hook.

"Handwriting also involves spacing, stroke sequence, the angle of entry, and how often the pen leaves the page," she explained. "The relative positions of Adrian's letters move naturally. On the historical images, even a spot of what appears to be stray pigment is repeated."

"So either someone preserved a signature of mine," Adrian said, "or someone used one image to restore two different copies later?"

"Those are among the possibilities. I realize you're looking for a shorter answer, but short answers tend to erase the very distinctions that matter."

Nora located an old restoration workshop number in the academy's archive.

The technician who'd prepared the training plate had retired, but he might still have retained a production log.

"We can contact him," she suggested. "He can explain the procedure. He can't tell us whether the marriage contract was legally formed simply because he worked on a copy."

Adrian wrote down the reference.

Another person who might help was not the same thing as another person who knew everything.

Carter next examined the signature attributed to Cecilia.

"Miss Saintclair, would you be willing to provide samples that don't reveal private information?"

"Yes."

She produced two pages she'd prepared voluntarily.

One was a handwriting exercise from her Temple education ten years earlier.

The other was a reply she'd sent to the independent review committee yesterday.

The Cecilia on the historical contract had an elegant, almost ornamental signature ending in a long, straight underline.

On both her childhood exercise and the recent reply, Cecilia's final letters curved upward instead.

"I don't remember ever writing in that style," she said.

"People's habits change. Memories are imperfect too," Carter reminded her. "What matters is that the image alone cannot establish your signature."

Cecilia nodded.

While Carter moved on to the next samples, Elena spoke quietly to the saint.

"You asked yesterday whether I minded your name being on the contract."

"Yes."

"I do mind," Elena admitted. "But I can't ask you to hide a document simply because it makes me uncomfortable. And I can't demand that Adrian promise never to look at anyone else."

Cecilia considered that.

"If we discover the historical contract was genuine, what will you do?"

"Find out what the people involved actually chose. Then decide what that means for us today." Elena met her eyes. "What about you?"

"I used to think that finding a record of love would tell me whether I loved someone. But written names aren't memories. Even if the paper is authentic, it can't tell me how I felt."

"You've hardly met the Adrian who's standing here now."

"Exactly," Cecilia said. "I'd rather get to know him than fall in love with a name in an archive."

Adrian returned with three cups of tea just in time to hear the final sentence.

"Are you discussing my salary?"

"No," both women replied.

"Then perhaps I'll keep out of it."

Nora bent over her desk, laughing.

The handwriting examination continued.

Carter asked for higher magnification on the two old signatures.

At the edge of both curled strokes, she found the same tiny dark speck.

"That's not part of the letter itself," she said. "It looks like a small flake of pigment that was present on the model and then copied into both images."

Adrian leaned closer.

"So we can look for the earliest master?"

"That would be sensible." Carter began her written findings. "But don't skip inspection of the originals. We've found possible common image provenance, not proof that a particular person forged a betrothal."

That afternoon, Hale filed a request for a physical examination of the Temple contract under independent observation.

The proposed examination would compare paper, ink layers, and the sequence in which the seals had been applied.

The Temple's administrative office replied that because the document had been classified as a *Fate-Mark Related Record*, ordinary handwriting analysis might not establish identity conclusively.

"They want to examine my hand?" Adrian asked.

Cecilia read the response carefully.

"Not necessarily. Their technical materials describe a noninvasive resonance test that can read residual registration marks in a sealed medium. It needs separate authorization and isn't guaranteed to succeed."

Elena lowered her pen.

"What risks?"

"A specialist should explain them formally. I won't agree on your behalf."

She turned to Adrian.

"If you're willing, I can ask an independent technician to explain the method tomorrow. Listening to the explanation is not consent to the test."

Adrian looked down at the crescent scar.

It didn't glow.

He remembered the unfinished bells in the old courtroom.

"I'd like to hear the explanation," he said. "But I'm not signing anything until I know exactly what the method can read."

Cecilia nodded.

Elena added, "I'll be there."

He smiled.

"Not because of the historical engagement?"

"No," she said. "Because I want to be with you."

Carter sealed her preliminary report before giving it to Hale.

"Some signatures are older than the people they supposedly belong to," she remarked. "That doesn't necessarily mean the people lived longer. Sometimes it only means one piece of writing was reused more than once."

By the time Adrian returned to the Rosenberg estate, evening had fallen.

Martha found him at the dining table practicing signatures on scrap paper.

"Mr. Reed. Are you opening your own accounting firm?"

"I'm checking whether I can write two completely identical signatures."

Martha examined the sheets.

"You didn't even make the letters the same size."

"That's what worries me."

Elena came through the room, took away his third sheet, and set a pen beside his hand.

"Stop. You've stared at your own name all day."

"What if someone used it before I ever arrived here?"

"Then from today onward," she said, "you'll write new ones."

"Is this a present?"

"A loan. A good pen might stop you tearing every page you write on."

He tried it.

The nib moved smoothly. His signature still ended in that familiar curve, but the line no longer felt like a puzzle that had to be solved immediately.

"Better," Elena said.

Cecilia, passing through the room on her way to collect her documents, glanced at it.

"Indeed. But more than the shape of your signature, I'd like to hear you say what you want to do now."

Adrian put down the pen.

"What I want right now is dinner. An evening without anyone discussing betrothals."

Elena nodded.

Cecilia nodded too.

For once, he'd obtained a unanimous decision.

Later, he looked at the sealed copies again.

He no longer only wanted to know whether he'd signed something seven years ago.

He wanted to know who needed his signature badly enough to preserve it as a template that could be used again and again.
