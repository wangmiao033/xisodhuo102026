# Chapter 253: The Door Wasn't a Battlefield

On the morning of April 16, Viola had a table moved away from the shelter entrance.

The man behind it protested that registration needed somewhere to write. She indicated an empty stretch beneath the covered walkway and asked him to try it. He could see the entrance there without blocking stretchers, and rain wouldn't reach his papers. Still grumbling, he moved his ink bottle. At least he no longer had to tuck in his legs whenever somebody passed.

Her approved assistance today covered a short period. Her left-arm support wouldn't fit inside a narrow sleeve, so she'd chosen a loose coat without her old shoulder insignia. The duty supervisor beside her assigned his own staff. Lending her a chair didn't restore military command.

Adrian brought the overnight reports. Leaflets had indeed invited people to demand explanations from the five marked women. Numbers remained unknown. Patients, stranded drivers, and families slept inside. None had volunteered to become scenery for a public debate.

“Should we shut the door?” the supervisor asked.

“First find out how people inside will leave.”

A woman taking her daughter for a dressing change had seen the new rope line and silently put down her bag. Adrian walked with them through the reserved side route until she recognized yesterday's treatment entrance. Returning, he could confirm the route existed beyond a drawing.

The first arrivals carried no weapons. They brought damp papers, empty baskets, and an elderly man on his son's back. Their lights had failed overnight. Was somebody refusing responsibility hidden here? A voice farther back demanded an inspection of the rooms: innocent people had nothing to conceal.

Viola stayed in her borrowed chair rather than climbing the highest step.

“You can ask about the shelter's arrangements,” she said. “You can't take strangers into somebody's bedroom.”

“Something to hide?”

“Yes. Changing dressings. Sleeping. Not wanting an audience while you cry.”

The son lowered his eyes and asked somewhere to set down his father. A duty caregiver led them toward an actual available reception place. Nobody demanded he tear up his leaflet first.

The young man had not promised to agree with Viola. His father needed somewhere to sit, and that was the need the caregivers answered. Adrian watched them go without trying to extract a grateful statement for everyone outside. For a moment he thought the simple sight of help being given would explain itself. Those behind couldn't see what had happened. Someone shouted that only supporters were admitted, and shoulders pressed forward in layers. The rope loosened. An empty basket fell. Viola gripped her chair with her right hand and leaned forward.

She didn't rise. Naming the supervisor, she identified the pressure point. He sent staff already present along both sides to stop more people entering the narrow lane and preserve the public cross-passage. Another requested help from the street patrol. Viola's former rank didn't stand in for that request.

Adrian approached the elderly basket owner. Somebody struck his back as he bent. Before he could push back, she said to forget the basket: her granddaughter was on the other side. He straightened and helped her along the cleared wall.

The girl stood beside the table's former position, holding bread and afraid to move. Adrian crouched so she could see her grandmother rather than lifting her over unfamiliar heads. She walked three steps into the woman's arms. They seemed to have reclaimed those steps inch by inch from the whole street's urgency.

People still demanded a guarantee that tonight would be safe. Viola admitted she couldn't give one. Actual outages could be reported; examiners must determine their connection to the calamity. She wouldn't promise safety merely to make people leave.

“Then what are you doing here?”

“Keeping you able to ask, and the people inside able to leave.”

A gray-capped man reached for the side-door curtain. His relative might be inside. He refused to provide a name through reception and demanded every room be opened. The supervisor directed him to the actual missing-relative procedure. The man raised his arm; two people behind him advanced.

Viola went pale. Adrian saw her arrest the movement to stand. Instead of shouting at her to stay still, he set his water bucket against the wall and called the patrol now at the corner. Staff kept the curtain closed without rushing out to strike back. Patrol members took the disputants aside for separate questions, clearing the entrance.

Later somebody praised her restraint.

“Restraint wasn't our plan,” she said sharply. “Having somebody actually take over was.”

Her shoulder hurt too much to maintain her position. The supervisor assumed the questions as arranged, and her assistant accompanied her into a side room. Retreat wasn't disguised as a clever feint.

Adrian brought water a little later. She looked at his dusty sleeve.

“A gift from the wall,” he explained.

“You're closer to qualifying for a casualty allowance than I am.”

“Only dust. Don't exaggerate the wall's achievements.”

She smiled, then tired. Would standing have made her look more like her old self? Yes, he admitted. But today they needed something other than the woman who blocked a doorway alone.

She studied her support. She wanted to learn to protect a place even when she couldn't stand. That wasn't surrender. He put the cup within her right hand's reach. The door remained open; nobody inside had been brought out for display. He didn't claim she'd learned never to be frightened again.

Near noon, the supervisor brought a flattened basket and asked about replacing it. Nobody knew who had stepped on it. Viola suggested asking its owner what she wanted before arranging a demonstration of kindness.

The woman arrived with her granddaughter. Compensation would help, but could she sell sewing thread tomorrow? Another leaflet said temporary stalls also needed supporter lists.

Adrian checked with the street's duty clerk and returned with the actual stall rules, not a personal guarantee. The woman folded them into her pocket, then asked whether the seller beside her could see them too. It mattered that an answer obtained here could be checked by somebody who had never met Viola. A private reassurance would leave tomorrow's argument exactly where today's had begun. The supervisor had another copy made for the public noticeboard without the woman's name. Somebody with the relevant spending authority accepted the basket claim; nobody quietly took money from food relief.

The girl studied Viola's sling. Had somebody stepped on her arm too? No, Viola said, but it needed time. The child divided her dusty bread and offered half. Viola thanked her without accepting the food, asking instead if she'd choose a cup that wasn't hot. After serious consideration, the girl selected the shorter one.

When they left, Adrian asked whether every patient had to learn to accept cup selection.

“No. Today's addition to rigorous training.”

He laughed, realizing he'd clenched his jaw all morning. Arguments continued outside, but he could loosen it a little.

The afternoon left several complaints, two missing-relative inquiries, and four outage reports—not a street's allegiance. Whether the gray-capped man followed instructions remained unknown. One attempted intrusion didn't prove a citywide plot, and their group didn't privately punish those removed. Medical staff confirmed no crush injuries; a scraped patient received ordinary care. The result wasn't a glorious battle report, but the mother and daughter returned from treatment on time.

Luna arrived after the worst congestion. She intended only to check installation dimensions going to the academy, not perform magic at the shelter. Her assistant carried the drawings. Luna stopped before her chair as though she'd forgotten why it was there.

Had she eaten? A precise question, she said, should specify when and how much. Adrian set down a lunch plate and asked for an answer to the crude version first.

“A biscuit.”

“Today?”

“You learn quickly.”

Once seated, her left hand still trembled slightly. During an authorized short period last night she'd read residual traces on an old test piece—not connected new nodes or people. The individual reading had remained within its allowance. Several days' expenditure hadn't vanished because each form separately passed. Morning rest hadn't entirely cleared the grayness at the edge of her sight.

Adrian reached for the drawings and stopped halfway.

“If you're about to put me under your management,” she said, “apply for a cell capable of holding me first.”

“I was going to say your assistant can copy the dimensions. You don't have to read them today.”

“I can also choose to.”

“Yes. But I won't describe you swaying as standing steadily.”

Her irritation didn't disappear. She feared more than missing a paper. Tomorrow's important test depended on recognizing faint traces. Stopping might hand everyone's hope back to Oswin's practiced old solution.

From nearby, Viola said she'd watched others protect a doorway while she sat. It had been uncomfortable without making her useless.

“Have you qualified to preach convalescence?” Luna asked.

“No. I'm simply one chair ahead.”

Luna finally laughed. Then she asked her assistant to have another qualified reader make an independent initial examination. Tomorrow's comparison depended on actual assessment. She wouldn't sign a guarantee of endurance beforehand. The assistant took the folder without reporting that Adrian had granted permission to rest.

He moved a piece of onion away from the edge of her plate. How did he know she disliked it? Last time she'd quietly relocated six pieces to his bowl while he spoke, apparently imagining he noticed only her eyes.

“You looked at the bowl too?”

“I wanted to survive long enough to hear you finish.”

She laughed down at her lunch, hair falling across the embarrassment she hadn't quite concealed. He didn't turn care into a promise of greater intimacy. He brought his own meal and sat at a distance she welcomed.

By evening, the academy sent the recheck arrangements. The isolated test would use stored test material and a calibrated charge unit only, with no human connection or old-core link. Independent monitoring, shutdown staff, and medical attendance were confirmed. Luna must first pass assessment, could withdraw, and mustn't conceal trembling to obtain her place. This wasn't permission for victory. It was a limited risk stated plainly.

She requested an accurate reply and turned the paper over.

Outside, supper distribution began. Some people remained angry; others remained frightened. Nobody had to support a faction to receive soup. Hearing ordinary bowls and spoons, Viola finally allowed herself to close her eyes.

Luna glanced at her, leaving part of her biscuit on the plate. Would Adrian be disappointed if she said stop tomorrow?

“Disappointed to lose the opportunity. I won't count it as a debt you owe me.”

He let the answer remain as it was, without hurriedly replacing disappointment with a more flattering word. She had asked about a feeling, not requested a cheerful prediction. She listened through the whole answer, then nodded. Tomorrow's risk hadn't become smaller. Tonight, at least, she needn't pretend to be a lamp that never ran out.
