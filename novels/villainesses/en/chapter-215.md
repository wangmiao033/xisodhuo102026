# Chapter 215: The Order's Last Line

On March 14, Viola asked Adrian to inspect the fastening behind her cloak.

Not armor. Not another injury. Her repaired shoes fitted well; today's difficulty involved a small iron clasp turned inward. With her left arm supported, she couldn't reach it with her right. She carefully explained that he needed only to flatten the fabric beneath it, not retie the whole garment.

“I miss when only neckcloths needed explaining.”

“Were you good at those?”

“That question undermines my qualifications.”

She smiled. Once he finished, she walked two steps to check the cloak wasn't pulling her support. Her assistant carried the papers. Adrian accompanied her along the level approach without carrying both her belongings and her decisions.

The military examiner placed two sets of documents on separate mats. Yesterday's liturgical note concerned an old register-transfer arrangement, not Viola's border recall. The central escort unit's original came from issuing custody; its receiving copy came from an independent quartermaster's handover folder. Even the texts weren't identical.

Viola examined the difference first.

The original specified persons in the attached register. The copy shortened that to five persons. Was the attachment present? Not yet, the examiner said. Despite the noble volume's associated reference, five couldn't immediately identify today's five women. Each connection required checking.

Adrian swallowed his disappointment. He didn't insist on centering the wording closest to the answer he wanted.

The third paragraph did agree.

Transfer would proceed despite requests for review. Objections would follow the persons afterward; awaiting an independent response must not delay collection. This escort offered no option to leave. Troops were also instructed to read yesterday's proclamation at handover, lest families mistake it for ordinary judicial transfer.

Viola read the final sentence quietly.

“A person can object. The order makes the objection another piece of luggage.”

The examiner didn't defend it. He displayed the issuing sheet and routine signature comparisons, explaining the verified reference, notation, seal register, and dispatch stub. The independent receiving entry matched the time, page count, and last line. This supported an order actually issued and delivered to the unit, not an unsent draft. Receipt didn't establish completion of every escort. Individual destinations remained to be investigated.

A retired military clerk said soldiers couldn't always wait for everyone to finish arguing during a calamity. His voice stayed level; his fingers pressed hard on a travel-expense slip.

“Does the order say that,” Viola asked, “or is it your judgment?”

“Mine.”

“Then preserve it as yours. We haven't established that waiting was impossible in every case, or that every refusal was selfish.”

He glanced at her arm support. She'd resisted an order at the border herself. Perhaps she too easily treated an exception as a universal rule.

Adrian's chair shifted. Viola raised her right hand. She hadn't finished.

“The November 14 recall was genuine. I signed an explanation for delaying, sent four riders ahead, and escorted the final wounded-person wagon. The review didn't transform me into someone who never received an order. Nor does being right then make everyone who implemented this instruction malicious. I'm asking how it treated the people being taken.”

The clerk released the slip. It recorded horse-and-wagon expenditure by the same unit, overlapping the collection arrangement. It supported preparations for transport, not missing names or final receipt. Viola requested separate treatment, not a louder conclusion that everyone had arrived.

Adrian then made his own choice.

Yesterday Cecilia had asked whether refusal had any place. He requested the full objection clause in the extract available to affected people, rather than a summary saying implementation was disputed. The examiner warned that this would also include the cited calamity justification. Did he still want it?

“Yes. Not only the half that helps us be angry.”

He read it back. The order cited a risk report not fully attached here and claimed concentrated delivery would protect outside districts. Neither the report's truth nor scope had been established in this volume. Yesterday's false oracle hadn't made calamity disappear. His throat tightened at the prohibition on waiting. He finished both the justification and the compulsion.

The examiner authorized a full extract within the existing affected-party scope, noting missing material. The originals returned to military custody, not their lunch table. Viola didn't demand blanket cancellation of military orders. She asked whether they could trace implementers who had raised objections, rather than collecting only agreement.

The question was accepted, not immediately answered.

Before the folder closed, she stopped the examiner again. A faint marginal inquiry asked whether an escort should await further instructions if a physician requested a halt. Its answer field was empty. That didn't prove treatment had been denied. Nor could they say nobody had asked.

The retired clerk leaned closer. He hadn't noticed that line. Ordinary soldiers might not have known the whole arrangement.

“Yes,” Viola said. “Preserve what they asked. And investigate why, when these papers reached them, refusal was already forbidden to obstruct the journey.”

Her right hand rested at the table edge, her left arm still supported. No drawn sword, no pain earning her greater authority. She gave a nearly buried question a line in the copy.

The examiner recorded the medical inquiry and absent answer separately. After reading them back, Viola asked him to retain her initial question about identifying all five people too. She'd nearly read too quickly herself. Other people's errors couldn't be the only ones leaving traces.

“It makes the conclusion less tidy,” the clerk said.

“People are larger than the words in their orders.”

Outside, Adrian asked whether she'd wanted him to speak. She considered. After she'd finished, yes.

“I only wanted him to stop. I wouldn't have asked whether it was his own judgment.”

“If he didn't say it, we wouldn't know what still needed answering. I won't always be that patient.”

She declined another stop and left for rest on time. Before going, she pointed out a fold in his collar. He reached three times, missing twice. Smiling, she asked her assistant to show him. A small kindness didn't make her responsible for him.

That afternoon, someone was repairing a leaking eave.

Elena had arranged to meet Adrian behind the bookshop. A bucket caught the drips; half the paved yard remained dry. She'd completed the financial referral and brought its receipt, not proof of her family's innocence. Adrian shared today's permitted conclusions, saying we three times while discussing forced transfer.

At the third, she closed her book.

“You're afraid of losing us.”

“Yes.”

“Who?”

Water struck the bucket. Everyone almost escaped him. She wasn't asking him to choose who should live, or whose death would cost more. She was asking about the person he'd hidden behind a number.

“You.”

Elena watched him. One word felt too light, perhaps like an answer required merely because she sat here.

“I'm afraid of losing Cecilia, Viola, Luna, Isabella. They aren't your supporting cast, or proof that I'm fair. But losing you wouldn't mean just one less companion. I'm afraid you won't come tomorrow. That I'll have some small thing to tell you and only be able to imagine your answer. My feelings for you are real. I want to keep being with you, not just get you off a list.”

She looked at her closed book.

“Less pleasant than saying we're all the same.”

His heart sank.

“More pleasant too,” she added. “I don't want to be cared for like compensation paid in equal shares.”

He exhaled. She didn't let that confession excuse him from another question.

“If I decide to finish investigating and you want to take me away?”

“I'll tell you I'm frightened and ask you to hear why. If you disagree, wanting dinner with you doesn't entitle me to bind you.”

“Will you be angry?”

“Possibly. I may say something wrong. I won't promise impossible composure.”

She put the book aside. Could she lean against him for a while?

“Yes.”

Her weight was slight and real enough to leave him without anything clever to say.

After a while he asked to kiss her.

“Yes. Not to celebrate finding a transport order.”

“My worst reason for celebrating.”

She raised her face. The kiss was brief. Afterward her ears were pink, but she insisted Luna would draw a dreadful diagram if she heard his equal-compensation phrase.

“Let's not provide a research topic.”

The shopkeeper opened the yard door to change the bucket. He'd thought they were discussing books.

“We finished,” Elena said composedly.

Adrian picked up an upside-down volume and missed his best chance at composure. Elena couldn't stop laughing.

The shopkeeper left warm water after changing the bucket. Elena didn't open her book immediately. Could she choose tomorrow's meal?

“Yes. Provided a single pie doesn't count as our entire dinner.”

“You're developing requirements.”

“I'm learning you can forget you haven't eaten enough.”

She considered him. He could remind her, not secretly cancel her afternoon arrangements. He agreed, then asked whether she'd like something now. She chose a biscuit the shopkeeper had left.

A small question. Neither lost the initiative by asking or answering it. Neither needed to fit every future decision into this afternoon. Adrian took another biscuit, finally turned his book the right way up, and read the passage she indicated. She corrected one word he stumbled over, then waited for him to finish.

That laughter wasn't an objection lodged with a military order, nor permission for someone to die in her place. He was happy to let it be an embarrassing afternoon belonging to them both.

Their evening meeting was short. Luna sat at the upstairs table, right palm padded, left hand not carrying everyone else's volumes. Viola sent her checked observations rather than returning to complete the gathering. Isabella unfolded the order's final-page drawing with her right hand. A royal reference appeared in the joint-approval field. She requested the retained royal counterpart; the symbol couldn't first become the king's signature.

An engineering cross-reference listed manufacturing acceptance records for the receiving institution's equipment. Luna requested the central assembly drawings and independent works records together for tomorrow. They would examine legally accessible manufacturing papers and exterior service views, not open a door to prove human construction.

“Could that only prove people built the frame?” Adrian asked.

“Which is why I won't examine only the frame.”

Her voice remained sharp, her eyes tired. When her session ended, she asked her assistant to pack the papers. Her injured hand wouldn't pay for insufficient patience.

Before leaving, Elena asked whether Adrian wanted dinner together tomorrow.

He did. Then he checked what time suited her. Not every future evening could be guaranteed. Admitting fear didn't demand an immediate lifelong contract.

Today, at least, he hadn't hidden behind we instead of answering her himself.

On the way home he remembered a loose strand of hair beside her laughing face, and the military order's missing attachment. Both were real. Neither needed to erase the other.
