# Chapter 165: Room for Six

Only after spreading the paper on the dining table did Adrian realize he didn't know how to write a protection agreement.

He could write don't follow me and don't agree for me. He could recognize the point where elegant wording became a rope. Without a rope, however, how would they find one another if something actually happened tomorrow?

It was the evening of January 29. The soup had gone; crumbs remained. Elena wiped a clear space without converting the room into a negotiating chamber.

“Start with something real.”

“If I'm delivering papers and I'm half an hour late, what would you do?”

“Ask the office expecting you,” Viola answered.

“And if it's closed?”

“Ask the driver. Unless you didn't take a carriage...”

She stopped herself and turned the page toward him with her right hand. Nobody accused a knight of not knowing how to protect someone.

Cecilia asked whether he wanted to give his destination beforehand. Luna warned against treating refusal as evidence of danger. Isabella pointed out that without any contact arrangement, everyone would guess.

Five cups trying to occupy the same space. Adrian did not select the gentlest answer as the right one.

“I'll tell an agreed contact when I expect to return and where to ask first. I don't want everyone to receive an account of everybody I met and everything we discussed.”

“Not a shared movements register, then,” Elena said.

Luna crossed out every journey. She had written it so easily that she hadn't noticed it exceeded his offer.

What if the sole contact was unavailable? Isabella asked. They settled on a primary and alternative contact chosen for each arrangement, with changes communicated separately. Living under the same roof did not automatically assign responsibility to someone who hadn't accepted it.

“A rotation?” Cecilia asked.

Several heads rose together.

She laughed first. “Receiving messages. Not distributing afternoons.”

“There aren't enough afternoons,” Luna said.

Adrian looked at her.

“I withdraw that last remark. From this document, at least.”

The first paragraph finally emerged. No light, no responding marks: just a pen on ordinary paper. This was not a magical contract transferring fate-related risk, supplied no such protection, and authorized no betrothal or compatibility registration.

Adrian asked Luna how to make nonmagical unmistakable. By adding no hidden medium, rite, or transmission clause, she said. They mustn't draw a reassuring diagram resembling a ward. Her suggestions obeyed that limit too.

Then came documents.

Elena offered ordinary secure storage. Isabella immediately asked whether House Rosenberg would control both keys and opening. Elena's eyebrow rose. She looked at Adrian.

He did not answer with a declaration of trust.

“Family help doesn't mean surrendering the right to take something back.”

She slowly nodded. Each person could deposit only copies they had authority to share, identifying custody, use, and return arrangements. Originals remained under existing procedures. Dinner together didn't turn private information into common property. The actual household custodian needed to confirm capacity; Elena couldn't promise unlimited storage. They could ask what the place could hold and under what conditions, rather than make her confidence stand in for the answer of the person who would have to do the work.

She looked annoyed, partly by the possibilities requiring caution and partly by her impulse to end the issue with don't you trust me?

Adrian noticed. He didn't hurry to kiss the frown away.

“I want to give you this copy. I also want to know how to retrieve it.”

Taking the paper, she said, “Both can be true.”

A kitchen maid knocked. A messenger didn't want to leave his home address but worried he couldn't prove delivery. What should she give him?

Isabella suggested inviting him in. Cecilia reminded her that he wasn't joining their agreement. Adrian went to the hall to ask what he needed.

The printer's assistant carried a receipt concerning Luna's public statement. The household custodian routinely requested a complete address. The assistant feared people would visit his home to question him about scholarly business.

Adrian asked whether the printer's name, traceable delivery number, and actual time would suffice. After checking, the custodian agreed. The assistant received his receipt and visibly relaxed.

Returning, Adrian narrowed the identity-checking provision.

“We nearly protected people by requiring every messenger to surrender his private history.”

The messenger had not asked to become a witness to their discussion or a member of their circle. He had wanted an ordinary record of an ordinary delivery. That distinction was easy to describe after they had almost overlooked it. Nobody claimed they had already mastered the principles. Halfwritten, the paper already needed changing.

Viola shifted against her chair, her left shoulder tiring. Before Adrian asked, she said it was time to rest. She would read the remaining discussion tomorrow. Her absence needn't prevent them continuing. They could finish discussing what remained and show her the actual result, rather than ask her to endure the entire evening merely so that everyone could appear to have agreed together.

“You'll miss the signing,” Luna observed.

“The paper can wait until tomorrow.”

She was so solemn that everyone smiled. She clarified that she valued what they were saying.

Adrian understood. Before leaving, she defined her contribution: advice on routes outside any duty assignment, and passing requests to those actually responsible for safety. While injured, no guard shifts, mounted pursuits, or promises of absolute safety on her honor. Knowing what she couldn't offer made her help more credible than a page of unconditional vows. Adrian could understand the offer and decide whether it met a particular need. He would not have to discover, in the middle of a difficult journey, that the promise depended on her doing something her injury made impossible.

After she left, they considered emergencies.

Cecilia could contact ordinary medical assistance, not substitute rank for a physician or spiritual comfort for treatment. Isabella could organize public institutional contacts without borrowing her expired coordination powers. Luna could examine existing technical explanations, not conduct new experiments. Elena could inquire about lawful guard services. Nobody had been hired; the draft could not claim round-the-clock protection existed.

Adrian could organize authorized copies, check receipts, and accompany someone where willing and safe. He refused to become everyone's sole messenger. Otherwise losing contact with him would collapse the entire arrangement.

“Nor can refusal be recorded as failure to cooperate,” Cecilia said.

That applied to everyone. Isabella read it twice and asked her to retain the plain words instead of finding something more dignified.

They tested the draft. Suppose Adrian went to a known office tomorrow, expected back that afternoon, then chose to visit a bookshop. Who could require him to return and explain?

Elena thought he should at least tell his contact. Isabella noted that before the agreed time, he didn't need to announce every corner he turned. Both sounded sharper: one imagined anxious waiting; the other imagined being questioned everywhere she went.

Adrian didn't laugh at their difficulty. He wrote down the actual promise: a time to begin further contact, not a guarantee to occupy one place all afternoon. Changing his return time required the agreed notice. Choosing another stationer didn't automatically break the agreement.

“And if you forget?” Elena asked.

“Then I admit I forgot. I don't accuse you of controlling me merely because you're worried.”

Her shoulders eased. Cecilia retained the explanation without making one forgotten message punishable by a permanent escort.

Luna made the case harder. If a message arrived incomplete, could they distribute his private notes to search for clues? Adrian agreed to use the authorized contacts first. Real, urgent danger could justify reporting known facts to someone able to act. Ordinary anxiety did not justify exposing everything.

Isabella admitted judgment remained necessary. Paper couldn't guarantee judgment would never fail. At least the reasons should be recorded and the affected person later told what had been done. They could not eliminate every uncomfortable decision beforehand. They could refuse to hide those decisions behind a sentence claiming that everyone had already consented to whatever might later seem convenient.

Two trials revealed that nobody accepted report at all times. Erasing it left fine paper debris on the table. Their agreement was learning to say less.

On withdrawal, Luna asked whether every record must immediately burn. Remembering that morning, Adrian shook his head. Stopping future help differed from resolving existing custody: return what should be returned, explain any grounds for retention, permit no secret reuse. Recovering one's property must not require accepting new arrangements.

Isabella crossed out obey the contact and wrote contact instead. The paragraph seemed weaker without the command. She did not restore it.

Before the evening grew late, they had a provisional text, to be reviewed on February 4. Anyone could request changes or leave sooner; specific assistance still required confirmation each time. Five people read it and signed only for themselves. Viola's space remained blank until she was awake and willing.

“Will that look divided?” Cecilia wondered.

“It looks as though she's asleep,” Luna said.

Even Elena laughed.

Adrian signed last. His name was not at the center of five women's competition. Each retained the same text and could ask who had actually accepted a particular sentence. A shared table had not made their answers identical. The blank signature space was as much a part of the arrangement as the five names already written beneath it.

Elena remained to clear the table. Folding her copy, she asked whether her annoyance earlier had looked ugly.

“I thought you were holding a sentence back.”

“Must you notice?”

“You still didn't say it.”

After studying him, she picked a crumb from the paper's edge. She didn't want every kindness to become proof, but neither did she wish to stop being kind.

“Reading tomorrow?”

“If the paperwork doesn't bury us.”

“That's not an answer.”

He offered a period he could genuinely reserve, with notice if circumstances changed. She accepted. Only then did she touch the back of his hand, away from the scar, without making her fingers into a lock.

Before breakfast the next morning, Viola read the entire text, queried two changes made after she left, and signed with her right hand. Her medical appointment remained. Agreeing to help did not restore her to duty. All six had now individually accepted an ordinary mutual-aid arrangement, not obtained institutional approval or magical effect.

Before breakfast ended, a parliamentary letter arrived. A proposal concerning clearer individual choice and simplified risk examination would be discussed that day. Supporters warned that objections had already been filed. Completing a sheet around their table would not make the vote gentle.

Adrian was about to send Viola her copy when her message arrived: she would read it after the appointment. There was no need to chase her with it.

He left the letter in the agreed place and did not follow her outside.
