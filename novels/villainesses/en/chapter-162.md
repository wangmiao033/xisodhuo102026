# Chapter 162: An Oath Isn't a Proposal

Rumor restored Viola to duty considerably faster than her physician.

By the evening of January 28, people waiting outside had decided that protecting Adrian meant she had accepted lifelong responsibility. One young man earnestly asked the duty guard whether he should call her the future Mrs. Reed.

“Please stand below the steps,” the guard replied.

This failed to satisfy the capital’s romantic needs.

Viola sat inside the porch, her left arm supported. Her right hand touched the chair as she prepared to rise. She stopped and considered the distance to the entrance.

Adrian asked what she wanted to say.

“Stop changing my name.”

“You can say that from here.”

She smiled. The guard established why people had come, retaining two with specific questions where they could talk outside the courtyard gate and asking spectators to clear the entrance. Viola issued no roster changes and borrowed nobody’s sword to strengthen her argument.

The young man copied news for local residents. His page contained no signed oath, only a secondhand account of her willingness to take responsibility for Adrian. Could he publish that as a proposal?

“No.”

His pen rose. “So you deny protecting him?”

“I deny that your second sentence follows from your first.”

Adrian remembered the carving labeled cat on its base. At least its seller hadn’t betrothed a fox because both creatures had tails.

Viola asked for the actual source. The young man hadn’t been present. Another visitor had told him, without a date or exact words. He lowered his page, dissatisfied, but finally stopped hovering his pen after her name to add a new surname.

“I can explain what I mean now. I believe in a knight’s duty to protect people and safe passage. That is no declaration of a chosen partner. Walking with me doesn’t make Adrian owe me a betrothal. Offering safety advice doesn’t place someone in my custody.”

The young man asked whether an original oath could be published. Viola refused the premise. His untraceable account did not establish an original with identical wording and oblige her to disprove it. She would answer for today’s words, not invent a solemn past oath for convenient typesetting.

Embarrassed, he left the next page blank. Adrian found that space more honest than the filled one.

“But you worry about him especially?”

She colored. Adrian didn’t intercept the question.

“Yes. Worry doesn’t let me agree to everything on his behalf.”

The young man eagerly wrote. She requested the surrounding limits too, not merely yes. Reading it back, he had to remove the invented words responsible for life.

The other visitor asked why guards couldn’t remain beside Adrian until the registration dispute ended.

The duty guard glanced toward the gate. Two people had already requested such arrangements. One suggested shutting the doors would be simplest.

“He isn’t detained,” Viola said. “My honor cannot sign for a lock.”

She guaranteed no outing’s absolute safety. Guards could explain visible problems and offer routes. Actual restrictions needed proper grounds; ordinary accompaniment required the person’s agreement. Treating someone’s disappearance from sight as the safest outcome forgot that the protected person had a life.

“If I want supper from the corner now, must I persuade three people?” Adrian asked.

The guard described the gathering outside. He could wait for the passage to clear or use another available exit. This particular route was awkward now; leaving wasn’t forbidden.

“I’ll let them through first.”

Outside, a man began moving a narrow-wheeled cart of household coal. Worried about darkness, he tried to pass between spectators. Someone stepped back onto thin ice formed by dripping eaves and caught a gatepost to stay upright.

The crowd pressed inward. The cart’s handle caught against the gate. On the other side, a maid escorting an elderly man stopped. With his walking stick, he couldn’t step over the handle or retreat up the steps he had just descended.

Viola’s right hand gripped her chair again.

Her breath shortened. Her left arm could not support such a movement. Adrian followed her gaze rather than blocking her bodily.

“The duty staff are there.”

One guard stopped the cart; another halted people descending the steps. The maid kept the old man beside the dry doorway. An attendant fetched him a chair, without requiring him to stand as proof that he could.

Viola released her grip and indicated the space behind the cart.

“Clear room for the turn first. Don’t reverse him into their feet.”

Adrian relayed advice, not an order. After checking, the guard adopted it. Spectators moved to open ground beside the street. The deliveryman steadied his cart while an assistant watched the wheels as he backed slowly. The handle cleared the gate. Nobody hurried the seated old man.

The porter fetched an unopened sack of grit. Someone suggested lifting the cart across the ice. Viola quickly said she couldn’t help lift it, and an injured person shouldn’t supply missing hands.

“I didn’t ask you to,” the deliveryman said, startled.

She paused. “I thought of doing it first.”

Her ears reddened. Adrian didn’t laugh. She had stopped the thought before it became action. She needn’t pretend it had never occurred.

Once grit was down, the guard and deliveryman checked the wheels. The old man would leave along the cleared inside passage first; coal would follow. The assigned workers would handle it. Visitors were neither invited to carry goods away nor all investigated as attackers.

Someone caught a falling hat. Someone else complained about moving from a perfectly good standing place. A guard patiently indicated the cart handle. The man retreated, still grumbling.

Viola watched from her chair. Her left shoulder ached from tensing. She adjusted its padding rather than pretending a resolved hazard should cure pain too.

Passing the porch, the old man thanked her.

“Thank them too.” She indicated those handling the chair.

He did, individually, then asked whether he might finally go home for dinner. Several people laughed. The entrance became an ordinary gate again.

The young copyist crossed out lifelong himself and asked to record the incident. The guard explained his own arrangements. Viola claimed only her suggestions, not heroic command from her chair. Her physical restrictions must remain in the account: this was no return to duty.

“That makes a long headline.”

“You can leave some names out,” Adrian said.

“Readers like names.”

“The people attached to them like accuracy.”

After a struggle, the young man laughed. He would remove the marriage claim from his own sheet. He couldn’t retract every street’s speculation.

After delivering the coal, the carter returned for a forgotten rope. Seeing Viola still seated, he hesitated. He hadn’t meant she was useless, he explained. He simply hadn’t realized someone unasked would already be considering whether she could help.

She understood. She needed to learn to let other people finish the things they could do. He nodded, put the rope over his shoulder, and wished her a good recovery. He requested no signature. At last, the porch held an ordinary good wish that nobody would turn into a lifelong obligation.

Once people left, Viola asked the porter to turn her chair slightly outward. Not a new patrol position; she wanted the last daylight before returning inside. He helped while she moved her feet carefully. Adrian did not carry her outside to give the misunderstanding a prettier answer.

Sitting on a nearby stool, he asked about her shoulder.

“A little sore. I’ll rest on time.”

“Not the little you tell physicians?”

She glanced at him, then admitted it was worse than earlier. If it worsened further, she would go in. He had her caregiver informed through the existing arrangements rather than supplying medication himself.

The carving stall was packed away, its table covered. Viola wondered whether the cat had sold.

“Perhaps a fox finally claimed it.”

“That doesn’t establish a betrothal either.”

They laughed carefully enough to spare her shoulder. Afterward, Adrian said he knew she cared. He also knew it wasn’t a debt for him to repay.

She studied the fading ink on her right hand.

“I have wishes of my own.”

“I’m listening.”

“When I can walk farther, I want to walk with you. No inquiry office. No arguing carters. Nobody waiting for me to prove useful.”

She paused.

“You can refuse. I want an invitation that sounds like an invitation.”

He didn’t manufacture a date tonight. Her injury remained. Gratitude wasn’t a reason to promise a future date he hadn’t yet agreed to.

“Ask again when you know your permitted range. I’ll answer seriously.”

She found a loose thread on her own coat this time. He offered help; she declined, removing it slowly with her right hand. Not every small task had to become his. She still liked being asked.

“If I don’t mention guarding you, will you forget to invite me?”

He made no promise never to forget. Today, she had been the first person he saw inside the door. If he failed to think of her some other time, that wouldn’t prove that only standing guard made her worth remembering.

She listened as though loosening something habitually tied too tight. A trader extinguished the street’s last stall lamp. She watched without planning his route home.

A little disappointed, she nevertheless nodded. Rising, she balanced with her right hand on the chair and waited until steady before walking inside. Asked whether she needed her coat carried, she said yes and explained where to put it.

At the hall table, Luna turned a page of a research statement with her left hand. Her bandaged right palm rested on padding. No apparatus stood nearby, only a draft addressing who could consent and when they could stop.

Hearing the account, she said people often thought writing willing at the beginning let them fill in everything afterward.

“Even for an escort,” Viola said.

“More easily in research. Each step can promise the answer is close.”

Luna crossed out an overbroad permission. She would not announce every rule tonight. Tomorrow she intended to publish an explanation of research ethics boundaries. First she had to check the demands she herself had made, not merely make the new wording attractive.

Adrian left the chair against the wall, clear of the passage. Tomorrow was January 29. Noon remained the original endpoint of the seven-day restriction; no renewal or subsequent disposition had been notified. Tonight’s clarification prompted no premature celebration.

Reaching to move her draft, Luna touched an empty cup. Adrian asked to remove it. She agreed and asked him to leave the pages where they were. Material not yet authorized for publication did not become quotable merely by lying in the hall. Viola remembered the copyist’s final readback. If his printed version differed, she would address it, not treat one oral promise as a citywide correction already accomplished.

Someone called them to dinner. Viola chose a seat that spared her left shoulder. When Adrian followed, she indicated the empty chair beside her without pulling it out for him.

“Anyone sitting here?”

“Not at present.”

He sat and picked up his own spoon. Tonight, that answer was enough.
