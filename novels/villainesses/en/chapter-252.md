# Chapter 252: Where the Light Didn't Reach

People were already queuing outside the little chapel on the morning of April 15.

They weren't waiting for a god. At least, the baker near the front said he'd come to ask whether names on last night's leaflet would determine today's food distribution. His wife had recognized a drawing of the relief hall and thought they couldn't enter without signing. He carried an empty cloth bag and didn't want another trip home to explain he'd asked the wrong question.

Cecilia showed him the ordinary notice beside the door. The staff responsible for this relief service had reconfirmed its existing rules. The leaflet wasn't theirs. She had an attendant indicate the actual collection entrance, without requiring the baker to stay and support her.

“Then why did you come?” he asked.

“Because you shouldn't need an extra trip every day to prove the food is still yours.”

Adrian followed her inside. Last night's inquiries had obtained the venue receipt. A registered preacher had rented the morning session for prayer and explanation of current protection. Permission didn't allow a private supporter list to become a condition of relief, or authorize examination of five women's bodily marks.

The receipt was genuine. So were the promises exceeding it printed on the leaflet.

They asked the duty supervisor to remain and arranged confirmation of the permitted scope with the registry. Cecilia's arrival didn't restore the pulpit to her. She was raising an objection and helping explain, not resuming her former office.

Curtains hung over the windows. Pale gold light covered part of the front wall. Within a decorative border, five narrow shadows appeared without scorching the plaster. Some people prayed; others stretched to see. The preacher said the light had identified the empty places. Those refusing to bear the burden were making the capital wait.

From the back, Adrian could see only the center. He couldn't tell whether the shapes resembled marks. Failure to see clearly wasn't evidence he'd witnessed fraud. Cecilia didn't immediately shout liar either. She looked first at the narrowing aisles.

A man carrying a child wanted to leave. Someone told him departing before the miracle ended was disrespectful. Cecilia went to his side and asked people to clear the established passage.

“Prayer doesn't require a blocked door.”

The preacher paused at her voice, then invited her forward to confirm the sign. If she still believed, surely she wasn't afraid to see her responsibility.

She didn't step into the patch of light he'd selected.

“I'm willing to look carefully. First explain where the light comes from, who observed it, and who may interpret it as requiring a particular person's sacrifice.”

Murmurs rose. An elderly woman protested that they wanted hope amid fear, not another argument. Cecilia said she wasn't asking her to stop praying or promising danger didn't exist. She objected to placing another person's body inside that hope without giving the person a voice.

The preacher produced a copy of current preparation procedure. External records had indeed begun. Noon on April 20 remained the earliest conditional final-action review. Reading the line aloud, he asked who would answer for further delay.

“It doesn't say today's light replaces the necessary examination,” Cecilia replied.

She asked the supervisor to retain the document's provenance rather than snatching it. A questionable display didn't invalidate real danger. A genuine document didn't make every subsequent act genuine.

A boy sitting at the side knocked over a spare stool. As his mother bent to retrieve it, her clothes caught the curtain. A strip of morning light crossed the front wall. The five shadows suddenly grew faint around their edges.

The room stirred. The preacher ordered the curtain closed: the ceremony mustn't be disturbed. Adrian noticed a cloth-covered wooden stand beside the chapel's registered lamps, directed toward that wall. He told the supervisor where it stood rather than forcing through the crowd to dismantle it.

The supervisor had seen the preacher bring equipment in when taking possession. He'd understood it to be explanatory pictures. Now he required the display paused while checking its permitted use. The preacher refused. Stopping would make people believe their god had fallen silent.

“I'm responsible for this building,” the supervisor said. “I'm asking you to stop equipment you brought. I'm not giving orders to a god.”

An assistant reached for a shutter behind the stand. Adrian drew attention to the actual movement, then stepped aside so the ordinary custodians could approach. He neither seized the assistant's wrist nor invited spectators to rush forward and collect evidence.

The display stopped during the argument. Gold faded. No five wounds remained on the wall, only plaster. A young man began crying. Had they already lost protection? Cecilia sat with him away from the aisle instead of recruiting his distress to her side. She asked him to breathe slowly.

He clutched a worn prayer ribbon. Should he throw that away too? His mother had left only the ribbon and a few letters. He wasn't looking at the pulpit. He watched Cecilia as though she might next tell him that everything which had sustained him was worthless.

She shook her head. Had the ribbon ever required him to decide somebody else's life or death? No. He held it when afraid.

Then keeping it was his choice, she said. Being misled by someone's display needn't require surrendering his mother's memory as well. He slowly wound it around his wrist again, without promising to join either side's prayers.

Adrian brought water and placed it within reach. Cecilia didn't demand immediate composure. If he still felt unwell after leaving, medical staff were actually on duty by the entrance. She returned continuing care to someone able to remain, rather than making every consequence of one gentle moment depend upon herself.

The supervisor and registry staff examined the apparatus, recording who had brought and operated it. The stand held ordinary illumination, reflective surfaces, and a thin sheet carrying drawn lines. Up close, people could see the same arrangement as the shadows. Material and function could still be examined by someone qualified. Cecilia didn't need to claim she knew how every ray had traveled.

“It was an illustration to help people understand,” the preacher now said.

“You said it had identified the empty places itself,” the supervisor replied.

Several who'd heard the earlier words offered separate accounts. One late arrival had heard only the latter part. The recorder didn't let him complete his statement from other people's memories. Adrian finally had a conclusion he could state accurately: equipment brought into the room displayed this picture. It wasn't evidence that five absent people had manifested signs unaided.

He still didn't know whose instructions the preacher followed. A discovery in one chapel couldn't simply be stamped with Oswin's signature.

Stopping the display didn't end the argument. A man in worn work clothes pointed at Cecilia. Even if the light was false, the danger was real. Why couldn't she go first? Someone called him a traitor. Two groups pressed closer across the benches.

Cecilia asked those at the back to stop pushing inward and helped keep the cleared route available to the man carrying his child. She didn't sort the crowd by loyalty. Attendants requested assistance from nearby duty personnel to maintain passage. This room wasn't a court empowered to select a victim.

Adrian helped move an elderly person's bench aside instead of turning it across the aisle as a barricade. Its legs scraped harshly. Sometimes letting one person leave was harder than winning an argument in front of everyone.

Outside, the baker still waited for his wife. She emerged looking miserable. She'd genuinely believed the light had recognized someone.

“I nearly did too,” he said.

Fear hadn't made them stupid. In daily lives repeatedly disrupted, they wanted something capable of giving an unambiguous answer. Cecilia handed them the verified collection information. If anybody demanded signatures in exchange for food, would they report the actual incident through the receiving office rather than argue alone? The wife nodded. She signed no supporter list.

The supervisor ended the session early because of the equipment display and crowd safety. Competent offices would handle complaints about misleading statements and use beyond permission. Ordinary prayer times would resume separately. One fraudulent display wasn't grounds to forbid all belief.

The preacher demanded his belongings. Registry staff specified what was retained for examination, who received it, and how he could object, issuing an inventory. Spectators weren't permitted to take pieces as souvenirs. Original leaflets and separate witness accounts were preserved; private requests for help weren't posted outside.

Adrian expected Cecilia to be satisfied. Beside the now-bright window, she said the hardest part remained outside. Proving the image wasn't a miracle hadn't repaired lamps, supplied food, or automatically secured the five designated women.

“But nobody had to sign for that wall today,” he said.

She nodded, smiling tiredly. “Keep that sentence. Don't enlarge it for me.”

When they returned to the resting rooms after midday, Viola was available to hear what had actually happened. She hadn't put on unnecessary protective gear again. Her left arm remained supported, the handed-over duty page beside her. Adrian first asked whether she wanted the report now instead of wrapping every item in urgent.

Hearing how two groups nearly blocked the aisle, she immediately asked whether similar leaflets had appeared near the shelter. Existing reports couldn't answer. She asked current duty staff to check through established channels, without summoning personnel she couldn't command or rising to hurry to every door herself.

Afterward, she asked him to leave the door open while she rested. Having watched a crowd's exit that morning, Adrian found himself sitting beside an ordinary doorway again. He laughed quietly.

“What?”

“I seem to have spent the day studying doors.”

“Remember they have two sides. Wanting you outside doesn't mean I never want to come out.”

“When you do, I'll move the chair.”

Her smile no longer resembled reluctant acceptance of help. Later, could she hear something unrelated to protection? Perhaps the very pretty box which hadn't been theirs to keep. Yes, he said, but nobody suddenly became engaged in the story.

“Then it sounds particularly suitable for me.”

The small complaint didn't require him to change his feelings. He didn't pretend not to understand. He would tell the story properly, not treat her as someone merely waiting for the next order.

Before dusk, the shelter liaison returned. Leaflets bearing the same drawing had indeed appeared nearby. Someone wanted people to gather there tomorrow and demand answers. No new miracle display was confirmed; nobody knew how many would come. But an ordinary place of refuge had been drawn as their destination.

Viola handed the page to the person still on duty and agreed on the next discussion time after her rest. What needed protection wasn't victory in an argument. It was the people indoors who hadn't agreed to participate in one.

Adrian moved his chair clear of the passage. No golden shapes appeared outside, only evening light on the threshold.

Tomorrow, keeping that entrance free might be harder than exposing today's light.
