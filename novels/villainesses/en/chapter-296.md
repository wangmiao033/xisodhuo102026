# Chapter 296: The Institute's First Expense

On June 16, Luna discovered that the research institute's door cost less than its sign.

Studying the carpenter's sketch, she asked whether removing the carved leaves would damage its academic reputation. He couldn't speak for the reputation. It would save a considerable amount of wood.

“Remove them.”

“Should we keep Ashcroft in the name?” her assistant asked. Her full name was already on the lease. Nobody would struggle to find the person responsible for paying.

“Public Contract Research Institute. Future researchers shouldn't need to study my family tree first.”

No bells accompanied the keys. The academy leased them its former drawing room and an adjacent, separately locked records room for three months. Public reading belonged in the front room. Controlled documents wouldn't be opened merely to make the premises lively. A flattened-crow ink stain marked one inspected desk.

Luna liked it. Her assistant observed that this wasn't a professional reason to choose premises.

“That's why I'm only telling you.”

She received the keys left-handed. Her supported right palm carried no luggage. Her caregiver adjusted the desk arrangement, confirmed today's permitted working periods, and left for another scheduled patient. Establishing an institution didn't automatically heal its founder's hand.

The sponsor whose conditions she'd rejected in May had refused independent review. No new instruments were coming. Luna paused before removing that prospective contribution from the income sheet, then handed it to the newly hired adult records clerk, Merrin.

Merrin had copied course records for the academy. At their first interview, she'd asked whether wages depended on donors feeling pleased. Now, after verifying the money actually received, she placed two employment agreements on the desk: hers and the existing assistant's, with defined pay and hours. Neither contained a beautiful promise to surrender weekends for free.

A limited academy research grant, Luna's available personal funds, and several small donations without control over review findings covered three months' rent, two wages, and modest consumables. Finances and research judgments went to different examiners. Without renewed funding, the work must shrink. Unpaid wages wouldn't sustain the sign.

“So the door is our first expense?” the assistant asked.

“Rent,” Merrin corrected, consulting the ledger. “Your dream comes afterward.”

The dream was a reliable chair. Luna laughed hard enough to move her shoulder and was promptly told to sit properly.

That afternoon, the authorized registration certificate and charter went into the front room. The institute began receiving inquiries within its defined scope: historical contract materials, bounded nonhuman bench work, and the intelligibility of maintenance information. It wasn't a new facility authority. Its establishment permitted no human connections, opening of the five locks, or restarting of the blocked selector path. Luna directed the institute; she didn't provide its final independent review.

Its first visitor cared little about any of those distinctions.

Perry, a maintenance worker between shifts, produced a public explanation folded four times. He'd promised a neighbor to ask whether the institute could certify that the old facility near their homes would never threaten them again.

“No,” Luna said.

“Then why open?”

She could have offered a long vision for research. Instead, she looked at his page. It summarized the April trial. The correct stop signal appeared on the front; the scope was on the back. Pointing to a stable reading, he asked why they couldn't display the same mark in the street.

“Because that describes a bench trial, not your neighborhood.”

“This page doesn't say bench trial.”

Her assistant leaned forward. The explanation was on page two. Perry refolded it, hiding that page inside. He wasn't playing a trick. Everyone who'd passed it around had held it that way.

Remembering May's red line, Luna asked her assistant to wait before proving the visitor had misunderstood.

“Our first review meeting is tomorrow. May we keep this? Your name needn't appear publicly. Or take it back, and we'll examine the question ourselves.”

Perry allowed an unnamed copy and kept his original. He wouldn't attend the meeting. He needed sleep before his day shift. Merrin recorded his choice without converting a question into unpaid voluntary work.

On June 17, the independent reviewer arrived before the sign.

It was the man who'd brought sandwiches in May. Seeing the new chair, he asked whether critics also had somewhere to sit.

Luna indicated a shorter chair. “I meant to influence your judgment through height. Unfortunately, the carpenter only had that one.”

He sat, checked that his lunchbox opened, and unpacked the papers.

They weren't approving another human study. This first working review would compare the institute's initial explanatory materials against their actual evidence. An engineer uninvolved in funding would distinguish bench results from operating maintenance conditions. Observers attended the public discussion only; private information and dangerous interfaces were examined in the locked room. Reviewers submitted findings directly. Luna couldn't remove the unattractive sentences first.

The problem quickly reached her own title.

Conditions for Dual-Buffer Stability.

Every word could be defended. Together, they implied conditions anyone could apply to any device. Perry's folding wasn't the sole cause. The original traces supported only April 26's particular nonhuman trial with independent power and limited inputs. Extending the result to a common return remained unverified.

“You wanted a short title,” the reviewer said.

“I don't want to print half a book every time.”

“Then at least tell people which corner of the world they're reading about.”

Looking at the words she'd removed to save space, Luna felt her face warm. She knew the limits too well. She'd assumed anyone reading stable would supply the restrictions already in her head.

Her assistant proposed recalling the copies and printing again. What about copies already taken away? Merrin asked.

Silence. Correcting a page behind closed doors wouldn't make new words appear on the papers outside.

They suspended distribution of that version, retaining it with an explanation of the error. Corrections went to recorded receiving desks; a dated notice appeared prominently in the front room. The new title identified the date and bounded bench trial. Its conclusion and limits would share one page, instead of leaving the restrictions behind a fold. Dangerous connection instructions remained withheld. Candor didn't require publishing every internal drawing.

An observer asked them to name Perry's neighborhood for a more convincing account. Merrin rested her hand on the intake folder. He'd authorized an unnamed copy, not details enabling neighbors to identify him. Luna hadn't noticed the request at first. She now removed a shop location she'd casually included in her draft. The controlled original record retained its established retention period. She asked the reviewer to note the near-disclosure too. Catching it didn't mean nobody had almost overstepped.

The engineer required another practical warning: residents should consult actual duty notices, never use research findings as permission to remove barriers. Luna thought the text crowded. Then she folded the page Perry's way. The important line vanished again. She moved it.

Their first meeting produced no unconditional certificate. It produced a decision requiring correction and another independent check before distribution resumed. Wages continued. Rent continued. Even on its first days, the institute couldn't save money by declaring itself perfect.

On June 18, Adrian delivered the catalog references authorized for release. After the handover was recorded, he asked whether Luna wanted him to find the old travel book again.

He stopped. She looked up. The shelf held no private reading.

“No. Our reading is still paused.”

“All right. I mixed looking for a book with an old habit.”

He didn't turn the acknowledgment into an apology she had to soothe. She checked the work references and identified a missing ordinary catalog number. Could he supply it next time? He noted it. The normal receiving channel would do.

Beside the door lay a notice seeking future collaborators. Luna considered asking his opinion of the wording, then gave it to Merrin. Sometimes she still wanted him to hear new things first. She needn't pretend that feeling had never existed. She needn't follow it every time either.

“You've lost a very demanding private audience,” she said.

“He often didn't understand.”

“True.”

They shared a small laugh. Then he left, and she went for the scheduled replacement of the lining beneath her right-palm support. She didn't follow him downstairs.

Before dinner, Elena asked whether he'd remembered his umbrella. He raised an empty hand. Together they looked at the rain.

“The institute can publish one finding,” she said. “The same man forgetting umbrellas doesn't require a magical explanation.”

He proposed sharing hers to buy tomorrow's bread. “Is this another scheme to squeeze us close together?” she asked.

“Yes. You can also leave me in the rain.”

“Closer, then.” She picked up the umbrella. “But you carry the bread. Romance doesn't waterproof the bag.”

When Perry returned on June 20, the sign was up. Without carved leaves, a broad patch of bare wood remained after the final word. The carpenter had left room for later titles, Merrin explained. Luna said there wouldn't be any.

The corrected explanation had passed independent review and was being distributed again. Perry compared his old copy with the new title. He didn't request a guarantee of eternal safety. Instead, he observed that a street noticeboard had no desk. People might stand and read only the first few lines. Merrin held the scope statement up and invited him to try that way. This time, he didn't turn it over to search.

At another table, the assistant and a qualified visiting technician performed an approved dimensional check on unenergized sample pieces. Any later powered bench work still needed separate review. Unapproved spaces remained blank. The records room received only copies with established sources and transfer limits; May's transferred historical originals remained with their existing custodians. Opening its doors hadn't moved all history and danger into the institute's cabinets.

“Shouldn't somebody cut a ribbon?” Perry asked.

Luna checked the approaching end of the working day. “Now? I'd have to pay overtime.”

Merrin immediately turned to a fresh ledger page.

“That was hypothetical.”

“Then please specify the limits of the hypothesis.”

Laughter reached the corridor. In her chair, which finally didn't hurt her back, Luna watched the assistant hand a reviewed explanation to an actual visitor. She remembered the room years ago that only she could unlock. Once, important work had seemed to require hiding it from everyone who might question her.

Some doors here still needed locks. The front room had people who could ask questions. She hadn't made a kingdom permanently safe today. An error had been found and corrected before it could keep misleading people, through something more dependable than her personal goodwill.

At closing time, Merrin shut the ledger. The assistant put away the borrowed measuring tools. Luna didn't invoke her title to keep them, or begin another trial alone. Left-handed, she placed tomorrow's tasks beneath a paperweight and had the duty custodian secure the controlled room.

The sign hung quietly outside. The institute had already worked through several ordinary days. Tomorrow it would open again. Tonight, she was going to eat a dinner that owed nothing to anybody's praise.
