# Chapter 274: Words Beneath the Crest

On the afternoon of May 3, Elena's family representative stopped her outside the reading room.

He brought no command from the duke. He brought a cufflink she'd left on a small table that morning. Martha had asked him to deliver it. The silver rose lay in velvet beside a written objection to the proposed disclosure.

“Which should I accept first?” Elena asked.

Looking embarrassed, he said, “The cufflink is only a cufflink, my lady.”

“Good.”

She took it and directed him to submit the objection at reception. Adrian waited until she'd fastened it before approaching. He didn't ask with his eyes whether the letter was a reprimand. She offered her wrist and asked whether both sides looked straight.

After careful inspection, he said the rose on the left seemed angrier.

“That's the right.”

“Perhaps I shouldn't identify heraldry today.”

She smiled. Some of the smile remained when she entered, but the crest at the center of the room already meant something different.

The display held approved pages from the register of noble bearers. On March 12, she'd read her name, the sequence of designation followed by fabricated charges, and two alternative formulas by which either family guarantees or refusal could lead to lost protection. These words were no longer restricted to extracts for affected parties. The custodians had approved the relevant general provisions, her own associated page, and necessary contractual annexes for public examination. Other people's addresses and family details remained covered.

Another volume came from the financial inquiry she'd requested in March. It hadn't established every contributor's knowledge. It had connected the outer-passage repair reference to a historical works contract retained by her family. The original, the receiving institution's counterpart, and an independently kept settlement ledger arrived through their respective custodians. Their movements could still be questioned step by step.

Her representative presented his objection first. He accepted verification, but wanted the family to approve wording concerning its name before publication, lest an ordinary contribution to construction become an accusation of purchasing lives.

“This wasn't merely ordinary construction,” Elena said.

“You haven't explained that to everyone.”

“Then I'll explain now.”

The chair seated her at the witness table, preventing the dispute becoming a struggle over original pages. A conservator raised the works contract on its support. The opening provisions concerned materials, wages, and maintenance, resembling any agreement to build a passage. A later use clause granted the designated receiving institution priority. Ordinary family access rights were not to obstruct transfers of listed persons.

Elena read it twice: once for the back rows, again to identify the register category cited by the annex.

Not every payment went directly to an altar. This money built a route. This agreement allowed certain people to be taken along it.

The verifier displayed the receiving counterpart from another archive. One corner was missing, but the text survived. Document number, final clause, and receipt date corresponded. The separate settlement ledger supported actual delivery of the completed works, not an unfulfilled family promise. Who had been taken through that passage, and how much each signatory knew of the attachments, required execution records and individual answers.

A representative of affected parties asked them not to turn the page. The public extract didn't show where annex and main document joined. Could two unrelated sheets have been placed together later?

Nobody asked Elena to guarantee authenticity on her family honor. The conservator rotated the support, showing continuous page numbers, the old mark crossing the fold, and the matching annex in the separate counterpart. The public copy received corresponding page references.

Was the damaged part of the mark torn deliberately? The verifier couldn't establish the cause. Available receipt records documented how far its condition could be traced; nobody would redraw an illegible portion as complete.

Elena was grateful she hadn't rushed to say impossible. She wanted evidence without a crack in it. But the thing before them had survived with cracks. She requested preservation of the question and invited further doubts after comparison.

“This is as far as we can establish,” the verifier reminded her.

“I understand.”

Someone at the back said nobles excelled at splitting guilt into a hundred things awaiting verification. Another voice objected: Elena herself was listed for sacrifice. How could her family have supported the system?

She didn't choose the more flattering side.

“My name is in that register. My family's works contract is here too. You needn't erase one to believe the other.”

Turning to her representative, she continued, “I can't confess to things people never did. Nor can I use my survival to exempt what they actually agreed to from questions.”

He studied his pen before replying. He didn't want records to disappear. He feared harassment of family employees, or current relief donations all being described as payment for guilt.

The chair specified that publication of necessary institutional and responsibility information excluded employees' private addresses. Claiming to investigate nobles wouldn't protect threats against others. Today's donations and historical contracts remained separate; neither canceled the other. The family's full written answer could be made available under the same limits. Prior family approval couldn't become a condition of evidence entering review.

Only then did Elena's fingers leave her cuff.

The conservator turned to her associated register page. The treason summary beneath her name still tightened Adrian's throat. September 20 was long past, and withdrawn charges wouldn't revive through display. Yet a case people called resolved had left, in another ledger, evidence of the purpose for which it had been manufactured.

“Designation first. Charges subsequently completed in judicial form,” Elena read.

She didn't raise her voice over whispers. The words were cold enough. Greater anger would only supply the writer with another story.

The alternative accusation formulas appeared side by side. A guarantee could facilitate transfer; refusal enabled removal of family protection. The verifier supplied the separate judicial reference and unusual matching word order connecting them to the original case. The actual drafter remained an unresolved question. The correspondence connected the documents without making every missing hand suddenly identifiable. Elena waited for that qualification too; it was part of the explanation she had chosen to bring into this room.

A noble asked whether the old arrangement had purchased protection for the family. Elena said any benefits and obligations required examination of actual clauses. Her victimization couldn't exclude the question, but nobody should invent an undocumented reward merely to establish harm.

“What part of your family do you take upon yourself, then?” he asked.

She paused.

She knew the posture expected when claiming a family name: stand straight, smile, never show the desire to retreat behind a door. Lessons learned in childhood returned together, almost offering to say she could answer for everything.

“Today I explain what I personally read, requested for inquiry, and chose to disclose. My willingness to stand here mustn't prevent specific responsibility reaching specific people.”

Adrian's hand relaxed in his lap. He'd wanted to shoulder every error so nobody else would suffer. Hearing her refuse the same beautiful shortcut, he understood how difficult sitting beside someone without answering could be.

At the break, she found him immediately outside. Without speaking, they walked to the window. The plant on its sill had grown a crooked leaf. Adrian considered it, then decided another comparison might cost him all remaining privileges as a maker of metaphors.

“I want something sweet,” she said finally.

“Very specific.”

He produced bakery biscuits. Seeing one wrapped separately, she asked whether it rewarded good witness performance.

“It's for you. Whatever you said.”

Before accepting, she asked whether he'd eaten. A broken corner, he admitted. She divided the whole biscuit, keeping the smaller half.

“You've given me the larger piece again.”

“I'm saving room for proper afternoon sweets.”

“Is this one an impostor?”

She laughed and ate slowly by the window. She hadn't cried inside and didn't now. Hard sugar caught her teeth on the second bite, making her frown. He didn't ask whether the sugar or the crest hurt. He passed water and waited.

“Did you see me almost say it? I represent my family. I accept everything.”

“I saw you pause.”

“I used to think that was how to avoid disgrace.”

“And now?”

“I still want to avoid disgrace.” She looked at him. “I just can't buy that with somebody else's chance to find the truth.”

He didn't immediately praise her. Eventually he admitted that, when she'd been asked what she claimed, he'd wanted to interrupt and turn the question toward himself.

“Don't steal it. I barely tasted breakfast for that exchange.”

He laughed. Folding the empty wrapper, she gently touched his shoulder with hers. No embrace could acquit her, and no old list could require him to stay solely as her protector.

“Coming back to listen?”

“Of course.”

During the final session, the family representative raised a specific correction. One transcription rendered contractor as director, enlarging what the agreement proved. The verifier checked the original and corrected public copies, explaining the change and supplying replacement pages to earlier readers.

Elena didn't insist on the harsher word because he had noticed it. In turn, he no longer asked to move the priority-use clause onto an unpublished reverse.

Publication wasn't a judgment upon every signatory, the chair explained. Contested terms, completed works, and register references remained together in the case. Today's examination authorized neither immediate confiscation nor prosecution of whole families. Each party still had its own questions to answer.

Near closing, an older woman requested the general provisions concerning family guarantees. She wouldn't explain why in public. The archivist supplied the approved version and offered a protected inquiry for another person's case. She needn't prove sufficiently painful suffering at this table before being heard.

Watching her fold the paper into an old bag, Elena remembered asking in March whether she could stop on any page. The ability to publish her own name didn't entitle her to require everyone else to do likewise.

After the woman left, a small question arrived from the back: did the oracle about being born to repay have a source too? Elena asked the archivist to record it with the precise contractual quotation for Cecilia's presentation tomorrow. She wouldn't independently announce the demolition of all faith on the saint's behalf.

As originals returned to their boxes, she loosened the rose cufflink. It had pressed a pale line into her wrist.

Outside, Adrian asked what she wanted to discuss now.

After consideration, she wondered whether it was too late for proper afternoon sweets.

There was still time to walk to the bakery. They descended together. She hadn't removed her surname or raised her crest to clear a route. Behind them, Rosenberg remained on the reading table. Family representatives would continue arguing, and the old clauses would not become easier to read merely because she had now read them aloud. Tonight, she could still choose a sweet she liked.

Tomorrow Cecilia would answer. The oracle that had spared old contracts so much explanation would finally have to explain its own origin.
