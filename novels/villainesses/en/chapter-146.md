# Chapter 146: The Man Who Collected the Receipts

Early on January nineteenth, Leon sent an invitation to breakfast, not a conclusion sealed in wax.

The small dining room was beside the examination office. Adrian found the prince wrestling with a badly peeled egg, broken shell scattered around the plate. Two documents lay apart from each other and safely clear of the coffee.

“Don't look,” Leon said.

Adrian stopped in the doorway.

“At the egg.”

He sat. Leon pushed an intact one toward him and resumed his struggle. Neither had an attendant to conceal the small defeat.

“I'll sit in the audience today. I won't answer for her.”

Isabella's exercise of eligibility remained suspended. Her appeal-registration deadline fell tonight; she had registered her intention to appeal the previous evening. That neither restored her status nor withdrew her from today's questions.

Adrian asked about the papers.

“Yesterday's reference to external liaison advances.” Leon cleaned his hands. “I had the authorized staff check our outgoing register. This time we're not guessing the Temple's name into it.”

One document was an extract from the advances office's correspondence index. The other copied an old request for closure receipts. The first linked to the personnel liaison project number. The second showed a Temple external liaison deacon requesting completion information for a designated receiving office.

His name was clear: Martin Veyr, external liaison deacon.

“Did he receive the money?”

“This doesn't prove that. He requested the receipt. The original payment ledger still hasn't arrived.”

Leon held down the paper. He would not let a new name cover a missing fact.

Earlier Temple records concerned the administrative forwarding of old packaging and maintenance papers. This letter concerned closure of personnel contact: another chain of duties to check. Different dates, too. The earlier transfer couldn't prove the later matter passed through the same hands.

“Are you going directly to him?”

“First we confirm it's the same man, whether he's still there, and whether his receiving authority was transferred. I used to summon people first and require them to prove I'd summoned correctly.”

He ate the ruined egg and grimaced.

“Cold.”

Adrian suppressed a smile and passed the salt.

At the morning hearing, Elena occupied the presentation place designated by her father's seat. The duke retained his vote; he had not handed her the chair permanently. Baroness Merriwell attended as seconder. The chair stated that the hearing concerned permitted old-project records and missing-person inquiries, not succession.

Isabella sat where questions would be directed.

From the document table, Adrian saw her flatten her hands, then withdraw them to her lap. She didn't demand her title. Neither did she pretend titles had ceased to matter.

Elena began with their revised question.

What evidence closed the old liaison project, and who confirmed actual contact?

The advances office representative had brought a handling index, not the full payment ledger. He said the cooperating body supplied a completion opinion and his office processed the forwarded papers. Hester asked for that body's document reference.

This time, the relevant authorities would not suffice.

After searching, he identified the receiving office in Leon's letter, but admitted a request for receipts was not itself a completion opinion. He undertook to provide the original supporting records by a specified deadline.

The chair recorded it. Willingness to supplement did not make the question answered.

Clara stopped in the second sentence of her notice. She hadn't forgotten the words. She had repeated them too many times: Matters concerning the personnel have been handled by the responsible unit.

She looked up and asked who had seen Milo.

The representative could name nobody.

Elena did not fill the silence with an elegant speech. She waited until the chair recorded that no direct contact person's identity could currently be supplied.

Isabella's old restrictive order came next. She acknowledged signing it, explained what she had been told, and admitted she had not followed the matter through to the individual families. New documents did not prove every old choice unavoidable.

Clara asked whether regret could bring her brother back.

Isabella pressed her lips together. “No.”

Nobody applauded.

At the midday adjournment, Isabella still had no restored power and the missing accounts had not magically appeared. But preservation and supplementation requests had named recipients. Searches would not stop because of an old closure sheet. Each decision stood separately, not compressed into everything resolved.

Leon did not intercept his sister to explain what he had won for her. He watched from a distance, waited for her to leave with her attendant, then gave the new reference to the liaison clerk.

While he collected his coat, Adrian visited a nearby temporary rest room. Viola sat with her left arm supported as instructed, her right hand holding the written report on the January seventeenth handover. She had neither guarded the door nor joined the escort. She had been invited to check whether her safety recommendation had actually been implemented.

“The old watch stayed,” she said first.

“I saw.”

“Nobody sent them away to satisfy a timetable.”

That relieved her more than a new witness's name might have. Adrian described the bridge, including his incorrect sack number. She reminded him to watch the outer side of a leaning wagon's wheels rather than stare only at paper.

“I didn't push it.”

“Good.” A pause. “Not because you weren't brave enough.”

He answered seriously, not merely to hurry the conversation toward something else. He knew. But she glanced at her unusable left hand, as though the words needed more time to reach herself.

He drew up a chair and offered the remaining breakfast. She wanted some, but not cut into tiny pieces for her. She preferred doing what her right hand could still manage.

He passed the plate without reaching in again.

Someone ran down the corridor. Her shoulder moved instinctively, then stopped. Only a young clerk carrying papers, not an attack. Adrian neither looked surprised nor praised her self-restraint. Slowly she steadied her fork.

“Going out again this afternoon?”

“With Leon, to verify a recipient.”

“If you're back early, will you tell me?”

“Yes. It may not be a handsome result.”

“I'm not a general in a dispatch.” She smiled first. “Not today, at least.”

She saved him an untouched piece of bread, then lifted her chin toward the door: don't leave the prince in the cold.

That afternoon Adrian accompanied Leon to the Temple's external affairs receiving room. Cecilia had confirmed visitor procedures through ordinary channels, not ordered doors opened as saint. She waited there instead of arguing Isabella's case at the hearing.

“I checked the current staff list. Martin Veyr still serves here. We may ask about his duties today. We may not search his private room.”

She told Leon plainly. He nodded and left his guards in the permitted position outside.

Martin was younger than Adrian expected, and more tired. He first asked whether this was an accusation. Told it was verification of documentary connections, he invited them to sit.

His hand stopped at the project number.

“I followed up outstanding closure receipts.”

“Did you personally meet the people marked contact completed?” Leon asked.

“No. I dealt with the administering office.”

“How did you confirm completion?”

“When the stamped summary arrived, I logged its return. I wasn't making household visits.”

Cecilia asked where the summary now was. Annual archives, he said. She didn't absolve him with that answer, but asked for the archive title and transfer date so a formal request could follow.

After hesitating, Martin added, “The form only had received and outstanding. There was no field for personal contact not verified.”

Leon didn't raise his voice. “But people had returned and not returned.”

The deacon looked at his hands and did not argue.

Someone brought water from the other side of the room. As Martin glanced at the tray, Adrian noticed a cracked mug on his desk, a film forming over its tea. It didn't make him innocent. It merely kept the new name from becoming nothing but a target on paper.

Who ordered these follow-ups? Martin indicated an ordinary forwarding entry in the duty instruction book. It came from the external affairs office, not a private command bearing Oswin's name. The entry could be copied with authorization. The next layer required the archives.

Leon did not insert the high priest's name.

Before they left, Martin volunteered a memory. One receipt had been returned for rewriting because its administering office wrote contact unclear, which did not fit closure wording.

The room became quiet.

“Was the returned draft preserved?” Cecilia asked.

“I don't know. Sometimes there was only a return reference.”

She did not close her notebook in disappointment. She asked him to record what he could establish: approximate period, his duties, and where the return entry might be indexed. Gaps could stay gaps. She would not improve his memory into more satisfying testimony.

Martin wrote slowly. Beside his signature he requested a note that he did not remember the individuals involved.

They retained that qualification too.

Outside, Leon exhaled at the foot of the steps.

“I wanted a villain to confess everything.”

“That would be easier,” Cecilia said.

“You think I'm childish?”

“I wanted it too.”

Her honesty took away his smile.

Adrian looked at the receiving-room door. They had found an actual participant in the correspondence and a traceable returned-document lead. They had not found eight people. Satisfaction at gaining a name must not conceal the difference.

On the way back, Leon avoided the royal carriage lane so pedestrians would not be moved aside merely to save him a little time. He had not become an ordinary man; guards still followed. Today, at least, he noticed whose feet paid for his convenience.

At the crossing, Cecilia asked Adrian for half an hour tomorrow.

“For what?”

“Breakfast. Not documents in exchange for your time.” She considered. “You can ask about today if you want. But eat first.”

He checked his commitments and agreed, before the examination office opened. She smiled without turning half an hour into a promise for the whole day. Then she asked what he wanted to eat. Eggs, he said. She remembered, treating the ordinary question as worth asking and the answer as worth keeping.

Back at the house that evening, Elena was circling unanswered items in the hearing record. Had he brought anything new? A return index, he said, and a man willing to confirm the limits of his involvement.

“No complete answer?”

“No.”

She made room beside her and turned her papers toward him.

“Then let's put our two incompletes together and see where tomorrow can go.”

He sat and moved the ink within her reach. Instead of thanking him, she passed him a clean sheet with room for errors and corrections. It felt more like their shared evening than any declaration.

Wind struck the glass outside, but the lamp steadily lit two people writing side by side.
