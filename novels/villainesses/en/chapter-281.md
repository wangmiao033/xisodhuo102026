# Chapter 281: A Blank Name Is Not Consent

On May 9, Luna rejected a seating plan.

Not because researchers sat too far back. A helper had placed Adrian in the middle, with the five women in a semicircle around him, labeled “for convenient consultation.”

“Afraid he'll escape?” she asked.

The young helper blushed. That was how people outside described them. Elena took the plan, noticed half an inch separating her chair from the princess's, and asked whether that measurement had undergone research too.

“I measured wrong.”

“Then arrange us by today's speakers and access needs. We aren't his five different opinions.”

Suppressing laughter, Adrian moved his name to the position for explaining materials. Cecilia earnestly added that, in a romantic discussion, he wouldn't be required merely to sit and listen either. The helper blushed harder. Finally, everyone had an appropriate place: Luna near the exit, room for Viola's supported left arm, Isabella's cushion no longer requiring ceremonial approval.

Before the real argument began, Adrian had already been told not to look pleased with himself.

He wouldn't dare, he said. Elena gave him a brief smile, then opened yesterday's offer of support, including its writing on the back.

Not every region had reached the capital overnight. Those present had already been here on business or could arrive in time. More distant opinions traveled by existing channels, dated when written and received. Regions still awaiting replies remained blank, not counted as supporters.

Devon, a river-port councilor, first offered ten dockhands and ordinary transport assistance already discussed locally. The chair asked whether those ten had agreed to move supplies or participate in future load-bearing trials.

“They're helping. We'll assign specific jobs later.”

Only afterward did Devon notice that nobody was writing. He hadn't deliberately tricked ten bodies into volunteering. His docks assembled crews this way whenever water rose: experienced hands leading newcomers, nobody writing a page about lifting sandbags first.

Viola asked him to open the roster. What had the workers actually been told?

“Maintenance support.”

“Then explain what transport arrangements you've discussed. You can't add another meaning for them here.”

“Asking about everything means days lost sending another letter.”

“Those days are real. Slow letters don't make it easier to give somebody else's consent.”

Dissatisfied, Devon nevertheless listed transport separately. The future-trials column remained empty. Nobody quietly counted the ten back in because their absence left a smaller number.

Across the table sat Agnes, a mill owner delegated to attend by several evacuated households. She brought her own expense notes and a sheet showing rent arrears. One worker, she said, would participate in anything if his family first received a room that didn't leak.

“If that isn't voluntary, what else does he have to offer?”

Cecilia didn't immediately invalidate his wish. Was the room ordinary shelter, or an additional condition offered for risky participation? Agnes explained that local staff were short-handed; the organizer had simply suggested priority for participants.

“Where do nonparticipants go?”

“They keep waiting.”

Having said it, Agnes stopped defending the arrangement. She'd come seeking a room for people still waiting, not to dismiss their courage.

“I can't judge his willingness from across this table,” Cecilia said. “But if refusal costs him accommodation he should already receive, we can't preserve only the part where he says yes. First let him know what remains available if he refuses. Then listen.”

Devon asked whether, if everyone received help, those doing extra became fools.

“Wages can be discussed,” Elena answered. “Transport wages can't secretly purchase a different risk. Extra work deserves compensation. That doesn't mean people without money lose the right to stop.”

Agreement didn't follow immediately. An estate accountant asked whether those staying would always suffer if everyone had to absorb what departing participants left behind. Luna had her assistant bring two ordinary, separate cups. No energy demonstration today; first, everyone should hear one sentence.

“Withdrawal doesn't erase consequences already produced. That's why materials and staff must remain available to receive residual load. Without them, we shouldn't tell departing people to be considerate. We shouldn't call the system ready for connection.”

“What if a hundred leave the same day?”

“Assessment must account for plausible simultaneous withdrawals. We can't assume people will conveniently tire one after another.” She looked at Adrian. “That lesson cost us real materials.”

He acknowledged that the April 23 failure remained on record. If someone consequently distrusted another attempt, he couldn't demand gratitude instead.

At noon, he queued beside Devon for an ordinary lunch. Watching him pay with copper coins, Devon asked whether they intended everyone in the kingdom to behave as though buying pastries: come willingly, leave whenever they wished.

“You can't return a bite you've eaten either,” Adrian said. “But lighting the oven doesn't entitle the baker to make me eat the entire batch.”

Devon laughed. Even less adequate than those two cups. Without malice, he shifted his stool to make room.

“Two of our ten will be upset if they're only allowed to carry boxes. They're willing to do more dangerous work.”

“Then let them say so after hearing what's involved. Poverty doesn't mean they can't understand. Respecting them doesn't mean you needn't ask.”

After two silent mouthfuls, the councilor produced his roster and changed the space beside “agreed” to “inquiry awaiting reply.” His transport support wasn't withdrawn wholesale. Confirmed assistance continued; the unconfirmed part could no longer borrow its name.

Agnes approached with her bowl. Could an actual refusal be included in the opinions sent home? She feared residents wouldn't sign if their lord might see.

Adrian couldn't create nationwide protection across a lunch table. He accompanied her to the existing reception desk to ask whether an authorized representative could submit separately, without personal circumstances appearing on the public list. Staff explained their present remit and what they couldn't guarantee. Agnes took the information away instead of leaving her principals' names on a lunch table.

Cecilia had finished eating when they returned. She didn't call this a new oversight system. Her question was harder: if the person allocating rooms also collected expressions of willingness, how far could this desk help?

Nobody could solve it merely by asking today. She retained the question for checking with those responsible.

Once the afternoon session resumed, Devon raised another discomfort. Suppose someone agreed to move supplies for a day, then left halfway through simply because they were unhappy. Could the employer not even complain?

Viola didn't pretend every promise could vanish at will. Ordinary employment and unauthorized human load-bearing must first be distinguished. Hours, pay, and changes could be agreed beforehand; disputes had appropriate venues. Injury, danger, or inability to continue especially required a real handover, not the word “obligation” blocking the exit.

“I'm not asking you to drop a box on somebody's feet,” she said. “I'm asking you not to wait until someone can't hold it before discovering that calling him a coward is your only solution.”

Silently, Devon lengthened his written question. Agnes asked whether someone leaving midway could still receive wages for work already done. The employment representative confirmed settlement according to the actual agreement and completed work. A signature for future trials couldn't be demanded in place of payment.

No extra relief worker immediately appeared at the docks. But anger previously bundled together now had different objects. For once, Adrian didn't resent how much room the expanding questions occupied.

Someone proposed a vote. If more than half supported voluntary sharing, could all local residents then be required to participate, avoiding individual delays? The chair explained that this meeting had no authority to enact kingdom-wide rules. Questions could continue.

Isabella turned over her regional liaison sheet.

“You can discuss public spending and the representative procedures it requires. Even if everyone here raises a hand, that won't answer for an absent person whether their body may be connected.”

“How is the kingdom to act together?” the estate accountant demanded.

“First, stop reducing cooperation to that one act. Some repair roads, some transport materials, some provide care or money. Some simply need care. We can discuss division of labor. We can't use unity to skip a person's dangerous choice.”

An accompanying assistant quietly asked whether he could listen without signing the declaration of support. His superior began saying that of course they must present a united front today. Viola looked toward him.

She didn't proclaim the assistant brave. She simply waited for him to finish.

“I understand some of it. I still don't understand withdrawal. I'd like to ask more at home.”

Embarrassed, his superior eventually put the pen away. Then don't sign yet. The assistant remained seated, not expelled. One person's concession wasn't a kingdom finally learning respect. It did let another person in the back put down their pen too.

Watching those two pens, Elena remembered a familiar family argument: someone must shoulder responsibility, so surely those raised with generations of education and honor should decide for everyone.

Before she spoke, a noble proposed something similar.

Families could continue financing and guaranteeing the arrangement, designate hereditary lines to bear the burden, and train each generation instead of seeking agreement anew whenever danger came. In exchange, those lines retained specific service exemptions and priority protection. Honor and order together. Could the five existing mark-bearers first have their responsibilities reaffirmed by their families, before future succession was discussed?

He spoke solemnly, even bowing to Elena first.

“Not fabricated charges again. Responsibility made dignified.”

Her face slowly paled. Adrian wanted to rise for her and say they'd discussed this too many times. She raised her hand first, asking the proposer to write everything down, including who could refuse and what their families would lose afterward.

“I'll answer the part concerning me tomorrow.”

The man relaxed, apparently hearing room for compromise. She didn't correct that fleeting misunderstanding. She submitted the whole page for preservation so that tomorrow's record wouldn't contain only the attractive word “honor.”

Afterward, Adrian waited by the door. She opened her empty hand toward him without claiming to have worked everything out. He gently took it. Would she like the courtyard first?

“Yes. Don't comfort me by saying he meant no harm.”

“I won't. What would you like to say first?”

Watching carriages rearrange below the steps, she suddenly gave a cold laugh.

“He thinks upholstering the killing seat in velvet will make me grateful for a good chair again.”

Adrian didn't answer with a louder sentence. He walked beside her to somewhere they wouldn't obstruct anyone and waited while she slowly let out her breath.

Later, Viola passed them sitting together. Raising her right hand, she told Adrian he'd observed the old list's second instruction admirably today. Nobody was standing in a doorway. Elena laughed despite herself. Her tense fingers finally loosened a little.

“Don't choose my words tomorrow,” she said.

“All right.”

“Tonight, you may choose something tolerable for dinner.”

This time he answered quickly. She leaned against him briefly, then straightened herself. The hereditary proposal remained on the table. She wanted dinner with him, but didn't need his love to prove how she ought to refuse tomorrow.
