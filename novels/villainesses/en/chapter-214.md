# Chapter 214: Before the Oracle Was Written

On March 13, Cecilia heard yesterday's oracle outside the meal hall.

Not from a pulpit. An elderly woman washing dishes moved a steaming basin and told a younger volunteer that the destined ones repaid the debt for everyone. Perhaps, she added gently, they weren't as frightened as other people. She sounded as though she were comforting someone afraid of another cold month without coal.

Cecilia didn't correct her across the room. She hung up her shawl and asked for a dry cloth.

“Leading prayers today?” the woman asked.

“Helping for a while. Then I have to leave.”

The week she'd lost had passed. No compensatory appearance had been arranged. The supervisor asked her to dry spoons, so she dried spoons. At the other end, Adrian packed bowls too closely into a rack and couldn't fit the third.

“They aren't nobles,” Cecilia observed. “They needn't crowd together to demonstrate affection.”

He left another space. “They aren't grateful either.”

The woman laughed, then noticed Cecilia had something else to say.

“I'm checking where those words came from today. I don't have all the answers yet. But people named on paper haven't necessarily agreed. They're frightened too.”

The woman's hands stopped. “Who should we pray to, then?”

Cecilia didn't say herself.

“You can still pray for people who suffer. You needn't first decide who was born to suffer.”

“I didn't mean I wanted some girl dead.”

“I understand.”

They finished the spoons. The exchange didn't become a public confession everyone had to witness.

Cecilia's cuff was still damp when they reached the repository.

The quotation in the noble register pointed to an old proclamation. The liturgical volume distinguished copying from public reading; the associated drafting folder had been retrieved through a printer's settlement attachment carrying the same reference. The custodian explained both routes. A pile delivered together wasn't automatically one continuous chain of proof.

Elena had authorized examination of her associated quotation. Other names remained concealed. She was pursuing yesterday's financial referral, not present today. Adrian didn't supply wishes on her behalf or explain her absence as an inability to cope.

The proclamation's heading stated that revelation had preceded selection. Beneath it, the words about being born owing restitution almost exactly matched the noble register.

Cecilia asked for the second draft sheet beside it.

An empty pale patch interrupted the margin. The text had originally read proposed persons. A correction required persons revealed once the register was confirmed, followed by a change of tense: already summoned. Another instruction deleted the family's opportunity to object because it “weakened the proclamation's certainty.”

Adrian remembered the woman's face over the washing basin. Certainty could comfort her. It could also make her unknowingly approve another person's destination.

“Someone will call this editing a revelation,” he said.

“Yes. We need to examine when that revelation supposedly occurred.”

Cecilia didn't rely on remembered prayers. The drafting submission explicitly described composition from an already received register, citing the same handover batch as the noble volume. The proclamation dated the revelation before that batch. Both used the same old liturgical calendar; comparing their sequence didn't require conversion to today's year. A later copied submission alone, however, wouldn't establish the deception.

The custodian produced an independently preserved printer's settlement stub. Filed long ago with payment vouchers in a separate collection, it recorded receiving the corrected draft. Page count, last line, and revision matched. The printer's returned question was worse: if selection had been confirmed afterward, why print the revelation as preceding it?

The reply instructed the printer to follow the proclamation date and omit the circumstances of composition.

Adrian noticed a different seal on the stub. Did that establish an independent source?

Not by itself, the examiner answered. Different seals wouldn't exclude later copying from one source. The accession inventory showed the printer's settlement bundle separately filed as expenditure, surrounded by ordinary paper purchases. This sheet hadn't been inserted for today's discovery. The old inventory's last-line count matched it too.

“We don't believe it merely because it contradicts the oracle,” Cecilia said.

Then she requested the inconvenient next sentence. The drafting attachment specified separate review of the associated calamity observations. Revising proclamation language didn't invalidate those observations.

Adrian's stomach sank. He'd hoped these pages might also tear away the danger hanging over everyone.

“They could use a real danger to invent a false command,” Cecilia said. “Exposing the command won't make people who need care safe.”

He copied the sentence now, rather than promising to remember it later. Cecilia moved her cup away from his paper. She wasn't sparing the lie. She wouldn't replace it with another comforting omission.

For a while nobody spoke.

This wasn't a guess based on paper color, or merely two versions differing. The drafting dependency, independent printing receipt, and publicly asserted sequence together established how this particular oracle had been fabricated: choose the names first, then present that choice as heaven's earlier instruction.

Cecilia confined the conclusion to this proclamation. She refused to add that all faith was fraud. Equally, the enormity of that claim didn't justify calling this fraud a stylistic oversight.

A liturgical assistant shifted uneasily.

“Historical oversight rules require internal review of conclusions questioning a proclamation. Readers can't simply circulate them. If people lose trust, meal-hall donations suffer too.”

He produced an actual restriction still being cited, not a fresh arrest order. Whether it covered today's independent finding lacked a supplementary determination. Cecilia didn't tear it up. She asked the examiner to record the objection and its basis, and the receiving office to clarify what affected people could use. Protecting believers couldn't become a blanket thrown over every name.

“People outside will hear that the Temple lied,” the assistant said. “They won't wait to hear which page.”

“Then explain it understandably. Difficulty explaining isn't a reason to keep them believing the page.”

“What if the meal hall has less bread tomorrow?”

She was silent for a long time.

Adrian wanted to answer. Instead, he moved her water closer. He couldn't dismiss a cost she might really face as empty intimidation.

“I'll keep looking for donors who want people fed. I'll explain that this finding doesn't establish wrongdoing by patients, dishwashers, or every priest. But I won't make someone's life the price of maintaining our respectability with donors.”

She was frightened. Her fingers gathered her damp cuff and released it. The assistant finally agreed to submit the applicability question too. He didn't promise every restriction would disappear tonight.

A limited determination arrived before lunch. Affected parties and their representatives could receive extracts preserving sources and objections. Publishing the full text required separate review. The city didn't suddenly know; the false passage hadn't vanished from every service book. Cecilia requested that the unknown drafter, reviewer, and approval route remain explicit questions. The word Temple couldn't shield specific responsible people from further examination.

At the page's foot she found the circulation list. Besides the liturgical office, the proclamation accompanied a military transfer instruction. Troops could read it aloud during implementation; family objections weren't to alter the arrangement. The receiving category overlapped Viola's earlier request. They needed the original order. A marginal liturgical note couldn't prove military execution.

“I'll send her this.”

“Tell her yourself?” Adrian asked.

“After lunch. Otherwise every question will sound like someone hurrying me.”

He approved. They sat at a courtyard table, keeping lunch away from the old documents. Cecilia broke her bread and suddenly asked whether he'd badly wanted to tell her she'd done well.

“Yes.”

“You may now. Not that every believer will feel safe with me here.”

“You did well. I didn't feel safe at all.”

She laughed, then nodded seriously. “Neither did I.”

She'd once liked the image of a saint who could answer everything in one breath. When the woman asked whom to pray to, Cecilia had almost believed she needed to supply a new name before earning the right to dismantle a false sentence.

Adrian placed half an apple on her lunchbox lid, after asking whether she wanted it.

“She asked you where the bowl rack went afterward.”

“So I can at least give directions?”

“I got that wrong too. It's high praise.”

Cecilia smiled down at the apple, her eyes a little red. She liked that he noticed things so unimpressive. His kindness wasn't another betrothal. That afternoon she would return to her own office and arrange tomorrow's patient-record checks, rather than accompany him to wait for Elena.

Viola received the lead at their agreed ground-floor desk. Her left arm remained supported; an assistant unfolded the paper. She checked its destination category before requesting limited retrieval of the original military order. It wasn't the genuine recall she'd received on November 14. That order, and her reasoned delay acknowledgment, remained unchanged. This was a different register-transfer chain.

“I can't accept it as a military order because it looks like one,” Viola said.

“And we can't call it rumor merely because we'd rather it weren't real.”

Viola asked where Cecilia wanted tomorrow's examination to begin. Cecilia studied the circulation list.

“With whether the actual order forbids waiting for objections. I want to know what it tells a soldier to do when someone has already said no.”

“Not who signed it?”

“That too. But finding a name shouldn't mean we stop reading the rest.”

Viola asked her assistant to note the question separately. Adrian remembered his own earlier objection: preserved in a record, yet unable to stop the handover. He didn't offer that experience as evidence about this military instruction. He said he would like to hear the full answer tomorrow.

Viola nodded. Neither spoke loudly. Adrian thought their exchange harder than shouting accusations together.

As they were leaving, the gatekeeper handed Cecilia a clean cloth from the elderly woman. Cecilia had hung the borrowed drying cloth on the wrong rack that morning. The woman hadn't wanted her thinking it lost. No blessing or reproach accompanied it, only the kitchen's familiar blue stitching.

Cecilia held it, then turned back. Please convey her thanks. Nobody needed to reserve a place leading prayers for her tomorrow. She would come to work according to her own arrangements.

“Aren't you afraid she'll be disappointed?” Adrian asked.

“Yes. But she asked me a real question today. I don't want to answer with another false certainty.”

Later, military viewing was arranged for the next day, with the issuing order and receiving copy retrieved separately. Cecilia left the false oracle's extract on her desk beside the patient names still needing attention.

Before putting out the lamp, she said her usual prayer.

Not that a designated person should repay everyone's debt. She asked to keep hearing what people actually asked of her tomorrow.

A flour cart still rattled outside. Listening to the driver call to a companion, she folded the clean cloth to take back to the kitchen in the morning.
