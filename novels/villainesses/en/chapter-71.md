# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Three: The Knight Says She's Responsible for Me
### Chapter 71: The Twelve Sacks Stolen from an Order

On the morning of November 18, Adrian Reed almost failed to recognize Viola at the top of the stairs.

She wore a dark gray coat. There was no sword at her hip, though her right hand still moved aside for one when she turned. The passage was wide enough for two people, but she made room for him anyway.

“Morning,” he said.

“There are no patrol arrangements today.”

“I meant good morning.”

She blinked, then smiled down at the floor. “Good morning, then.”

Hooves sounded below. She glanced toward the window and stopped when she recognized the riders heading out for the change of watch. They took their orders from someone else now. She looked away and smoothed a cuff that hadn't needed smoothing.

Adrian didn't tell her everything would be fine. He pointed toward the dining room.

“The landlady's saved us hot porridge. Much longer and she'll come upstairs to take command.”

“She took command of me yesterday.”

“What for?”

“Leaving wet boots in the warmest spot by the fire.”

Apparently that order was harder to appeal than an evacuation route.

They hadn't finished breakfast when a town hall messenger arrived. The documents delivered that morning had been checked and sealed jointly by the military and the magistrate's office. The comparison would take place before noon. At last, the courier sent on the thirteenth had completed the journey over those mountain roads and back.

Viola put down her spoon, paused, then finished the last two mouthfuls.

Across the table, Elena watched and gave a small approving nod.

They skirted newly strung washing lines on the way to the town hall. Sheets from Willowford flapped in the wind. A pair of small boots hung from the lowest line, still damp. An old man carrying a wooden basin asked Viola whether he could go back for the rest of his pickled vegetables tomorrow.

She glanced toward the south gate before answering.

“I can't arrange an escort now. Please register with the town office. The duty officer will assess the road.”

Disappointed, the man shifted his basin to let them pass. After two steps, Viola turned back to show him which shelter held the registration desk. She couldn't get him home. She could still show him the right desk.

Elena drew up her shawl to avoid a drip from the washing and waited without hurrying her.

A military clerk in a traveling cloak stood in the town hall's small meeting room. He had come from the supply office that had issued the disputed supplement. Frozen mud clung to his soles. His escort set a thick, bound register on the table. One of the legs gave an ominous creak.

Tobin Quill moved his water out of the way.

“I thought you'd send a few sheets.”

“The major requested the original dispatch records.” The clerk unwound his scarf. “I don't intend to be accused of picking the flattering pages.”

Major Garrick Voss, standing by the window, made no reply.

Ada Finch supervised the opening and inventory. Viola sat at a side table with an adviser authorized to assist her defense. Adrian and Elena were still allowed to see only the passages concerning civilian transport and grain reserves. Other deployment destinations remained covered. Nobody lifted the covers as a courtesy.

First came the local copy of the November 4 supplementary order.

The cover was authentic. So was its reference number. That document had stopped Viola's grain wagons at Willowford and brought the charge of disobedience after her to Greymarch.

Its annex required all remaining grain to go to West Slope Depot, with the civilians accompanying the wagons directed across the high bridge.

Adrian looked at “all” and thought of the empty grain bins carried into temporary lodgings.

The visiting clerk opened the retained issuing counterpart. Its folio numbers ran in sequence, the binding thread remained unbroken, and the issuing officer's verification mark extended across two leaves. Ada had the town clerk record each join before asking the military clerk to read the relevant line.

“Reserve twelve sacks for Willowford, fifty pounds per sack. Military grain wagons and accompanying civilians are to avoid the high bridge and take the lower river road.”

The room went silent.

“Could it have been amended afterward?” Voss asked.

“There would be an amendment entry.” The clerk turned forward. “None under this number between the fourth and the receipt of your verification request. Two neighboring orders were amended. Both their original text and the changes remain here.”

The issuing officer's sealed reply was also opened before them. He confirmed the reserve and route instructions, stated that he had never authorized their cancellation, and made himself available for further questions from the review board. The visiting clerk passed the reply to Ada, then stepped aside for a second clerk to check his work independently.

Adrian resisted looking at Viola. He was studying a small notation at the top of the local annex.

“This says it arrived under the original cover. When the packet was received, did anyone record what the annex said?”

“There is an acknowledgment of delivery.”

“Please don't show the person checking it our version first. Let him read the acknowledgment.”

Ada glanced at him and turned the local copy facedown.

The acknowledgment had traveled back with the register. It was the portion the receiving office had returned through the courier on the day of delivery. Besides recording the number of pages, the recipient had copied the reserve quantity and route instructions, as required for orders affecting civilian grain allocations. Tobin located the corresponding entry in the town's separately kept receipt register and read out the time and acknowledgment number.

They matched.

The second clerk read, “Retain twelve sacks. Avoid the high bridge.”

Adrian's fingers loosened on the table edge.

“Then this isn't the annex issued on the fourth. And the words recorded at receipt aren't the words we have here now.”

The military clerk laid the retained counterpart, acknowledgment, and local annex side by side. He checked the endorsements, number, and page sequence again before speaking.

“This annex was substituted after issuance. Its present text has appropriated the original order's reference number and cover.”

Viola said nothing at first.

Her hands rested firmly on her knees. She looked as though she were waiting for the rest of the sentence. At last she asked, “The original really instructed us to leave the grain?”

“Twelve sacks. Six hundred pounds.”

She nodded slowly. Adrian remembered their first meeting, when she had preferred an empty space to a convenient estimate of something she hadn't seen.

Now there was a verified quantity against which they could begin checking that gap.

“I thought someone above us had decided the villagers could manage on their own,” she said quietly.

Voss's fingers moved.

“Why wasn't the complete acknowledgment retrieved?” he asked the local receiving clerk.

The young man's face went pale. “We checked the cover number when the packet was filed. Whenever anyone requested it afterward, we provided the papers bound together here.”

“Write down how it was filed,” Voss said without raising his voice. “Check the duty register for anything you can't remember. Don't fill in an account for anyone.”

The clerk looked at the major before taking up his pen. His hand shook so badly that the first stroke missed the paper. Tobin gave him a fresh sheet and moved the ink closer.

The visiting clerk examined the physical annex next. It had two sets of sewing holes, only the newer one aligning with the cover. Held to the light, its watermark also differed from the original batch. He drew each feature, but pointed first to the three written records.

“Paper can be replaced. Pages can be resewn. Neither would settle this alone. The contradiction between the retained issue, the acknowledgment, and the local document establishes the substitution.”

Tobin lowered his injured hand onto its cushion. A young clerk offered to sign for him. He shook his head and slowly entered his witness signature with his sound hand.

Someone brought hot water to the door. Viola rose to take the kettle, then stopped and asked the attendant to set it on the empty table. She did not take charge of the urgent military message being passed down the corridor.

That brought home her suspension more sharply than the empty sword belt had.

Voss noticed too.

He did not return her weapon.

“This forged annex can no longer be used as an authentic order,” he said. “Preserve the military file, the issuing register, and the acknowledgment. I'll authorize an inquiry into every stage between dispatch and filing.”

“Including your own receiving office?” Ada asked.

“Including it.”

The answer landed heavily.

Adrian passed the comparison sheet from the twelfth to the town clerk. The six-hundred-pound consignment had an original reserve allocation. Yet the summary counted it once as a depot delivery and again as a separate transfer whose destination was unknown.

“Start by comparing them as the same consignment,” he said. “They can't keep adding the entries together and telling the village it lost two loads.”

Voss studied the sheet, his brow deepening. “Put it before the review board. The other shortages still require investigation.”

He turned to Viola.

“I signed the suspension on the seventeenth. That order wasn't substituted. The delayed redirect on the fourteenth, and your subsequent decisions, remain under review. Today's findings will go before the board tomorrow. Your provisional patrol command is not restored.”

“I understand.”

Her voice was as controlled as ever, but it no longer sounded as empty as it had that morning.

Before they adjourned, the military clerk confirmed that the regional fort had sent Colonel Helen Ward to preside. She had left her station the day before and was expected in Greymarch that evening. Neither of the two military board members had served in the local dispatch chain. The town would supply separate witnesses for the evidence custody arrangements.

Only outside did Adrian discover that he'd crumpled his notes. A tightness lingered in his chest. Following the doctor's advice, he stopped and sat on a sheltered bench instead of following the people carrying the registers away.

Elena sat beside him and loosened his scarf with her uninjured hand.

“You're allowed to be happy today. You don't have to stand up to do it.”

He laughed once, then gave the urge to cough time to subside.

Viola approached with an empty cup. She looked from the two of them to the tea table at the end of the passage.

“I'd like to arrange—”

She stopped.

Adrian waited without supplying the rest.

“I'd like to ask you to have tea with me,” she tried again. “For a little while. Another day, if you're tired.”

It had plainly cost her more effort than ordering riders to hold a road. She didn't even consult a clock afterward. Her gray-green eyes stayed on his face.

“I can sit for ten minutes or so,” Adrian said. “Weak tea, please.”

“I'll ask.”

Two steps away, she turned back. “Lady Elena, would you like some?”

“I'm going to the inn for his medicine in a moment. Save me half a cup.”

Viola nodded and went in search of the pot.

Elena watched her go before turning to Adrian.

“I'm a little jealous.”

He sat straighter.

“Don't panic. I'm not asking you to return the tea you agreed to.” A small smile touched her mouth. “I still want my place beside you when we read tonight.”

“I'll save it. I'd like you there.”

This time, she looked away first.

When the tea came, Viola didn't choose the seat facing the door. She took one in a patch of sunlight and set down her cup. Her hands no longer needed to rest on a sword or hold a stack of orders in place.

They talked about the inn's cat, which was forever getting into the kitchen. Adrian asked whether she had ever managed to stop it.

“It has never listened to me once.”

“What have you tried?”

“Shutting the door. It came through the window. When I shut the window, it followed the landlady in.”

Adrian laughed, felt the pull in his chest, and stopped. Viola reached for his water, saw that he already had his cup, and drew her hand back.

“You can laugh,” he told her. “Just don't say anything too funny for the moment.”

“I wasn't trying to tell a joke.”

“That's the problem.”

She looked down at her tea without quite hiding her smile. Her braid was looser today, a strand of brown hair falling beside her cheek. With no helmet to put on, she seemed unused to that small freedom.

“Apparently you weren't all-powerful even before the suspension,” Adrian said.

She laughed. Halfway through, moisture brightened the corners of her eyes. She lowered her face to her cup and took a moment.

Adrian didn't move his chair away. He didn't reach to wipe her eyes either.

Inside, people were still reading the sentences that had changed the course of wagons and villagers. Outside, sunlight slowly crossed the empty space at her hip.

After a while, she said, “Tomorrow, I want to explain the things I actually did.”

“We'll listen.”

Viola held her warm cup.

For once, she didn't ask whether they would stay until the very end.
