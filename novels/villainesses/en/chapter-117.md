# Chapter 117: The Warning Wasn't Written for the Future

On December 25, the proprietor added a dish of honeyed peel to every breakfast table.

Luna Ashcroft asked whether the room rate had increased. It hadn't. The kitchen had leftovers from sweet bread, and he was tired of hearing arguments over the last piece.

“He's thoroughly researched us,” Cecilia Saintclair said earnestly.

Adrian Reed served himself. Elena Rosenberg pushed another piece onto his plate. When he looked up, she pointed her fork toward the page he'd left open last night.

“The reader gets extra.”

“I thought it was bribery.”

“Will you keep reading?”

He ate it. “Yes.”

Luna considered calling love expensive. The peel was barely a knuckle long; she couldn't find a suitable price. Viola Blackwood cut her tough bread for her, then ate her own breakfast, without making care for others a queue that never reached herself.

As they left, the tribunal recorder was closing his umbrella outside. He brought the receipt for processing yesterday's maintenance documents and invited them to the reading room. Dry separation had made the pages safe to turn. Fragile tears remained untouched. No magical revealing had forced out missing words.

When the folder opened, Adrian noticed the heavy underline before the signature.

If discharge is incomplete, do not use repeated readouts to recover missing text.

The line beneath said that once a load-limiting component was damaged, a bypass connection must not be used in its place. Both circuits had to be isolated first, before anyone examined the residual load. Several words after that sentence had been lost to damp. The restoration report marked those words unconfirmed; the copy supplied nothing.

Luna straightened as she studied the reference to bypasses.

“This is a specific maintenance warning for the old device. We can't simply make it a rule for every contractual instrument. But it distinguishes stopping the main source from ending return flow.”

Adrian remembered the main lever suspended there. How easily people called stopping an ending, particularly when they had exhausted themselves.

The recorder produced another document. A dispatch ledger stored with the eastern warehouse's equipment recorded a maintenance warning forwarded four years earlier under the name of the church recorder Evelyn Vale. Its registration number matched the old number on the appendix. The signature at the foot read E. Vale.

Elena's fingers stopped on her cup handle.

Adrian recognized the name too. Leads in her earlier case had pointed toward Evelyn, who had subsequently been temporarily unreachable. This wasn't a newly delivered letter. The maintenance appendix had lain there long before their arrival.

“Did she write it?” Elena asked.

The recorder didn't assent. The custodian supplied another traceable forwarding receipt from the same ledger. Final flourishes and several joined strokes looked similar, and the old numbers formed a connected record. That linked the warning to records registered under Evelyn's name. It wasn't handwriting authentication, nor proof that she had composed its technical content.

“She may only have forwarded it,” Cecilia said.

“Yes. Forwarding is the action recorded.”

Luna withdrew her hand from the page. “Please preserve that distinction.”

Elena studied the underline. Adrian understood her reluctance. A name that had cast a shadow over the coldest part of her life now shared a page with a potentially lifesaving warning. It resisted easy classification.

“Why did she write it then?” Elena asked quietly.

“We don't know.”

“I don't like that answer.”

“Neither do I.”

She looked at him, then slowly released the handle. She didn't demand the name be struck out. Nor did one warning forgive the earlier suspicions.

The church advocate argued that records originating in the church's old equipment office should return to internal church interpretation, lest outsiders exaggerate them. Before the academy representative could protest, Luna gently pushed the folder into the table's center.

“I agree we shouldn't exaggerate. Then preserve its provenance, and let everyone see the words before anyone expands them.”

Pain beneath her dressing slowed her quiet speech, making each sentence distinct.

“If we'd only called it an old flow-stabilizing base yesterday, we might never have sought this paper. We can't rename it again because the warning is inconvenient.”

The advocate stressed ownership. The recorder reminded him that the attachments were under tribunal-authorized review. The device remained in its warehouse. Any transfer required another decision. One paper neither abolished ownership nor obliged everyone who'd seen it to pretend otherwise.

Adrian requested separate preservation of the yellow-handle field drawing and the warning. Later readers mustn't assume the tower's emergency release had been copied from that device. Nor could the old warning establish that Luna's accident design had been adequate.

Luna agreed. She personally confirmed she'd never previously seen either the limiter itself or this appendix.

Cecilia requested another reading of the heading. It said maintenance forwarding. It contained neither Adrian's name nor Luna's, and no date anticipating their arrival.

“It isn't a letter waiting specifically for us,” she said.

Adrian recognized his own temptation. Since the warning helped explain their injuries, perhaps everything revolved around them. Perhaps someone had known they'd enter that warehouse.

But the paper concerned a person who'd needed to repair something then, equipment that had needed withdrawal then, and a precaution that hadn't subsequently accompanied the old tables to every reader.

“Please check who received it at the time,” he asked. “Don't put me there first.”

The recorder added a request to verify distribution. That was a question they could actually continue investigating. It was far smaller, and much more dependable, than wondering whether Evelyn had reached out to him from four years in the past.

“The old tables supplied the lead. Yesterday's inspection identified the corresponding object,” Luna said. “Our mistakes don't become someone else's simply because someone warned other people four years ago.”

The recorder wrote that down.

Before noon, Elena lingered beneath the reading room's high window, watching a flower seller discard frozen stems. Adrian stopped beside her without immediately asking whether she was all right.

“If she really wanted to warn people, why didn't she leave something clearer?”

“Perhaps it was clear enough to whoever repaired that equipment. Perhaps we haven't seen everything.”

“Perhaps she never meant to help anyone.”

“Also possible.”

Elena exhaled. “You're not very obliging today.”

“I can buy you a flower. I can't invent her motive.”

She turned and finally smiled. “Not those flowers. They're frozen.”

They went outside together. They bought no flowers, only looked awhile before choosing a small bag of roasted chestnuts. The flower seller recommended a sweeter batch at the neighboring stall. Elena thanked him and took Adrian there, without insisting on an unwanted purchase for appearances.

Walking back, she placed a peeled chestnut in his palm.

“Nothing to prove today. I simply wanted to give you this.”

He tasted it seriously and agreed it was sweet. The invisible chill of the morning finally eased from her shoulders.

At the guesthouse, Tessa Quinn was checking supplementary records with the night watchman. Last night's inspection had found the street-end address to be an empty short-term rental. The owner had seen only whoever paid. Details remained unverified; there was no sufficient basis to identify whoever was behind it. The messenger's copper coins and hiring location were recorded. Further inquiries would not arrive on anonymous notes.

Tessa didn't insist on naming an enemy to satisfy everyone. She only asked whether she could return to tower work after the review as planned.

“That depends on the decision,” Luna said. “You needn't stay today to praise me.”

“I wasn't praising you.”

Luna blinked, then laughed. “Your remark about disconnecting the main source being enough made me want to crawl under the table.”

“I used to think that too. And if you crawl under there, I'll have to pull you out.”

Tessa put away her maintenance copies. The original provenance of those copies, the testimony she had given this time, and the incident involving the threats had all been filed separately. At last, she no longer had to bear their weight alone, clutching a sheaf of papers until daylight.

That afternoon, the tribunal received the final supplementary statements. Adrian clarified the limits of his observations of the yellow handle and old diagram. Cecilia completed citations distinguishing old records from present acceptance of vows. Viola confirmed only escort and witness events, signing no technical conclusions.

Luna wrote slowest. Proposed external mechanical regulation remained a design for future reassessment, not grounds for immediate reopening. Any resumed research must first establish how to handle the residual side circuit after stopping the main source. No brave person could stand inside it as a guarantee for the data.

Finishing, she put down her pen and inspected her ink-stained left fingertips.

“My finest spell lately is writing on my hand.”

Adrian offered a damp cloth and asked whether she wanted help. She shook her head. With the damp cloth, she slowly wiped the ink away herself. By the time she had nearly finished, one tiny dot remained on her finger.

“Does that look like a star?”

“Ink.”

“Could you cooperate with my romantic imagination occasionally?”

“A very honest star.”

Luna turned away, laughing hard enough to nearly bump her right hand. Elena warned her to keep still, without entirely hiding her own smile. Cecilia observed that stars didn't interfere with dinner. Viola had already gone to ask whether the kitchen had hot porridge.

At dusk, the registry delivered formal notice. All supplementary materials had arrived. A scope review and directions would follow on the morning of the twenty-sixth, specifying whether any seals would be lifted and which restrictions remained. Nobody congratulated Luna prematurely. Nobody packed to return to the tower that night.

“What do you think will happen tomorrow?” Luna asked Adrian.

“I hope ordinary doors can open and dangerous things stay shut.”

“That doesn't sound like a glorious victory.”

“Returning to your desk and working without injuring your hands would be good too.”

She looked down at the notice. After a moment, she said, “It would.”

That evening, they finally finished the travelogue's chapter together. The captain hadn't recovered the lost years. He'd learned to deliver his next letter to the right person. Luna suddenly asked whether they could visit the bookshop before leaving East Ridge.

“What do you want?” Elena asked.

“A notebook with a lock.”

Cecilia looked up. Viola lowered her cup. Adrian stopped turning the page.

Luna surveyed them, then laughed.

“For my own affairs. Why are you nervous?”

Adrian laid the book flat. He was only worried she'd start recording his heartbeat on page one again. Propping her face on her left hand, Luna considered this seriously. She promised at least to ask whether he wanted to read it.

“Shouldn't you first ask whether he wants to be written about?” Elena said.

“Must I submit an application whenever I think of him too?”

Cecilia quietly said no. Viola nodded, her ears red, then realized how obvious she'd been and hastily raised her cup.

Firelight flickered in Luna’s eyes, soft and warm. East Ridge remained cold beyond the windows. The warning's author was still beyond their reach, and the research tower's seals remained intact.

But that night, nobody tried reading dangerous light once more to buy a certain answer for tomorrow.
