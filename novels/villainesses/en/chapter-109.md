# Chapter 109: He Did Not Wait for the Witch to Finish

The yellow lever resisted more than it had without power.

Adrian pressed it fully with his right hand. A ceramic neck cracked beneath the enclosure. A gray isolation plate dropped, shutting D-17, the control ring, and the last uncertain light from view.

“Assembly discarded!” he called.

The administrator locked the dissipation box. Inside came a scattering hiss, like sand falling into a hot pan, then rapidly diminishing.

Luna's hand came free.

She stumbled backward. Adrian did not cross the line where the residual indicator still shone. He went around the shield's open end, supported her left elbow and upper back, and helped her to a chair two steps from the bench. Her right hand hung before her. A patch beside her palm was red; her fingertips trembled.

“Sit.”

“I can—”

“Sit first.”

She did. Her pallor tightened his chest. Cecilia had already called for Hensley outside; Elena moved a footstool to clear access. Nobody opened the box to find out how much they had lost.

Hensley checked Luna's breathing and awareness, then asked what hurt. After staring at her hand, Luna reported burning along the palm. Thumb and forefinger felt strangely dulled, as though she had just taken them out of cold water.

“No magic now,” the doctor said.

“I wasn't going to.”

“Good. Not a little to prove you still can, either.”

Luna looked away from her fingertips.

Adrian released her elbow and moved where the doctor indicated. It was harder than he expected. He wanted to hold something warm and know she was still here. But Hensley needed an unobstructed view, and Luna needed to answer for herself.

He only removed an empty glass so her injured hand wouldn't strike it.

The main supply was disconnected. Residual energy was still falling. Tessa and the administrator waited outside the enclosure until every indicator settled, then attached an out-of-service seal. The clerk did not invent someone charging into magical fire. He recorded the sequence: stop, main disconnection, persistent return flow, mechanical discard. Elena gave the times. Her voice shook slightly on the last number.

This loss could not be repaired into an ordinary conclusion.

When D-17 dropped into the dissipation box, its unfixed layer image was destroyed. The control ring's ceramic neck could not simply be rejoined. Yesterday's independent drawings and observations survived. C-17, the original gray-silver interface, had never been on this bench; its distant cabinet seal was intact. But the decay sequence they meant to examine had vanished with that crack.

Luna closed her eyes.

“That line—”

“Tell me first whether the numb area is spreading,” Hensley interrupted.

Luna opened her eyes and answered the doctor's questions as she used an ordinary wooden probe.

The reddened area did not expand during the initial checks. Mild fingertip numbness persisted. Hensley treated the small injury beside her palm and applied a loose dressing. Luna was to remain nearby for observation. No magic today. Unbroken skin on her fingers was not proof that they had recovered. Any change in numbness, pain, or breathing meant calling the doctor immediately.

“Can I hold a cup?” Luna asked first.

“With your left hand. Not a full one.”

“Your expectations have suddenly become wonderfully achievable.”

Hensley waited until she genuinely held it securely before briefing the others.

Luna walked slowly out of the workshop. Nobody carried her through the tower for the sake of a graceful gesture. Adrian stayed on her left, ready to help. When she said the stairs felt unsteady, he had her rest on the landing bench.

“Do I look dreadful?”

“Your own desk nearly kept you.”

“Naturally. I'm very difficult to let go of.”

He watched her. The joke stopped there. Her eyes lowered.

Someone downstairs closed a window. Without the wind, her breathing seemed louder. Adrian remembered her unguarded face before he pulled the yellow lever. He could not produce the easy answer she wanted.

“You don't have to make me laugh yet.”

Her left hand curled on her knee.

“I don't know what else to say.”

“You can drink first.”

She nodded and took a sip. The cup clicked faintly against her teeth.

Elena brought the coat Luna had left behind and draped it over her shoulders. Gathering the collar with her left hand, Luna noticed the excessively secure cuff button and gave a small laugh.

“At least it's still here.”

“It wasn't operating the control ring,” Elena said. “Don't take responsibility for everything.”

“Should you be the one telling me that?”

“Yes. Once you're settled, I'll also ask everything that needs asking.”

Luna looked up at her and nodded.

Lunch came to the guesthouse sitting room. There was no celebration. The soup was warm rather than hot; the bread was cut into manageable pieces. The kitchen added orange marmalade originally intended for afternoon tea.

The empty blue-ribbon basket still stood by the door.

Adrian glanced at it. Luna noticed.

“The pastry shop will think I forgot again.”

“We can send a message with the delivery cart. Collect them another day.”

“The administrator's busy enough.”

“I'll ask the driver.”

She agreed, then called him back.

“Don't say I was struck down by magic. She'll send an entire jar of restorative broth.”

“I'll just say you can't come today.”

“Thank you.”

There was no flirtation around it. Adrian went outside to arrange a very ordinary postponement. The driver confirmed that he was only taking a message, not collecting or paying for the order, and slipped the note beside his delivery list.

When Adrian returned, Luna was attempting to butter bread left-handed. The butter had disregarded her instructions and remained attached to the back of the knife.

Cecilia offered help.

“I'm still negotiating.”

“It appears to have maintained its position long enough.”

The saint waited for agreement, then took the knife. Luna gave the first slice to Adrian. She did not call it payment or suggest eating it would prevent him from being angry.

He accepted but didn't immediately bite.

“I was frightened,” he said.

The room grew quiet. Luna's smile faded.

“I knew the handle would destroy the record. I was afraid I'd hesitate, wondering whether we could wait a little longer.”

“You didn't hesitate.”

“I know. I wanted you to know I'm not someone who never does.”

Her left hand tightened around her cup. She did not say heroes must never hesitate, or that trusting her should remove fear.

“You chose me.”

“I chose to stop. You were the person at the ring today. If it had been someone else, I hope I'd have done the same.”

She lowered her gaze. The sudden brightness in her eyes changed rather than disappeared.

“That's a little better than saying you'd only save me.”

“Only a little?”

“Any more and I won't know what to say.”

Elena set down her spoon.

“Your hands can shake now,” she told Adrian softly.

He discovered that the fingertips holding his bread were indeed unsteady. He lowered his hand to the table. Sitting beside him, Elena didn't conceal the reaction. She placed her hand nearby and waited until he reached for it.

He held her fingers, avoiding the tender skin of her palm.

Viola returned near the end of lunch. Hearing what happened, she briefly pressed her right hand against the sword beneath her cloak.

She did not draw it or demand that they should have waited.

“I missed it.”

“You went to your appointment,” Adrian said.

She looked at Luna's dressing and moved her hand away. Her medical instructions remained in their envelope: healing was progressing, but no weight through the left arm or prolonged standing; short ordinary walks were permitted, with return to duty assessed separately. Someone else's accident did not invalidate those restrictions.

“If you'd been here, you'd have been outside,” Luna said. “Like yesterday.”

“I know.”

Viola chose a backed chair and opened her biscuit box. None were covered in chalk. She distributed them, leaving Luna an easily broken one.

“When you can use your hand, I could teach you to sew something else.”

“What this time?”

“A pocket sleeve to keep your pen in place.”

Luna laughed, then inhaled sharply as her injured hand moved without thinking. Viola stopped teasing and brought the plate within easy reach of her left hand.

That afternoon, Luna requested a preliminary briefing. Hensley allowed fifteen minutes in the sitting room, followed by rest. Tessa brought an exterior diagram, not the sealed wreckage.

They could rule out failure to disconnect the main supply: independent witnesses had seen both the removed power cell and separated contacts. Isolation that worked with teaching plates had not necessarily worked against D-17's reverse decay. They could describe unexpected coupling on the control side. Identifying where it crossed the isolation required safe disassembly.

“Don't write that I refused to let go,” Luna said.

“I saw you release it,” Adrian answered.

She turned toward him.

“Thank you. This time was different.”

“I know.”

On the thirteenth she had wanted to prolong a reading. Today she had called stop herself. Those facts could not become the same mistake merely because that made an easier story. Her risk assessment and isolation design still required scrutiny. She did not owe an admission to disobedience that had not happened.

Elena asked the clerk to preserve the original witness accounts separately, rather than copying them into one agreeable version. Tessa put untested explanations on an appendix. Unfamiliar failure did not automatically mean sabotage.

After listening, Luna asked for the gull drawing.

“In your coat pocket,” Elena said.

She extracted it awkwardly. The paper was creased. The captain still rode his gull, hat threatening to plunge into the sea.

Looking at something with no academic value, Adrian recovered part of the answer he'd wanted that morning.

“When you're better, you could give him a boat.”

“Insufficiently steady in the air?”

“I'd like him to have somewhere to sit and eat.”

She smoothed the paper slowly with her left hand. She did not reach for a pen.

By the evening check, the injury remained limited. The fingertip numbness had eased but had not vanished. Hensley required another examination in the morning and forbade solitary nighttime attempts at control patterns. The administrator locked the workshop. The report was preserved for the next day's official post. Nobody had died; that was not a reason to hide it in a maintenance book.

Adrian walked into the courtyard with Elena. Their bookshop carriage had been canceled. Thin snow lay gray underfoot.

“I haven't forgotten the next volume.”

“I know.”

“Or the better thing I meant to say.”

She waited.

“I like that you remember what I want to eat. And that you let me be frightened. You didn't hurry me into pretending I was fine.”

Her eyes grew bright, though some familiar pride remained.

“Better than saying my handwriting beats a map.”

He laughed and gently untangled the end of her scarf from the railing. She stepped closer and rested her forehead against his shoulder. Neither hurried to add anything.

Upstairs, Luna sat beside her bed and touched the folded gull drawing with her uninjured hand. Her right palm hurt. No fresh mystery waited outside the window for her to seize.

She laid the paper by her cup. Someone at the door asked what she wanted for breakfast.

This time, she did not say anything would do.

“Marmalade,” she said. “And soft bread.”
