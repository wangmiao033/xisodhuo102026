# Chapter 283: The Saint Gives Away Her Key

On May 11, Cecilia discovered that her complaints box couldn't be opened.

The carpenter had followed her drawing exactly. Its narrow slot admitted letters but prevented prying fingers. Unfortunately, reaching the lock on its side required removing the entire box from the wall.

Crouching beneath it, Adrian asked whether every complainant would receive a complimentary carpenter.

“I wanted to keep other people from taking the letters.”

“You've certainly kept yourself out.”

Suppressing a laugh, she stopped the second nail. They hadn't begun collecting anything; taking down an empty box would lose nobody's words. What troubled her was the notice she'd written herself: If you need help, ask Cecilia.

“What if I'm away?”

Adrian didn't promise that the saint would always return. He handed her the notice and waited while she crossed out the line.

The existing inquiry office had lent them this room near the courtyard. Visitors needn't pass through the meal queue. Yesterday she'd met the reception supervisor and a housing-review officer. Today they brought the shifts they could actually spare. Her proposed daily opening became three afternoons a week, with sealed submissions accepted at the original window otherwise.

A clerk observed that an obligatory interview might cost someone half a day's wages. Another wondered whether a visitor might meet their employer outside.

Cecilia put her attractive opening notice aside and listened.

They weren't founding an office with jurisdiction over the kingdom. The hearing authority had approved an independent reception group for concerns about voluntary-sharing proposals and associated housing pressure. Criminal threats went to existing judicial officers; accommodation disputes went to authorized reviewers. The public register listed issue categories, receipt dates, referrals, and responses. Names and addresses remained sealed separately. Two staff uninvolved in the original allocation registered the envelopes; submitters received matching numbered receipts.

Complaints concerning those staff could instead go to another judicial reception desk without their permission.

“Who checks that you actually send anything?” Agnes asked.

The miller had arrived early, flour still clinging to her coat. She hadn't brought residents' names to the public table, only her earlier questions about housing conditions.

The supervisor showed her the agreed checking times. Each week, a separate recorder would compare dispatch and receipt entries. Unanswered cases remained visibly unanswered. “Being considered” couldn't close them. People could use their receipts to ask about their own cases without publicly retelling their experiences.

Agnes studied the arrangement. “What if someone has to leave tomorrow night?”

The room fell quiet. A beautifully organized weekly timetable couldn't speak before tomorrow's door closed.

The housing officer added his actual duty location to the urgent-referral instructions. With a concrete notice to leave, he could seek a temporary stay pending examination. A letter alone couldn't guarantee permanent accommodation. Reception staff must contact the duty officer immediately, using the judicial duty desk if he was unavailable. Threats to safety likewise belonged in existing emergency channels, not in a wooden box followed by a patient journey home.

“May I take that section back?”

“Yes,” Cecilia said. “You can also explain that we haven't guaranteed anyone a new room.”

“They know houses don't grow out of paper.” Agnes drew the notice closer. “They're afraid the paper will say they've already been given one.”

Adrian sat down to make her a copy. She pointed to the public register's first column. Why must someone state whether they supported or opposed the new proposal?

He recognized the boxes. Yesterday he'd borrowed an opinion-summary form and left its original question intact.

He replaced the blank sheet. Reporting a housing condition shouldn't require joining a side. He kept the mistaken version with the reason for correction rather than asking Agnes to excuse how busy they were.

Before noon, the supervisor approved a trial through the end of the month. The room, secure cabinet, and named staff were present; continuation would be assessed afterward.

Before signing, a Temple administrator demanded publication of every complaint. Only complete openness, he argued, could prevent damaging material disappearing behind confidentiality again. Cecilia nearly agreed. Agnes drew her paper closer to her chest.

“If I report someone trading rooms for signatures, the neighbors may guess who came here without seeing a name.”

They adjusted location descriptions to identify the responsible office without pointing to individual households. Delays could be public; necessary responsibility information required independent verification. Unexamined allegations wouldn't become judgments posted on a door. Those complained against needed enough detail to answer, not irrelevant addresses. If an investigation couldn't proceed while concealing an identity, staff must explain that to the person concerned rather than secretly choosing for them.

The administrator frowned. It hardly sounded transparent.

Cecilia turned the register toward him and asked him to find the matter he wished to question.

In the demonstration row, he found the dispatch date, then tapped the empty receipt column.

“That,” she said, “is something people should be able to see.”

He considered, left his observations, and stopped demanding Agnes's sealed submission. Oversight began with the right to question an unfilled space, not with everyone knowing everyone else's secrets.

Cecilia had originally listed herself as a custodian. She now moved her name to the advisers' column.

“You understand these problems best,” a helper protested.

“Someone may also need to complain about me.”

She gave the duty clerk her tested key. It made a small sound on the table. The carpenter rebuilt the box with a front-opening door and placed it away from waiting visitors' scrutiny. A conversation room offered another way to submit concerns.

Agnes delivered the first actual envelope, showing her authorization to act for its writer. The clerk issued a receipt and privately asked whether there was a definite removal date. Cecilia stayed outside without even asking who the person was.

Adrian watched her withdraw her hand from the doorframe.

“Want to go in?”

“I want to know whether they're frightened.”

“They can decide whether to tell you.”

“I know.” She exhaled. “Knowing and not opening the door are different things.”

Agnes emerged with a stamped receipt. Her concern had reached a reviewer independent of the original allocation; no housing arrangement had instantly changed. At least she had something to take back, she said, putting the paper inside her coat. Instead of praising the saint, she checked the next opening time on her way down the steps.

Lunch arrived with far too much mustard on Cecilia's portion. Her first bite brought tears, though she maintained that the kitchen simply had firm convictions.

Adrian offered water. “Would you like independent review?”

“I'd like you not to laugh.”

“Full compliance may prove difficult.”

She laughed too, dabbing her eyes. These weren't tears shed for every suffering visitor. They belonged to an excessively spicy lunch. Complaining about that felt easier than being eternally gentle.

Would he like a taste? He declined. For once, he'd correctly understood the risk disclosure. She scraped the remaining mustard aside without asking three more times in the name of sharing.

That afternoon, the staff practiced exchanging duties. One carried a submission to another office. Left alone, the other couldn't conduct a private interview while supervising the cabinet and waiting visitors. Two names on paper didn't always supply two available pairs of hands.

The supervisor postponed longer interviews until a third worker could attend tomorrow. Urgent submissions still went to the judicial duty desk. Cecilia didn't quietly replace the missing person. She could sit in this particular room for this particular afternoon, but her presence wouldn't make the arrangement adequately staffed. Staying today would disguise a shortage she couldn't fill every day.

A laundry worker heard about rescheduling and angrily turned away. She'd exchanged shifts to come; tomorrow might be impossible. Cecilia caught up at the courtyard and asked whether she'd hear the available alternatives. The woman stopped but wouldn't immediately explain her problem.

Eventually she chose to dictate a sealed submission at the original window, have another person read it back, and take a receipt. She didn't want Cecilia beside her. The saint remained outside until her own promised working hours ended.

“I don't think she likes me today.”

Adrian collected the empty dishes. “Today she can dislike you and still submit her complaint.”

Cecilia studied the closed door and nodded slowly. The public register's first entry said referred; the second awaited acknowledgment. Nobody's name had purchased applause. Two concerns that might never have been voiced in the meal queue now had destinations.

Toward evening, they brought the approved short explanation to Luna's room. She avoided small print and listened while her assistant read. At the provision for custodians to recuse themselves, she asked whether people could question her experiment records that way too.

“If you open them to review, you can't choose only flattering questions,” Adrian said.

“I'd planned a chair specifically for compliments.”

“Give it a cushion. There may be a wait.”

She raised an eyebrow and considered throwing her little pillow with her left hand. Then she glanced at her supported right palm and asked the assistant to put it behind her waist instead. A joke didn't require moving the hand that needed rest.

Cecilia went to arrange the next visit with the caregiver. Adrian waited near the door until Luna called him back. Did he still have the travel book?

Yes. He'd found a passage featuring neither ships nor bridge repairs.

“A rare discovery. Save it for me. Not today.”

He placed it on the shelf she indicated. Before he could leave, she spoke again.

“I used to think letting me study your scar meant you trusted me more than anyone.”

“I trusted you with the part we'd agreed upon.”

“I understand now.” She looked at her bandaged palm. “I like you seeing my work. But today, you heard 'not now' and put the book down. That made me happier than being told I'm clever.”

He didn't turn her admission into another joke. “Being injured isn't what gives you that right. You can decline when you're well too.”

Her slow smile shifted something at the corners of her eyes.

“You do ruin experimental conditions.”

“You aren't in an experiment today.”

This time she didn't cover the words with flirtation. She asked him to leave the door ajar for the evening air, then said goodbye.

In the courtyard, Viola was reading the reception office's account of the day. The laundry worker could reach another window in daylight. Two people with limited mobility couldn't manage even that journey. Staff needed help with access and delivering receipts, without opening correspondence or treating accompaniment as custody.

Viola looked up. “Tomorrow I'll speak to the people who actually manage this street. Ask what they need before I give myself a regiment.”

Adrian nodded. Inside, the rebuilt box closed softly.

The saint hadn't gained more keys today. Giving one away had finally allowed other people's words to reach their destination without first passing through her kindness.
