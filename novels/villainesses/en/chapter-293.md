# Chapter 293: Look at Me First

On May 29, Adrian lost the words he'd prepared.

No enemy had stolen them. He'd left the page beside his breakfast plate. Martha, assuming it was scrap, had put it beneath a dripping flowerpot. By the time he found it, the third sentence about their future had blossomed into a brown stain.

“An important document?” she asked.

“I'd hoped it wouldn't be a document.”

She examined the surviving opening. “Then don't begin with whereas.”

He accepted that correction without independent review.

Meanwhile, Elena sat in her father's study. Between them lay her May 10 statement and the letter she hadn't withdrawn. Duke Edmund had ordered the almond biscuits she'd loved as a child. He hadn't touched one.

“You know what they'll say about our family.”

“Yes. Some of it will be unfair. Some we'll need to answer.”

His hand stopped beside his cup. “You could have asked me first.”

“I asked where the old accounts should go. You told me to wait.”

Outside, someone clipped branches. Her father hadn't become a stranger or a monster. That made this harder. He'd hired lawyers and protected witnesses when she was accused. He also wanted certain inconvenient matters to remain within the family.

Would she keep the letter if people called it a daughter's accusation against her father?

“I hope you'll hear what your daughter is asking. I can't erase what happened to make you listen.”

Her voice tightened. He broke a biscuit and returned both halves to the plate.

“You used to hate being told you resembled me.”

“I still don't always enjoy it.”

They exchanged a brief smile. It didn't settle the accounts. It let their sentences be something other than final words.

He agreed that the representative should continue providing documents under the lawful requests received. He wouldn't ask Elena to withdraw her statement for him. Records and further questions would establish who had authorized particular spending. He didn't promise a family without secrets. She didn't pronounce him innocent.

As she rose, he asked about Adrian.

“He didn't come?”

“This was something I needed to discuss with you.”

“I meant dinner.”

Her ears warmed. “Not tonight. We have plans.”

The duke looked at her and sighed.

“At last. Now I know which question makes you lose that courtroom expression.”

She took two almond biscuits for the journey. He wasn't forbidding her plans, but that didn't turn those plans into a permission he'd bestowed. She could leave carrying something he'd offered her without handing back responsibility for the choice she'd made.

Adrian received word that she'd be half an hour late while speaking to a clerk. Could he return that evening to check a catalogue? Only briefly. He checked the actual handover, confirmed that a qualified replacement had everything needed, and directed the clerk there. The man looked surprised, not offended. Apparently the world didn't always collapse when Adrian left.

Waiting on the porch, he read a page of the travel book. Luna's canceled reading time didn't become a debt to anyone else. The traveler got lost in a small town and eventually discovered his inn behind him. Suitable reading for today.

Elena arrived and offered a biscuit.

“My father hasn't agreed to everything.”

“Would you like to tell me?”

“Yes. Let me sit first.”

She described the broken biscuit, and nearly saying perhaps another time simply to shorten a silence. Adrian didn't congratulate her on escaping her family. He listened, then shared half his biscuit back.

“You needn't be wonderfully energetic tonight.”

“I'm tired, and I want to go out with you. Both are possible.”

He smiled.

“Then I'll accompany the half that wants to go out. The tired half can ask us to stop whenever she likes.”

She nudged his shoulder lightly. “Barely acceptable.”

He hadn't asked her to choose between being tired and wanting his company. Neither feeling canceled the other. She could bring both with her, without making the evening a performance of how well she'd handled the morning.

The riverside restaurant had no window tables left. Recognizing her name, a waiter began to ask an earlier couple to move. Elena stopped him. Their table stood near the kitchen, where someone was vigorously reprimanding a leaking ladle.

“Perfect for discussing the future,” Adrian said.

“Because it's quiet?”

“It reminds us we'll probably need spoons.”

Smiling, she opened her folder. He expected a betrothal draft. His heart stumbled. Instead, two drawings showed available rental homes: one near work, another with wider windowsills.

“Nothing paid yet,” she said. “Nobody instructed to move your possessions.”

A tiny cup stood beside one drawn window. When he smiled, she explained that it indicated scale, not an attempt to make the drawing affectionate.

The nearer property had steep stairs. He didn't want every homecoming to resemble climbing a tower. She worried about the longer evening walk to the other one. They each explained, without immediately yielding.

Once, he would have thought criticizing stairs ungrateful when she was offering a shared home. Now she pushed the pencil toward him. Which route had she drawn wrongly? None, he explained. He wanted a home reasonably close to the ordinary work he might undertake himself.

“Not every job will be at your family's estate.”

After a moment, she nodded. “Then we'll find a third possibility. We needn't choose tonight so one of us can endure it every morning.”

He hadn't resigned or acquired a new career over dinner. But she hadn't written his income and choices into a footnote labeled family provision. That made him want to live with her more than the windowsill did.

Bringing cutlery, the waiter set the bill folder before Elena and asked whether her attendant required a simpler meal. There was no malice. Adrian stiffened anyway. Elena began to answer, noticed him looking up, and stopped.

“We're dining together. Please put the bill between us.”

The waiter apologized. After he'd gone, Adrian still watched the folder. Elena asked whether it bothered him. A little, he admitted, then felt ashamed of minding. She put down her fork. He needn't pretend no form of address could hurt.

“I don't want every meal you buy me to feel like joining your family establishment either.”

“Then don't give one dinner bill so much responsibility.”

She suggested each buying what they actually wanted to offer tonight. He'd buy dinner, she'd buy dessert. No hidden coins beneath the table to prove dignity survived. When they shared a home, they'd discuss shared expenses. The longer family name wouldn't determine who got to speak.

His name was short, Adrian observed. Under that system, he might only decide where to put the salt.

That might still be inconveniently far from the kitchen, she said. Worth discussing.

He moved the folder within both their reach. He'd once stood behind her at meals. Sitting opposite her didn't require pretending he'd never worn a servant's clothes. She knew the frightened man who counted guards. He hoped he could keep telling her about less dignified fears.

Their food arrived without soup. Each had assumed the other would order it. Elena requested a small bowl. Shared decisions apparently required somebody to speak. Adrian agreed and produced the page watered by the flowerpot.

She read the opening and laughed against the table.

“Respect the plant,” he pleaded. “It removed considerable nonsense.”

“I'm thanking it.”

She turned the page over without asking him to recite the surviving draft. The ladle stopped being scolded. Soup arrived. When her laughter settled, he held his cup and spoke about his real fear.

“I kept thinking I'd ask when every danger was over. Then I realized that meant letting danger decide when we could have a life.”

She listened. He couldn't promise always to know the right road, or recover every lost memory. Those facts had made him think commitment must wait until he became a sufficiently complete person.

“And now?”

“I want you to know that the person here today wants a serious future with you. I won't ask an earlier version of myself to sign for me. Or wait for a wiser future version to answer.”

Her hand left the bowl. She had fears too. That anyone needing help would always come before the person he'd promised dinner. That, in a hurry, she'd again mistake arranging everything for him for kindness.

“I can't promise never to do it,” she said. “You can tell me. I'll listen. You won't have to prove you love me enough before you're allowed to be unhappy.”

He nodded. When he wanted to escape a difficult conversation, he'd try telling her rather than finding a seventh urgent document. They didn't transcribe unlimited guarantees. The soup cooled. She drank and told him to eat. Honesty needn't require starvation.

Rain limited their walk to a short stretch of riverbank. They sheltered beside the bridge while droplets threaded from the roof. Adrian produced neither a ring nor an unexpected orchestra.

“Elena.”

She turned. He used her name often now. Today, it felt as though he'd put down every excessively careful introduction before finally reaching her.

“Will you be betrothed to me? By our choice, now, while we seriously prepare to marry. No transferring calamity. No proving anyone innocent.”

Her eyes brightened. “Are you asking me or House Rosenberg?”

“You. I'll be nervous about your father separately.”

She laughed, and tears came. “Then look at me first. Not as though you're waiting for a duke to read the result.”

He looked. She offered her hand and said yes. Not because of names in an old archive, or because he'd helped her survive September 20. She wanted his cup at breakfast. Arguments about windowsills and stairs. To stay when he behaved foolishly without first questioning whether she had the right.

“I want to marry you too.” He blushed. “That was probably already in the question.”

“Repeating it doesn't count as duplicate testimony.”

So he said it again, carefully. She stepped closer and asked to kiss him. He agreed before she raised her face. No fall, misunderstanding, or accident requiring an explanation to the capital. Just two people who knew what they were doing, and rain around them.

Walking home, they discussed the practical next steps. They would prepare the necessary arrangements themselves and each tell the people close to them. Their four friends wouldn't be required to celebrate immediately.

“We'll look at rings together,” Elena said.

He agreed. Neither had to guess what the other wanted and turn a surprise into an obligation. The wedding date could wait for their actual schedules too. They wouldn't ask a ceremonial officer to calculate the most advantageous day and then fit their lives around the answer.

Martha sighed at their wet shoes, then noticed Elena's smile.

“Did the plant rescue your opening?” she asked Adrian.

“Possibly me as well.”

Elena asked what sort of plant. Ordinary mint. It deserved a tray that didn't leak, she decided.

Adrian agreed. At last, they'd found the first small thing for their shared future that both of them wanted to buy.
