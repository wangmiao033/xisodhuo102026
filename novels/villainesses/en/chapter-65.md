# Why Do All the Villainesses Want to Marry Me?
## Volume Three: The Knight Says She's Responsible for Me
### Chapter 65: Captain, Your Protection Plan Has No Off-Duty Hours

Before Greymarch's supply meeting began on November 13, Adrian Reed had an argument with a chair.

Viola had chosen a position against a thick wall, close to a side door. It offered a view of the chairman's table and an immediate exit in an emergency.

The only problem was the broad-shouldered sergeant in the row ahead.

Once seated, Adrian could see nothing but a particularly stubborn loose thread on the man's uniform.

“I'd like to see as well as hear,” he murmured.

Viola checked and moved his chair six inches.

Elena sat beside him and caught his sleeve before he could shift it farther.

“Any more and you'll be in the aisle. You aren't here to be evacuated.”

“I'm thoroughly prepared for it.”

The captain's mouth had just begun to relax when footsteps at the door made her straighten.

Major Garrick Voss didn't arrive with a roomful of attendants. He was about forty, with an old scar interrupting his left eyebrow. He placed his hand warmer in front of a clerk whose fingers looked blue, then took his position beside the map.

“Two posts at the pass are waiting for fresh grain. The town's carters want to know whose instructions they're supposed to follow.”

He skipped the courtesies and pointed at the agenda.

“Refer the local supply dispute for subsequent review. For now, conclude the provisional debate and resume preparations for Willowford transport. Calculate what's due under the current supplement. No further unilateral changes to quantities.”

Someone sighed with relief. The clerk turned a page.

Viola remained standing.

“Major, I request correction of the expected quantities before those preparations resume.”

Voss looked at her.

“You submitted a request yesterday.”

“It identifies the same six hundred pounds counted twice. Today's agenda still uses the original total.”

She passed the WF-18 comparison copies to the clerk. The twelve sack tags, the merchant's November 4 copy, and the municipal weight receipt were placed on the table. Adrian and Elena remained in the witnesses' seats without answering for her.

Voss read the papers and turned to Rusk.

“Your acceptance record?”

“The material found so far doesn't establish that these six hundred pounds reached the depot. The summary treated a planned transfer as completed receipt, then deducted the same suspended shipment separately. I've requested the original acceptance register.”

The lieutenant didn't sound pleased, especially with so many people present. But he didn't dilute yesterday's finding into a vague remark about figures awaiting confirmation.

Viola's hand loosened slightly on the table edge.

“The old total cannot continue to support the claim that two separate consignments disappeared,” she said. “Secondly, Willowford's stone bridge remains unrepaired. The supplement routes residents across it with the wagons and into the pass beyond. That route is still dangerous.”

Voss turned the map.

“If these two posts withdraw for lack of grain, how long do you imagine your safe river road will remain safe?”

His fingertip struck the paper hard enough to make it tap.

“I cannot send everyone off to escort furniture.”

“Keep the men guarding the pass,” Viola said. “I'm requesting daylight preparations for civilian transport, with escort inside our current patrol area. The lower river road avoids the bridge. There are people in that village who can't move quickly.”

“Everyone can produce a reason. Eventually, not a single order gets carried out in full.”

The room went still. Even the sergeant blocking Adrian's view stopped worrying at his loose thread.

Viola paused.

“The bridge pier is cracked. The grain has been counted twice. Neither fact changes because I'm under investigation. Please include them in today's orders.”

She hadn't raised her voice. The air in the room seemed to draw taut anyway.

Voss stared at her without moving.

Ada rose from the civil representatives' bench and unfolded the registered pickup list. Some residents needed to bring in elderly relatives. Some were sleeping in their families' kitchens. One patient was still waiting for suitable transport.

“These people don't disappear when the meeting adjourns,” she said. “The town will arrange the wagons. I need to know where the patrol boundary lies.”

Voss picked up the bridge inspection report and turned to the diagram of the cracks prepared jointly by the wheelwright and the inspecting officer.

A log split in the fireplace. Adrian didn't hurry him. Nor did he try to recall some impassioned speech from the game that might purchase victory here.

Finally, the major lowered the report.

“The lower river road, in daylight. Use the old oilpress's stone courtyard as a collection point. It's inside our existing patrol line. Register the civilian wagons first. The men at the pass remain in place.”

Viola nodded and waited.

“You retain your existing provisional patrol authority. You may protect an evacuation in a local emergency. You may not use that as grounds to pursue anyone outside your jurisdiction. Report route changes to the duty officer.”

“Understood.”

“I'll send a courier to verify the supplement at the issuing office.”

He had the clerk specify the November 4 reference and request the main text, the sequence of annexes, and the delivery record. The courier would change horses along the route; the round trip would still take several days.

Finally, Voss looked back at Viola.

“The matters already under review remain there. Permission to prepare today doesn't establish that all your previous decisions were correct.”

“I'll account for them separately.”

Her chair made a soft sound as she sat. Only then did Adrian discover he'd been gripping his untouched cup throughout.

Voices filled the corridor after the meeting. Voss kept Rusk behind to organize actual deliveries. Ada took the municipal clerks to check available wagons and rooms. At last, the two kinds of work were no longer tethered to the same mistaken figure.

Owen caught up with Viola.

“Your expression didn't change once,” he said quietly.

“My back is soaked in sweat.”

The corporal stared.

“Don't advertise it.”

Adrian couldn't help laughing. When Viola looked at him, he raised his empty hands.

“I haven't put it in my statement.”

Her face finally relaxed. A tired brightness returned to her eyes.

At the stairs, Rusk caught up and gave Owen a little bag of chalk.

“The old road signs have split in the frost. Mark fresh arrows at the forks. Don't let the drivers decide the mountain road looks shorter.”

Owen took it and asked whether the grain wagons would also use the river road.

“The dry grain has its own convoy. Attend to your civilian wagons first.”

Rusk looked at Adrian.

“The clerk has preserved both sources we corrected yesterday. Next time you find something wrong, submit it promptly.”

“We intend to.”

Rusk nodded and left. No handshake. No sudden warm declarations of friendship. He had brought the chalk and said what needed saying.

Watching him, Viola slowly breathed out.

“I was afraid he'd only say the accounts weren't fully checked.”

“They aren't,” Adrian said. “But he told them what he had checked.”

She nodded and touched the little brush in her pocket, apparently remembering that she was carrying a gift poorly suited to use as a weapon.

“When you gave me this, did you already know I'd be nervous?”

“When I gave it to you, all I knew was that someone had to clean your armor.”

She lowered her head with a brief laugh, removed her hand from her pocket, and continued downstairs.

That afternoon, they accompanied the civil officials to inspect the old oilpress. Thick stone walls sheltered the courtyard. Two rows of old oil barrels still stood beneath the eaves. The clerks planned to clear them away and lay straw for arriving villagers to rest on.

Elena discussed temporary rental of the empty shed with its owner, asking the price and where the roof leaked instead of immediately promising to pay every unspecified bill. Her injured hand stayed inside her shawl. Hired workers handled the lifting.

Adrian received a light task: stand inside the entrance and indicate how much wagon clearance the men carrying long boards needed to leave. He was rather pleased with himself until an elderly carpenter called him a useful “talking doorframe.”

Viola passed just then.

“More appropriate than letting him hold up a grain wagon,” she said seriously.

He decided against seeking any more promotions today.

During the break, she brought him a new schedule assigning escorts from morning departure until his return at night. The last line even stated that if he needed water downstairs after dark, he should inform the duty attendant first.

Adrian reached it and looked up.

“Captain, I don't agree to round-the-clock close protection.”

Viola stopped.

“People were asking about your movements two days ago. Today you attended the meeting.”

“I know I need to be careful. I'll use routes confirmed safe and follow instructions when there's immediate danger. But I don't want to report to somebody every time I get out of a chair.”

She looked at the schedule as though discovering, for the first time, that it contained more than the safety she'd arranged.

“We'd work in shifts. You needn't worry about our rest.”

“It isn't that I don't want to trouble you.” He laid the paper gently on the table. “I want time alone with Elena. I want to sit by a window doing nothing. If I'm in danger, I'll be grateful for your protection. The rest of the time, please ask me first.”

Viola didn't answer at once.

Someone tried to shift an empty barrel in the yard. Its base scraped across the stones for a long time. Her gaze stayed on the line she'd written about nighttime. Her ears slowly reddened.

“I thought more detail meant fewer gaps.”

“It's certainly detailed. Even my water jug has no freedom.”

She looked at him, finally showing a little resignation.

“The jug wouldn't have to report.”

“Then I'd like equal treatment with the jug.”

She really laughed this time. Then she crossed out the nighttime entry.

Elena had sat quietly beside them, neither accepting nor refusing for him. When Viola asked about their plans for tomorrow, she said they were willing to come by carriage and help with registration, provided the town confirmed the courtyard was suitable as an aid point.

“We won't be following the patrol into the village.”

“Then we'll agree on a meeting time, with someone who knows the ground to guide you,” Viola said. “If conditions change, we discuss it again.”

Adrian nodded.

The captain folded the schedule without pressing it on him. She still seemed unaccustomed to having a carefully made plan refused. Her fingers rested on the crease before she went to find Owen.

Once she was out of earshot, Elena plucked a piece of straw from Adrian's cuff.

“You really want time alone with me?”

“Consider that a formal application.”

“Approved.”

She didn't make the application wait until dinner. They sat against a sheltered wall and shared a baked pear bought by one of the clerks. Adrian used a small spoon to cut the hot, softened fruit for her. She raised the spoon and offered him the first taste.

The sweet juice was hot enough to make him mumble. She laughed, then hastily handed him water.

“No unnecessary bravery during observation.”

“Have you and the captain exchanged training notes?”

“I'm responsible for the baked-pear section.”

Viola passed the courtyard gate. Seeing him blow on Elena's next spoonful, she slowed. Adrian looked up, thinking she had something to say. She only indicated the sky and reminded the clerk not to leave their return until sunset.

This time, she set no deadline for finishing the pear.

Before dusk, the town council sent an ordinary request for relief. With Willowford residents already displaced into Greymarch, the infirmary needed more beds and nursing help. The capital relief liaison office was asked for medical supplies, bedding, and qualified assistants.

Hearing the familiar office's name, Adrian thought of Cecilia. He asked about the relay: the express letter should reach the capital early the next morning. Riders and loaded wagons traveled at different speeds. Even if someone could come, the journey required proper planning.

In a separate personal note, he wrote that he and Elena were safe and included the town's receiving address. He didn't write, Come immediately. She had made promises to people there, too.

The formal request and private letter were sealed separately. The courier left before dark, hoofbeats receding down the street.

Dinner had barely reached the inn table when Owen returned.

Fresh mud marked his boots. He hadn't drawn a weapon, but his expression made Adrian lower his spoon.

“A firewood hauler on the lower road saw several unfamiliar riders. They stopped on the high ground outside Willowford at dusk. We've sent someone to check. No report yet.”

“Any fighting?” Viola asked.

“None confirmed.”

She stood, sending Owen to inform the duty officer and recheck tomorrow morning's civilian wagon arrangements. Elena wrapped the bread she hadn't finished. Adrian touched the brass whistle against his chest and left it beneath his collar.

No alarm bell had sounded.

The last daylight faded beyond the window. Viola reached the door and looked back at them.

“Wait for the road report before setting out tomorrow.”

“We will,” Adrian said.

Half a bowl of hot soup remained on the table.

This time, he hoped she'd be back in time for someone to warm it for her.
