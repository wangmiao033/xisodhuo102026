# Chapter 96: Lady Elena Doesn't Trust a Pretty Reason

On the morning of December 11, Adrian came downstairs to find Luna fighting a drawer.

She wasn't using magic. One foot braced the cabinet, one hand pulled the handle, and the other protected an ink bottle from falling. There was very little mystery about her.

“Need help?” he asked.

“No. I'm demonstrating the dignity of research.”

The drawer shrieked and abruptly came free. Its top sheaf slid to the floor. The dignity of research crouched to pick it up.

Adrian steadied the ink bottle without opening any papers. Luna looked up and asked how the night had gone. He told her he'd slept reasonably well, with no new warmth or chest tightness, and would make today's medical contact as arranged.

“And dinner?”

“Very good.”

“No diagrams?”

“None. Elena also banned the captain from bringing a fourth compass to the table.”

Luna sighed. “I've lost to a man who can't even find his way.”

“He wasn't there.”

“An even more comprehensive defeat.”

Her tone stayed light, but her gaze flicked toward his gloved left hand. It was brief enough that, before yesterday, he might never have noticed.

Elena came in from the porch and put two merchant letters into her own folder. She had been dealing with House Rosenberg's audit correspondence; his examination hadn't become the only thing she was allowed to do all day. Seeing Luna gathering papers, she waited until the drawer closed.

“I'd like to ask about yesterday's application.”

Luna straightened.

“Which part?”

“You intended to study temporal residue before we arrived. When you proposed examining his scar, were you helping us answer our questions, or looking for someone who could answer yours?”

Adrian turned to her. Elena hadn't framed the question as an accusation she could already prove. Nor did his presence make her retreat from it.

Luna removed the short pen from behind her ear.

“Possibly both.”

“Then why did we hear only the first before signing?”

Dishes clattered in the dining room below. Luna rolled the pen between her fingers, then stopped.

“I told you I wanted to understand the old instruments.”

“That's a research interest. What the application could give you, and what losing it would mean, are different questions.”

Luna looked at Adrian. “Do you want to see it too?”

“The parts relevant to my involvement. If there's anyone else's personal information, cover it first. I don't want to find out who's concealing things by snooping.”

She nodded and went upstairs for her notebook. She didn't demand an apology from Elena, or pour out every private paper as proof of honesty.

Half an hour later, they sat at the wide table. Cecilia joined them after finishing her correspondence with the clinic. Viola came to the door and asked whether she was needed; Adrian invited her to listen. Yesterday's equipment cabinet remained locked. His healthy appearance hadn't become permission to open it.

Luna brought out two documents. The first was a research proposal submitted to the regional academy on November 19. The other was the equipment scheduling draft she'd left unsent on December 9. The earlier document concerned old recording media, delayed layered traces, and the search for suitable observation conditions. It contained no name, scar drawing, or itinerary belonging to Adrian. The administrator's outgoing register could verify the date and number of attached pages.

“The proposal had already gone before your inquiry arrived,” she said. “I didn't know you were coming. Nobody gave me your medical history in advance.”

Elena checked the dates and acknowledged that the record supported this.

“So this wasn't an invitation arranged specifically to draw him in.”

“No.”

“But when he arrived, you wanted to include him.”

Luna didn't answer at once. She turned to the middle of the proposal and uncovered a paragraph: if a suitable, willing participant became available, a separate near-subject observation plan would be presented to that person for specific informed agreement, to distinguish an instrument's spontaneous aftereffects from a carried state.

Below it was another line. Preliminary findings submitted before the year's end could qualify the work for consideration for dedicated instruments in the next quarter.

“Supplementary materials are due on the fifteenth,” she said. “That isn't a date you're required to stay until. Without results, I can submit the old-instrument work alone. It would probably reduce my chances of getting dedicated equipment.”

Adrian remembered the temperature-controlled cupboard struck from the schedule, and Luna running up and down over the wrongly sized bearing. All those things that weren't quite sufficient acquired another weight.

“So you need more than an answer.”

“I need a place and equipment to keep looking for answers.”

“Why didn't you say that yesterday?”

Her fingers pressed the corner of the paper.

“I thought it didn't change the twelve-second limit or where the light would fall. I considered it my business.”

“But it changes how badly you want me to continue.”

She didn't move her hand. A white crease appeared beneath her fingers.

“Yes.”

Elena leaned back a little. She had clearly prepared more questions, but the admission stopped her from immediately pursuing them.

Cecilia read the whole application and noted the dates on a separate sheet. First she asked something easier to establish than motive.

“Has yesterday's result been sent out?”

“No. It isn't in any external attachment, and nobody has been shown the scar drawing. You can check the outgoing register and the records for the sealed mailbags. The equipment draft hasn't gone either.”

Luna asked the administrator to bring the relevant records. He explained which letters had been sent and which remained pending. After checking, Elena changed the question on her notes to a provisional finding that no transmission had been found. She didn't preserve a suspicion as an offense that had already occurred.

It didn't answer everything.

“Did you regard him as material you had to use before the fifteenth?” she asked.

Luna looked up, plainly irritated for the first time.

“I stopped yesterday.”

“I saw you. That's why I'm still sitting here asking.”

“You care about him, so you don't want him taking risks. I need results, so I hope he'll participate. Why is only the second motive required to stand under a light?”

The room went quiet. Adrian was about to speak when Elena placed her hand beside her notes, without reaching for him.

“He knows I care about him. I told him earlier that I wanted to refuse on his behalf. If I used a Rosenberg bill to imply he couldn't stay unless he agreed, you should question me too.”

Her voice didn't rise.

“That doesn't answer why you didn't tell him about the deadline.”

Luna looked away. The plant by the window had finally been watered. Its soil was damp, but its brown leaf tips hadn't instantly become green.

After a while, she faced Adrian again.

“I made the part that benefits me sound smaller than it is.”

“What else do you want from me?”

“Right now, to find out whether yesterday's traces can be distinguished from ordinary material errors. Beyond that depends on the evidence. I want to establish that certain old instruments really did preserve different states, rather than having every anomaly dismissed as a copying mistake.”

“That's your hypothesis.”

“Yes. Not a fact you're obliged to prove for me.”

“What if the two shadows hadn't appeared yesterday?” Adrian asked.

“I would have been disappointed. Then I would still have had to write that nothing was found.”

“What if I never participate again?”

She looked at the empty spaces in her application. “I'd have to find another approach. There might not be one for a while.”

This time, she didn't say there would always be somebody else. He heard the weight in the pause. He also heard her refrain from putting it on his shoulders.

Elena laid the application beside yesterday's record. One described what Luna hoped to obtain. The other described what she'd actually seen. No elegant arrow connected the two. Elena pointed out that the stop at eight seconds couldn't be erased, and the unresolved possibility of material error couldn't quietly vanish from a future application.

“If use is permitted later, these go with it.”

“I understand.”

“Understanding now won't mean you never get impatient again.”

“Then I'll need someone to remind me.” Luna looked at the three women across from her and added softly, “Apparently that's one resource we have in abundance.”

Adrian looked down at his glove. The witch in the game could always lead people onward with laughter. A player clicked an option, and another beautiful picture appeared. Now he sat on the other side of her table and understood what the game had skipped. It wasn't a tedious procedure. It was the part someone hadn't agreed to yet.

“I don't require helping me to be your only reason for doing research,” he said. “Having ambition doesn't make everything you did yesterday false. But I need to know what you're inviting me to help answer, and why you're in such a hurry.”

Her lips moved as though she might catch the sentence with a joke about proposals. She let it pass.

“Is it too late to tell you now?”

“Not too late to decide the next step. It can't turn yesterday into something I already knew.”

Luna nodded. She drew the unsent draft out of its folder. Instead of tearing it up, she added the date and reason it had been suspended and put it back where it belonged, preserving what had happened.

“I won't use your result to meet the fifteenth,” she said. “Even if you later agree to continue, we'll discuss the use and my interests first. Yesterday's material stays within the recording and checks you've already allowed.”

Elena didn't immediately say she believed her. She asked Luna to put that addition in writing for Adrian, then asked whether somebody who wouldn't gain equipment from the proposal could take part in reviewing the next step.

The witch was silent for longer this time.

“That will be slower.”

“Yes,” Adrian said.

She looked at him and finally smiled, with real resignation behind it.

“You don't vary your wording much when you refuse.”

“Some answers are worth using twice.”

They broke for a while so the administrator could make copies of the relevant pages. Viola went to collect the military acknowledgment that had arrived for her. Cecilia stayed to examine the review process mentioned in the application. Adrian and Elena went out onto the porch for air. The winter wind lifted one end of her scarf.

“Part of it was jealousy,” she said first. “When she looks at you, she always seems to see something the rest of us can't.”

“You noticed the application cover nobody else did.”

“I'm not sure that's a comparison worth complimenting.”

He laughed and freed her scarf from the door, asking before settling it properly.

“Then I'll try another one. I'm glad you asked whether I wanted to see the papers instead of keeping me outside the conversation.”

Her expression softened. “I almost went straight to the administrator.”

“You didn't.”

“I don't want to learn how to protect you and forget you can answer for yourself.”

Adrian looked at her. Over dinner last night, she'd complained about a merchant manager who had turned transport costs into an endless list of extras. They'd shared the last piece of baked apple. Today's suspicion hadn't canceled those ordinary things, and they didn't need a new promise to prove themselves.

“Are we still reading tonight?” he asked.

“Yes. The captain can't stay in port forever because the witch needs equipment.”

Luna happened to open the door just then. Hearing her name, she lifted the watering can in her hand.

“Please allow the witch a few ordinary uses. Such as watering a plant.”

Elena looked at her and finally let a small smile appear.

“No additional procedures as necessary this time.”

“I'll ask before it receives a second can.”

The joke didn't remove the problem. It did let them return to the table together instead of leaving with their own attractive reasons.

Cecilia had laid out two papers: yesterday's clearly written stopping conditions and the academy application's provisions for participants, records, and later uses. She hadn't applied a saint's seal or announced anyone innocent or guilty.

“I'd like to continue working through these questions this afternoon,” she said. “Dr. Hensley should join the later review too. For now, let's have the administrator bring the relevant research rules. This isn't another explanation of how long the light stays on.”

Luna set down the watering can. “What else do you want to ask?”

Cecilia looked at the agreement bearing both their names.

“When you need a result most, who makes sure the person who says no still has somewhere to go?”

Slowly, Adrian put his own folder beside hers.

Yesterday, they had stopped an examination light. Now he wanted to know whether a researcher's wishes could stop at the place where somebody else's agreement ended.
